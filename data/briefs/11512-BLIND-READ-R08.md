### F2. 0049 @1167C — *cera mellis* for *cera mollis* (class 5 / Pattern 18 candidate)
- **Latin:** `Quis vero nesciat quod mel dulce, cera mellis est?`
- **English as it stands:** `But who does not know that honey is sweet, that wax is of honey?`
- **Proposed:** `But who does not know that honey is sweet, that wax is of honey [cj: *mellis*; read *mollis*, "soft"]?`
- **Why:** the next sentence expounds exactly this pair: Christ *dulcis* from his divinity and *mollis atque tractabilis* from his humility, then *quasi ceram mollem*. The work's own gloss three chunks later confirms the pair: 0052 @1178C `In favo mel et cera est, mel vero dulcis, cera est mollis et flexibilis`. The question sets up "honey is sweet, wax is soft". The English is a literal rendering and is not false, so this falls under Pattern 18 only if the bar is "misleads". It is weaker than a polarity case.
- **Confidence:** probable
- **Already in the cruces?** YES. `cruces-0045.md` 1167C: plate confirms *mellis*, *mollis* wanted, "crux only". What I add: the work's own gloss at 1178C (*cera est mollis et flexibilis*) settles the intended word. Whether it clears the Pattern 18 bar (does the English assert something false?) is for the adjudicator. I would not re-raise it if the ruling stands.

### F3. 0050 @1172A–C — *Cypri/Cyprus* rendered "Cypress"; the same work renders it "Cyprus" elsewhere and keeps "cypress" for *cypressus* (class 5: lemma word swapped, within-work drift)
- **Latin:** VERS. 14 `*Cypri cum nardo, nardus et crocus, …*` (twice, lemma + requote); `*Cyprus* est arbor aromatica, … et interpretatur *moeror* vel *tristitia. Cyprus* ergo in Ecclesia est quaevis sancta anima`
- **English as it stands (4 sites in 0050):** `VERSE 14.-- *Cypress with spikenard,` · `*Cypress,* he says, *with spikenard,` · `*Cypress* is an aromatic tree,` · `*Cypress* therefore in the Church`
- **Proposed:** `Cyprus` at all four (`VERSE 14.-- *Cyprus with spikenard, …`, `*Cyprus,* he says, *with spikenard, …`, `*Cyprus* is an aromatic tree, …`, `*Cyprus* therefore in the Church`), matching 0049 @1168D `namely *of Cyprus, spikenard and saffron,…*` and 0023 (`A cluster of Cyprus…`, `For Cyprus is interpreted…`).
- **Why:** the Latin is *cyprus* (the henna shrub). This work uses "cypress" for a different tree, *cypressus* (0025 `cypressina`, 0026 `cedrus et cypressus`). So "Cypress" here names a tree the plate does not print, and it breaks the within-work single voice the Song ruling requires. The etymology *moeror/tristitia* is the one 0023 attaches to "Cyprus". 0004 `a cluster of cypress` (Latin `cypri`) is the same drift, outside my range, and I did not read it for context.
- **Confidence:** probable (a terminology and consistency defect, not a false assertion)
- **Already in the cruces?** NO as a defect. ⚑ `cruces-0020.md` records the convention: *"Ct 1:13 = A cluster of Cyprus … (the gloss interprets Cyprus as tristitia, so the island name stays)"*. 0050 breaks that logged convention on the same etymology. `cruces-0050.md` 1172A notes only the lemma's shape.

### F4. 0053 @1182B: *apud nosmetipsos* rendered "equal with ourselves" (class 3: mis-parsed prepositional phrase)
- **Latin:** `quia et in ipsas easdem virtutes, quas diximus, fidem scilicet, spem, charitatem atque operationem, quandiu in hac vita vivimus, aequales esse apud nosmetipsos inveniemus.`
- **English as it stands:** `we shall find that they are equal with ourselves.`
- **Proposed:** `we shall find that they are equal in ourselves.`
- **Why:** the image is the square: the four sides are equal to *one another*. The following sentence spells that out (*tantum credit quantum sperat…*). *apud nos* is "in us / among ourselves", the place where they are found equal. "Equal with ourselves" reads as "equal to us", which the Latin does not say.
- **Confidence:** probable
- **Already in the cruces?** NO.

### F5. 0054 @1187D: *Spiritus Patri loquitur* rendered "the Spirit speaks to the Father" (class 2, who does what to whom; Pattern 18)
- **Latin:** `Ubi autem Spiritus Patri loquitur, [1188A] loquitur et Filius, quia quorum una est substantia, una est et operatio.`
- **English as it stands:** `But where the Spirit speaks to the Father, [1188A] the Son also speaks,`
- **Proposed:** `But where the Spirit speaks to the Father [cj: *Patri*; read *Patris*, "of the Father"], [1188A] the Son also speaks,`
- **Why:** the sentence glosses the verse quoted just before it, *Spiritus Patris vestri qui loquitur in vobis* (Matt 10:20). The argument is that the Father's Spirit speaks in the preacher, and so the Son speaks too, since the persons share one operation. The literal dative makes the English say that the Spirit addresses the Father, which the author does not say. The printed word is real, so this is `[cj:]` and not `[sic:]`.
- **Confidence:** probable (near certain on the sense; whether the defect is Migne's or our TEI is unchecked at the plate)
- **Already in the cruces?** YES, declined. `cruces-0050.md` 1187D: plate p. 600 confirms *Patri*, "probably *Patris*. Not a negation or correlative; crux only." ⚑ Pattern 18a says that "negations and correlatives only" is the *misstated* bar. The actual test is whether the English asserts something false, and wrong speaker/addressee is on the ✅ list (the 0502B *Sponsus/Sponsa* precedent). "The Spirit speaks to the Father" asserts what the author does not. This site was declined on the wrong bar, so I recommend the `[cj:]`.

### F6. 0054 @1186B: "I can not absurdly refer" reads as "I cannot" (class 1, polarity misreadable in the English)
- **Latin:** `Unde et hanc eamdem sententiam quam ad memetipsam retuli, non absurde ad auditores quoque meos referre possum,`
- **English as it stands:** `I can not absurdly refer also to my hearers,`
- **Proposed:** `I can, not absurdly, refer also to my hearers,`
- **Why:** *non* negates *absurde*, not *possum*. Printed as "can not", the English naturally reads as "cannot", which reverses the claim: the bride *can* apply the verse to her hearers, and she goes straight on to do so.
- **Confidence:** probable (the sense is not mistranslated, but the surface English reverses it for a reader)
- **Already in the cruces?** NO (an English-surface issue; the translation of *non absurde* is right).

### F7. 0054 @1187B: VERS. 6 lemma, *Anima liquefacta est ut dilectus locutus est* given two "my"s the plate does not print (class 5, lemma conformed, Pattern 7a″)
- **Latin:** `Anima liquefacta est ut dilectus locutus est: quaesivi et non inveni illum;`
- **English as it stands:** `My soul melted when my beloved spoke: I sought and found him not;`
- **Proposed:** `The soul melted when the beloved spoke: I sought and found him not;`
- **Why:** Migne's lemma drops the Vulgate's *mea* and adds *dilectus* (Vulg. *anima mea liquefacta est ut locutus est*). The English restores the familiar "My soul" and adds a second "my" before "beloved". Every other possessive in this verse (*ostii mei*, *dilecto meo*) is printed. Here none is. Optionally add `[var: Vulg. *anima mea liquefacta est ut locutus est*, "my soul melted when he spoke"]`.
- **Confidence:** probable
- **Already in the cruces?** PARTLY. `cruces-0050.md` 1187B logs the Latin's divergence from the Vulgate (*mea* omitted, *dilectus* supplied, "Same sense; no marker"). It does not address the English restoring "My" and adding "my beloved", which is the conformation.
