## Candidates for adjudication

### A. 0002 · 0408B · class 3 (whose will) · probable-weak
- **Latin:** *post hanc aetatem dominus ejus illam capere potest, cum sua voluntate.*
- **English now:** "after this age her lord can take her, with his own will."
- **Proposal:** keep the English, and log a crux (if there isn't one) saying that *sua* may be the
  girl's will. The alternative is "with her own will". I would only make that change if the
  orchestrator reads the reflexive by sense rather than by grammar.
- **Why:** strictly, the reflexive points to the subject *dominus*, so "his" follows the print. But
  whose consent is required is the legal point of the sentence. The chapter heading is about the young
  governing themselves (*seipsos gubernare*). The next sentence insists on the son's own will (*absque
  filii voluntate*). The Theodorian parallel requires the girl's consent. In a calqued Latin, a loose
  *suus* is plausible. If the English is wrong here, a consent rule is turned round and still reads like
  law. Adding "own" makes the lord reading more emphatic than the Latin requires.
- **Cruces (checked after):** ALREADY LOGGED at cruces-0000 0408B. It keeps the grammar, names the
  likely sense "with her consent", and records "Not repaired". Nothing new here unless the
  orchestrator wants to reverse that.

### B. 0002 · 0409B · class 2/1 (tariff logic) · the Latin is probably defective, the English is faithful
- **Latin:** *Si quis praemium aliquod magnum saltem diabolis immolet, jejunet per annum; si aliquid magnum sacrificet, jejunet decem hiemes*
- **English now:** "If anyone sacrifices some great reward at least to devils, let him fast for a year; if he sacrifices anything great, let him fast ten winters"
- **Proposal:** probably crux only. If the orchestrator wants a gloss: "some great [cj: *magnum*; read
  *parvum*, "small"] reward".
- **Why:** as printed, the two tiers do not contrast: something great costs one year, and something
  great costs ten. The source tradition (Theodore, *in minimis* one year / *in magnis* ten) and the
  *saltem* ("even") both point to "small" in the first tier. This is a word substitution, not a
  negation, so it is outside the narrow Pattern 18 practice. I am raising it because a penitential
  tariff is exactly where such a slip reads as law.
- **Cruces (checked after):** PARTLY LOGGED. cruces-0000 0409B says the sense is "strained" and notes
  the *immolet* patch. It does **not** record that the two tiers do not contrast (great → one year,
  great → ten winters), and it does not give the *in minimis / in magnis* parallel. **This is the one
  new point in my report.** It is a crux addition at least.

### C. 0002 · 0410B · class 1 (a possible negation lost in the Latin) · the English is faithful
- **Latin:** *Ita Romanus abit ad eucharistiam, nisi quod excommunicet eum qui nequeunt.*
- **English now:** "So the Roman goes to the Eucharist, except that he excommunicates him who cannot."
- **Proposal:** crux only. The Theodorian parallel reads *qui autem noluerint, non excommunicantur*
  ("those who do not wish to are not excommunicated"). The contrast with the Greek rule just before it
  (three Sundays missed means excommunication) needs that negative sense.
- **Why:** the English renders the print correctly, and *nisi quod* may itself be carrying the negation
  in calqued Latin. The number clash (*eum … nequeunt*) is concealed by English "cannot", which could
  be singular or plural. That is minor and not a marker case.
- **Cruces (checked after):** ALREADY LOGGED at cruces-0000 0410B: the number clash, the Theodore
  parallel, the possible lost negative, and the decline for `[cj:]`. Nothing new.

### D. 0001 · 0407D · class 1-adjacent (a possibly missing *in-*) · the English is faithful
- **Latin:** *vel alteruter eorum sit infidelis, vel sit sanus, attamen cum consensu mutuo separentur si velint.*
- **English now:** "or either of them is an unbeliever, or is healthy, yet with mutual consent let them be separated if they wish."
- **Proposal:** crux only (*sanus* for *insanus*?). As printed, "being healthy" is listed as a ground
  for separating, which is nonsense. This is not a Pattern 18 case (it is not a negation word the plate
  prints), and the sentence is loose throughout.
- **Cruces (checked after):** ALREADY LOGGED at cruces-0000 0407D, plate-confirmed and declined for
  `[cj:]` under 18a. Nothing new.

## Candidates considered and rejected

- **0000 · 0404B, VI, *detineatur*.** The English "is held back on his journey" is indicative, not a
  command. I checked whether it should be "let him be held back". I rejected that: a penalty of being
  "detained on his journey" makes no sense. The clause reads as the reason for the refusal, which is
  the chapter heading's *ob itineris sui necessitatem*. It also sits alongside *proficiscatur*, which
  is equally subjunctive and correctly rendered "travels". The English is right. (Cruces, checked
  after: logged at cruces-0000 0404B, reaching the same conclusion.)
- **0001 · 0406D, XX, *tertia etiam pars haereditatis ejus*, rendered "her inheritance".** The *ejus*
  is ambiguous and the English has to pick one. "Her" fits the logic: the husband is compensated
  because the wife leaves. Not reported. (Cruces, checked after: not logged. It is a small
  disambiguation that could go in a crux line if wanted.)
- **0002 · 0409B, XXXI, *Uxor non deserat maritum suum, licet adulteretur*, rendered "although he
  commit adultery".** Grammatically the default subject would be the wife, but that reading is
  incoherent. The husband reading matches the source tradition (*licet sit fornicator*). Rejected.
  (Cruces, checked after: logged at cruces-0000 0409B.)
- **0002 · 0411A, XXXVIII, *neque … non sunt edendae*.** This is already handled correctly with a
  `[cj:]` of the approved form: the printed words come first, verbatim, and "read …" follows. The
  `[var:]` on Acts 15:29 is also correctly placed and worded.
- **0002 · 0410B, *duas missas celebrare debes*.** The English "thou oughtest to celebrate two Masses
  daily" is faithful. The positive sense is odd but not demonstrably a lost negation. Rejected.
  (Cruces, checked after: logged at cruces-0000 0410B.)
- **0000 · 0402B, preface, *tibi expedit pudefieri … quam*, rendered "more profitable … than".**
  English supplies a comparative that Latin *expedit … quam* implies. This is idiom, not an added word.
  In 0003 V, *expedit … quam* is rendered "rather than", which is equally acceptable.
- **0003 · 0412D, II, *opinio esset hominis veram confessionem*.** *hominis* could go with *opinio*
  ("man's opinion") or with *confessionem*. The English takes it with *confessionem*. Either is
  defensible, and the sense does not change enough to matter.
- **0003 · 0412C, Sylvester, *nolumus ut … ejiciantur, et legem … annuntient*.** The English keeps
  "we do not wish" over both clauses, exactly as the Latin does. This is literal by rule, since the
  Latin does not construe.
- **Literal renderings the Latin forces, not reported:** *Furi necesse est* ("For a thief"); *sub
  Brunonis regulis* ("the rules of Bruno"); *inimicis* ("enemies", probably OE *feond* = fiends);
  *cum extis ejus*; *vel sit sanus* (D above); *Baptizatus [ Lege baptizans]*, which is Migne's own
  bracket and is carried.
- **Markers:** the letter-keyed `[cn: a | …]` notes at 0401 (Wilkins on the title) and 0409C (*In domo
  vertendum est*) each have a translated `[nt:]` twin in the same place. That is correct under
  Pattern 19, including the letter-key ruling.
- **Anchors** that step backwards (`[0401]` under I.1, `[0411]` under Pars altera I.1) and anchors
  placed a few words off in the English are not defects under the brief.
