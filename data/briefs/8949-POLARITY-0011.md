## Findings

### F1 — chunk 0018, col. 0230B — OURS. Unrendered `nisi`; the sentence asserts the contrary of its Latin.

Latin: *Fieri tanquam lapidem non est natura esse lapidem. **Non enim fieret nisi quod non erat.***
English: "To be made as a stone is not to be a stone by nature. **For that would not be made which was not.**"

The Latin is an exceptive: *nothing "becomes" except what it previously was not* — which is exactly what licenses the first sentence (they were made stone, therefore they were not stone by nature) and what the whole following argument needs, against those "qui malae naturae Pharaonem vel Aegyptios dicunt fuisse". The English drops `nisi` and turns the exception into a flat negation: "that would not be made which was not" says the opposite — that what was not, does not come to be. So rendered, the sentence contradicts the sentence immediately before it and destroys the anti-Marcionite argument that immediately follows (the stone-nature is temporary, *donec pertranseat populus tuus*). This class leaves no trace: the negation particle count matches (Latin one `non` + `nisi`, English one "not"), so nothing mechanical can see it.
Suggested sense: "For nothing would be made [such] except what was not [such]."

### F2 — chunk 0021, col. 0239A — MIGNE. `Nono` where the neighbour requires `Novo`.

Latin: ***Et mane.** **Nono die** quando Christus, sol justitiae, adventu suo credentibus reparavit perdita.*
English: "*And in the morning.* **On the ninth day,** when Christ, the sun of justice, by his coming restored to believers the things that were lost."

Immediate neighbour, four lines above (0238D): "sed adventu suo, cum sit sol justitiae, ***novum mane*** credentibus restauravit; ***novum,*** scilicet scientiae lumen". The lemma being glossed is *Et mane*; the gloss repeats that sentence almost verbatim (adventu suo — sol justitiae — credentibus — restauravit/reparavit). A "ninth day" has no referent anywhere in Cap. XVI and no place in the morning/evening/noon scheme the passage has just built (mane / meridie / ad vesperam). One letter: *Novo die* → *Nono die*. The English is literal, which is right; this belongs in the cruces, not in a repair of the English.

## Rejected candidates — what I looked at and why it stands

- **0011, 0210C, Origen's hardening series.** "in sexta plaga *non* dicitur … Rursus in septima … induratur quidem cor Pharaonis, sed *non* a Domino." Sixth/seventh/eighth/ninth all check out against each other and against the English; the sequence is internally consistent and the English carries every particle.
- **0011, 0210B, "Neque enim nihil."** English "For neither is it nothing" is clumsy but the double negative is intact and points the same way (the diversity does mean something).
- **0011, 0211C, Procopius.** "*ne* jumenta interirent omnia" … "Verum divina providentia *aliter* totum gubernavit negotium." Reads as self-contradiction but is Procopius correcting a proposed reason (chariot-horses for the pursuit) with a better one; the English follows the Latin exactly and adds nothing.
- **0012, 0215B, Augustine on the greater/lesser.** "etiam minus posse qui plus potest; *non autem continuo* cui minora conceduntur, posse majora" — asymmetry preserved in both directions in the English.
- **0013, 0216D, "Nec non locustarum … subducit exempla."** `Nec non` = "and also"; English "Nor does it fail to draw off" is a periphrasis of the same polarity, not a flip.
- **0014, 0218C, Gregory: "de fermento … laudem immolat, qui Deo sacrificium de rapina parat."** Leaven/unleavened is live in these chapters, so I checked: *fermento* is right (leaven = rapine), the English keeps it, and `azyma` at 0220D ("epulemur in azymis sinceritatis") is likewise correct and not `zyma`. No azyma/zyma site anywhere in 0014–0021.
- **0014, 0220C vs 0220D, Augustine quaest. 44 vs 45.** "hac vero omnes percussi sunt" (all the Egyptians in Gessen were struck) sits four lines from "nisi forte quis dicat etiam ista plaga *non* fuisse percussos Aegyptios qui cum Hebraeis habitabant." Genuine tension — but it is between two separate *quaestiones* excerpted side by side, and the English renders each faithfully. Not ours; not obviously Migne's either.
- **0015, 0221C, "Nolebat ad locum signorum pervenire filios Israel, ut possent tertii diei frui mysteriis."** The purpose clause reads oddly positive after `Nolebat`; but the negation sits in *nolebat* in the Latin too, and the English keeps it there. Migne's own loose syntax.
- **0016, 0225D, "Aegyptii insequentes merguntur in abyssum, ***et si*** rogare videntur Jesum ***ne*** eos iterum mergat in abyssum."** The Latin is broken (`et si` for `etsi`, and the sentence has no apodosis); the allusion is Lc 8:31. English is literal and dangling, which is the correct treatment of a broken plate — flagging for the cruces file rather than as a translation defect.
- **0017, 0227A, the glorificatus / gloriose glorificatus sequence.** Six turns of "glorificatus est … non gloriose", each with its own qualifier; every one matches, including "de se humiliter, non gloriose".
- **0017, 0229D, the four-fold "ad coelum non respiciunt, futura non cogitant, judicium Dei non metuunt, nec promissa ejus desiderant."** All four negations present in the English — this is exactly the cadence-driven site where one usually goes missing, so I counted it twice.
- **0018, 0231B–C, Origen on planting.** Seven consecutive `non … sed` antitheses (non in Aegypto / non in vallibus / non iterum in humilibus / non per terram repere / non ultra … dejectos / non in humilibus arboribus). All seven intact and all pointing the same way.
- **0019, 0233B, "Amarum est enim poculum legis, populus Dei, ***non*** qui in Mose baptizatus est in nube et in mari, sed qui in spiritu et aqua ***non*** possit bibere."** Origen's sense is that the cup is bitter to the people baptized in Moses but **not yet** in spirit and water; Migne's `non` sits before the wrong clause, and `populus Dei` is nominative where the sense wants a dative. The English renders what is printed. **I flag this as a probable Migne-side negation displacement worth adjudication** (direction 1), not as a defect of ours — but it is the one place in the range where the printed Latin's polarity is itself suspect and a plate check would settle it.
- **0019, 0234A, Ez 20:25 "praecepta et justificationes ***non bonas*** in quibus ***non*** vivent"** — and the two sentences that turn on it ("Idem enim, si servetur, vitam; si non servetur, mortem generat"). All intact.
- **0020, 0235B vs 0235D, "quinta decima die mensis" vs "Quarta decima die mensis secundi murmuravit populus."** Internal numeral clash in Migne (Ex 16:1 is the fifteenth). Not polarity; noted only so the next reader does not spend time on it.
- **0020, 0236A, "in die Dominica pluit Deus manna, et ***non*** in sabbato … ***non*** in ipso Dei gratia ad eos descenderit … panis coelestis … ad eos ***non*** pervenerit."** Three negations in one period, all carried.
- **0021, 0239D, Gregory's chiasmus "in supremis appetat quod contempserat, et contemnat in infimis quod appetebat."** The single most invertible sentence in the range; the English has it the right way round.
- **0021, 0239B, "exuitur / exutum est"** (husk stripped, mortality stripped) — checked specifically against `induitur`; correct in both places.

## Note on yield

Two sites in eleven chunks is below the 11064 rate. The genre here is partly to blame: 0011–0013 and 0020–0021 are dominated by *usque ad*-abridged excerpts and by list-structures (plague-by-plague, precept-by-precept, camp-by-camp) whose parallelism makes a dropped negation loud rather than quiet. The dense antithetical prose — Origen hom. 5–8 in 0016–0019, Greg. Nyss. in 0014 — is where I spent most of the read, and it is where F1 came from.
