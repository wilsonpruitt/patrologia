## Site 1 — 0011 @0094B · *afferret* for *auferret* · direction: MIGNE/TWIN

**Latin as printed**

> *Ne forte tradat.* Sic temperavit, ut poenitentiae locum **non afferret**:
> putaretur enim, quod si obiisset adversarius et non posset ei in via esse
> benevolus, necessario judici traderetur. **Sed et locum poenitentiae
> reliquit**, et impoenitentem judici tradidit…

**English as it stands**

> He so tempered it, that it should not bring in a place of repentance: …
> **But he both left a place of repentance**, and delivered the impenitent up
> to the judge…

The sentence denies what the third clause asserts, five words later. The
required sense is *ut poenitentiae locum non **auferret*** — "so as not to
**take away** the place of repentance"; the mistaken supposition that follows
(*putaretur enim…*) and the corrective *Sed et locum poenitentiae reliquit*
both require it. `afferret`/`auferret` is the sense-reversing-letter class.
The corpus uses the *tollere* verb for exactly this idea elsewhere
(9000/0020 @0305A: *reprobis locum poenitentiae tollet*).

**Disposition.** The English is already literal to the printed type, which is
correct — do **not** repair the English on the Latin alone. Log the crux,
plate-verify col. 0094B. If the plate reads *auferret*, English →
"He so tempered it, that it should not **take away** the place of repentance,"
and a TEI patch is filed; if the plate reads *afferret*, the crux stands and
the English is unchanged.

## Site 2 — 0018 @0112D · dropped `non` before *posset* · direction: MIGNE/TWIN

**Latin as printed**

> *Tetigit.* **Non quia sine tactu sanare posset**, sed ut indicet se non
> subjectum esse legi, sed Dominum legis, qui non timet contagium.

**English as it stands**

> **Not because he could heal without touch**, but that he might show himself
> not subject to the law…

As printed, the reason offered for touching is that he *could* heal without
touching — which is not a reason for touching, and is not what the `sed` clause
answers. The school's own parallel on the same pericope survives in this
repository, with the negation intact:

> 9001/0002 (Glossa on Mark, 0185A): *Non ideo tangit, quia sine tactu curare
> **non** poterat, sed sic probat, quia legi subjectus non erat.*

So a `non` has dropped between *sanare* and *posset*.

**Disposition.** English is literal to the printed type — leave it. Log the
crux, plate-verify col. 0112D, and if the plate carries the `non`, English →
"Not because he could **not** heal without touch," with a TEI patch. This one
is quietly dangerous because the defective sentence still *reads*, which is why
it survived.

## Site 3 — 0020 @0119C · splice tail rendered as an affirmative assertion · direction: MIXED (twin drop + ours)

**Latin as printed**

> VERS. 30.-- *Numerati.* Hoc est, scientia Dei, etc., *usque ad* parva
> otiosaque dicta eum lateant.

**English as it stands**

> That is, by the knowledge of God, etc., *as far as* **small and idle words
> lie hidden from him.**

The English asserts that small and idle words **are** hidden from God, in a
gloss on *the very hairs of your head are numbered*, immediately after its own
neighbour "by the knowledge of God." That is the reverse of the argument. Two
things went wrong and both matter:

1. The twin has lost the negatives. The same sentence survives intact in the
   Glossa on Luke in this repository —
   **9000/0020: *Ubi magna providentia Dei, quod **nec** parva **nec** otiosa
   dicta eum lateant.*** Note that our twin's `otiosaque` looks like the fused
   residue of `nec otiosa`, which is the *fused* half of the Pattern-10 class
   that no mechanical test sees.
2. Ours: *lateant* is **subjunctive**, i.e. the visible head of the clause was
   cut by the `usque ad` splice. The English flattened it into a flat
   indicative statement. The formula removes words; it does not license turning
   a dependent fragment into an assertion — least of all an assertion of the
   opposite.

**Disposition.** Plate-verify col. 0119C. Expected reading:
`…*usque ad* **nec** parva **nec** otiosa dicta eum lateant`, English →
"*as far as* **neither** small **nor** idle words lie hidden from him," with a
TEI patch filed. This is the strongest finding in the batch.

## Site 4 — 0021 @0121A · agency mis-bound (`aliis` dative read as agent) · direction: OURS

**Latin as printed**

> Jam prophetia cessat, jam lex expletur; **Christus aliis praedicatur, et ab
> aliis recipitur: sui respuunt, inimici accipiunt.**

**English as it stands**

> Christ is preached **by some**, and received by others: his own reject him,
> his enemies accept him.

*aliis* is dative with *praedicatur* — "is preached **to** some"; the
deliberate `ab` on the second member is what fixes it (had both been agents the
author would have written `ab` twice). The whole point of the antithesis is
that the group preached **to** is not the group that receives — which the very
next clause spells out (*sui respuunt, inimici accipiunt*) and which the
paragraph's thesis restates (*gloria Israel… fide gentium occupatur et
rapitur*). Rendered as "by some… by others" the sentence becomes a bland
statement about two sets of agents and the antithesis evaporates.

**Proposed:** "Christ is preached **to** some, and received by others."

## Site 5 — 0019 @0117A/B · unwarranted, polarity-bearing supply in a splice head · direction: OURS

**Latin as printed**

> *Et turbam.* Quia Synagoga laetitiam Dominicae inhabitationis, etc.,
> *usque ad* paruit in susceptione.

**English as it stands**

> Because the Synagogue **[lost]** the joy of the Lord's indwelling, etc.,
> *as far as* it was obedient in the receiving.

The Latin's verb is inside the part the `etc.` cut away. The English invents
one, in square brackets that belong to no sanctioned marker class, and the
invented verb carries a **negative valence** the fragment does not license —
while the resumption tail we do have (*paruit in susceptione*, "it was obedient
in the receiving") points the other way. This is the 7a′ failure in miniature:
a bracket used as a place to put a conjecture.

**Proposed:** remove the supply and let the head break where the Latin breaks
("Because the Synagogue the joy of the Lord's indwelling, etc."), or restore
the verb from the plate and record it as an `[ed: …]`. Either is honest; a bare
`[lost]` is not.

## Borderline — 0012 @0098A · *eum* for *cum* (1 Cor 5:11)

> *Benefacite.* Ministrando cibum mentis vel corporis. Quod facit etiam qui
> dicit **eum hujusmodi nec cibum sumere**.
>
> English: …Which he also does who says that **such a one is not even to take
> food**.

The clause is Paul's *cum ejusmodi nec cibum sumere* (1 Cor 5:11), so the
printed `eum` is very likely `cum`. Taken as printed, `eum hujusmodi` is a
grammatical accusative subject, and the English's reading is defensible — but
it is defensible in exactly the way the brief warns about: **it reads well**,
and it reads well because it silently converts "not to eat *with* such a one"
into "such a one is not to be fed." I flag rather than call it: the negation is
carried either way, and the difference is who is being denied the table.

**Recommendation:** crux + plate-verify col. 0098A. If the plate reads *cum*,
English → "who says that **with** such a one one is not even to take food."

## Candidates examined and REJECTED

Named, so this report can be told apart from a shorter one.

- **0011 @0093A** *Qui non solverit, et sic docet, non minimus, non tamen
  magnus.* Looks self-contradictory against *hic magnus vocabitur*. Rejected:
  the `sed facit quod docet` tail carries the concession; English is literal
  and correct.
- **0011 @0093C** *Sed non econtra, licentia irascendi causa homicidii.* Comma
  splice in the Latin, reads oddly in English. Rejected: "But not conversely"
  is on the right host; nothing is relocated.
- **0011 @0096A** *Non juremus per creaturas ut veneremur eas ultra quam
  debemus per eas, ut viles existimemus…* — probably lost a `nec` before
  `per eas`. Rejected as a **polarity** site: with or without the `nec` the
  English stays inside the prohibition, so nothing is asserted that the Latin
  denies. Worth a crux line, not a repair.
- **0014 @0102D** *deserens in tentatione sinas vinci* — English supplies "do
  **not** suffer us to be overcome" where the Latin prints no negative.
  Rejected: the `ne` of the quoted lemma (*Et ne nos [inducas]*) governs
  *sinas*, and the structure proves it — the following `etsi sinas tentari`
  concedes the tempting precisely against the deprecated overcoming. Correct as
  it stands.
- **0014 @0106A** *Haec exempla non prohibent providentiam ne laboret.*
  Redundant `ne` after a verb of forbidding; the English's literal "do not
  forbid providence, that it should not labour" is clumsy but carries both and
  is parsable. Rejected as a defect; noted as a readability nit only.
- **0015 @0107B** *non debet germinari malitia Dei* — already handled honestly
  with an inline `[cj: *Dei*; read *diei*]`. Rejected: the printed reading is
  rendered and the conjecture is visible, which is the rule working.
- **0018 @0112C** *De voluntate non quasi pietatis incredulus, dubito.* Tested
  hard, because the negative rests on a verb with a `non … sed` correlative
  nearby (test 2's exact shape). Rejected: the `sed quasi meae colluvionis
  conscius non praesumo` arm proves the host is the *quasi* phrase, not
  *dubito*. English correct.
- **0021 @0122C** *Et erant tunc qui in Corozaim essent vocandi: nec tamen de
  contemptu gravius puniendi.* The argument would run better with *in Tyro*.
  Rejected: coherent as printed, and a conjecture here would be exactly the
  emendation-from-the-Latin the plate gate exists to stop.
- **0021 @0121D** *Lamentavimus ad Joannem, cecinimus ad Christum.* Checked for
  a swapped pair, since the mapping is the kind that inverts silently.
  Rejected: correct, and confirmed by its own neighbour *nec plorat cum Joanne
  plorante, neque gaudet cum Christo gaudente*.
- **Lord's Prayer exposition (0013 @0100B–0102C) and the Sermon's close.** The
  conformation hazard flagged in the brief did **not** fire here. Every
  scripture lemma in range that diverges from the Vulgate has been followed as
  Migne prints it and marked: *Soli enim factores legis justi sunt apud Deum*
  (0011), *Et maxillam dedi percutienti* (0012), *Sol justitiae non illuxit
  nobis* / *Super unam civitatem pluit* (0012), *Filios genui et exaltavi*
  (0013), *Percipite regnum quod vobis paratum* (0013), *Hic est panis vivus*
  (0013), Mc 11:25 (0014), *Thesaurizare autem* (0014), Gn 30:33 and 1 Thes 2:9
  (0015), *Duplici contritione conteret eos Dominus* (0019), *Euntes autem
  dicite* for *discite* (0019), *appropinquabit* for *appropinquavit* (0020),
  *At illi dixerunt: Non* for *Nihil* (0020), *operatur in coelis* for *in
  nobis* (0021). That is a good record, and I checked every one word by word

   `[says]` (0018 @0112D), `[could bind]` (0018 @0114C), `[wondered]`
   (0017 @0112B), `[chose]` (0021 @0120C), `[it be]` (0016 @0107D). Site 5

   from the lemma, but the class should be one marker or none.
3. **Marker placement**, 0019 @0115C: the `[var:]` for Jer 17:18 sits after the
   *following* lemma (*Which is easier,*) instead of adjacent to the quotation
   it glosses.
4. **Same sentence, two renderings.** The gloss *oves pelles suas deponere…
   lupi se contegunt* occurs twice in range and is Englished twice:
   "lay aside their own **skins**" (0014 @0103C) vs "lay aside their
   **fleeces**" (0016 @0110C). In a lemma-and-gloss work a repeated sentence
   should repeat.
5. **0016 @0108A** *Salutem in eo quod temere judicant in oculo habent trabem*
   is garbled in the Latin (accusative *Salutem* governing nothing) and the
   English reproduces the garble, correctly. Worth a crux line so a later
   reader does not "fix" it.
