## 2. The site

### 0024 @ 0130A — VERS. 2, *Turba stabat* — **OUR defect** — a negation attached to the wrong host

**Latin as printed:**

> VERS. 2.— *Turba stabat.* Hi sunt qui nuper audire venerunt, qui et a fluctibus maris secreti sunt, **necdum** coelestibus mysteriis quae desiderant imbuti **cum Christo ascendunt**.

**English as it stands:**

> VERS. 2.— *The crowd stood.* These are they who have lately come to hear, who are also set apart from the waves of the sea, and **not yet steeped** in the heavenly mysteries which they desire, **they ascend with Christ**.

**What is wrong.** Nothing is dropped and nothing is added — every negative in the Latin is present in the English. The `necdum` has been **relocated**: the English hangs it on the participle *imbuti* and lets the finite verb *ascendunt* stand affirmative, so the paragraph ends by asserting that the newly-arrived crowd **does** ascend with Christ.

**Proving the host from the sentence's own structure, not from what reads well** (test 2):

1. `necdum` here is the coordinating `nec` + `dum`. It stands at the head of its clause, coordinating with the preceding finite clause `qui … secreti sunt`, and the only finite verb in its own clause is `ascendunt`. A participle would take `nondum`, and would take it adjacent (`nondum imbuti`); Migne prints it four words upstream, at the clause head.
2. The construction is the standard `qui … sunt, necdum … ascendunt` pair, not a parenthetical `necdum imbuti`.

**The contradicting neighbour is the lemma itself.** The gloss is on Mt 13:2 — *ascendens in naviculam sedebat, et omnis turba **stabat in littore***. The verse being glossed says the crowd **stayed on the shore** while Jesus went up into the boat. And the gloss says so again twice on the same plate:

- 0130D, VERS. 10 — *dudum cum ipso navem conscenderint* — going up into the boat is what marks out the **disciples**, and the glossator has to argue for it specially.
- 0131B, VERS. 14 — *Haec de turbis prophetata sunt, **quia stant in littore**, et Domini non merentur audire sermonem* — the crowd stands on the shore and does not even deserve to hear him.

The English as it stands has the crowd ascending with Christ in VERS. 2 and standing undeserving on the shore in VERS. 14, nine columns apart in the same exposition.

**Proposed reading:**

> *The crowd stood.* These are they who have lately come to hear, who are also set apart from the waves of the sea, and **do not yet ascend with Christ**, steeped in the heavenly mysteries which they desire.

(Or, keeping the English word order: "…and, steeped in the heavenly mysteries which they desire, they do not yet ascend with Christ." Either puts the negative on *ascendunt*.)

**Note on the smoothness tell.** The present English reads perfectly well; the Latin is the rough one. That is precisely the shape §3 of the brief names as a suspect rather than a success.

## 4. A second class, found while reading — silent normalisation of defective type (Pattern 7 / 12)

Not polarity, but the same family of invisible defect: **the English silently reads the intended word where Migne's type prints something else, with no `[sic:]` and no `[cj:]`.** The work already uses the markers correctly elsewhere — `[sic: Constristatus]` at 0026 @0135B, `[sic: Excaevavit]` at 0024 @0131A, and a model `[cj: *Jesus*; read *laesus*]` at 0030 @0146B — so the practice exists and is being applied unevenly. Six misses:

| chunk | col | Migne prints | English gives | class |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0022 | 0124C | *Et **vos** ostenderet* (VERS. 7) | "And that he might show **you**" (reads *ut vobis*) | real words, unconstruable |
| 0024 | 0130B | ***Scaagesimum.*** (VERS. 8 lemma) | "*Sixtyfold.*" | non-word |
| 0028 | 0142A | *alii autem **Etiam*** (VERS. 17) | "and others **Elias**" | real word, one letter, for *Eliam* |
| 0029 | 0143D | *quod **enimus*** (VERS. 2) | "what we shall be" (reads *erimus*) | non-word |
| 0031 | 0148C | ***eum** possent esse viri* (VERS. 12) | "**though** they could be husbands" (reads *cum*) | real word, one letter |
| 0031 | 0148D | *quod **honorate*** (VERS. 14) | "in that you **honour**" (reads *honoratis*) | real word |

The 0028 *Etiam*/*Eliam* case is the sharpest: a **real Latin word**, corrected silently, in a list the reader would otherwise have no way of checking. Under Pattern 7's main rule (unamended for real words) it should stand as printed with a crux; under Pattern 12 it wants `[sic: *Etiam*]` or `[cj:]`.

⚠ **One thing this reader cannot settle:** whether these forms are on Migne's plate or are OCR damage in our Latin twin. Both possibilities need the same escalation and produce different repairs, so all six are handed over for adjudication rather than being called defects of the English outright.

## 5. Candidates examined and rejected

- **0031 @0149A/0149B — BED vs HIER on the rich young man.** BED: *Non putandus iste … vel de sua vita mentitus esse*; HIER, three lines later: *Mentitur.* A flat contradiction on the same plate, and both are rendered faithfully. **Rejected:** this is genuine catena juxtaposition of two authorities, not a defect. The same pair recurs at 0148D–0149A on whether he came *voto discentis* or *tentantis*, likewise preserved. Repairing either would destroy the evidence.
- **0022 @0125A — *Non sit manus tua ad accipendum porrecta, **sed** ad dandum collecta* (Sir 4:36).** Migne's *sed* leaves the negative governing only the first member, against the Vulgate's *et*, which negates both. The English follows Migne ("Let not thy hand be stretched out to receive, but closed to give") and carries a var note saying exactly this. **Rejected — correctly handled.**
- **0026 @0135D — *Omnes gentes quasi non sint, **sed** sunt coram eo* (Is 40:17).** Migne's *sed* for the Vulgate's *sic*: a one-letter sense reversal of just the kind this sweep hunts. Rendered literally, var note present. **Rejected — correctly handled, and a good example of the rule working.**
- **0023 @0128B — lemma *Nisi signum Jonae* rendered "*But the sign of Jonas.*"** The `nisi` is not lost; "but" after a negated verb is its standard force and the Douay wording. **Rejected.**
- **0027 @0139D — *et eorum **non** recordatur Dominus, sed discipuli*.** English supplies the elided verb ("the Lord does not remember them, but the disciples do"). Checked against the source contrast — in Mt 14:15 it is the **disciples** who raise the crowd's need, in Mt 15:32 the **Lord** — so the ellipsis is filled correctly. **Rejected.**
- **0027 @0140A — *quia hic **non** (ut in lege) vitale alimentum animae figuris … tegitur*.** Negative on a verb with a parenthesis intervening — exactly the shape test 2 fires on. Host is `tegitur`; English puts it there. **Rejected.**
- **0028 @0141A — *cum **non** parco meis bonis **quin** patiantur, **non** parcam aliis*.** Three negatives, one of them a `quin` clause. Each is on its own verb in the English. **Rejected.**
- **0031 @0149A — *Non se magistrum bonum esse negat, sed magistrum absque Deo nullum bonum esse dicit*.** Four negatives in three clauses about the goodness of a master; a doctrinally load-bearing spot where a single slip inverts the christology. All four sit on their printed hosts. **Rejected.**
- **0025 @0134C — *Vel despectus pauca facit, **ne** penitus excusabiles sint*.** Tested for an inverted purpose clause (few miracles would seem to make them *more* excusable). The printed sense is right as it stands — he does a few precisely so that they are *not wholly* excusable. **Rejected.**
- **0026 @0136C — *Non quod omnia **non** habens in potestate, sed ut formam hominis impleret*.** Double negative, preserved intact. **Rejected.**

## 6. Reader's note

The hazard the brief flagged for this range did materialise, but not where expected. The **parables** (XIII, XVIII) and the **hard sayings** (XIX on divorce and riches) came through with their negatives intact and, in several places, conspicuously un-softened — *Divites non manentes, sed qui esse desierunt intrabunt*, and *velimus nolimus sustinenda est* of an evil wife, are both rendered without flinching. The one live site is in the frame around a parable rather than in a parable, at the very first gloss of CAPUT XIII, and turns on a construal rather than on a softened doctrine.

The larger finding of this read is arguably §4, not §2: the marker practice in this work is real but patchy, and six defective forms are currently reaching the reader as clean English.
