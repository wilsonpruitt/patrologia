### F1. 0009, col 1672 [b: C] (Instr. IV §10), class 1 (polarity added under a plate-loss marker)
- **Greek (plate-read, p-0872, right col., ~4 lines above [b: D]):** `ἀλλ᾿ οἷον εὑρεθῇ τὸ πρᾶγμα, εἴτε μικρὸν εἴτε μέγα, καταφρονήσει αὐτοῦ καὶ ἀμελήσει. Κακὴ γάρ ἐστιν ἡ ἀδιαφορία.` The plate is clean and continuous here: no gap, no worn sort, no room for a lost `μή`. Our file matches the plate.
- **Latin (twin, col 1671):** *sed uti res inventa fuerit vel parva vel magna, ita eam permittat et negligat. Est enim malus ejusmodi rerum contemptus.* The Latin has no negative either: Allatius translated the positive as printed.
- **English as it stands:** `but whatever the matter turns out to be, small or great, let him ⟨not⟩ despise it and neglect it; for indifference is a bad thing.`
- **Proposed English:** `but whatever the matter turns out to be, small or great, let him not despise it and neglect it [sic: *καταφρονήσει αὐτοῦ καὶ ἀμελήσει*]; for indifference is a bad thing.` (Alternatively, render the plate literally, "he will despise it and neglect it", with a crux. That is for the adjudicator. Either way the ⟨⟩ has to go.)
- **Why:** ⟨…⟩ asserts that the plate has lost words. It has not. Migne prints the positive, and his Latin column follows it. The *sense* the English gives is very probably right: the next sentence ("for indifference is a bad thing") and the following `Μήτε πάλιν` ("nor again") both need a preceding negative. But the negative is an editorial conjecture against an intact plate, and it reverses the printed polarity. It needs a crux and a marker that says so, not a plate-loss supplement that hides it.
- **Confidence:** certain about the plate. That the sense needs "not" is probable.
- **Already in the cruces?** Yes, as to substance: `cruces.md` 1672C says "The Greek has no negative, and the Latin follows it … ⟨not⟩ is supplied." So the conjecture is disclosed in the notes. What remains is the marker: the brief defines ⟨…⟩ as a supplement for *plate loss*, and here the plate is intact, so the reader sees the wrong signal. The same pattern is logged at 1697D (`does ⟨not⟩ keep this way`, outside my range). Whoever owns the convention should rule on both together.

### F2. 0009, col 1676 [b: C] (Instr. V §1), class 5 (wrong term)
- **Greek (file only):** `οὐχ οὕτως ἐστὶν ἀφυὴς περὶ τὸ κακοποιῆσαι, ὥστε ὑποβάλλειν αὐτῷ, οὕτως ἀφαιρῇ τις τῶν προφανῶν ἁμαρτημάτων`
- **Latin (col 1677):** *neque est adeo amens et ineptus ad male agendum, ut statim illi suggerat aut persuadeat quidpiam malorum profanissimorum.*
- **English as it stands:** `he is not so untalented at doing evil as to suggest to him so crudely [sic: *ἀφαιρῇ τις*] one of the manifest sins`
- **Proposed English:** `he is not so untalented at doing evil as to suggest to him straight off [sic: *ἀφαιρῇ τις*] one of the manifest sins`
- **Why:** `ἀφαιρῇ τις` = `ἀφαρεί τι`. Migne's own note on the same misprint in this chunk (col 1673 B, `ἀφρῇ`) glosses it *Ἀφαρεί. Festinanter*, the English renders that one "straightaway", and the Latin here has *statim*. "So crudely" swaps the adverb of time for one of manner. It is a small slip, but the point is that the devil does not open with the obvious sin.
- **Confidence:** probable. This is a choice between two conjectures.
- **Already in the cruces?** Yes, in the print-defect table: `p0874.43 | ἀφαιρῇ τις | ἀφελῶς τι`. The translators read ἀφελῶς ("simply, crudely"), so "so crudely" was deliberate. I would still prefer ἀφαρεί, for three reasons: (a) the same table resolves the sister misprint `ἀφρῇ` at p0873.23 as ἀφαρεί; (b) Migne's own note there confirms that reading; (c) the Latin twin reads *statim*. This is for the adjudicator. Downgrade it to a note if ἀφελῶς is preferred.

### F3. 0010, col 1685 [b: A] (end of Instr. V), class 3 (the source of the danger turned outward)
- **Greek (file only):** `Ὁ Θεὸς σκεπάσει ἡμᾶς κινδύνου τοῦ ἀπὸ τῶν στοιχούντων ἑαυτοῖς`
- **Latin (col 1686):** *Deus nos eripiat ab hoc periculo instituendi nos ipsos* ("from this danger of guiding ourselves").
- **English as it stands:** `May God shelter us from the danger that comes from those who are guided by themselves`
- **Proposed English:** `May God shelter us from the danger of those who are guided by themselves`
- **Why:** The whole Instruction is about the danger a man runs when he guides *himself*: he trusts his own heart and does not ask. "The danger that comes from those who …" makes other people the source of the threat, as though self-guided brethren were a hazard to us. The genitive phrase is better rendered neutrally, so that it covers our own self-guidance, as the Latin takes it.
- **Confidence:** probable.
- **Already in the cruces?** No. 1685A carries only the Agathon source note.

## Candidates considered and rejected
- 0006, 1649 [b: B] `Αὕτη δὲ ἡ ταπείνωσις, θεϊκή ἐστι` → "But humility itself is divine". The rough breathing gives "this humility", not "itself". It does not change the claim. Rejected.
- 0006, 1652 B `καὶ οἷον συμπάσχει` → "And the wretched soul suffers with": "as it were" (οἷον) is dropped. Minor, and the sense is unaffected. Rejected.
- 0006, 1652 C Evagrius `ὁ κόπος οὐ διὰ τὴν βλασφημίαν ἐγένετο, ἀλλὰ διὰ τὴν ταπείνωσιν`: the polarity and the οὐ…ἀλλά are kept. Clean.
- 0007, 1656 A `ὡς τί ποτε ἰδικῶς διαμαρτυρόμενος, καὶ λέγων αὐτῷ` → "as though bearing solemn witness to each one in particular". This looked like an added addressee, but the Latin reads the same way (*quasi unum e multis contestatus ait illi*). Rejected.
- 0007, 1656 B the Greek prints `;` after the Matt 5:26 quotation, and the English renders it as a statement. It is part of the rhetorical "Καὶ τί;" frame, and no sense changes. Rejected.
- 0007, 1657 C `ὡς πολλάκις δεὶ λέγω` → "as I always say" (rather than "often"). Trivial. Rejected.
- 0007, 1661 A `ὥσπερ ἡ γάνωσις` → "as tinning guards a vessel". "A vessel" is added unmarked, but it only explains the image. Rejected.
- 0008, 1665 C `τὸ ἐκτεῖναι χεῖρα κατά τινος γελῶντος` → "at someone who is laughing". I suspected the laughter belonged to the subject, but the Latin agrees with the English (*extenderitve manum in alium ridentem*). Rejected.
- 0008, 1668 A `μηδὲ ἀνανεύειν, μηδὲ εἰς τὰ πρόσωπα ἀλλήλων` → "not to toss our heads, nor ⟨to stare⟩ into one another's faces". Two acts may be one ("not even to look up into one another's faces"). The Latin is too free to decide it (*ne nictemus, ne vagemur oculis, ne vultus nostros crebro contemplemur*), and the supplement does not reverse the sense. Rejected, but the adjudicator may wish to look.
- 0008, 1668 B `ἢ διὰ ἰδίαν βλάβην` inside `καὶ μὴ λέγῃ διὰ …`: the negative's scope over both members is kept in English. Clean.
- 0009, 1673 C `κἂν ἐστιν εὐλογοφανὲς ταράξαι ὑμᾶς` with the English "⟨than⟩". I suspected that "out of obstinacy or self-justification" had been moved from the troubling to the doing, but the Latin parses exactly as the English does (*neque per contumaciam vel justitiam fieri … quam turbari vos*). Rejected.
- 0009, 1672 D / 1673 A `ἥμισυ τέταρτον` → "three and a half eighths". The idiom is defensible and logged (cruces 1672D). Rejected.
- 0009, 1676 C `Οὐ λέγει πολλῇ βουλῇ … καὶ τὰ μὲν σιωπᾶν, τὰ δὲ λέγειν` → "and not to keep silent on some things": the "not" comes from the scope of the opening Οὐ λέγει, and the Latin has *Neque hoc ut taceat, illud loquatur*. Rejected.
- 0010, 1681 A `Οὐ τόδε σοι ἔχει εἰπεῖν ὁ λέγων.` (printed with a full stop) → English question "Will not the one who answers tell thee just this?" Read as a statement, the Greek would say the opposite. But the thought's own later words (`ἰδοὺ αὐτό ἐστιν ὃ εἶπον`) require the affirmative-expecting question, so the English is right. I did not plate-read the punctuation because the English stands either way. Rejected.
