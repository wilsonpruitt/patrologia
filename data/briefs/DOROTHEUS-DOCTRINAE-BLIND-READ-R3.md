## Findings

### F1. Chunk 0013, col 1701 C (p1 s9)
- **Class:** 3 (mis-parsed grammar / logic) bordering 4 (a printed dittography rendered as text).
- **Greek (plate-read, p-0887, left column, band C):** Ἔστι δὲ ὅτε οἰκονομεῖ τινα καὶ ὑπὲρ τὴν χρείαν, ἐπειδὴ ὁ Θεὸς, ὡς ἐλεήμων ἑκάστῳ παρέχει τὴν χρείαν αὐτοῦ. Ἔστι δὲ ὅτε οἰκονομεῖ τινα καὶ ὑπὲρ τὴν χρείαν, δεικνύων αὐτῷ τὴν ὑπερβολὴν… The plate prints the duplicated head exactly as our file has it, so the defect is Migne's.
- **Latin:** *Ex quo fit ut alius praeter necessitatem suam plerumque habeat, alius multis egeat; quoniam Deus misericors unicuique necessaria impertitur. Cumque alii dederit praeter necessitatem, huic ostendit humanitatis suae magnitudinem…* Allatius gives "beyond the need" only once in this form (the *Cumque alii* clause). His *quoniam… necessaria impertitur* hangs on a both-ways sentence, not on a second "beyond the need".
- **English as it stands:** "And sometimes he provides for someone even beyond the need, since God, being merciful, supplies each one his need."
- **Proposed English:** "And sometimes he provides for someone according to his need [sic: *καὶ ὑπὲρ τὴν χρείαν*], since God, being merciful, supplies each one his need."
- **Why:** The passage sets out three cases: provision according to need (the reason given: God "supplies each one his need"), provision beyond need (the reason given: to show his love for man), and provision withheld (to teach patience). As printed, case one repeats the head of case two word for word, and its stated reason ("supplies each one his need") does not fit "beyond the need". The English now gives two identical sentences with different reasons and drops the first case.
- **Confidence:** probable. The intended word (κατά?) is conjecture. That the printed duplication is wrong is near-certain from the three-case structure.
- **Already in the cruces?** Yes. 1701C is logged: "a possible dittography; the Latin renders both, so the English follows the print." I read the Latin differently (see above). For adjudication: either keep the print and mark it `[sic:]`, or adopt the sense. Leaving it unmarked tells the reader the duplication is intended.

## Candidates considered and rejected
1. **0011 @1692A, p5 s7** οὐ πάντως μετανοῶ, οὐ πάντως φθάνω… is rendered "I surely do not repent… I surely do not get there in time". οὐ πάντως can mean "not necessarily" (perhaps I won't), which would be a modality shift. Rejected: οὐ πάντως also means "by no means", and the Latin reads it as a plain assertion (*ego autem poenitentiam nullam ago, nec praevenio*). The English matches both.
2. **0011 @1692A, p4 s5** καὶ σὺ γινώσκεις αὐτό is rendered as the question "and dost thou know it?" The Greek prints no question mark at that point. Rejected: read as a rhetorical question, it fits the run of πόθεν οἶδας challenges around it, and its polarity matches the sense. The Latin omits the clause.
3. **0011 @1692A, p4 s7** Migne prints Ἔσθ᾿ ὅτε μόνον ὅτι οὐ κατακρίνομεν, ἀλλὰ καὶ ἐξουδενοῦμεν with the negative out of place. The English "not only condemn but also despise" gives the right sense and marks the form with `[sic:]`. The `[sic:]` sits after "despise." rather than after the clause it marks. That is cosmetic, not a finding.
4. **0011 @1689C, p3 s17** ἡ ὀργὴ ἐκείνη is rendered "that fury". The Latin has *furia illa*. Accepted.
5. **0012 @1697B–C, p5 s10** "or take what comes from them, as fine dogs take what comes from men?" This was a candidate for a who-is-who flip. The traditional sense is "accept things from them as from men? They are dogs", and s7 just before reads μηδὲ προσέχων αὐτῷ ὡς ἀνθρώπῳ. Rejected: Migne prints no break, and the Latin parses it as the English does (*ut ab hominibus blandi canes*). Logged in the cruces at 1697C.
6. **0012 @1700, p6 s8–9** κρατήσει δὲ τὴν ὁδὸν ταύτην is rendered "but does ⟨not⟩ keep this way". The Greek prints no negative. Rejected as a finding: the supplement gives the only sense the argument allows. Note for adjudication: this ⟨⟩ supplies a word the sense requires, not one the plate has lost, so it stretches the ⟨⟩ convention.
7. **0012 @1700B, p7 s8** λαμπούθιόν τι is rendered "something [?]". The reader sees an unresolved gap, but it is not a reversal. Left to the logged crux.
8. **0013 @1701A, p1 s3** The plate reads τὸ μέμψασθαι ἑαυτόν τινα (verified on p-0887). The English "to blame anyone" leaves ἑαυτόν untranslated. Rendering it ("blame himself") would contradict the instruction's whole thesis, and the Latin has *neque ex hoc potes quemquam accusare*. Rejected. The English follows the sense with no `[sic:]`. Adjudicators may want a `[sic:]` there.
9. **0013 @1708, p3 s18** Ἀνδρὶ φονεῖ ἐλάλει ὁ Θεὸς… is printed as a statement and rendered as a question. Rejected: the next sentence (Πῶς οὖν ὁ Θεὸς εἶπεν αὐτῷ;) shows it is rhetorical, and the sense is kept.
10. **0013 @1705C, p3 s15** τὸ ὀζόμενον ἔλαιον μετέτρεπεν εἰς μέλι is rendered as a counterfactual. That matches the counterfactual use without ἄν that comes just before (μέλι εἶχε ἀδελφός). Accepted.
11. **0014 @1713, p7 s10** Θέλει δέ τις καὶ χαίρειν is rendered "a man should rather rejoice". This is θέλει in its "one ought" sense. Accepted.
12. **0014 @1716, p7 s21** "unless you work at these things, ⟨you cannot⟩ take them in by word". The supplement fills an ellipsis and keeps the sense. Accepted.
13. **0015 @1717A, p4 s2** ἐπειραζόμην is rendered "I was tempted". It could mean "I was trying". The Latin (*fallebar plerumque a me ipso, credens…*) treats it as a fault, which supports "tempted". Accepted.
14. **0015 @1720C, p5 s3** ἀπὸ τοῦ μὴ προσέχειν ταῖς ἰδίαις ἁμαρτίαις is rendered "away from heeding his own sins". The English leaves μή untranslated. The net sense (suspicion makes a man not heed his own sins) is the same either way. Rejected.
15. **0015 @1720B, p4 s23** "that the devil be put to shame and the sower of suspicions be made an example" might look as if it fuses or splits the "three things". The Greek groups them the same way. Accepted.
