## Findings

### F1. 0028, col 1825 C (§4 opening): who does what, "we" turned into "he"
- **Class:** 2 (who does what)
- **Greek (plate-read, p-0965):** Δ΄. Προσῳκειώσαμεν καὶ διὰ τοῦτο τὸν ἄνθρωπον, οἷον εἰπεῖν, ἰδιοποιήσαμεν.
  Both verbs are first person plural on the plate, set cleanly; neither is a misprint.
- **Latin (twin, col 1826):** *Quare recte diximus nullum esse animal quod Deo esset magis accommodatum, magis
  domesticum, magis familiare ipso homine.* The Latin takes the "we" as the speaker's own.
- **English as it stands:** `And so he made man closely his own, so to speak, appropriated him. [sic: *Προσῳκειώσαμεν*]`
- **Proposed English:** `For this reason too we have made man God's close kin; we have, so to speak, appropriated him to God.`
- **Why:** The plate gives two first-person-plural verbs. The English turns both into "he" (God). Only the first
  verb carries a `[sic:]`, so the second was silently changed as well. The "we" is Dorotheus drawing the conclusion
  of his argument (man is οἰκειότατον, "most his own"), and the next sentence goes on: "Well then the saint said...".
  The Latin reads it the same way (*recte diximus*). The `[sic:]` claims a plate misprint that the plate does not
  show, so it should go.
- **Confidence:** probable. The "we" is certain; whether "God's" should be supplied or left implicit is a matter
  of wording.
- **Already in the cruces?** Logged, but as the opposite claim. `cruces.md` rows p0965.33 and p0965.34 call
  both verbs a "print" error by Migne and emend them to προσῳκειώσατο / ἰδιοποιήσατο. This finding disputes that
  emendation. The plate sets both forms cleanly. It is very unlikely that two consecutive verbs share the same
  misprint (‑σαμεν for ‑σατο). The Latin column also reads a first-person "we". The English also disagrees with its
  own log: it marks only the first verb with `[sic:]`.

## Candidates considered and rejected
1. **0026, 1804 B**, ὡς συμβαίνειν τὸ σὸν πάντοτε γίνεσθαι, ὡς θέλεις. Ἀλλὰ θέλεις ὡς γίνεται. I suspected a lost
   οὐχ, because the Latin reads *Ne optes ad vota tua res evenire, sed eas velis… ut eveniunt* and 1809 C has the
   parallel Οὐ γὰρ θέλει τὰ πράγματα γίνεσθαι, ὡς θέλει. The plate has no negative. The Greek does make sense as
   printed: thy will is always done, because thou willest as things happen. The English renders it with "rather",
   which is faithful. Rejected.
2. **0026, 1804 C**, διὰ τὴν κακίαν ἡμῶν against the Latin *malignitate aliorum*. The plate confirms ἡμῶν and the
   English follows it. Allatius's "of others" is free rendering, so I do not think a `[lat:]` is needed. Recorded
   here only as a possible Pattern 16 question for the adjudicator.
3. **0027, 1817 B**, εἰ ἀνάσχωμαι, εἰ μὴ φέρει μοι τιμὴν, rendered "should I consent, or not, [sic] since it brings
   me honour". The Latin (*sustineamne, aut non feram honorem*) reads the same way, and the crux (p0961.25,
   ἢ μή; φέρει) is logged. The English does not change the sense. Rejected.
4. **0027, 1816 A–B**, the fire simile. ἐπιπέσῃ δὲ ἐπ᾿ αὐτὸν ἄνθραξ is rendered "let a coal of fire fall on him".
   The jussive form carries the conditional "if a coal falls" in idiomatic English. Rejected. The untranslated
   Οὐδὲ θίξειν carries `[?]` and is logged as a doubt.
5. **0027, 1816 B**, τὸ πατρικὸν τοῦτο πῦρ, rendered "fatherly fire" against Migne's note *Legendum πνευματικόν*. The
   English follows the printed text, and the brief excludes `[n:]` notes. Rejected (possibly worth an editorial
   look).
6. **0028, 1824 B**, ἐνιαυτῷ θυσιάζεται rendered "sacrificed at once [sic]". This is logged in the cruces
   (p0964.22). The English sense ("the beast dies once, the saints die daily") is right. Rejected.
7. **0029, 1829 D**, ...λέγονται ἱερεῖα ἔμψυχα, ὁλοκαυτώματα δὲ λογικά. Διὰ τί; The English moves
   "rational whole burnt offerings" from the statement into the question ("And *rational whole burnt offerings*:
   why?"). Nothing is lost and the question still asks about the second title. Rejected.
8. **0029, 1833 C**, ἐπεὶ ὁ Θεὸς τὰ πάντα γινώσκει… τὰ μὴ ὄντα. Διὰ τί οὖν λέγει… The plate prints a full stop after
   ὄντα (checked). The English turns the ἐπεί clause into the protasis of the next question ("Since God knows all
   things…, why then does he say…"). That crosses the plate's stop, which is a Pattern 8 point, but the logic
   (God knows everything, so why single out the saints?) is the Greek's own. The Latin (*Dominus enim omnia
   novit… Cognosci autem dicuntur*) agrees. The sense is not changed. Noted, not reported.
9. **0029, 1833 D**, τοσοῦτον λέγεται ἀγνοεῖν ἐγγίζων, with ⟨him and not to be known by him; and⟩ supplied. The
   plate is intact here (checked): the gap is in Migne's printed text, not type lost from our copy. The crux logs
   it as a Migne lacuna (p0969.48). The supplement gives the sense the argument needs and does not distort it.
   Rejected.
10. **0026, 1805 A**, κἂν δὲ ἀπὸ ἀσυστροφίας, ἢ καταφρονήσεως κλασθῇ… μὴ μεριμνήσῃς. I checked the reading of
    whose "contempt" this is (another's, not the cellarer's). The English "through disorderliness or contempt it is
    broken" keeps the ambiguity. Rejected.
11. **0026, 1809 B**, ἡδέως ἔχω γίνεσθαι γνώμῃ τοῦ πλησίον καὶ ἀσυστροφῆσαι… ἢ καὶ στοιχῆσαι τῇ ἰδίᾳ γνώμῃ καὶ
    εὐσυστροφῆσαι, with ἢ rendered "rather than". This is the right comparative sense; the polarity (fare badly by
    his counsel rather than well by mine) is kept. Rejected.
