## Findings

### F1. Ch. 75, col. 1417A, chunk 0002: class 4 (an added pronoun that steers the referent), so the teaching reads reversed
- **Greek (plate-read, p. 722, Greek column left):** τοῦ δὲ τῆς ἁμαρτίας, οἱ τῆς ἐνταῦθα ζωῆς ὀλίγοι τῶν ἡδέων προσαπολαύσαντες. The plate prints ὀλίγοι, as our file does, and the Greek has no possessive.
- **Latin:** *peccati autem corpore, pauci ex illis qui vitae hujus deliciis potiti sunt*. This is a third construal, "few out of those who enjoyed the delights of this life". The Latin supplies *deliciis vitae hujus* and makes the pleasures this life's.
- **English as it stands:** `but of that of sin, the few of the life here who have enjoyed its sweet things.`
- **Proposed English:** `but of that of sin, the few of the life here who have enjoyed the sweet things besides.`
- **Why:** The Greek τῶν ἡδέων has no pronoun. The English "its" ties the sweet things to "the life here". The chapter then says that the people stripped of the body of sin are the ones who enjoyed this life's pleasures, which turns the ascetic sense upside down. Ch. 76, the twin, makes it "only those … who have hated it from disposition". Ch. 74, just before, has the soul smelling the "sweet" (ἡδείας) odour of passionlessness's garments. Read that way, τὰ ἡδέα are the sweet things of ch. 74, and προσ- means "besides", in this life ahead of the next. Without "its", the English leaves this open. The bare Greek can carry either reading, so the English should not pick the worldly one. If the editors read ὀλίγοι as predicate ("who have enjoyed the sweet things sparingly"), that rendering needs a T-note, because ὀλίγοι does not agree with τῶν ἡδέων.
- **Confidence:** probable. It is certain that "its" has no basis in the Greek. Which construal Maximus meant is open.
- **Already in the cruces?** Partly. cruces.md 1417 has a T-note: rendered with the Latin, and ὀλίγοι may be predicative, "which gives the expected sense". The construal question is therefore logged. The added «its», which pushes the text onto the unexpected sense, is not flagged. The T-note's own gloss also says «its sweets».

### F2. Ch. 87, col. 1420B, chunk 0002: class 3 (a statement made a question; Pattern 8, a mark supplied)
- **Greek (plate-read, p. 723, Greek column right):** … πόσην οὐκ ἂν οἰκειότητα σχῇ ἐν εὐχῇ συνεχεῖ· καὶ γὰρ ἐν οἷς, φησὶ, χρονίζει (o), ἐν αὐτοῖς καὶ πλατύνεσθαι εἴωθεν. The plate prints a raised stop (·) after συνεχεῖ, not a Greek question mark (;).
- **Latin:** *quantam, rogo, non nanciscetur, si continue orationi incumbat?* The Latin makes it a question. It is a witness only.
- **English as it stands:** `how great a familiarity would it not have in continual prayer? ⟦s1+2⟧ For in the things`
- **Proposed English:** `how great a familiarity would it not have in continual prayer; ⟦s1+2⟧ for in the things`
- **Why:** Migne prints an exclamatory period, πόσην οὐκ ἄν, closed by a raised stop. The English supplies a question mark the Greek does not have (Pattern 8: never supply) and follows the Latin's *rogo … ?*. No sense is reversed: the rhetorical force is the same. This is a mark and sentence-type issue only.
- **Confidence:** certain on the plate. The fix is low-stakes, and the editors may prefer to T-note the "?" instead of changing it.
- **Already in the cruces?** No. The 1420 notes on this chapter deal only with the unidentified «φησί».

## R1 cases (judged at the plate)
- **Ch. 4, «neither of them» (οὐδ᾿ ἑτέρα): KEEP.** Plate p. 714, col. 1401C: Ἧς ἄνευ, οὐδ᾿ | ἑτέρα τὸ αὐτῆς ἰδεῖν πέρας δυνήσεται. The words are split at the line end, but οὐδ᾿ ἑτέρα = οὐδετέρα, "neither of the two", i.e. mercy and truth, the pair just named. The Latin agrees (*neutra suum finem videre poterit*). Small point, no change asked: the plate has αὐτῆς with a smooth breathing, «its», where the English has «its own». That is a harmless reflexive reading.
- **Ch. 7, εἰ μὴ … μὴ διαλύηται: KEEP.** Plate p. 715, col. 1404A: εἰ μὴ καὶ διὰ τῆς | ἀκουσίου ἐκπυρουμένη μὴ διαλύηται. Both negatives are printed. The sense holds: voluntary hardship is not enough unless the soul, fired by the involuntary as well, comes through without dissolving. The Latin keeps both too (*nisi … non dissolvatur*). The English «unless, fired through the involuntary as well, she is not dissolved» is correct.
- **Ch. 15, the negative chain: KEEP.** Plate p. 716, col. 1405A: Οὐχ᾿ ὁ τοῖς ἔργοις μὴ ἀδικῶν … οὗτος καὶ τοῖς λόγοις ἀμίαντον συνετήρησεν. Οὐ δὲ (printed as two words, = Οὐδὲ) ὁ τούτους φυλάττων, ἤδη καὶ λογισμοὺς οὐκ ἐμόλυνεν. The English has all four negatives in place: «It is not he who does not wrong … ; nor he who guards these who has thereby also not stained his thoughts.»
- **Ch. 81, μήτε … μήτε … ὅτι μὴ … οὐδέν: KEEP.** Plate p. 722, col. 1417D: τὸ μήτε ὁρᾷν, μήτε ἀκούειν, ὅτι μὴ | πᾶσα ἀνάγκη, τῶν ἐγκοσμίων οὐδέν. This is negative concord, one negation in sense, and ὅτι μή = «except». The English «to see nothing and hear nothing of the things of the world, except where it is of all necessity» is correct. The Latin agrees (*nihil … aut videre, autve audire, quin omnino necesse sit*). Side note, our file only: the Greek file reads «περιῤ-ῥαγῆναι ῥαγῆναι», but the plate has only περιῤ-|ῥαγῆναι. The doubled ῥαγῆναι is an OCR dittography in our Greek file. The English («broken all round») is unaffected.

Rejected:
- **Ch. 53**, περὶ τὴν κρείττω τῆς σιωπῆς ποσότητα → «in a quantity better than silence». I considered whether τῆς σιωπῆς is possessive («a greater quantity of silence»). Read as a comparative, «better than silence» could be taken as favouring talk. Rejected: the English keeps the parallel with ἥττω τρυφῆς («lower than luxury») and agrees with the Latin (*in potiori quam pro silentio … quantitate*). The Greek is ambiguous, and the English is a defensible construal, not a reversal.
- **Ch. 78 s4**, κατὰ σχέσιν μὲν τούτου, κατὰ δὲ φαντασίαν ἐκείνου → «by relation in the former [darkness], by imagination in the latter [light]». On grammar alone τούτου would be the nearer word, light. But the English uses τοῦτο/ἐκεῖνο the same way in s2–s3, where the sense makes τούτου the sensible light, and the Latin reads the same as the English (*tenebrarum … ex affectu; lucis … ex animi informatione*). Rejected.
- **Ch. 66**, ἡ μέν … τὸ δέ: the English takes ἡ = truth and τό = mercy, which is right by gender.
- **Ch. 83**, ἣν ὁ ἐξορίζων: the feminine pronoun points to εὐτραπελία (jesting), and the English «this» falls on «jesting» as the nearest noun. That is acceptable. «he who banishes jesting» would be clearer, but nothing is reversed.
- **Ch. 22 s4**, ὡς αὐτὴ ἀπατεῖ → «as she herself demands». The English reads ἀπαιτεῖ. The plate form «deceives» does not construe, so this is presumably one of the listed misprints and not my class.
- **Ch. 23 s4**, κατὰ τὴν ἔλλειψιν … καὶ τὸ φρονεῖν ἐκληρώσαντο: the English is literal and the sense (prudence allotted in proportion to the lack) is kept.
- **Ch. 28**, μὴ ἐπὶ πλεῖστον συγχωρηθῆναι: the negative is placed right («not to be granted … for very long»).
- **Ch. 43**, τὸν δεδοιότα μὴ ξένον … ὀφθῆναι: μή after a verb of fearing, rendered right («fears to be seen a stranger»).
- **Ch. 95**, οὐδεὶς ἀταπείνωτος: the double negative is kept as «no one comes unhumbled».
- **Ch. 99**, μὴ κατ᾿ ὄψιν → «out of sight»; οὐκ ἀπονητί → «not without toil». Both correct.
- **Sentence and clause counts** checked chapter by chapter. The ⟦s⟧ merges in chs 1, 3, 15, 18, 34, 47, 74, 91, 102 and 108 are joins with nothing lost. Ch. 34's plate «δεῖ. ἢ» is merged in the English: that is punctuation only and not in my classes.
