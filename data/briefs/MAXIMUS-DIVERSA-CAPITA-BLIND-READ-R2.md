Method: every chapter read Greek → English, Latin consulted where a point turned on it; every negative in the Greek checked against the English.

## Findings

(written progressively below)

### F1. II.94, col. 1256 [b: C–D] (chunk 0007): class 5 (with a class-3 edge) — the English renders the earlier edition's bad reading, not Migne's
- **Greek** (file only): «ἀντίδοσιν φέρουσα τῆς τῶν προηγμένων [male edita προηγουμ.] καλῆς διαθέσεως»
- **Latin**: «eorum, qui proba mente oblata sunt, ferens retributionem» — *oblata*, the things offered.
- **English as it stands**: "bearing a return for the good disposition of the things that went before."
- **Proposed**: "bearing a return for the good disposition of the things that have been offered."
- **Why**: προηγμένων (pf. pass. of προάγω, "brought forward / offered") answers the vow clause just before it (τῶν … Θεῷ … προσαγομένων καλῶν): prayer is the return for goods already brought to God. "The things that went before" is the sense of προηγουμένων, which is the reading Migne's bracket condemns as «male edita». The brief: a bracket is a finding when the English follows it. Not a polarity reversal, but it cuts the vow/prayer correspondence the chapter is built on.
- **Confidence**: probable (certain that "went before" = προηγουμ.; the exact English for προηγμένων is a judgement: "offered" per the Latin, or "brought forward").
- **Already in the cruces?** Yes, logged (1256, «…the things that went before»), checked after. The T note says "both readings mean «what went before»". That is the point I dispute: προηγμένων is "brought forward / offered" (or "advanced"), and "gone before" is προηγουμένων / προηγησαμένων. The T itself cites the Latin *oblata*. If the editor keeps "went before" as the sense of the vows of the preceding clause, the loss is small. Change recommended, at low priority.

### F2. II.53, col. 1240 [b: D] (chunk 0006): class 5 — «ὀρινομένην» rendered "stirred-up", losing the mountain the sentence turns on
- **Greek** (file only): «τὴν φαραγγωθεῖσαν ψυχὴν ἀναπληροῖ, καὶ τὴν ὀρινομένην τῶν πονηρῶν πνευμάτων ταπεινοῖ δυναστείαν»
- **Latin**: «anima vallis instar depressa adimpletur, montiumque supercilio elata nequissimorum spirituum potentia, deprimitur ac humiliatur»
- **English as it stands**: "brings low the stirred-up dominion of the evil spirits."
- **Proposed**: "brings low the dominion of the evil spirits that has been raised up into a mountain."
- **Why**: the pair is φαραγγωθεῖσαν "made a valley" / ὀρινομένην "made a mountain" (ὄρος; probably Migne's spelling of ὀρεινουμένην, or the form from ὀρεινόω), closing Isa 40:4 "every valley filled, every mountain brought low" that the chapter has just expounded. Read as ὀρίνω "stir up", the parallel is lost. The Latin reads it as mountain. Low stakes: no reversal.
- **Confidence**: probable.
- **Already in the cruces?** Yes, logged as a doubt (1240, «…the stirred-up dominion»: T notes the play on ὄρος and the Latin *montium supercilio elata*). I would resolve the doubt toward the mountain.

## R2 cases (judged)

- **II.18 οὐ λήψεται (col. 1228 [b: B–C]): KEEP.** The negative is split from its verb by the band letter («οὐ [b: C] λήψεται»), and the English has it: "he who, without this cause, seeks either other things or the things aforesaid, will not receive." It is parallel to the positive «πάντως λήψεται» before it, and the Latin agrees («non accipiet»). The next sentence's «ὡς ἅπωστος» does not parse. It is read as ἄπιστος ("as an unbeliever", Latin *tanquam infidelis*), which is right whether the slip is Migne's or the file's. It changes nothing.
- **II.55 [Regii duo ὀδύνης] (col. 1240 [b: D]): KEEP. The English is right to leave "pain" out.** The printed word is ἡδονῆς ("the death of pleasure, the mother of death"). The bracket is apparatus. The chapter's own last sentence fixes the sense: «τοῦ μὲν θανάτου μήτηρ ἐστὶν ἡ ἡδονή· τῆς ἡδονῆς δὲ θάνατός ἐστιν, ὁ πόνος» (toil is the death of pleasure). The Latin reads *voluptatis ac libidinis*. Reading ὀδύνης would make toil "the death of pain, the mother of death", which contradicts that sentence.
- **III.12–14 «limit» (cols. 1264–1265): KEEP.** ὅρος = limit is a fixed term (brief, "Fixed by ruling"), and it construes in all three places. In III.12 and III.13 the «ὅρος καὶ νόμος» of providence is "a divine limit and law". In III.14 God is «αὐτὸν τῆς σωτηρίας τὸν ὅρον», "the very limit of salvation", in the sense of a term or goal. Nothing there is reversed. The Latin's *regula* is looser, but the Latin is not the text. III.15 confirms the sense: God is "the beginning and end of virtue".

## Candidates considered and rejected

- **II.28 «πρὸς τῆς ἐν ὅλοις σοφίας»** → "before the wisdom in all things". Unusual (πρός + gen. could be "on the side of / from"), but the Latin reads *ante sapientiam*. Rejected.
- **II.36 «ἔλαβε ψεκτῶς τοῦ ξύλου τῆς ζωῆς»**: the tree *of life* taken blameworthily is odd, but it is what is printed and the English follows it. Not a finding.
- **II.68 «πάσης αὐτῷ γινομένη παρασκευῆς ὅπλων πνευματικῶν πρὸς τὸ θαῤῥεῖν παραμύθιον»** → "an encouragement toward confidence consisting in every equipment of spiritual arms". "Consisting in" is a construal of the genitive ("in place of"/"belonging to" are also possible). Nothing is reversed. Rejected.
- **II.57 «τῆς … φερούσης ἀεικινησίας»** → "since the ever-moving according to aspiration bears him". This is a noun (ever-motion) rendered as a participle-like substantive. A style point, not a sense change. Rejected.
- **II.93 «τῶν παρεχομένων φθορᾷ»** → "the things that pass away". The English follows the sense (παρερχομένων, Latin *fluxarum rerum*) against a misprint, which the brief permits. Logged in the cruces. Rejected.
- **III.7 «… τὴν ἀγαθότητα; δεχομένου …»**: the file has a Greek question mark mid-sentence, and the English runs on as a statement. The sentence is not a question by sense (a participle chain continues after the mark), so this is a misprint, or an OCR misreading of a raised point. Plate not opened. The English is right either way. Rejected.
- **III.12 «ὡς ἂν μὴ … ἀκαθαίρετον παρὰ τῆς Προνοίας ἔχειν συγχωρηθέντες τὴν οἴησιν, … κατολισθήσωμεν»** → "so that, being permitted by Providence to keep … undemolished our conceit …, we may not slip down". The participle could be read as conditional ("lest, if permitted…"), and the English as fact. But the Greek is just as bare, and the Latin construes it the same way (*ne … permittente providentia … nacti … prolabamur*). The μή governs the purpose clause, and the English keeps it there. Rejected.
- **III.18 «Ἔτι [edit. et unus Reg. ὅτι]»** → "Further". Follows the printed word. Rejected.
- **III.29 «ὅσα τῷ Δεσπότῃ δωρουμένῳ παρίσταται»** → "which he presents to the Master who gives". Matches the Latin (*quae donanti Domino adhibeant*). Rejected.
- **II.24 s4 «ἐπαινουμένην … νέκρωσιν»**: "the praised deadness of passionate concepts" looks like a reversal (death unto death to the wrong people?), but the Greek means a praised mortification *of* passionate concepts, and the Latin agrees (*laudabilem … cogitationum … mortificationem*). The English keeps the Greek's genitive. Rejected.
- Sentence count, paragraph by paragraph, across all four chunks: no clause lost or added. (The marker irregularities, a doubled ⟦s2⟧ in II.35/III.21 and the merged ⟦s3+4⟧ in II.87 and ⟦s1+2⟧ in III.7, are segmentation only. Text is complete.)
