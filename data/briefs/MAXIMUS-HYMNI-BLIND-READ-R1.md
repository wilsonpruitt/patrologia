## Findings

### F1. Hymn I (chunk 0001), col. 1419 l. 23 (H I.31): the Greek file has ἀγνῶς "unknowably"; the English renders ἁγνῶς "purely"
- **Class:** 5 (mistranslated word) — or a defect of the Greek file; one of the two must move.
- **Greek (plate-read, p-0772, col. 1419, at 3× native):** Ἀνθρωπόμορφον ἀγνῶς — the word is clear (ἀγνῶς, circumflex, final ς); the BREATHING on ἀ- **cannot be told** on this scan: at 3× it is a solid blob identical to the certain rough breathing of ἁμαρτάδεσσιν (p-0773, col. 1421 l. 28), which I checked side by side. Our file prints the smooth breathing.
- **English as it stands:** `In human form, purely,`
- **Proposed:** `In human form, unknowably,` (if the file's ἀγνῶς stands)
- **Why:** ἀγνῶς (smooth) is the adverb of ἀγνώς, "unknown, past knowing"; ἁγνῶς (rough) is "purely". The English follows a Greek our file does not print, silently. Migne's Latin translated the smooth-breathing word: *Humanam invisibiliter … Sumens*. Both readings make theological sense (ineffable incarnation / pure virgin birth). Because the scan cannot settle the breathing, the decision is: either keep the file's ἀγνῶς and change the English, or correct the file to ἁγνῶς (the brief's "standard form" rule would then apply) and log it; what may not stand is the present mismatch, unlogged.
- **Confidence:** certain that file and English disagree; probable that "unknowably" is right (Latin witness).
- **In cruces?** Yes, at 1419B: the translator chose ἁγνῶς "purely" and says the breathing cannot be told at the plate (I agree on that). But the crux writes "ἀγνῶς, 'purely'", i.e. it glosses the smooth-breathing form our file prints with the rough-breathing word's meaning. So the mismatch is logged without being seen; the choice stays open and either the file or the English must move.

### F2. Hymn I, col. 1419 l. 14 (H I.22): who set Adam as tiller — the Father, not the serpent
- **Class:** 2 (who does what).
- **Greek (plate-read, p-0772, col. 1419):** Ἐξ ὀρχάτων τε Πατρὸς, / Ὧν θῆκε, φεῦ, γεωργὸν, / Πάμπαν με ὀστρακίζων.
- **English as it stands:** `Whose tiller, ah, he had set me,`
- **Proposed:** `Whose tiller, ah, the Father had set me,`
- **Why:** the subject of θῆκε is the Father just named (Gen 2:15: God sets Adam in the garden to work it); the serpent is the subject of ὦσεν and ὀστρακίζων around it. In the English the only "he" in the sentence is the serpent ("he thrust me … Banishing me"), so "he had set me" reads as the serpent having made Adam gardener. Naming the subject adds no idea the Greek lacks; it only blocks the wrong one. (If repeating the noun is unwanted, `Whose tiller, ah, he had set me` must at least not sit between two serpent-verbs; there is no other cheap fix in English.)
- **Confidence:** probable (the Greek is unambiguous; the English is ambiguous rather than wrong, but the ambiguity resolves the wrong way).
- **In cruces?** No.

### F3. Hymn I, col. 1420 l. 52–53 (H I.120–121): Ἄκραντα is adverbial, not an epithet of χρηστότητα
- **Class:** 3 (mis-parsed grammar).
- **Greek (plate-read, p-0772, col. 1420, last lines of Hymn I):** Ὅπως τεὴν ὑμνήσω / Ἄκραντα χρηστότητα / Πατρός τε καὶ πνοῆς σῆς. — the plate prints Ἄκραντα, not ἄκραντον.
- **English as it stands:** `That I may hymn thy` / `Endless goodness,`
- **Proposed:** `That I may hymn endlessly` / `Thy goodness,`
- **Why:** ἄκραντος is two-termination; with the feminine accusative singular χρηστότητα it would have to be ἄκραντον. ἄκραντα is neuter plural, i.e. adverbial accusative with ὑμνήσω ("hymn without end"). The English moves the endlessness from the hymning to the goodness. The sense of the word ("without end") is kept; only its attachment changes. Migne's Latin (*Infinitae bonitatis*) made the same attachment, which is how the English could inherit it.
- **Confidence:** probable (the grammar is clear; a poet's loose agreement under the metre is conceivable, which is the only reason this is not "certain").
- **In cruces?** No.

### F4. Hymn II (chunk 0002), col. 1421 l. 32–33 (H II.34–35): Ὕπουλον belongs to φρόνημα, not to the subject
- **Class:** 3 (hyperbaton resolved wrongly / participle-adjective on the wrong noun).
- **Greek (plate-read, p-0773, col. 1421):** Λευκάζον ἥγ' ὀθνεῖον / Ἔξω φέρουσα χρῶμα, / Ὕπουλον ἀγνοοῦσι / Μορφάζετο φρόνημα / Ἐντόσθιον καλύπτον / Ἀσχήμονα πρόσοψιν,
- **English as it stands:** `Festering beneath, to the unknowing` / `Put on a way of thinking`
- **Proposed:** `To the unknowing a festering` / `Way of thinking put on,` (lines 32–33 and 36–39 unchanged: `It, a strange, whitening` / `Colour wearing outwardly,` … `That hid within` / `An unseemly face,`)
- **Why:** the subject is ἥ γε, nominative feminine (the heart, κραδίη, of the line before). ὕπουλος is two-termination, so a nominative feminine would be ὕπουλος; ὕπουλον can only be accusative (or neuter) and goes with φρόνημα, like καλύπτον two lines on: "she put on, before the unknowing, a festering-under disposition that hid an unseemly face within". The English makes the heart itself "festering beneath" and leaves the φρόνημα without its epithet; the point of the image (the hypocrite's assumed mind is itself the sore under a whitened skin) is shifted.
- **Confidence:** probable.
- **In cruces?** Partly: 1421C explains ὕπουλον (a wound festering beneath) and identifies ἥ γ' as the heart, but does not address which noun ὕπουλον agrees with. The attachment is not logged.

### F5. Hymn II, col. 1421 l. 41–43 (H II.43–45): pride hurled the Typhon; the English lets the Typhon do the hurling
- **Class:** 2 (who does what: subject/object flipped in the English reading).
- **Greek (plate-read, p-0773, col. 1421):** Δόξης ὅσοις μέμηλε / Ψευδωνύμου, φύσσης τε, / Καὶ ἣ πάλαι τυφῶνα / Πόλοιο ῥίψεν αἶψα / Ἑωσφόρον σκοτώδη / Κρίσει Θεοῦ δικαίᾳ. (The plate's ἡ/ἣ carries a breathing and an accent too small to tell grave from none at this scan; either way it is NOMINATIVE feminine. τυφῶνα is printed with -α, accusative.)
- **English as it stands:** `And which of old the Typhon` / `Hurled swiftly from the sky,` / `The darkened Morning-star,`
- **Proposed:** `And which of old hurled the Typhon` / `Swiftly from the sky,` / `The darkened Morning-star,`
- **Why:** ἣ (feminine, nominative) picks up φύσσης, "puffing-up, pride": it is the subject of ῥίψεν; τυφῶνα and Ἑωσφόρον σκοτώδη are accusatives, the object, in apposition (the devil as Typhon and fallen Morning-star, Is 14:12 — cast down by pride, "by God's just judgement"). English "which … the Typhon hurled" is naturally read with the Typhon as subject and "which" (pride) as what he hurled, and the following "The darkened Morning-star" then floats. Moving "hurled" up one line restores the Greek roles. (On τυφῶνα itself: Typhon, the monster, is right; it plays on τῦφος "conceit" beside φύσσα, a pun the English cannot carry and should not try to.)
- **Confidence:** certain (the case endings decide it; the current English is at best ambiguous and its natural reading is the reverse).
- **In cruces?** In substance, yes: 1421C says "The relative is the puffing-up", so the translator parsed correctly. The defect is the English word order, which does not deliver that parse; the finding stands as a wording fix.

### F6. Hymn II, col. 1421 l. 55 – col. 1422 l. 2 (H II.57–59): ἡ is the soul, not "a slipping"
- **Class:** 2 (who does what) / 3 (antecedent).
- **Greek (plate-read, p-0773, top of col. 1422):** Ψυχῇ δ' ἐμῇ ὄλισθος, / [1422] Ἄγειρεν ἡ τοσαύτην / Βλάβην ἔνοικον αὐτῇ / Θυμόν τ' ἄνωθεν αἰνὸν / Βροτησίαν τε ἔχθραν. — the plate prints ἡ with a rough breathing and no accent visible (the article used as a pronoun, Homeric "she"), not the relative ἣ.
- **English as it stands:** `And for my soul a slipping,` / `[1422] Which gathered so great` / `A harm to dwell within it,`
- **Proposed:** `And a slipping for my soul,` / `[1422] Which gathered so great` / `A harm to dwell within it,` — or, closer to the plate's pronoun: `And for my soul a slipping;` / `[1422] She gathered so great` / `A harm to dwell within her,`
- **Why:** ἡ (or ἣ, if the accent is lost) is feminine; ὄλισθος is masculine and cannot be its antecedent. The only feminine in reach is ψυχῇ ἐμῇ, and αὐτῇ "within her" (feminine) is the same soul. Migne's Latin reads it so: *Animae autem meae praeceps via. Ipsa tantam adduxit In se calamitatem*. In the English "Which" follows "a slipping" and makes the fall itself the thing that gathered harm, anger from above and mortal enmity; the Greek says the soul brought them on herself, which is the point of a confession.
- **Confidence:** certain on the antecedent (gender); the choice between the two proposed wordings is the editor's.
- **In cruces?** In substance, yes: 1422A says the antecedent is "either way the soul", and quotes the Latin *Ipsa*. As with F5, the parse was right and the English line order hangs "Which" on the wrong noun; the finding stands as a wording fix. (The crux calls the accent indeterminate; I read ἡ with no accent, but agree it is small.)

### F7. Hymn II, col. 1422 l. 5–6 (H II.62–63): διέγνων + infinitive is "I have resolved to", not "I have discerned"
- **Class:** 5 (mistranslated word) / 3 (construction).
- **Greek (file; the line is legible on the p-0773 overview, not crop-read):** Πέτραν δὲ νῦν διέγνων / Θέμεθλον ἐμβαλέσθαι, / Σώζοιτο ὡς ἂν οἶκος,
- **English as it stands:** `But now I have discerned the rock` / `To lay down as foundation,`
- **Proposed:** `But now I have resolved the rock` / `To lay down as foundation,` — or, plainer across the two lines: `But now I have resolved to lay` / `The rock down as foundation,`
- **Why:** διαγιγνώσκω with an infinitive is "determine, resolve to (do)" (LSJ s.v. II; the Thucydidean use). "Discerned the rock to lay down" is not English for anything and hides that the line is the penitent's decision, which is the hinge of the hymn (the foolish builder now chooses to build on the rock, Mt 7:24–27; next lines: "that the house may be kept safe"). Migne's Latin agrees in substance: *Nunc autem novi petram … fundamentum ponendum esse* — he too read a judgement about what must be done.
- **Confidence:** probable.
- **In cruces?** No.

## The brief's "places to judge" (verdicts, with the plate)

- **Col. 1420 l. 43 (H I.111), ἤρυξας — KEEP `And thou didst ward off more besides`.** Plate (p-0772) prints Πλείω δ' ἤρυξας ἄλλα, clean. The only parse is the aorist of ἐρύκω, "hold back, keep off" (ἐρεύγομαι has ἤρυγον, not ἤρυξα). Its object ἄλλα is "other things" in the sense of further ills kept off, which suits the run of healings before it (fever stilled, flux stayed). The Latin *egisti* is a paraphrase, rightly flagged `[lat:]`. "Restrain" or "check" would also do; "ward off" is not wrong.
- **Col. 1421 l. 38 (H II.40), Οἰκέτριβας — KEEP `house-slaves`.** Plate prints Οἰκέτριβας δὲ εἶχον (with ε, not ο). It is οἰκότριψ "house-bred slave" (τρίβω "reared in the house"). The Latin *Damnosos … hospites* reads it as "house-wreckers" (τρίβω "wear out"), which is the `[lat:]` already in the English. The allegory supports the slave sense: they are the household of the house the speaker built, they have children, and they obey. (If the editor wants the precise term, `house-bred slaves`; not a finding.)
- **Col. 1421 l. 41 (H II.43), τυφῶνα and what stands beside it:** see **F5** (the word is right; the grammar of the English is not).
- **Col. 1422 l. 1 (H II.58), ἡ / ἣ:** see **F6** (antecedent is the soul; the English hangs "Which" on "a slipping").
- **Col. 1422 l. 31 (H II.88), ἦν / ἤν — KEEP `If it is pure from above,`, and log the plate.** Plate (p-0773, at 3×) prints **ἦν with a circumflex**, plainly. As printed, ἦν ἁγνεύων would be a periphrastic imperfect ("was keeping pure"), which does not sit with the present πάσχει and leaves the sentence without its condition. The conditional ἤν (= ἐάν) with the participle (ᾖ understood) is the only construal that makes the sentence go, and Migne's own Latin read a condition-like clause too (*Desuper ita fit purus*). So the English is right, but it translates ἤν over a printed ἦν. **Cruces (checked after):** logged at 1422B, which says the accent "is not clear at the plate"; at 3× I read a clear circumflex, so the crux should say ἦν is printed, not that the accent is unclear.
- **Col. 1419, the serpent's whispering (H I.17), Δεινῶς ψιθυρίσας μοι — KEEP `Whispering dreadfully to me,`.** Plate (p-0772) as file. Aorist participle with the subject ὁ ὄφις; "dreadfully" for δεινῶς is fine. (The nearby problem in this sentence is F2, the subject of θῆκε.)
- **Hymn III, Ξένον τ' ἄημα ἀμφοῖν / Ἶσον γόνῳ καὶ ἀρχῇ — KEEP.** Plate (p-0774) as file. ἄημα is the Spirit (addressed, nominative-for-vocative), ἶσον agrees with ἄημα, and ἀμφοῖν is expanded by γόνῳ καὶ ἀρχῇ: the Breath is equal to both, the Offspring and the Source. The English (`And strange breath, to both` / `Equal, to offspring and to source,`) has exactly that.

## Candidates considered and rejected

- **H I.1 τλήμον'** vs Latin *beatum*: English follows Greek; `[lat:]` correct.
- **H I.11–12 Λάμπον … μεθέξει**: neuter, with ζῶον; "Shining by participation / In the image" is right.
- **H I.26–27 Ἰατρὸν οὐχ ὑπέστη** `Would bear no physician`: negative kept; "would" is a mild modal colour for the aorist ("admitted no physician"), not a change of sense.
- **H I.37–40 μόρου … Εἵμαρτο ὅς … ἀπεκπεσόντι**: relative and dative participle correctly resolved ("fated for me, who … had fallen away").
- **H I.67 Ἅ μοι φλέγει κακόφρων**: neuter plural ἅ (darts) as object, κακόφρων the subject; English correct, if long.
- **H I.93 Θερμὴν δακρυῤῥοούσης**: θερμήν is adverbial/elliptic ("hot [tears]"); `As she shed hot tears` is fair.
- **H I.105 Λύσιν μελῶν ἀθροίσας** `Having gathered the slackness of his limbs`: literal and right.
- **H I.113 εἶ κ'** (plate: εἶ with circumflex): it is εἴ κε, "if"; the English's "if" is right; at most a crux note on the accent.
- **H I.118 Ἥν** antecedent δρόσον (dew): English "Which" after "coals?" across a question mark is slightly loose but the Greek is equally so.
- **H II.4 ἀνάγκης;**: the Greek ; here cannot be a question (the genitives run on with τε); the English semicolon is the right reading of the plate's mark.
- **H II.14 Θεμέθλων, ὃν δ' ἔβαλλον**: ὃν does not agree with θεμέθλων, but the English "the foundations I was laying" is the only sense; a crux, not an English finding.
- **H II.21–22 Βροχὴ … Ἔξυγρον ἐμφανίζει** `Shows soaked through`: object (the sand) understood; acceptable.
- **H II.32 ἥγ'** feminine (the heart) rendered `It`: after "the heart's / Will" an English reader may take "It" as the will; minor, covered by F4's note, not reported separately.
- **H II.47–49 Νήριθμοι … κοῦραι, Πειθήνιον διδοῦσαι**: διδοῦσαι is feminine, strictly the daughters; English "Who lent" follows "daughters" directly, so it reads correctly.
- **H II.80 ἁγνοῦ** (plate breathing unreadable; Latin *agno* = "lamb"): English "pure" right; `[lat:]` already present.
- **H II.84–85 παριστάνοντος**: agrees with συνειδότος (the conscience presents the mortal like the angels); English "Which … Presents" right.
- **H II.92–93 κ' ἄημα, / Ζωηφόρον γε πνεῦσιν**: πνεῦσιν may be the accusative of πνεῦσις ("a life-bearing blowing", apposition) or a dative participle; `Life-bearing in its blowing` covers the first fairly.
- **H II.98–99 ἐν χρόνοισιν … ἀεὶ ἐοῦσι**: right.
- **H II.106 ὃς αὐτὸς ἔπλες** `which thou thyself art`: ἔπλες is a past form used of timeless being; "art" is right.
- **H II.113–117**: "Neither … Shall then be able to shake my house / So much as a little" keeps the negation (οὐδὲ τυτθόν = not even a little).
- **H II.150 Ἄσβεστον**: may go with γέενναν or πῦρ; English's "unquenchable fire" is a legitimate resolution.
- **H II.154–155 Ἐνθήσεαι … βιοῦντα** `thou wilt set me`: "me" supplied from the masculine accusative participle; right.
- **H III.5 Μορφὴ τρισήλιός μοι** `My three-sunned form;`: μοι is an ethic dative; "my" with a vocative ("my God") is idiomatic and does not make the speaker's form three-sunned.
