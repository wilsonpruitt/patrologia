## Findings

### F1. 0007, col 213 A–B (Calfa p.112 a) — class 3 (mis-parsed grammar / scope that can read as a reversed polarity) — probable
- **Greek (plate-read):** Ὁ τὰ τοιαῦτα ποιῶν κατάκριτος· καὶ ὁ τὰ τῶν ἀσεβῶν δεχόμενος βρέφη, εἰ μὴ πρότερον καθομολογήσωσιν οὗτοι ἅγιον εἶναι καὶ θεῖον τὸ βάπτισμα, … καὶ Χριστιανὸν εἶναι τὸν τοῦτο δεχόμενον (εἰ γὰρ τοῦτο, … ὑποκρύπτεται), καλὸν δέχεσθαι καὶ ἁγιάζειν τὸ βρέφος, μήποτε καὶ ληφθείη οὕτω παρὰ Θεοῦ. (Plate prints καθομολογήσωσιν; file has a garbled ἅαθομολογήσωσιν.)
- **Latin:** *Maledictus qui talia agit, necnon qui impiorum infantes suscipit, nisi prius isti confiteantur …* — Allatius carries κατάκριτος over to the second subject.
- **English as it stands:** "He who does such things is condemned; and he who receives the infants of the impious, unless these first confess that baptism is holy and divine,"
- **Proposed English:** "He who does such things is condemned; and so is he who receives the infants of the impious, unless these first confess that baptism is holy and divine,"
- **Why:** The Greek leaves κατάκριτος understood for ὁ … δεχόμενος. Left unrepaired, the English subject "he who receives…" has no verb, so the only main clause a reader finds is "unless these first confess …, it is good to receive and sanctify the infant." That says the reverse of the rule: receive the child *unless* the parents confess. Supplying the understood predicate ("and so is") follows the elided κατάκριτος, as the Latin also does, and puts the negative condition back on the condemnation. The clause "(…), it is good to receive" then reads, as Symeon means it, as the case where they do confess. The plate's own syntax is an anacoluthon, which is why this is probable and not certain.
- **Already in cruces?** Partly. cruces.md (0213 μήποτε entry) notes "The sentence is also anacoluthic (ὁ τὰ τῶν ἀσεβῶν δεχόμενος βρέφη … καλὸν δέχεσθαι), carried" and logs *Maledictus qui talia agit* as looseness. The anacoluthon is known, but the cruces do not record that carrying it unrepaired makes the English read as the reverse rule. The fix proposed here is new.

### F2. 0008, col 220 C (Calfa p.115 a–b) — class 2 (who does what) — probable
- **Greek (plate-read):** Τοῦτο οὖν ποιησαμένου τρὶς, καὶ ἐρωτηθέντος αὖθις τρὶς, «Συνετάξω τῷ Χριστῷ;» καὶ ἀποκριθέντος τρὶς ἐκ καρδίας, καὶ εἰρηκότος, «Συνεταξάμην αὐτῷ,» ὁ ἀρχιερεύς φησι, «Καὶ προσκύνησον αὐτῷ,» καὶ σφραγισαμένου σταυρῷ διὰ τῆς χειρὸς αὐτοῦ, … καὶ προσκυνήσαντος τρὶς, ἐνδεικνυμένου τὴν δουλείαν, καὶ τὴν ἀληθῆ μετὰ τῆς ὁμολογίας ὑποταγὴν, στρέφεται πρὸς ἀνατολὰς, καὶ εὐλογεῖ τὸν Θεὸν ὁ ἀρχιερεὺς τὸν πάντας σωθῆναι θέλοντα.
- **Latin:** *postquam ter adoraverit …, ad orientem convertitur, et benedicit pontifex Deum*, which leaves the subject of *convertitur* just as open.
- **English as it stands:** "he turns toward the east, and the bishop blesses God, who wills all to be saved."
- **Proposed English:** "the bishop turns toward the east and blesses God, who wills all to be saved."
- **Why:** Everything the candidate does in this sentence is in the genitive absolute (σφραγισαμένου, προσκυνήσαντος, ἐνδεικνυμένου), so the subject of the finite verbs is someone else. That is the postposed ὁ ἀρχιερεύς, shared by στρέφεται and εὐλογεῖ. The same section keeps this pattern throughout: the candidate in the genitive, the bishop in the nominative (Ἐρωτήσας … τοῦ βαπτιζομένου συνθεμένου … ὁ ἀρχιερεύς). The candidate also cannot be the one turning: he was already turned east a few lines above (Εἶτα στρέφει τοῦτον κατὰ ἀνατολάς), and that turn was done by the bishop (active στρέφει), not by the candidate himself. The English, with "he … and the bishop", reads as two different people and makes the candidate the one who turns. Probable rather than certain, because Byzantine use of the genitive absolute is loose.
- **Already in cruces?** Yes, as an open question. cruces.md (0220 entry) records the subject as unexpressed and says it was rendered "leaving it as open as the Greek". This finding disputes that: the genitive-absolute pattern and the earlier active στρέφει point to the bishop, and the English "he … and the bishop" is not neutral but leans toward the candidate.

## Candidates considered and rejected
- **0006 col 208 D:** "grants leave … in nothing at all to be hindered, or to partake of defilement, or to waver".
  I considered whether "grants leave … to partake of defilement" could be misread. Rejected: the negative
  κατὰ μηδὲν ὅλως governs all three infinitives, and the English keeps that scope ("in nothing at all … or … or").
- **0007 col 212 B:** "and it does obeisance to him who made it" (καὶ προσκυνεῖ τῷ ποιήσαντι, comma before it on
  the plate). Could the subject be the priest? Rejected: it reads naturally inside δηλῶν ὡς … ("showing that it
  is an offering to God and does obeisance to its maker"), and the Latin *factorem adorat* is just as open. Defensible.
- **0007 col 213 B:** μήποτε rendered "lest". Already argued in the cruces; "lest" is its first sense, and the Latin
  (*ne*) agrees. Not re-reported. If the editor takes the "perhaps" reading, it should be decided together with F1.
- **0007 / 0008:** τρίτον rendered "three times" (ἐμφυσᾷ … τρίτον; Τρίτον δὲ ἐμφυσᾶται). Rejected as a
  thrice/third error: τρὶς οὖν ἐμφυσήσας in the same passage shows that τρίτον means "thrice" here.
- **0005 col 204 D:** "to those only who approach with repentance" (τοῖς μετανοίᾳ μόνον προσερχομένοις). Where does
  μόνον attach? Rejected: either attachment gives the same restriction, and nothing turns on it.
- **0007 col 212 A:** ἧξ γεγεννημένον (file) rendered "having itself also been born". The plate prints the Iassy number **76**
  there, not a word, so the English is right.
- **0008 col 216 B:** "The bishop and priest who baptizes" (ἀρχιερεὺς καὶ ἱερεὺς with a singular participle).
  Rejected: the printed "and" is carried, which follows convention.
