## 1. Chronology facts (every dated event, with page cites)

### Early life and ministry
- **1800–1875** — Migne's life dates (front matter, CIP data). Bloch notes he came from the **Auvergne**, whose folklore of stubborn persistence he treats as a key to Migne's character (p. 2). Native region ties: special predilection for workers from **Saint-Flour** (p. 20). NB: this chunk gives no birthplace, parents, or education details — Bloch opens with Migne already a priest.
- **1825** — Migne named **curé at Puiseaux**, chef-lieu de canton in the Gâtinais — per his earliest biographer Hippolyte Barbier, a place of "the lowly shops and the little clerks in notary offices, that is, a liberalism of the kitchen sink" (p. 6). Bloch dates the whole arc "from Migne's installation in Puiseaux in 1825 to his death in Paris half a century later" (p. 9).
- **Early July Monarchy (c. 1830–31), Puiseaux** — episode one: a fellow priest, evicted from his parish, arrested at the entrance to town by the garde nationale; Migne arrives to "guarantee" his freedom, encourages the priest to lie down (proto–civil disobedience), faces down bayonets (p. 6). *Flagged by Bloch as unverified — see §6.*
- **Fête-Dieu 1831** — episode two: "anti-clerical liberals" place the tricolor flag on the church altar as a prank; Migne denounced by the authorities for showing insufficient respect for the flag (pp. 6–7).
- **1831–32 (aftermath)** — Migne composes a **two-hundred-page brochure**, *De la Liberté, par un Prêtre* (*On Freedom, by a Priest*), analyzing church–state relations. Called before the **Bishop of Orléans, Mgr de Beauregard**, who confiscated it without having read "a single one of the lines he condemned" (Barbier). It never saw the light of day. Bloch: the conflict "was directly responsible for Migne's decision to leave the provinces for Paris" (p. 7).
- The **exeat** signed by the Bishop of Orléans makes no mention of patrologies (which "almost certainly had not yet entered his consciousness") but states Migne "is of sound moral character. … He has distinguished himself by his faith, his piety, his zeal, his love of work. … he only leaves the diocese, under difficult circumstances, much to our regret, in order to devote himself to a newspaper called the *Univers religieux*, by which he hopes to do good works" (pp. 23–24).

### Paris and journalism (1833–1836)
- **Early 1830s** — Migne "arrived in Paris almost penniless … and with only a meager education" (p. 1). Part of the era's provincial migration to the capital: Garnier Frères installed in the Palais-Royal toward 1833, Boucicaut arrived from Normandy 1835, Larousse came to Paris 1839 (pp. 8–9).
- Installed **on the rue des Fossés-Saint-Jacques**; sent prospectuses to all the bishops of France announcing two newspapers, *L'Univers religieux* and *Le Spectateur* — the latter "merely a screen, a 'straw newspaper,'" the first of his double-newspaper publicity stunts; it disappeared as soon as *L'Univers religieux* was established (p. 24).
- **September 1833** — prospectus dispatched; **by October 1833**, before the first number even appeared, Migne had acquired a second paper: the timid and unworldly **Emmanuel Bailly** proposed that *L'Univers religieux* absorb *La Tribune catholique* (itself an obscure avatar of Lamennais's *L'Avenir*, which had folded financially in 1831) (pp. 24, 26).
- **November 3, 1833** — first volume of ***L'Univers religieux*** appeared, printed at the Presses de Bailly, 2 Place de la Sorbonne; thereafter every day but Monday (p. 26). Renamed ***L'Univers*** the following year (1834) (p. 26).
- **Late 1833** — Bloch suggests it "may in fact have been in the early days of *L'Univers religieux*" that Migne **first conceived the project of editing the Church Fathers**; one of the earliest articles, signed "Jean d'Aure" (pseudonym of Melchior du Lac), was titled *Lettres familières sur les Pères de l'Église — Première lettre: Des connaissances les plus utiles à l'étude des Saints Pères* (pp. 24–25).
- **October 23, 1834** — **Picot**, editor of the rival *L'Ami de la religion*, publicly accused *L'Univers* of **plagiarism** ("annals of plagiarism" passage, quoted in §4) (p. 26).
- **December 24, 1835** — Migne **convicted by the Tribunal de Police Correctionnelle de la Seine of attempting to bribe a postal employee**; fined two hundred francs (p. 26).
- **1836** — Migne **left *L'Univers*** (p. 26). Melchior du Lac edited *L'Univers religieux* between Migne's 1836 departure and Louis Veuillot's arrival in the early 1840s (p. 24); Louis Veuillot was editor of *L'Univers* from 1843 on (p. 18).

### Founding of the Ateliers Catholiques (1838–39) and the great series
- **By 1838** — Migne installed at **Montrouge** and founded the **Ateliers Catholiques** — "one can assume with the **five thousand francs** obtained from sale of his shares of *L'Univers* to Bailly" (p. 26).
- **February 15, 1839** — Ministre de l'Intérieur document accords a **brevet de libraire (bookseller's license) to Victor Migne, his brother**, who is also granted by the mayor and town council of Montrouge permission to install a print shop (pp. 26–27).
- Location: "le Petit Montrouge, près des barrières d'Enfer et du Maine" — presently number 189, at the corner of the rue Thibaud (p. 14).
- **The Bibliothèque universelle du clergé** — general rubric of the whole corpus, more than a thousand books; ~**400 books published before the first page of the patrologies had even gone to print** (pp. 1, 14). Component series with dates (pp. 1–2):
  1. *Scripturae sacrae cursus completus* — 25 volumes, **1838–40**
  2. *Theologiae cursus completus* — 25 volumes, **1840–42**
  3. *Démonstrations évangéliques des plus célèbres défenseurs du Christianisme* — 18 volumes, **1842–43**
  4. *Orateurs sacrés* — two series (66 and 33 volumes), **1844–66**
  5. Theological encyclopedias: *Première encyclopédie théologique* (50 vols, **1844–52**); *Nouvelle encyclopédie théologique* (53 vols, **1851–59**); *Troisième et dernière encyclopédie ecclésiastique* (66 vols, **1851–59**)
  6. *Cours complet d'histoire ecclésiastique* — 27 volumes, **1862–**
  7. *Summa aurea de laudibus B. Mariae virginis* — 13 volumes, **1866–**
  8. 150 diverse volumes published between **1840 and 1868**
- **Patrologia Latina** — 217 tomes in 218 volumes, issued in two series between **1844 and 1855** (p. 1).
- **Patrologia Graeca** — Greco-Latin edition, 161 tomes in 166 volumes, **1857–1866**; plus an edition of the Greek Fathers in Latin only, 81 tomes in 85 volumes (p. 1). The patrologies combined weigh in at over a million pages, yet represent only about one-half of Migne's total output (p. 1).
- **Solesmes contract (c. 1841–42)** — **Dom Guéranger**, "who in fact first suggested the project of the patrologies," signed a contract with Migne under which the Benedictines of Solesmes would edit the Cours, with sole responsibility for doctrinal contents, typography, and tables, at **three volumes per month**. Abandoned when Guéranger failed to obtain his monks' consent — they feared that in case of delay Migne would not only withhold honoraria but sue for damages with interest (letter of **Dom Gardereau to Dom Guéranger, April 20, 1842**) (p. 13).
- **October 14, 1841** — letter to the editor Dübner (whom Migne called "Dupner," later "Dubner") pressing for delivery of the *City of God*: 60 typesetters idle (pp. 16–17; BN n.a. 6143, fol. 240).
- **1843** — **Dom Pitra** signs a contract with Migne, assuming the role of "chief architect" of the Cours complets (p. 13). Guéranger continued collaborating by collecting books for the patrologies and recommended Pitra to Migne (p. 13).
- **March 20, 1844** — Pitra complains to Dom Guéranger about Migne's extravagant in-folio title page for the patrologie ("salesman of suspicious goods" letter, §4) (pp. 2–3).

### The newspaper empire (1846–1861)
- **1846** — Migne reenters journalism, founding ***La Voix de la vérité***, in daily and triweekly editions (p. 27).
- **August 20 and October 29, 1847** — Migne **censured by Mgr Affre**, Archbishop of Paris (p. 31).
- **1848** — Migne voted for General Cavaignac (per police report) (p. 21).
- **Law of July 27, 1849** — submitted all book distribution to the appropriate authority (p. 28).
- **May 31, 1850** — founded the ***Journal des faits***, aimed at a secular rather than clerical readership. Victor Migne again held the brevet; actual manager was **Guillaume Vassal**, who just two months later formed a separate corporation with Victor excluding Migne altogether. Migne sued Vassal and his brother in the Tribunal de Commerce; settled out of court at the behest of the Migne family; the ownership dispute lasted over three years (pp. 27–28).
- **Coup d'état of December 1851** — reinstated the government stamp for books; decree placed newspapers under a regime of surveillance (warned, suspended, or eliminated by simple administrative decision) (p. 28). A commission established in **1854** examined all books submitted for required official authorization (p. 28).
- **February 10, 1852** — letter to the Ministre de l'Instruction Publique et des Cultes vowing to pursue a delinquent debtor — a fellow priest, let out of sight by the Bishop of Algiers, owing **190 francs for thirty volumes of the Cours d'orateurs** — "even as far as California" (p. 3).
- **July 1853** — **Victor Migne died unexpectedly**. Vassal named a certain **Frédéric Delbrel** as editor; Migne rejected him, accusing both of disinheriting his brother's children (p. 28).
- **August 13, 1853** — deposition to the Directeur de la Sûreté Générale: "property rights, that is to say, the most sacred thing in the world after religion" (p. 21).
- **November 11, 1853** — three-column broadsheet to the stockholders of the *Journal des faits* against opponent M. Vassal (apocalyptic "employee revolts against his boss" flourish; also "Can a father not love several children?") (pp. 21–22, 37).
- **December 11, 1853** — police report: the *Journal des faits* is "a newspaper without editorial costs since it specializes in reproducing that which is edited elsewhere" (p. 37).
- **December 13, 1853** — Migne to the Ministre de l'Intérieur: a reprint digest "does not need an editor-in-chief" (p. 36).
- **December 15, 1853** — Ministre demands the names of those legally responsible; Migne responds proposing "M. Pierre Blot de Poly, from Yonne" as managing editor (under the first article of the statute of **February 17, 1852**) (p. 30).
- **December 20, 1853** — the *Journal des faits*, auctioned because of the ownership squabble, becomes Migne's for **forty-one thousand francs** (p. 28).
- **December 21, 1853** — confidential letter to the Minister exposing the Blot de Poly fiction: "the man is a worker, a typesetter, in his print shop and his real name is Pierre Blot, born in Poly (Yonne)" (p. 30). Same date, Ministre note on the Gasnier quarrel "which practically ended in blows" (p. 18).
- **December 23, 1853** (report, repeated by **Inspector Gaillard, February 1854**) — Migne characterized as "un homme violent qui a souvent des rixes avec ses employés" (p. 18).
- **January 31, 1854** — Cabinet du Préfet de Police report: Migne "is known as a schemer (*intrigant*) and surrounds himself with all the priests on the index throughout the dioceses of France" (p. 20).
- **February 6, 1854** — "The editor is a pair of scissors" letter to the Directeur de la Sûreté Générale (p. 39).
- **February 10, 1854** — the ***Journal des faits* closed down by the government**, following two condemnations: one for printing false news, the other for fraudulent copying (*contrefaçon*) of articles from *Le Constitutionnel* (p. 28).
- **1854** — *La Voix de la vérité* **divided** into a daily called simply *La Vérité* and a biweekly keeping the original name — Bloch: "This amounted to a stock split, since Migne's chief motive seems to have been the sale of two Vérités where there had once been only one" (p. 27). *La Voix de la vérité* condemned by **Mgr Sibour** in 1854 for advice offered to two clerics banned by the archbishop (pp. 31–32).
- **May 3, 1854** — Ministre de l'Intérieur: "[Migne] is known for his troublesome, pesky, confused spirit … One must watch out for constant abuse on his part" (p. 18).
- **June 26, 1854** — *La Vérité* condemned by the Bureau de la Presse et Colportage for an article about the Imperial family (p. 32).
- **June 28, 1854** — "I am one of the busiest men in France…" letter to the Ministre de l'Intérieur (inability to read a tenth of what he prints) (p. 37).
- **1856** — Migne sold *La Vérité* to **Jules Bordot**, who sold it in turn; it became the *Courrier de Paris*, then disappeared completely in **1859** (p. 27).
- **September 29 / October 27, 1857** — Préfet de Police correspondence on the "very bad political spirit" of Migne's workers; 350–400 workers on average, women included (p. 17).
- **1857** — *La Haute-Auvergne* article on the "cordial hospitality" workers from Saint-Flour find chez Migne (p. 20).
- **February 17, 1860** — after several attempts to dispose of *La Voix de la vérité*, Migne finally sold it to **Eugène Taconet**, authorized that day to change its name to ***Le Monde***, having merged it with the surviving version of Migne's original *L'Univers* (p. 27).
- **September 12, 1860** — Migne to the Ministre de l'Intérieur on the planned *La Vérité canonique*: it "will contain no substantive article … will remove the clergy from all opinions by positioning it in a superior realm" (p. 38).
- **1861** — launch of the weekly ***La Vérité canonique, liturgique, historique, bibliographique, anecdotique***; first edition **April 6, 1861** (Pierre's shed real-estate query); issue of **December 21, 1861**: curé vs. mayor over keys to the church annex (pp. 32–33). Alert to clergy who invested with **M. Mirès**, judgment of the commercial court of the Seine dated **April 4, 1861** (p. 34).
- **March 12, 1862** — letter to Dom Pitra: notices to be carried out "in my old age or while I will be serving in Purgatory … if my cours complet de Patrologie does not cause me to go directly to heaven" (p. 8).
- **1864** — letter to Dom Pitra: "I am curious to know if the Holy Father knows of my existence. My well-established destiny … is to go to Rome as soon as I will have finished and to deposit myself a copy of all my great publications" (p. 8). Same year, in the *Annales de philosophie chrétienne*: even the Bibliothèque nationale ("that of the rue Richelieu") "would not contain the elements necessary to constitute our Cours in its entirety" (pp. 5–6).
- **October 27, 1865** — inspector's report: majority of workers "appear healthy and favorable to the government of the Emperor"; **360 workers** of the Ateliers subscribed at the birth of the Prince Imperial (1856), money forwarded to the Commissaire de Police (p. 21).
- **At his death (1875)** — the Ateliers Catholiques of Montrouge worth **over 3,000,000 francs** (p. 1).

### Church-context chronology (Bloch's background frame, pp. 9–12)
- **1801** Napoleonic concordat; **1814** Charter — Catholicism religion of state; Church under Napoleon in "liberté surveillée" (p. 10).
- **1814**: 9 episcopal seats vacant; 34 bishops older than sixty. **1801–1814**: ~6,000 priests ordained vs. 6,848 deaths **1806–1814**; as many as 3,345 parishes without local pastoral care (p. 10).
- **Guizot law of 1833** — removed primary/secondary education from Church jurisdiction; "France's last religious war" (David Pinkney's phrase) (p. 10).
- Restoration recovery (pp. 11): 36,000 priests in 1814 (42% over sixty) → 40,600 in 1830 (29% over sixty) → 47,000 in 1848 (only 5.6% over sixty). Seminaries 103 → 224 during the Restoration. Ordinations doubled from 1,185 (1816) to 2,357 (1830). Female clergy from 12,343 (1808) to some 31,000 (1831).
- Catholic Renewal landmarks Bloch lists: Lamennais's *Essai sur l'indifférence* (1817), Chateaubriand's *Génie du christianisme* (1802), Montalembert's *Du Devoir des catholiques* (1843); revival of Solesmes by Guéranger and Pitra; Dominican, Carthusian, Trappist revivals; d'Alzon's Assumptionists (p. 11).

## 2. Named people

### Family
- **Victor Migne** — brother; held the bookseller's brevet for the Ateliers (Feb 15, 1839) and for the *Journal des faits*; strawman owner ("legal fiction") behind Migne's multi-newspaper operation; died unexpectedly July 1853; Migne accused Vassal and Delbrel of disinheriting Victor's children (pp. 27–29). Also listed among the people Migne quarreled with: "with his brother" (p. 18).

### Bishops and hierarchy
- **Mgr de Beauregard** — Bishop of Orléans; confiscated *De la Liberté, par un Prêtre* unread; the formative antagonist. Signed Migne's exeat (pp. 7, 23–24, 31).
- **Mgr Affre** — Archbishop of Paris; censured Migne Aug 20 and Oct 29, 1847; ordered suspension of any cleric who edited, subscribed to, or even advised subscribing to *La Voix de la vérité*; accused Migne of "lying with a skill and a perfidy which contrast curiously with his title" (pp. 18, 31–32).
- **Mgr Sibour** — condemned *La Voix de la vérité* in 1854 (pp. 31–32).
- **Cardinal Bonald, Archbishop of Lyon** — compared Migne to the monks of the Middle Ages saving the writings of the Fathers (p. 4).
- **Bishop of Algiers** — let the California-bound debtor priest out of his sight (p. 3).

### Collaborators and editors
- **Dom Pitra** — "the man most responsible for the general plan as well as for the editorial execution of the patrologies" (p. 2); signed the 1843 contract, "chief architect" of the Cours complets (p. 13); his name "practically erased from the patrologies" (p. 17); called Migne's ads "abominable advertisements" (*abominables affiches*) (p. 41); fought Migne over the bombastic title page (pp. 2–3); recipient of key Migne letters (Purgatory, Pope).
- **Dom Guéranger** — reviver of the Benedictine priory at Solesmes; **first suggested the project of the patrologies**; early collaborator and contract signatory; continued collecting books after the Solesmes break; recommended Pitra (pp. 11, 13). Per his sister: hopeless with figures.
- **Cécile Bruyère** — Guéranger's sister, first abbess of Sainte-Cécile de Solesmes: "Dom Guéranger has never managed to understand the raw reality of a figure" (p. 13).
- **Dom Gardereau** — Solesmes monk whose April 20, 1842 letter records the monks' fear of Migne's lawsuits (p. 13).
- **A. Bonnetty** — Migne's "friend and publicist," editor of the *Annales de philosophie chrétienne*; insisted Migne did it all alone, without government or church help, most subscribers being "foreigners, Protestants, or Greeks"; called the d'Alembert–Diderot *Encyclopédie* "a pygmy of science and utility" next to the patrologies (pp. 2, 4, 6).
- **Melchior du Lac** — early editorial associate (pseudonym "Jean d'Aure"); wrote the early *Lettres familières sur les Pères de l'Église*; edited *L'Univers religieux* 1836–early 1840s; companion in the midnight umbrella incident (pp. 18, 24).
- **Louis Veuillot** — journalist, editor of *L'Univers* from 1843; recipient of Migne's "I am neither a saint nor a learned man" letter; characterized Migne as abrupt and capable of fits of rage (pp. 9, 18).
- **Eugène Veuillot** — Louis's brother; source for the umbrella anecdote (p. 18).
- **Abbé Prompsault** — "eminent jurist," aided Migne's legal-advice column in *La Voix de la vérité*; also listed among collaborators Migne quarreled with (pp. 18, 20, 31).
- **Inner circle of bishop-opposing abbés** — l'abbé Prompsault, l'abbé Clavel, l'abbé Raymond, l'abbé André, and "the defrocked occultist **Alphonse Constant (Éliphas Lévy)**" (p. 20). [Bloch's text prints "Levy"; the standard spelling is Éliphas Lévi.]
- **Dübner** ("Dupner"/"Dubner" in Migne's letters; real name Dubner) — editor pressed mercilessly for the *City of God* text (pp. 16–17).
- **Emmanuel Bailly** — "timid and unworldly" editor who merged *La Tribune catholique* into *L'Univers religieux*; his presses printed the first issue; later bought Migne's *L'Univers* shares (~5,000 francs) (pp. 24, 26).

### Rivals, antagonists, business figures
- **Picot** — editor/director of *L'Ami de la religion*, the rival paper Migne feared would pull the religious press toward Gallicans and Legitimists; leveled the 1834 plagiarism accusation; also among Migne's quarrels (pp. 18, 24, 26).
- **Guillaume Vassal** — "strawman manager" (*gérant de paille*) of the *Journal des faits*; "the vassal of an ecclesiastical lord"; tried to seize the corporation; three-year legal war (pp. 27–30).
- **Frédéric Delbrel** — editor named by Vassal after Victor's death; rejected by Migne (p. 28).
- **M. Gasnier** — "Papetier," paper supplier and creditor of the *Journal des faits*; dispute over ownership "came practically to physical blows" (p. 18).
- **Pierre Blot ("Blot de Poly")** — typesetter in Migne's shop whom Migne dressed up with a fabricated "title of nobility" to serve as front managing editor "intended to fool the Legitimist party"; born in Poly (Yonne) (p. 30).
- **Jules Bordot** — bought *La Vérité* in 1856 (p. 27).
- **Eugène Taconet** — bought *La Voix de la vérité*, authorized Feb 17, 1860 to rename it *Le Monde* (p. 27).
- **Jules Gordon** of *La Voix de la vérité* — among Migne's quarrels (p. 18).
- **M. Mirès** — financier against whom Migne warned clergy investors (p. 34).
- **Chanoine Virbonnet** — intervened for a poor young worker's wages; **son Ajalbert of Saint-Flour** — complained he could not earn a livelihood (p. 20).

### Witnesses, biographers, comparators
- **Hippolyte Barbier** — Migne's **earliest biographer**; source for Puiseaux color, the orator portrait, the 1841 workshop visit, "un palais à l'industrie catholique," and the philanthropic image of the Ateliers (pp. 6–7, 14, 19).
- **A.-G. Hamman** — Migne's "modern-day biographer": "Migne could not tolerate defeat, and his revenge would consist in publishing whole libraries instead of a brochure" (pp. 7–8).
- **Pierre Pierrard** — coined "Rastignac auvergnat en soutane"; catalogued Migne's quarrels (pp. 13, 18).
- **Abbé Bremond** — "imposing Titan" purple passage (p. 4).
- **R. du Merzer** — visitor writing in *L'Illustration* (Bible Street / Fathers of the Church Square anecdote) (p. 16).
- **The Goncourt brothers** — "brewer of Catholic books" mockery (pp. 19–20).
- **Ambroise Firmin Didot** — publisher; source of "the greatest publishing enterprise since the invention of printing" (p. 1).
- **Émile Girardin** — Migne's journalistic precedent: founded *Le Voleur* (1829, reprinting "the best articles of the week"), *La Mode* (1830, with Balzac contributing), *Le Journal général de la France*, and six years later *Le Siècle* (pp. 36–37).
- **Balzac** — Bloch's constant comparator: embarked on his own printing venture in 1826, chronicled the world in *Illusions perdues* (1837); employed only 36 workers in the late 1820s; the Finot/prête-nom passage from *Illusions perdues* mirrors Migne's strawman tactics (pp. 1, 9, 14, 27, 36).
- Catholic Renewal figures listed as context (p. 11): Lamennais, abbé Genoude, Joseph de Maistre, Louis de Bonald, Jean-Baptiste Lacordaire, Félix Dupanloup, the theologian Gerbert, Charles de Montalembert, Chateaubriand, Emmanuel d'Alzon; press figures Picot (*L'Ami de la religion*), Michaud (*La Quotidienne*), Lamennais (*L'Avenir*).
- **Frédéric Barbier** — historian of the printing industry (1851 survey) (p. 14). **David Pinkney** — "France's last religious war" phrase (p. 10). **Jesse Gellrich** — "originally put me on the track of Migne" (Acknowledgments, p. ix).

## 3. Numbers (exactly as Bloch gives them)

### Output and scale
- A book **every ten days for thirty years** (vs. the average American academic's two in a lifetime) (p. 1).
- Ateliers Catholiques worth **over 3,000,000 francs** at his death (p. 1).
- **PL: 217 tomes / 218 volumes** (1844–55); **PG Greco-Latin: 161 tomes / 166 volumes** (1857–66); **Greek Fathers Latin-only: 81 tomes / 85 volumes**; combined patrologies **over a million pages** — about **one-half** of total output (p. 1).
- **~400 books** published before the patrologies began printing; whole Bibliothèque universelle du clergé = **more than a thousand books** (pp. 1, 14).
- Volume counts by series: 25 + 25 + 18 + (66 + 33) + 50 + 53 + 66 + 27 + 13 + 150 diverse (pp. 1–2). Bremond's (inaccurate, rhetorical) count: "Two hundred and thirty-one volumes of the Latin Patrologie, one hundred and sixty-one of the Greek" (p. 4).
- **240 Index Tables** of the patrologies took **49 editors 500 men-years** and cost the editor-in-chief **more than half a million francs** (pp. 3–4).
- He published **fifty to one hundred pages a day** (Bloch's arithmetic) (p. 2).
- Migne owned/edited **at least ten newspapers** over his lifetime (pp. 2, 23).

### Money
- Fine of **two hundred francs** for attempted bribery of a postal employee (Dec 24, 1835) (p. 26).
- **Five thousand francs** — presumed founding capital of the Ateliers, from sale of his *L'Univers* shares to Bailly (p. 26).
- **190 francs** owed by the debtor priest for **thirty volumes** of the *Cours d'orateurs* — pursued "even as far as California" (p. 3).
- ***Journal des faits* bought at auction for forty-one thousand francs** (Dec 20, 1853) (pp. 28, 30).
- Balzac's Finot passage (comparator): a third share for thirty thousand francs down, editor salary 250 francs/month (p. 27).
- Cunin-Gridaine sheet factory: 500 workers, annual production **over five million francs** (p. 14).
- Two-hundred-page brochure *De la Liberté* (p. 7).

### Workers
- **~300 employees in 1842, almost doubled by 1854 (to 596)**; Migne claimed an equal number working on the outside (p. 13).
- **140 typesetters and printers** at Barbier's 1841 visit ("The master visits his hundred and forty typesetters and printers") (p. 14).
- Police (Oct 27, 1857): **"Three hundred and fifty to four hundred workers on the average, women included"** (p. 17).
- **60 typesetters** would be out of work without Dübner's *City of God* copy (pp. 16–17).
- **360 workers** on the Prince Imperial birth subscription list (p. 21).
- Comparators (p. 14): 1851 survey of 77 departments (Paris intra muros and Rhône excluded): **6,657 workers for 627 print shops ≈ 11 workers per operation**; Balzac employed **36**; Firmin Didot, **200**; the 125,000 largest French manufacturers of the Restoration averaged **10 workers each**; Cunin-Gridaine **500**; Nicolas Schlumberger **700** in his metallurgical works — both considered major enterprises.

### Machines
- **Five great mechanical presses** driven by steam (Barbier, 1841) (p. 14).
- Migne's broadsheet: steam-driven presses "can give birth to **2,000 volumes in quarto every 24 hours**" (p. 15).
- 1861 figures: 74 départements, 684 print shops, **117 steam-driven presses** total; **44 départements (60%) had none**; one in three of the 364 printers in the remaining 30 départements used steam; **57 machines in "La Seine."** Migne could claim **~10 percent of the steam-driven printing capacity of an entire highly mechanized département** (p. 15).
- "the hand of a monk of yesteryear could not copy in three years what is done in the Imprimerie catholique in a single minute" (p. 15).

### Church demography (Bloch's context, pp. 10–11)
- 1814: 9 vacant sees; 34 bishops over sixty. 1801–14: ~6,000 ordinations vs. 6,848 deaths (1806–14); 3,345 parishes unserved.
- Priests: 36,000 (1814, 42% over sixty) → 40,600 (1830, 29%) → 47,000 (1848, 5.6%).
- Seminaries: 103 → 224 during the Restoration. Ordinations: 1,185 (1816) → 2,357 (1830). Female clergy: 12,343 (1808) → ~31,000 (1831).

## 5. Bloch's interpretive claims (HIS argument, not documented fact)

- **Migne did not read what he published.** Bloch's opening deduction: unless Migne read 50–100 pages of Latin/Greek a day on top of publishing 50–100 pages a day, "Migne did not read, could not have read, as much as he wrote — or at least as much as he edited" (p. 2). Supported by Migne's own admission (couldn't read "even a tenth"), but the framing — the editor who doesn't read as the key to the whole enterprise — is Bloch's.
- **The Beauregard trauma thesis.** Bloch reads the confiscation of *De la Liberté* psychoanalytically: "It is as if the confrontation with Mgr de Beauregard were the traumatic event, the repressed wound, at the core of his being, for which the rest of Migne's career would compensate"; the muzzling was "the equivalent of a conversion … toward using the writing that had been suppressed in order to convert others" (p. 7). The "hysterical profusion of exorbitant writing" and the "plethora of compensatory self-promotions" flow from this wound (p. 8). The patrologies as revenge (citing Hamman approvingly). Note Bloch's own hedging: events "supposedly occurred," "if not produce, at least influence" (p. 6).
- **The moment and the man.** Migne's personal motive shouldn't obscure historical conditions: the July Monarchy's industrial/print-capital boom plus the post-Napoleonic Catholic Renewal "produced an extraordinary conjunction of the moment and the man" (pp. 8–9). Migne as Balzacian type — the provincial self-made man in Paris — is a framing device Bloch uses throughout (pp. 1, 9, 24, 27, 36).
- **The patrologies as counter-Revolution.** Bloch argues Migne aimed to reverse the French Revolution conceptually: the Bibliothèque universelle to surpass the Imprimerie nationale and the Bibliothèque nationale, and to "undo the very conceptual foundation of the Revolution — the secular science of the Philosophes," rivaling the "funeste" *Encyclopédie* of d'Alembert and Diderot (pp. 5–6); also to undo the great schism and the Reformation (p. 5), positioned "against the rationalism of the Enlightenment, against contemporaneous positivism, and against the Protestantism of the Reformation" (p. 39).
- **An industrial City of God.** The Ateliers were "above all, a book factory"; Migne "fashioned an autonomous universe dedicated to the book," conceived along the lines of the cathedral building site, the medieval scriptorium, the monastery and cloister — "a miniature City of God" in the banlieue (pp. 14–16). But the reality "was far from ideal": "the Ateliers catholiques resembled more a feudal manor, of which he was the lord, than the ideal city rendered by the names of its streets" (p. 16). The imposed rule of silence made "the medieval monachal rule … indistinguishable from that of the factory worker of the industrial revolution" (p. 17).
- **Migne as exploiting capitalist.** "Within the walls of the Ateliers catholiques Migne appears to have exploited his editors and workers in the mold of any nineteenth-century capitalist entrepreneur" (p. 17); he "deliberately recruited workers from the most dispossessed element of the population … vulnerable by definition to his offers of low wages" (p. 19), including censured and defrocked priests. Methodological aside: bad pay is "not without its hidden blessing for the historian" — discontent → police attention → surviving records (p. 17).
- **The undecidability of motive.** A recurring Bloch move: "it is impossible to tell whether the abbé's motivations were profoundly self-interested or altruistic, whether he was drawn to the capital by the love of capital or … because 'he hopes to do good works'" (p. 24); the strawman scheme is "so entirely of a piece with the editorial modus operandi … that one wonders, again, to what extent Migne's motives are selfish or altruistic" (p. 31).
- **The central thesis of Ch. 2 — the absent editor.** Migne's journalism was "reprint digest" journalism (*journal reproducteur*), edition by scissors. Bloch argues this displacement of voice was at once (1) a money-saving measure, (2) a legal strategy (no editor → limitless ownership via strawmen), and (3) a rhetoric of impersonal universal truth that detached Migne "from anything resembling individual responsibility or intent" (pp. 37–38). "In his poly-positioning of an unascribable veracity of others, Migne becomes simply the vessel through which the truth, unmediated, seems to pass" (p. 39). Scissors + steam "transform Migne the man into a machine for the publication of the Fathers" (p. 39). And the payoff claim: "the figure of the absent editor, as the invisible instrument of a voiceless universal truth which speaks itself, not only occupies pride of place in Migne's journalism, but defines as well the essence of his fabulous industrialization of the Church Fathers" (p. 40) — i.e., the plagiarist-journalist and the patrologist are the same operation.
- **Journalism as fiction-machine.** "The twisted fate of the *Journal des faits* captures much of Migne's journalistic career, which takes the appearance of an accretion of fictions" (p. 29): straw newspapers, strawman owners, fabricated nobility, the "editorless" paper. Bloch also argues the government's condemnations (false news, contrefaçon) "merely served as excuses to repress a printing operation … simultaneously censured by the Archbishop of Paris and considered to be potentially unfriendly to the regime" (p. 28).
- **Migne's politics.** Bloch presents Migne as a political chameleon (police: "knows how to adapt his behavior to the situation") whose entrepreneurship "would seem to have discouraged anything resembling a progressive social attitude" — property rights as "the most sacred thing in the world after religion" (p. 21); the "journaux reproducteurs" as "instruments of political quietude" (p. 38).
- **De-provincializing the rags-to-riches story.** Though the trajectory is the classic provincial-in-Paris success story, "it is hard to equate his trajectory from rags to riches with anything resembling either social ascension or economic ambition, since, for the abstemious abbé, who lived more like a miser than a bourgeois, the social hardly counted at all; and his fortune was … hardly personal" (p. 24).
- **Compression as signature.** Bloch links Migne's encyclopedism, his love of the digest, his telegraphic epigraphs, and "the condensation of intellectual effort associated … with the mechanical efficiency of the steam engine" (pp. 25, 40) — one aesthetic uniting newspapers and patrologies.

## 6. Legends flagged (anecdotes Bloch marks as unverifiable, embellished, or self-manufactured)

- **The sixteen-hour day and zero recreation** — "Migne is *alleged* to have worked sixteen hours a day … and never, *by his own account*, taking recreation, not even one hour a year" (p. 2). Sourced to Migne's own letterhead and self-report.
- **The Puiseaux garde-nationale episodes** — "several biographical events which *supposedly* occurred in the early days of the July Monarchy"; the guard "is *purported* to have replied"; Migne "is *alleged* to have encouraged the priest"; "he *reportedly* called their bluff" (p. 6). Source: earliest biographer Barbier, a partisan.
- ***De la Liberté, par un Prêtre*** — the 200-page brochure "which Barbier *claims* to have read," whose contents are "no less provocative than the title" per Barbier — but "which never saw the light of day" (p. 7). The text survives only in Barbier's claim.
- **The Oxford Protestant prospectus anecdote** — the visiting Protestant's fulsome praise of the Ateliers ("I do not judge the fruit by the tree, but the tree by the fruit…") appears in one of Migne's own publicity prospectuses; Bloch: "an answer that could not have pleased him more had he written it himself (which, as we shall see, he *probably did*)" (pp. 4–5).
- **Third-person self-praise** — in the broadsheet "Curieux détails sur la Bibliothèque universelle du clergé," Migne "speaks of himself in the third person, as if he were simply reporting dispassionately sanctioned public opinion"; "out of modesty, Migne will cite only seven" bishops (p. 41). Self-glorifying reviews were also placed in Bonnetty's *Annales de philosophie chrétienne* (p. 8).
- **The umbrella / "Couac! couac!" incident** — reported at second hand by Eugène Veuillot about his brother Louis's account (pp. 18–19).
- **Barbier's philanthropic Ateliers** — the shelter-for-any-hungry-priest image ("he will be saved"): "Yet, reality, again, differed from the rhetoric of both prospectus and biography" (p. 19).
- **Migne's Legitimism** — "suspected repeatedly of a disloyalty to the regime that can, in any case, never be proven" (p. 21).
- **Migne's reprinted "news"** — the Rev. George Andrews murder-hanging story from "Sumter (sic) County, Florida" and the Texas barbarism letters: "all the earmarks of a rumor-driven grapevine rather than of firsthand reporting," truth "at second or third hand," even as Migne insists "its authenticity is incontestable" (pp. 34–35).
- **"Blot de Poly"** — the fake title of nobility Migne fabricated for his own typesetter, exposed by police (p. 30) — a legend Migne manufactured, caught in the act.

## 7. Texture — concrete, vivid detail for the essay

### The man
- Physical portrait (Barbier): "large physique, large and nobly carried head, the look of an eagle which exhales nonetheless the most unctuous sensitivity, a limpid and sonorous voice, a forceful and colorful manner of speech, a rare capacity for improvisation" — Migne was known first as an **orator**, before Beauregard drove him into print (p. 7). "As stocky as he was and armed with his umbrella" (p. 18).
- Letterhead stationery announcing that the hour of **1 p.m. to 2 p.m.** was reserved for visits (p. 2); epigraphs and letterheads as a signature genre (p. 25); recruiting slogans printed on the stationery itself (p. 19).
- Ate no more "than a simple seminarian or a worker"; "lived more like a miser than a bourgeois" (pp. 2, 24).
- Auvergnat stubbornness: in everything touching the patrologies "he is reputed never to have relinquished any point" — Pitra's three-day standoff over the title page (pp. 2–3).

### The workshops at Petit-Montrouge
- Barbier's 1841 visit: "The master visits his hundred and forty typesetters and printers; he perhaps revises the proofs of St. Augustine or St. Chrysostom … He takes care of his vast correspondence with the bishops around the world." Then the machine-room sublime: "you will see functioning through the power of steam the five great mechanical presses which he just set up in the back of the building; you will cross this immense workshop filled, like billowing black clouds, with sheets laid out for drying. Here the foundry men who mold typeface before a furnace; there tables set for folding; there again … the proofreaders' office" (p. 14). Barbier calls it "un palais à l'industrie catholique" (p. 14).
- The trades painted **on the entrance**: "print shop, bookstore, foundry, stereotyping, glazing, smoothing, collating, and binding … which cannot be found together elsewhere, not even in the Imprimerie nationale" (p. 5).
- The compound contained "a library, a bookstore, a chapel, a bindery, a foundry, a warehouse, an artist's atelier, and Migne's own apartment" (p. 15).
- Internal streets named for the holdings: "take Bible Street on your right, then Bossuet Street on your left … M. l'abbé Migne on Fathers of the Church Square … the place reserved for Tertullian's building" — aisles formed of enormous piles of books in quarto (p. 16).
- Work force: typesetters, smelters, manuscript and copy editors, printers, proofreaders, binders, couriers, accountants (p. 13). Women included (p. 17). High turnover — workers "leave as soon as they find something better elsewhere" (p. 17). A **rule of silence**: "expressly forbidden to sing or to engage in even the most frivolous conversation while working" (pp. 17–18).
- The demimonde: political refugees of 1789, 1830, and 1848; priests in trouble with the hierarchy; married clergy, some with dependents; "all the priests on the index throughout the dioceses of France"; the Goncourts' defrocked rogues who "jump for the doors" at the sight of a police inspector (pp. 19–20).
- Auvergnat pipeline from Saint-Flour: "he is for them a father more than a master" (*La Haute-Auvergne*, 1857) — against which stand the wage complaints of chanoine Virbonnet's protégé and the son Ajalbert; Migne's "almost comic" response: today's Auvergnats aren't what they used to be, and those who leave Auvergne are corrupted by Paris (p. 20).
- One minute of the industrial present worth three years of a medieval copyist (p. 15); 2,000 quarto volumes per 24 hours (p. 15); ~10% of the steam capacity of a mechanized département in one shop (p. 15).

### The journalism (texture of the papers themselves)
- *La Vérité canonique*'s advice column: the curé who can't get the **keys to the church annex, bell tower, and vestment room** from the mayor's absent assistant — "What's to be done?" (p. 32); Pierre's shed on a four-meter strip buttressed against the parish church wall (he gave himself permission — he was the mayor), answered with the 1-meter-90 setback rule, Law of 18 germinal year X, articles 75–76, and *Nec usucapiuntur res sacrae, sanctae, publicae* (pp. 32–33); the priest whose commune wants to cut the trees bordering his horse-pasture to build a school (p. 33).
- Liturgical queries: single antiphon and prayer for the octave of Saints Peter and Paul; honoraria for the three masses of Christmas "as the De Lugo teaches (*Cours complets*, tome XXIII, page 847)"; binages and the three masses of the day of the dead as in Spain and Portugal (p. 34).
- Financial alerts (the Mirès warning) sliding into rumor, gossip, and scandal-sheet fare: the Florida Methodist minister Andrews story with its gallows line — "I am only sorry about one thing in dying, that is, not having killed three or four more!" (pp. 34–35); the "Anecdotal News" rubric with M. Boyer, the "most distracted of men," wandering Paris in his vestments with his square bonnet for a hat (p. 35); the Ancien Régime noble's secret annual debauch, ending "It was a good and effective lesson" — "a narration that resembles nothing so much as the medieval morality tale or exemplum" (pp. 35–36).
- The catchphrase texture of the reprint digest: "One reads in the *Italie*," "One writes from Hondschoote to the *Propagateur de Lille*," "One reads in the *Gazette du Midi*," "One reads in the *Gazette des tribunaux*" (p. 36). Peer "journaux reproducteurs": *L'Estafette*, *Le Compilateur*, *Le Journal des journaux*, *L'Écho des feuilletons*; Bloch's modern analogue: *Reader's Digest* (p. 36).
- *L'Univers religieux*'s promised subject matter, theology mixed with daily life: "dances, theatrical balls, novels, interest-bearing loans, various taxes, divorce, the salaries of clergy … the whole with the greatest discretion" (p. 25).

### Archival texture (Bloch's sources, useful for notes)
- Core dossiers: **Archives Nationales F18 369, F18 1803, F18 283, F18 333a, F18 423, F19 5842** (police and press files); **BN n.a. 6138 and 6143** (letters). Bloch worked in the Bibliothèque nationale and Archives nationales (Acknowledgments, p. ix). Migne's frustrated legal letter-writing "must have been compensated by the pleasure of offering legal advice to others" (p. 31).
