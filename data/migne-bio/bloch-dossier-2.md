## 1. The Advertising / Self-Promotion Machine

### 1.1 The "Curieux détails" broadsheet (AN F18 369)
- An anonymous broadsheet, "Curieux détails sur la Bibliothèque universelle du clergé," "coming out of nowhere," assembles extracts of endorsement letters from the French episcopate (pp. 42–43). Endorsers quoted:
  - **Cardinal de Bonald, Archbishop of Lyon** — "we cannot recommend highly enough to our dear colleagues the editions published by M. l'abbé Migne" (p. 42).
  - **The late Mgr Affre, Archbishop of Paris** — applauds "a work aimed at reproducing excellent books at a moderate price" (p. 42).
  - **The current Archbishop of Paris** — encourages "by my word and by my subscription your theological publications" (p. 42).
  - **The Bishop of Saint-Flour** — names Migne "honorary canon of my cathedral" (p. 42).
  - **The Bishop of Luçon** — offers prayers for "the prodigious work of your Patrologie" (p. 42).
  - **The Bishop of Montauban** — reminds his diocesan clergy they can receive bonus books by subscribing to the *Cours de Théologie et d'Écriture Sainte* in **28 volumes** (pp. 42–43).
  - **Mgr de Gap** — no priest should be without "some of the cours complets... published, at such a low cost, by M. l'abbé Migne" (p. 43).
- The long Bonald quotation (p. 43, AN F18 369): "He places at the disposal of even the most moderate presbytery collections that in times gone by only the very rich could afford. Thus, the most learned commentators, the most profound theologians, the most eloquent apologists of religion, the most exalted ascetic writers, an entire tradition, so to speak, will pass at little cost from the presses of M. Migne to your library shelves, and you will find there weapons to combat error..."
- **Bloch's analysis (his argument):** the Bonald letter "resembles both substantively and stylistically nothing so much as Migne's acknowledged 'official' publicity" — the same obsessions (accessibility, completeness *la tradition entière*, perfection *la plus haute perfection*, low price *à peu de frais*) and the same tic of serial superlatives ("the most learned," "the most profound," "the most eloquent," "the most exalted") (p. 43). Bloch is explicit that this is **supposition, "not verifiable absolutely because of the absence of documents,"** but "more than a reasoned hunch" based on internal evidence plus surviving letters (pp. 42–43).

### 1.2 The Veuillot letter — the smoking gun (pp. 44–45)
- **Migne to Louis Veuillot, February 13, 1858** (BN n.a. 24633, fol. 478) — the one surviving document that shows the mechanism nakedly:
  - Migne boasts: "I have **fifty thousand letters** of this type in my files; I have in addition the praises of **more than a hundred Catholic newspapers** spread out throughout the whole world," citing the *Annales*-Bonnetty, the *Civiltà* of Rome, the *Revue* of Louvain (p. 44).
  - He argues Veuillot "would not compromise yourself in the least in praising in my publications that which appears to you to be praiseworthy" (p. 44).
  - The bribe: "I am offering you again, **not two hundred francs worth, but three hundred francs worth** of your choice of my publications, if you will only include the list between a head and a tail of your hand" (*la nomenclature entre une tête et une queue de votre main*) (p. 44). ["The list" = the elaborate advertising list of Cours complets titles, to be slipped inside an article in Veuillot's own voice — head and tail his, body Migne's (p. 45).]
  - In the same letter Migne writes: "I believe that I am rendering to the Church the greatest service that has ever been rendered it; and I hope to die as the priest who will have done the most good in resuscitating in its entirety its tradition" (p. 44).
- **Bloch's inference (marked as his argument):** if Migne bribed Veuillot, nothing prevents assuming he did the same with the editors of the *Annales*, the *Civiltà*, the *Revue* of Louvain; and that he wrote to each of the high clergy citing the others' commendations — telling Bonald of the Archbishop of Paris's praise, telling each bishop of Bonald's, and so on. "The possibilities of self-glorification are almost infinite" (pp. 44–45). The praise-cycle: Veuillot's purchased praise gets traded to other editors for more praise (p. 45).

### 1.3 Bonnetty and the *Annales de philosophie chrétienne* (pp. 45–46)
- **Bonnetty** publishes Migne's title-lists with an endorsing note in his own voice: "this is not a prospectus announcing what an editor proposes to do, but an account of what has been done, in part" — then enumerates "advantages": complete series, not only an edition of the Fathers but reproduction of all secondary works, and "one of the great advantages of this edition is that it is very cheap" (pp. 45–46; p. 46 OCR garbled). Bloch: Bonnetty's description "sounds awfully like that of Migne, right down to the enumeration of a series of 'avantages'" (p. 46).
- Bonnetty also publishes as an insert a specimen page ("A sample of the Greek and Latin type used in printing the PATROLOGIE") Migne obviously sent him (p. 46, garbled).
- Migne gets direct inserts in the *Annales* in his own voice: letter to Bonnetty of **January 15, 186[1?]** (OCR-garbled date) announcing "the greatest and the most fortunate typographical news" (*la plus immense et la plus heureuse des nouvelles typographiques*): "the completion of the Cours complet de Patrologie in **3[2?]6 volumes in-4o, with 2 compact columns**" (p. 46; volume figure OCR-garbled — verify against print). Migne calls it "the most moral, the most precious and the most significant [*la plus monumentale?*] publication which has ever come off a printing press in the whole world" (p. 46, partly garbled).

### 1.4 "An Echoing Chorus of Laudatory Voices" — the AN F18 1803 prospectus (pp. 46–50)
- Migne reciprocally **reproduces newspaper articles inside his own prospectuses**. One of the longest and earliest surviving broadsheets contains a long excerpt from *l'Univers religieux*: "The Editors of the Cours complets feel it their duty to reprint, following their prospectus, the following article... a paper no less known for the talent of its Editors than for the purity of its doctrines, and **the only religious daily of the Catholic world**" (pp. 46–47, AN F18 1803).
- Bloch prints a **two-column comparison** (pp. 47–48) showing the *Univers religieux* "review" and Migne's own signed prospectus prose to be nearly word-for-word identical (the borrowing "in its entirety and from whatever author" of the best work on each book/treatise; the *lettre de consultation* to "all the bishops, head vicars, theological canons, heads of orders and of congregations, superiors, and professors" "without exception"; admitting only works receiving "the greatest number of episcopal or theological voices"; responses "from all points of Europe" proving the great commentators "known everywhere and designated everywhere").
- The *Univers religieux* editors don't even hide it: "We will borrow here in this review some of their words, believing that we could not say it better ourselves" (p. 48, prospectus p. 17).
- The review even reviews the prospectus itself: "Ordinarily, nothing is more pompous than a prospectus, and nothing is more paltry than the works which it touts. Here, it is the opposite: if the prospectus is beautiful, the works are even more so" (pp. 48–49, prospectus p. 19).
- **Bloch's verdict (his argument, flagged as such):** "textual evidence as well as extra-textual circumstance point to the overwhelming fact that **he wrote it all**: the original prospectus reviewed in AN F18 1803, the part... which he signs with his own name, the review in *l'Univers religieux* of the original prospectus, the reproduction of this review" (p. 50). He calls the effect "a hall of mirrors," an "infinite voiceless regress" (p. 48), and "canonical echolalia" (p. 50).
- **Dating of the prospectus:** internal evidence places AN F18 1803 in **1838** — "we sent last year an opinion poll [lettre de consultation]" (the poll went out late 1837); Migne states "the first twelve volumes were on sale **March 20, 1838**"; the *Univers religieux* extract says "we have under our eyes the first twelve volumes" (p. 50). Migne founded *l'Univers religieux* (first issue 1833) and sold it in **1836**; relations with its editors "must have remained cordial" (p. 50).
- The double-column pro/con editorial concept of the Cours complets is continuous with the *journal reproducteur* concept: *l'Univers religieux* (1833) promised to track "all that is done or is published... for and against Catholicism"; the 1838 prospectus promises each page's two columns will give opposed positions "drawn from the two authors who have best maintained them" — e.g., the treatise on Contracts gives all arguments for and against interest-bearing loans; the treatise on the Church lets Ultramontanes and Gallicans each argue (pp. 49, 76). Cf. Migne in *l'Univers*, **November 23, 1833**: "We will give the opinion of newspapers on every major event because the truth almost always emanates from the clash of opinions" (pp. 49, 74–75).

### 1.5 Historical placement: Bon Marché, Balzac, Girardin (pp. 50–53)
- **Michael Miller's** history of the **Bon Marché** department store: identical puff articles in *L'Illustration* and *Le Monde illustré* (1870s–80s), handwritten copies in the Bon Marché archives, articles reprinted and given to house-tour visitors; Miller speculates the **190,000 francs** spent in 1891–92 for "various work in defense of the interests of the grands magasins" went to friendly writers and direct payoffs to newspapers (pp. 50–51). Bloch: Migne "was apparently in advance of his time but hardly unique" (p. 50).
- **Balzac, *Illusions perdues*** as the panorama of the same corruption (pp. 51–53): the Finot–Lousteau bookshop dialogue ("you'll put Paul de Kock over Ducange... You'll make out I did a savage review and that you toned it down: he'll be grateful to you"); Dauriat's costs ("Nathan is claiming **six thousand francs** in review articles... Blondet's two articles... cost me **a thousand francs** and a dinner which set me back **five hundred francs**"); Chateaubriand's unsalable book on the last Stuarts sold out in a week after one *Journal des débats* article; the commercial theorem "a ream of blank paper is worth **fifteen francs**; once printed it is worth **five francs or three hundred francs** according to the success it obtains" (pp. 51–52); Finot's prophecy "Journalism is in its infancy... In ten years from now, everything will be subject to publicity" (p. 52); Dauriat and Ladvocat inventing the book poster (pp. 52–53).
- The **first paid newspaper advertisements appeared apparently in 1836**; **Émile de Girardin** — "the equivalent in many respects of a secular Migne" — announced in the first number of *La Presse* that subscriptions would no longer cover costs: "It's up to advertising to pay for the newspaper" (*C'est aux annonces de payer le journal*) (p. 52).

### 1.6 Planted press, letterhead, preaching, pilgrimage (pp. 53–57)
- "Curieux détails" quotes the newspapers ***L'Estafette* and *La Banlieue de Paris*** describing a visit to the works at the **barrière d'Enfer** (full quote in §8 Texture below) (p. 53, AN F18 369). Bloch: both papers were themselves **reprint digests**, so the "news" most likely originated with Migne, recycled through his own "journaux reproducteurs" (*La Voix de la vérité*, *Le Journal des faits*, *La Vérité canonique*) or his other prospectuses: "What seems most certain is that it came at some point from Migne himself... the wider this circle gets, the more univocal it becomes" (p. 54).
- **The letterhead/prospectus standing invitation** (AN F18 369, printed on Ateliers de Montrouge stationery): "TO ALL MEMBERS OF THE CATHOLIC CLERGY / If you would like to see carried out all at once and on a grand scale all the arts relating to printing, you are invited to honor with your presence the Ateliers catholiques of Petit-Montrouge... one need only take a look at the words *print shop, bookstore, foundry, stereotyping, glazing, smoothing, collating, and binding*, which are on the entrance to the establishment, and which cannot be found together elsewhere, not even in the Imprimerie nationale" (p. 54). Also on the stationery: "M. l'abbé Migne is available every day and at any hour of the day, except between eleven o'clock and noon, and except also on Sundays and holidays" (p. 57).
- **The 50,000 letters:** Migne "claims (or threatens)" in "Curieux détails" that once the Cours complets are finished, "More than **50,000** [similar letters] coming from the [ecclesiastical] hierarchy at every level and from all countries could be produced! This unbelievable but true number of congratulations... will be gathered in **10 or 12 volumes** of the format of the Cours" (pp. 54–55, AN F18 369). Bloch calls this planned collection "a self-generated simulated *summa* of all the good that Migne has accomplished at a fair price — *du bon, à bon marché*" (p. 55).
- He did, he claims (insert in the *Annales*, rubric "**Avis important**"), actually publish a prospectus of **almost 200 pages** containing "more than **1,000 Episcopal Letters** and more than **500** from the leaders of Catholicism in favor of our Work" — followed by the *Réflexions instructives et curieuses sur la Patrologie*, in the same pose of objective detachment (p. 55).
- **Preaching as ad channel:** "In ecclesiastical retreats, by order of the Ordinary, or through his own motivation, the preacher... rarely forgets to put in a word of praise about the number, the importance, and the attractive price [*le prix minime*] of that which is produced by the Ateliers catholiques" (pp. 55–56, AN F18 369).
- **International reach claimed:** encouraging letters recently from "the cardinals, archbishops, or bishops of **Bordeaux, Sens, Avignon, Aix, Moulins, Dijon, La Rochelle, Tulle, Autun, Belley, Meaux, Versailles, Montpellier, and Soissons**"; and the **4 archbishops and 24 bishops of Ireland**, gathered in council at Dublin, heard **Mgr de Tuam** give, as the third reason for the accomplishment of the Propagation of the Faith, "the reproduction of Catholic tradition by the ateliers of Montrouge and its distribution throughout the whole world" (p. 56, AN F18 369).
- **Herd-marketing:** "Mgr **Dupanloup**, whose name itself is sufficient praise," designated the theology series "the cornerstone of the libraries established in the **30 deaneries** of his diocese [Orléans]. Almost all the bishops of France intend to follow thus their illustrious colleague." Stop-press note: "In the very moment this goes to press, Mgr the Bishop of **Versailles** just... opted, in his letter, for **21 publications** of the Ateliers catholiques" (pp. 56–57).
- **Bloch's summary mechanism (his argument):** Migne "creates a chorus of voices speaking on his behalf," each supporter "persuaded others have spoken... each seeking to buy because the others have bought" (p. 56); the chorus of witnesses is transformed via the prospectuses "into subscribers paying either in advance or in biannual deposits" (p. 57).

## 2. The Production System (how the Patrologia was actually made)

### 2.1 The genuine scholarly effort (small but real)
- "There can be no doubt that Migne did make some small bona fide effort to locate and to publish original manuscripts" (p. 58).
- **Dom Pitra**, "chief editor of the patrologies," was also charged with finding manuscripts; made numerous voyages of discovery through Italy and France (p. 58).
- **The Metz cartridge-paper rescue, 1846:** Pitra discovered in the artillery arsenal of Metz a remarkable cache of parchment — manuscripts of the cartulary houses of the dioceses of **Metz, Toul, and Verdun**, requisitioned at the Revolution because ordinary paper was not tough enough "for the packing of cartridges." Pitra alerted the director of artillery and wrote memoranda to the Ministre de la Guerre and the Ministre de l'Instruction Publique; **within weeks all manuscripts in military possession were ordered handed over to the library of Metz** — "a historic change in government policy toward the preservation of original documents." From Metz he went to Strasbourg, where he discovered the long-sought *Clé* of Meliton (pp. 58–59).
- **Genuinely new volumes:** the edition of **Tertullian**, prepared by Dom Pitra especially for the first volume of the Latin cursus; the text of the ***Vies de Métaphraste*** in the second Greek series, in print for the first time (p. 59).
- **Price ceiling on originality:** letter to the editor **Dübner** — if the hard-pressed classicist cannot produce an annotated edition of the *City of God* for **100 francs, or 120 at the limit**, Migne "is willing simply to reprint that of the Benedictines" (p. 59).

### 2.2 Wholesale reprinting — the source collections (pp. 59–60)
"In the overwhelming majority of cases... Migne simply reprinted wholesale the editions of others" (p. 59). Great collections drawn on:
- *Maxima bibliotheca veterum Patrum et antiquorum scriptorum ecclesiasticorum*, **27 volumes in-folio, Lyon 1677**.
- *Bibliotheca SS. Patrum* (6th ed.), **8 volumes**, ed. Marguerin de la Bigne, Paris **1575**, completed by the *Bibliothèque des pères* (1616).
- The liturgical collection of **Hittorp**; the publications of **P. Fronton du Duc** (1618–24).
- The **14-volume** *Bibliotheca veterum Patrum...* of **A. Galland** (1765–81), which added the Greek and Eastern Fathers (pp. 59–60).

Individual/special collections (partial list, p. 60):
- **Polemics:** Gesner (1559–60); J. Simler (1571); G. Mauguin (1650); P. F. Foggini (1754–71); Fr. Oberthür (1754–71 and 1780–91).
- **Ascetic writings:** P. Manutius (1562); M. H. Goldast (1604); C. de Chantelou (1661–73); P. de Poussines (1684); B. Pez (1723–40).
- **Predication:** G. Florimonte (1556); L. Cumbelius and Mosanus (1588); Fr. Combefis (1662).
- **Apostolic Fathers:** J.-B. Cotelier (1672); Th. Ittig (1699); R. Russel (1746).
- **English Fathers:** H. Wharton's *Ven. Bedae, Egberti et Aldhelmi Opuscula nonnulla* (1693).
- **Christian poets:** A. Manutius (1501); F. Arevalo, *Hymnodia hispanica* (1787).
- Plus "various collections of Trésors, Spicilèges, Analecta, Anecdota, Miscellanea, Monumenta, Reliquae Sacrae, and Catenae."

Bloch's contextualization: in this Migne "invented nothing new" — a tradition of copying the Fathers into collections going back to at least the 12th century, with the 13th-century scholastics arranging by topic; given the rarity of pre-Carolingian manuscripts, "it is hard to imagine things otherwise" (pp. 60–61).

### 2.3 The editorial method: correction, not composition (pp. 76–77)
- Migne "quite explicitly transforms the act of reprinting what already has been printed into a guarantee of perfection": "The Benedictines, like the Jesuits, almost always worked from manuscripts, which was a constant source of a myriad of errors, while the Ateliers Catholiques, whose goal is above all to revive Tradition, only works for the most part on printed sources" (pp. 76–77).
- "Printing for Migne did not mean beginning from the beginning, working from a manuscript... It meant refining what had already been set in print. Thus the emphasis on the process of correction, and not, say, on transcription or composition" (p. 77). Bloch contrasts this with the philological reconstruction from manuscripts characteristic of the later 19th century, "especially after the Franco-Prussian war" (p. 77).
- Bonnetty: "The essential part of a work like this is, one could say, **the correction of proofs**" (p. 77). And: "To begin with, the copy is most often a printed source, which reduces greatly errors made while typesetting" (p. 77).
- Book production at the Ateliers = "more a question of the refinishing of a pre-existing product than beginning from scratch" (p. 77).

### 2.4 The proofreading corps (pp. 69–70)
- Migne's boast: "there are at Montrouge proofreaders from all nations, and **in greater number than in twenty Parisian printshops combined**" (p. 69).
- Via Bonnetty ("like a ringmaster presenting great circus performers," Bloch p. 69): "The greatest attention is given not only to the purity of the text, but even to **accents**, something that was quite neglected by the old Benedictines" (p. 69). The named correctors:
  - **Three Greeks: MM. Sypsomo, Pantazides, and Dobriades** (for the Greek accents).
  - Recruited abroad: **Lehmann** (Leipzig), **Reitchardt** (Stuttgart), **Sidon** (Cologne), **Beyerle** (Hanover), **Guardia** (Palma), **Laas-d'Aguen** (Holland), **Tarlet** (Brussels) (p. 69).
  - **Paul Drach**, former rabbi converted to Catholicism, librarian of the Congregation de la Propagation de la Foi in Rome — "the most learned Hebrew specialist who exists, and, what's more, the most exacting proofreader we know" (p. 70).
- **The international editorial team:** **Malou** (Belgium/Louvain), **Henri-Joseph Floss** (Bonn), **Georg-Hay Forbes** (Glasgow), **Fr. Oehler** (Halle), **Jean Henri Nolte** (Vienna), and from Würzburg **Henri J. Denzinger** and **Joseph Hergenröther** — the last of whom arranged Migne's **doctor honoris causa from the University of Würzburg in 1861** (p. 70).

### 2.5 Quality reality vs. claims
- "A good proportion of the Patrologie Latine and the Patrologie Grecque was **pirated**; and the rest, with the exception of a couple of volumes, was either reproduced from other editions or reproduced along with a critical apparatus, which was in some instances also pirated and included only minor additions or changes" (pp. 65–66).
- "Migne often **reprinted editions mistakes and all**, or he reprinted older or even recently published works without indicating his sources. His dictionaries were sometimes simply lifted without acknowledgment from eighteenth-century works or from more contemporary ones" (p. 65).
- The **Leo VI Novelles** case (p. 66): in **1861** Migne published **PG vol. CXVII** (the *Novelles* of Leo VI) reproduced from **Zachariae von Lingenthal's 1857 edition** "without," in **Alphonse Dain's** phrase, "missing a single typographical error" (*sans en manquer aucun, les lapsus typographiques*). Since the 1857 edition was still under copyright, Migne gave a **false provenance**, citing older editions of the *Corpus juris civilis*; the editors "feigned a new critical apparatus when they had, in fact, merely transcribed that of Zachariae von Lingenthal's," plus a few notes "taken, at random, from here and there" (Dain) (p. 66).
- Bonnetty's public annoyance over the *Évangiles* of the Arian **Ulfilas** ("Uffilas"), merely reproducing the recent **Gabelentz and Loebe** edition with no place or date of publication: "M. Migne should never have forgotten them" (pp. 65–66).

### 2.6 Erasing the editors' names (pp. 66–69)
- Why the patrologies were cheap — Bloch's four-part answer: "not only because **he paid his workers badly**, not only because **he drove hard bargains with his editors**, not only because he achieved an **economy of scale**, but because he managed for the most part to **avoid paying royalties**" (pp. 66–67). He "rendered those who edited the Cours complets completely invisible. He made them disappear" (p. 67).
- **Contract with Guéranger and Pitra, article 3:** "the name of the reverend fathers individually or collectively will appear nowhere [*ne sera mis nulle part*], neither in the prospectuses and the announcements, nor in the titles and the frontispieces of volumes" — while Migne's own name would appear wherever he "judges it appropriate" (p. 68).
- **Pitra's near-total absence:** his name figures in the **469 volumes of the combined Cours complets** essentially twice — once in connection with the annotations of the *Démonstrations évangéliques* ("an attribution that may, in fact, be gratuitous"), and once at **column 338 of volume 218 of the Tables**: "EDITORES PATROLOGIAE apud Montem Rubrum luce donatae inter quos praecipuus R. P. Pitra, ordinis S. Benedicti" (p. 68).
- **Payment of Pitra:** **300 francs per revised volume** of the patrologies and **50 francs per sheet of the Index Tables** — with silence imposed: "All this in confidence — as in the penitential booth" (*Tout cela confidentiellement comme au tribunal de la pénitence*) (p. 67).
- **Fr. Oehler to Migne, April 1857:** "I cannot send you the original Greek and Latin text in good shape... The rest is your business. This is why I do not insist that my name figure in the title: I only ask that you mention in the preface that you have obtained from me a copy corrected against the manuscripts" (p. 68).
- Migne's own mark on every acknowledgment page: "**ACCURANTE J.-P. MIGNE**," "ACCURANTE ET DENUO RECOGNOSCENTE J.-P. MIGNE," or "ACCURANTE ET AD ULTIMUM RECOGNOSCENTE J.-P. MIGNE" (p. 63).
- The 1838 prospectus had promised the opposite: "All the names... which have served as a frontispiece for the first prospectus are the names of the true authors of the Cours complets... **We will reveal one day the part that each will have played in it**" (pp. 67–68, AN F18 1803) — a promise never kept.
- Migne's public justification for namelessness — the vanity of names: "It is not rare to see publications... announced ostentatiously with a long and wide list [*queue*], all diamond-studded [*toute diamantée*] with the most imposing names, without even consulting those whose names appear there... This is a **peacock's tail [*queue de paon*], poised to leave, at the first chance, the jay which has stolen it**" (pp. 71–72, AN F18 1803). Bloch notes the irony against the man who begged Veuillot for "the list between a head and tail of your hand" (p. 72).

### 2.7 "Editing by Collation" — the 5,000-letter poll (pp. 72–75)
- Before launching prospectuses or selling a single subscription, Migne sent out **five thousand lettres de consultation** to "bishops, head vicars, theologians, superiors, and professors of all the seminaries of France, without exception" (p. 72, AN F18 1803), asking three questions (quoted pp. 72–73):
  1. Which author has produced the best commentaries/treatises on each list — "It does not matter whether the designated author is ancient or modern, French or foreign, dead or living... The only thing that counts is that his commentary or his treatise be in your opinion the best of all those with which you are familiar."
  2. "What is the best edition of each of these commentaries or treatises?"
  3. "What defects are contained in each of the following commentaries which we believe to be wise to faithfully reproduce?"
- Migne "has, upon receipt of the responses, supposedly collated them" to select editions and commentaries (p. 73).
- **Bloch's image:** "if the editor of the reprint digest is a pair of scissors, the editor of the Cours complets is a **pot of glue**" — a community of respondents whose collective decisions "seem to come out of nowhere and to belong to no one" (p. 73).
- **Bloch's demographic check:** given clergy numbers (**40,600 priests in 1830, 47,000 in 1848**), the 5,000 "editors" must overlap heavily with the 50,000 claimed enthusiastic supporters (p. 73) — i.e., the marketing produced "an identification between the producer and the buyer, who has, even before making the first payment on a subscription, a vested interest in the success of the colossal endeavor" (p. 73).
- **The three advantages Bloch identifies in the "originless/collective" ideology (his analysis):**
  1. Moral cover — self-promotion acquires "the moral patina of humility": "Our praise is only directed toward works praised always and by everyone... works that we have not even ourselves selected" (p. 74, AN F18 1803).
  2. A truth-guarantee — a committee of 5,000 "insures a neutrality which... is synonymous with universal unshakable certitude"; editorial maxim "**In dubiis libertas**" (pp. 74–75). Key prospectus/PL quote: "There is no longer any question of individualism... this will not be just one Father, but all the Fathers. What are we saying? Not only all the Fathers, but all the writings of every Father where he treats the same thing! We will proceed in every subject, then, supported by the entire Tradition" (**PL 218, col. 4**; Bloch p. 75).
  3. Mass-market safety — the absent, neutral editor "avoids the possibility of offending anyone, and thus of alienating any segment of the market," crucial amid the Gallican/Ultramontane conflict (pp. 75–76). Cf. the Directeur de la Sûreté Générale's finding that Migne adapted "his behavior to the situation," suspected of being both for the Empire and a Legitimist (p. 76). The reader is flattered with freedom: "every reader, free in his choice of an opinion, can adopt that which seems to him the most rational, and owes his conviction only to himself. The flame of truth is not held hidden from him under a bushel" (p. 76, AN F18 1803).
- Chapter's closing frame (Bloch's argument): the excision of editors' names and the depersonalized print process together serve "the attempt to depersonalize the industrial production of the Church Fathers," reinforcing "standardization of production and precise quantification of labor, print, and especially price in the period of industrial takeoff from **1840 to 1860**" (p. 77).

## 3. Piracy and Copyright

- **Legal baseline:** most republications were legally clean — public domain under the **law of July 19, 1793** (copyright, *les droits d'auteur*, protecting original works and editions for **ten years**), extended by the **decree of February 5, 1810** to protect the author's heirs for **twenty years after death**. "The simple fact is, then, that in the vast majority of cases Migne did nothing legally wrong" (p. 61).
- **The fine line — still-protected 19th-century editions Migne appropriated** (p. 61):
  - R. Klotz, *Bibliotheca sacra Patrum ecclesiae graecorum* (Leipzig, 1831–34)
  - E. G. Gersdorf (d. 1874), *Bibliotheca Patrum eccl. latinorum selecta* (Leipzig, 1839–47)
  - Rheinwald (d. 1849) & Voigt's revision of Th. Pelt's *Homiliarum patristicum* (Berlin, 1829–34)
  - C. F. Horneman (d. 1830), *Scripta genuina graeca Patrum apostolicorum* (1829)
  - C. J. Hefele (d. 1893), *Patrum apostolicorum opera* (Tübingen, 1839)
  - W. Jacobson (d. 1884), *S. Clementis Rom., S. Ignatii, S. Polycarpi... quae supersunt* (Oxford 1838, Basel 1840)
  - *Bibliotheca Patrum ecclesiae catholicae... delectu presbyterorum quorumdam Oxoniensium*, 10 vols. (Oxford, 1835–55)
  - A. Mai (d. 1854), *Spicilegium Romanum*, 10 vols. (Rome, 1839–44) and *Classici Auctores*, 10 vols. (Rome, 1828–38)
  - F. H. Rheinwald, *Anecdota ad Historiam eccl. pertinentia* (Berlin, 1831–35)
  - G. Heine (d. 1848), *Bibliotheca Anecdotorum* (Paris–Leipzig, 1848)
  - G. H. Pertz (d. 1876), *Monumenta Germaniae Historica* (Berlin, 1826– )
  - M. J. Routh (d. 1854), *Reliquiae sacrae* (Oxford, 1814–18; 2nd ed. 1846–48)
  - John-Anthony Cramer (d. 1848), *Catena Graecorum Patrum in Acta SS. Apostolorum* (Oxford, 1840)
  - Jean-François Boissonade (d. 1857), *Anecdota Graeca* (Paris, 1832)
  - *Scriptorum veterum nova collectio*, 10 vols. (Rome, 1825–38)
- **Partial self-disclosure:** Migne "is not duplicitous about the fact that the Bibliothèque universelle is, at bottom, taken from elsewhere" — **PL vol. 218, columns 368–69** contains a partial list of borrowings; his publicity never promises originality but "the best available editions," "the chronological and complete reproduction of the first twelve centuries of Catholic tradition, according to the most respected editions" (p. 62, AN F18 369).
- **The near-confession of 1838:** "there are some [works] that are so rare that, not succeeding in finding them anywhere, we have been obliged to borrow them [*contraints de les emprunter*] from the Bibliothèque Royale, and to have them copied from the first to the last line [*et de les faire copier depuis la première jusqu'à la dernière ligne*]" (p. 62, AN F18 1803). And the modus operandi in which "piracy is transformed into a virtue and originality into a vice": "the editors fill in any lacunae which edited books contain from time to time with extracts borrowed from other equally important books, and... they do not resort to their own devices except in the case of the strictest necessity... **The editors have understood very well that their chief power lies in the older books they reprint and not in new ones that they might compose**" (p. 62, AN F18 1803).
- **The 1864 source-acknowledgment ad** (*Annales* of 1864 — "confession to the self-conscious level of an advertising ploy," p. 62): completely new Tertullian; Cyprian from **Fell and Baluze**; Arnobius from **Orelli**; Lactantius from **Lenglet-Dufresnoy**; Juvencus, Dracontius, Sedulius, Prudentius, and St. Isidore from **Arevalo**; **Dom Coustant** for the papal letters; **the Benedictines of Saint-Maur** for Hilary, Ambrose, Augustine, Gregory the Great, Cassiodorus, Gregory of Tours; "and so on and so forth right to the end" (pp. 62–63). Per-volume acknowledgments appear on the page after the title page — e.g., Cyprian "AD STEPHANI BALUZII EDITIONEM EXPRESSA"; Lactantius "AD PRAESTANTISSIMAM LENGLETII-DUFRESNOY EDITIONEM EXPRESSA"; Jerome, Ambrose, Augustine after the Maurists/Louvain theologians (p. 63).
- **Acquisition tactics:** bought books new and used; **a seminary sold him the Galland collection** (letter of **May 21, 1842**) — which he sometimes reprinted when better editions were too hard to procure; borrowed from the **library of Solesmes** through Dom Guéranger; **Malou** tracked hard-to-find editions to "send to print" (*passer par l'imprimerie*) (pp. 63–64). Malou letter of **November 7, 1843**, on Nicetas of Aquileia (works published by Cardinal Borgia and Denis, combined in an in-quarto, Utini, 1810): "I just received from Venice a copy of these works, which is in fairly bad shape; it is still good enough, however, **to send to print**" (p. 64).
- **The Schönemann puzzle (Bloch's supposition, flagged as such):** Malou (Feb 24, 1844) hunted Schönemann's edition of the *Epistolae Romanorum Pontificum* (Göttingen, 1796) — which was itself merely a re-edition of **Pierre Coustant** (Paris, 1721). Why prefer the edition that was both more recent and further from the source? Bloch: "the answer lies, and this again is just a supposition, precisely in the realm of availability" — Migne preferred an edition that "was less available... one whose source would be less visible," to avoid *la contrefaçon* (pp. 64–65).
- **Borrowing vs. keeping:** Malou to Dom Pitra, **October 26, 1848**, on lending Migne his Rufinus ("my Ruffin"): "in order to insure the return of the volume, I will make him sign a considerable monetary bond [*un engagement pécuniaire considérable*]... **I believe that the man's interest in money is greater than memories or promises even**" (p. 65).
- **Lawsuits and reputation:**
  - Sued in **1834** by **Picot's *Ami de la religion*** for plagiarism (p. 66; case treated earlier in the book).
  - Sued again in **1853** for *contrefaçon* — reprinting in *Le Journal des faits* articles belonging to *Le Constitutionnel* — while complaining to Dom Pitra that "these legal proceedings absorb all my time" (p. 66).
  - **The Goncourts'** satire of the Ateliers catholiques: the "defrocked scoundrels" and "death defiers" who, spotting a police officer, "scamper for the doors," while Migne shouts: "**Nobody move! This is not for you, but for a case of plagiarism** [*une affaire de contrefaçon*]" (p. 66, Bloch's translation).
- **The ideological justification (the "God's plagiarist" thesis proper — Bloch's argument):** Migne's insistence on the internationalism of his team and the "naturalness" of the idea implies the Cours complets "belong nowhere — that is, to no one"; "if no one can claim it as his own, then the question of literary property becomes nul and void" — the patrologies exist "in a de facto public domain of sorts, not because the material conforms necessarily to the law, but because it belongs to a **shared Catholic heritage**" (p. 70). Even the Société de Saint-Sulpice had the idea; indeed "there are few members of the two clergies, regular or secular, who have not conceived the same idea" (p. 70, AN F18 1803). Prospectus wish-fulfillment: "May it Please God that each reader of this Prospectus might also shout: 'Me too, I have often thought of these Cours'... this idea must be natural, since it is so universally shared... everyone... believes, when hearing it publicized, that someone had stolen his secret and his dreams of the good!" (p. 71, AN F18 1803). Bloch's capstone: "the neutral universalism of the patrologies **justifies ideologically the enterprise of the reprint**... that which... belongs to no one resolves once and for all the question of copyright" (p. 76).

## 5. Named People (with roles)

**Migne's circle / collaborators**
- **Dom Jean-Baptiste Pitra** — Benedictine of Solesmes; **chief editor and "mastermind of the patrologies"**; said to have conceived the outline of the Cours complets in three days; manuscript hunter (Metz 1846, *Clé* of Meliton at Strasbourg); prepared the new Tertullian edition for PL vol. 1; paid 300 fr./revised volume + 50 fr./Index-Table sheet under enforced confidentiality; almost wholly erased from the published volumes (pp. 58–59, 67–68).
- **Jean-Baptiste Malou** — professor at Louvain, later (context) bishop in Belgium; "Migne's other principal editorial collaborator"; reviewed systematically all Pitra did; specialist tracker/purchaser of hard-to-find editions; lent books from his own library and Louvain's; demanded a monetary bond before lending Migne his Rufinus; source of the sharpest character judgments of Migne (pp. 63–65, 68, 70).
- **Dom Prosper Guéranger** — abbot of Solesmes; arranged the "marriage" between Migne and Pitra (which Malou would later call a "divorce"); channel for Solesmes library loans (pp. 64, 67).
- **Adolphe Bonnetty** — editor of the *Annales de philosophie chrétienne*; Migne's "friend and publicist"; printed Migne's inserts, specimen pages, and endorsements; occasionally bit back (Ulfilas complaint) (pp. 45–46, 65–66, 69, 77).
- **Louis Veuillot** — journalist, editor of *l'Univers* (which Migne had founded); recipient of the Feb 13, 1858 bribe letter (p. 44).
- **Fr. Oehler** — editor at Halle; April 1857 letter waiving name credit (pp. 68, 70).
- **Paul Drach** — former rabbi converted to Catholicism; librarian of the Congregation de la Propagation de la Foi, Rome; Hebrew specialist and proofreader (p. 70).
- **Proofreaders:** Sypsomo, Pantazides, Dobriades (Greeks); Lehmann (Leipzig), Reitchardt (Stuttgart), Sidon (Cologne), Beyerle (Hanover), Guardia (Palma), Laas-d'Aguen (Holland), Tarlet (Brussels) (p. 69).
- **Editorial team:** Henri-Joseph Floss (Bonn), Georg-Hay Forbes (Glasgow), Jean Henri Nolte (Vienna), Henri J. Denzinger (Würzburg), Joseph Hergenröther (Würzburg; procured the 1861 honorary doctorate) (p. 70).
- **Dübner** — hard-pressed classicist/editor; offered 100–120 fr. for an annotated *City of God* (p. 59).
- **Family:** the **Delcros** family — Migne's sister's family; the **Maison Delcros-Migne of Saint-Flour**, "one of the most prosperous drygoods houses of Cantal," Migne's provincial branch office; **Guillaume Delcros**, brother-in-law, mutual lender/borrower (pp. 78–79).

## 7. Bloch's Interpretive Claims (argument, not documented fact)

Flag these clearly as Bloch's readings when writing the essay:
1. **Migne wrote his own endorsements.** The claim that the "Curieux détails" episcopal quotes, the *Univers religieux* review, and the planted *Estafette*/*Banlieue de Paris* article all come "at some point from Migne himself" is argued from style (serial superlatives, identical obsessions) and circumstance (reprint-digest venues, his founding of *l'Univers*) — Bloch himself flags it as "just a supposition... not verifiable absolutely because of the absence of documents, but... more than a reasoned hunch" (pp. 42–43, 50, 54). Only the **Veuillot letter is a documented bribe**; the rest is extrapolation from it.
2. **The "hall of mirrors" / "canonical echolalia" / "infinite voiceless regress"** — Bloch's structural reading that Migne multiplied one voice across seemingly unrelated publications (pp. 48, 50).
3. **Continuity thesis:** Migne's journalism (journal reproducteur), publicity, and patristic editing are one and the same "reproductive personality" — scissors-and-glue methods, voiceless neutral truth, suppression of sources and names (pp. 50, 58, 66–69, 77). This is the book's spine.
4. **The Schönemann preference = deliberate source-obscuring** to dodge contrefaçon — explicitly "just a supposition" (pp. 64–65).
5. **Universality-as-copyright-ideology:** the internationalism of the team and the "naturalness" of the idea function to void literary property — "the neutral universalism of the patrologies justifies ideologically the enterprise of the reprint" (pp. 70–71, 76). Interpretive synthesis, not a claim Migne stated.
6. **Marketing-theory reading:** the 5,000-respondent poll manufactured producer-buyer identification; the chorus of testimonials manufactured herd purchase ("each seeking to buy because the others have bought") (pp. 56, 73). The poll's *existence* is documented (prospectus text); its *function* is Bloch's analysis.
7. **Neutrality as market strategy:** In dubiis libertas / no esprit de système read as maximizing the market by offending neither Gallicans nor Ultramontanes (pp. 75–76).
8. **Migne as proto-Barnum:** placing Migne in "the nineteenth-century tradition of P. T. Barnum, Henry Ford, or Buffalo Bill," subordinating all names to a brand ("le Migne") (p. 69).
9. **Cheapness decomposition:** low prices rested on badly paid workers + hard bargains + economy of scale + **avoided royalties** (pp. 66–67) — part documented, part inference.
10. **Depersonalization thesis:** erasing editors' names and correction-based production as two faces of industrial depersonalization, 1840–60 (p. 77) — frame for Chapter 5.

**Documented facts to rely on confidently:** the Veuillot letter (BN n.a. 24633); the AN F18 369 and F18 1803 prospectus texts; contract article 3 with Guéranger/Pitra; Pitra's payments; the Malou letters (Nov 7, 1843; Feb 24, 1844; Oct 26, 1848); the Oehler letter (Apr 1857); the 1834 and 1853 lawsuits; Dain's demonstration of the Leo VI piracy; the source-collection lists; PL 218 cols. 368–69 and col. 4; the 1864 *Annales* acknowledgment.

## 8. Legends Flagged & Texture

### Legends / unverifiable claims (handle with care)
- **The 50,000 letters of praise** — Migne's own number ("This unbelievable but true number"); never produced; the promised 10–12-volume collection never published (pp. 44, 54–55).
- **The 1,500-letter, 200-page endorsement prospectus** — Migne "claims" to have published it ("Avis important"); Bloch reports the claim, not the artifact (p. 55).
- **Pitra conceiving the outline of the Cours complets "in just three days"** — "who is said to have conceived" — reported legend (p. 67).
- **"Almost all the bishops of France intend to follow" Dupanloup** — Migne's stop-press puffery (pp. 56–57).
- **The episcopal endorsement letters themselves** — genuine letters may underlie them, but their wording as printed is suspect (Bloch's central supposition, pp. 42–43).
- **The Goncourt scene** (defrocked scoundrels fleeing the police officer) — hostile satire, "hardly generous"; use as caricature, not reportage (p. 66).
- **OCR-garbled figure:** "completion of the Cours complet de Patrologie in 3[2?]6 volumes in-4o" (p. 46) — verify against the printed book before citing.

### Texture — vivid concrete details for the essay
- The planted tour piece (p. 53): leaving the **barrière d'Enfer** you find "one of the greatest print industries of Europe"; the main print house "bigger than a church"; a series of small printshops in action; "**more than three hundred workers — smelters, stereotypers, glazers, smoothers, collators, binders, typesetters, printers, mechanics**"; warehouses "unequaled anywhere in the world: these are **armed camps of in-quarto** [*des fortifications d'en-4o*]."
- The trades painted over the entrance gate: *print shop, bookstore, foundry, stereotyping, glazing, smoothing, collating, binding* — "not even in the Imprimerie nationale" all together (p. 54).
- Visiting hours on the letterhead: any day, any hour — except 11:00–noon, Sundays, and holidays (p. 57).
- Title pages as typographic peacockery: acknowledgments "all in capitals, with varying type size and fonts, as well as bold and italics," always crowned at bottom by "ACCURANTE J.-P. MIGNE" (p. 63).
- Malou's Venice copy of Nicetas "in fairly bad shape; still good enough, however, to send to print [*passer par l'imprimerie*]" — the physical book as raw material (p. 64).
- Manuscripts stockpiled since the Revolution in the Metz arsenal to pack artillery cartridges; rescued by Pitra's memoranda within weeks (pp. 58–59).
- Greek accents set by three Greeks — Sypsomo, Pantazides, Dobriades — because accents were "quite neglected by the old Benedictines" (p. 69).
- **Ch. 5 opening texture (pp. 78–79, continues next chunk):** the Maison Delcros-Migne of Saint-Flour as branch office — taking orders, collecting payments, stocking and distributing the Cours complets to priests who came to town to buy their ecclesiastical robes ("to this day... the only explanation" for the region's disproportionate number of surviving volumes); sister's cloth shipments for soutanes, underpants, gloves; Migne's stocking order ("Black... I have a large calf and a high ankle, only I need them very high above the knee"); receipt books printed on the Ateliers presses — "the double columns of the patrologies were easily adapted... to the double-account bookkeeping records"; 5% interest to Saint-Flour lenders; 25 annual lottery tickets from the Ladies of Charity — and the **1860 jackpot: a sketchbook, a pair of pink candle-drip rings (*bobèches*), a soup tureen, a small knife, the *Paroissien romain*, and two volumes he himself had donated to the prize kitty**; Migne's 1838 pitch to the Ministre de l'Intérieur: "My fixed plan is to popularize among the European clergy the master[pieces]..." (cut off at chunk boundary). Key scholar here: **Joël Fouilheron**; key phrase: "a virtual bazaar of familial mutual aids" (*tout un bazar d'entraides familiales*) (p. 78). Section motto: "**Du bon, à bon marché**" — "the good at a good price" (p. 79).
