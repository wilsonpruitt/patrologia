## 1. THE MONEY CHAPTER (Ch. 5, pp. 80–104)

### 1a. Pricing and sales machinery of the patrologies (pp. 80–84)

- **Headline sales pitch:** the 469 volumes of the patrologies, bought separately, "would amount to over 100,000 francs"; as a series the price drops to 1,000–1,200 fr for the Latin Fathers, "only slightly more for the Greek" (p. 80).
- **Base price:** 6 francs/volume for subscribers to only one Cours (Latin *or* Greek); 5 francs/volume for double subscribers — "Les deux Cours marchent de front" (pp. 80–81).
- **The "six advantages"** for double subscribers (p. 81): (1) the 5-fr price; (2) subscribing without paying letter postage; (3) credit — pay only for volumes shipped, the rest "semester to semester" (a layaway plan); (4) free home delivery (*franco*) to the seat of the arrondissement — aimed at the *curés de campagne*; (5) COD collection at the buyer's own house at no extra expense; (6) access to Migne's discount catalogues "for all the diverse bookstore items and ecclesiastical goods" (AN F18 1803).
- **Expiring offers / urgency:** "Beginning in January 1838, the price of each vol. will be raised to 6 fr., even for subscribers to the two Cours" (p. 81). Early prospectus: "200 vol. in-4o. Price: 1,000 f. for the first one thousand subscribers; 1,200 fr. for the rest" (p. 81).
- **Prepayment discount on the whole Bibliothèque universelle:** 2,000 volumes at 10,000 fr, "only 8,300 fr. for those who pay all at once" — a bottom price of **4.15 fr/volume** (p. 82). A later publication deepened this: 10,000 f for the whole, **7,000 f** paid entirely in advance (Ch. 5 n. 15, AN F18 369, p. 141).
- **Bloch's interest arithmetic (his reconstruction, marked as such):** at the planned output (~35 vols/year) the corpus would take ~60 years; 4.5 fr invested at 5% (Migne's own loan rate) = 6.42 fr after 10 years; amortized over 30 years ≈ 16 fr; after 60 years, 4.15 fr ≈ 65 francs. Migne, Bloch argues, had adjusted the prepay figure so that money-in-advance plus interest equaled full retail (p. 82). "Time, for the director of the Ateliers catholiques, was the equivalent of money" (p. 82).
- **The pyramid scheme (Bloch's own term):** "a nineteenth-century version of a pyramid scheme" — a single-Cours subscriber qualifies for the reduced combined price only by finding a colleague-subscriber; the second must find a third, "and so on and so forth" (pp. 82–83; Ch. 5 n. 17, AN F18 1803).
- **Referral bounty ladder** (p. 83, AN F18 1803): 1 procured subscription = a free volume of Saint Theresa; 2 = a complete Saint Theresa; 3 = three volumes of Pallavicini or the *Démonstrations évangéliques* (pay only 5 fr for the fourth); 4 = a complete *Histoire du Concile de Trente* (Pallavicini) or the *Démonstrations* of Eusebius, Huet, Leland, Stattler, Duvoisin; 5 = the *Summa* of St. Thomas; 6 = the *Perpétuité de la foi* (Nicole, Arnauld, Renaudot); 10 = the eleventh subscription free — "un avantage précieux" for seminaries, reducing each man's cost by 18 fr.
- **Bonus-book discounts for prepayers** (pp. 83–84): double subscribers billed by a single draft pay 8 fr instead of 24 for a free 4-vol. *Démonstrations* or Pallavicini; single subscribers pay 4 fr instead of 12 for Sainte Thérèse; those who already paid the first semester owe 10 and 5 fr instead "to offset the costs of this first draft." Subscribers must declare intentions "immediately after reading this Notice, so that we might know in advance if we should prepare drafts for 50 fr. or for 200 fr." (AN F18 1803). Whatever the combination, "the SOMME de saint Thomas ne pourrait descendre que jusqu'au prix de 7 francs le volume" (p. 84).
- **Postage/customs fine print** (Ch. 5 n. 11, p. 141): outside France, customs and handling extra; but favored-nation shipping for episcopal towns of Holland, Rhenish Prussia, Switzerland, Bade, Catalonia, Navarre, Savoie at 75 c./bound volume, 50 c. for Belgium and Corsica (AN F18 369).

### 1b. "Industrial Modernism" (pp. 84–88)

- Migne converts machine productivity into man-years: "the hand of a monk of yesteryear could not copy in three years what is done in the Imprimerie catholique in a single minute" (p. 85, AN F18 369). Bloch flags that the claim "may or may not be accurate" — and n. 37 (p. 131) notes Migne attributed the phrase to two newspapers (*L'Estafette*, *Banlieue de Paris*) but per Hamman it also appears on Migne's own letterhead, and Migne "was entirely capable of having written the newspaper article himself."
- **Spatial compression:** the Index Tables shrink the world — "Thanks to our Tables, these great Series are rendered small; distances are closer together, the first and the last volumes touch" (PL 218, col. 4; p. 85). Migne compares himself to Hercules and his publications to the tunnel blasted through Mount Cenis (p. 85); the Twelve Labors vs. "nos 231 Tables!" (Ch. 1 n. 9, p. 129).
- **Temporal compression:** the famous electricity quote — "Quelle économie de temps! c'est plus que le chemin de fer, et même le ballon, c'est l'électricité!" (PL 218, col. 4; p. 86). Also the wine-press metaphor: the Patrology "squeezed and pressed like the grape under a press from which the least drop of precious liqueur could not escape" (PL 218, col. 4; p. 86).
- **Quantification of the Tables' labor** (p. 87, PL 218, cols. 1 & 4): **231 tables** for the 217-volume PL; each table required reading all 217 volumes, done 231 times = "an analysis of more than 50,000 volumes of our format"; a man analyzing one volume per fifteen days "would need more than 1800 years"; instead "more than fifty men for more than ten years"; at "the small salary of 1,000 francs a year per man, makes more than **500,000 francs**, without even counting the cost of printing."
- **CEO of the Cours complets** (p. 88): Migne as "chairman of the board of the patrologies," a captain of industry; metaphors drawn from heavy industry and metallurgy — the plates as "the plate armor of Christian tradition."
- **The five-proofreadings quote** (pp. 88–89): five complete readings each verified against the last, plus revisions, then stereotyping, then a final full proofreading against the copy — "the printing only comes after these innumerable precautions." (Cf. Ch. 1 n. 40, p. 132: in 1865 Migne hired forty proofreaders for ten years "under onerous conditions" to revise the plates; proofreading cost as much as typesetting; 25-centime bonus per error found.)
- **Standardization down to the letter** (pp. 89–90): type "completely new and molded especially for us" by the Didot foundry; paper from the Marais factories near Coulommiers (Seine-et-Marne), jésus format; "Each page contains 108 lines of 38 letters each, which makes 4,180 letters on each page, 66,880 on each sheet, and 2,508,000 in each volume" (AN F18 1803; p. 90). Migne called this his "révolution typographique" (pp. 89–90). N. 20 (p. 142): the engraver Friry created special roman and italic Greek characters to distinguish citations from text — "a first in the printing of Greek" (per Marchal).
- **Bookstore revolution / eliminating the middleman** (p. 91): Dom Cabrol: "It was a revolution for bookstores... the complete works of the Fathers have been made available to the purse of a simple country priest." Low prices came not just from scale but from subscription sales direct to the consumer — "All of Migne's sales schemes are, finally, the very essence of wholesale."

### 1c. The first conflict with the Archbishops of Paris (pp. 91–92)

- Paris booksellers, threatened by direct discount sales, appealed to **Archbishop Quélen**, who (per Hippolyte Barbier) "gave in to the greedy jealousies of the bookstore owners," tried to close the Ateliers, and forbade Migne to continue, citing canons on a passage of St. Paul condemning commerce (pp. 91–92).
- Barbier's canonical defense of Migne: (1) the canon predates printing; (2) Paul condemned "profane commerce, not a commerce which has as its goal the propagation of Catholic doctrine"; (3) both print-shop and bookstore licenses were registered in the brother's name, not Migne's (pp. 91–92). ⚠️ **Textual discrepancy:** Bloch's main text (p. 91) says the print-license request was "in his brother **Etienne's** name," while n. 24 (p. 142) quotes Barbier: the two licenses "belong to M. **Victor** Migne, his brother and his worthy collaborator." Verify before using a name.
- When Migne refused to cease publication, Quélen reversed doctrinal course and, "several months before his death," proposed a **diocesan takeover**: "to convert this individually owned speculation into one of the works of the diocese," Quélen as superior, Migne as director (p. 92). Migne, "who did not fancy at all" this proposition (Barbier), refused, **was suspended** and "treated as a 'priest unrecognized by the diocese of Paris'" — prohibited from saying mass in Paris; he thereafter officiated in **Versailles**, welcomed by **Mgr Gros** (p. 92). He escaped being formally "interdit" only because he remained officially attached to the **diocese of Orléans**, not Paris (p. 92).
- **Mgr Affre** (Quélen's successor, who had twice condemned *La Voix de la vérité*) "continued the attempted hostile takeover" but, recognizing Migne's steadfastness, **lifted the interdiction on November 10, 1847** (p. 92). **Mgr Sibour**, Affre's successor, ordered **5,000 francs** worth of books from the Ateliers and had them print an edition of the *Actes de l'Église de Paris* (p. 92).

### 1d. "Churchly Kitsch" — the religious-objects trade (pp. 92–95)

- Beyond books, the Ateliers housed Migne's "**museum**," selling paintings, statues, altarpieces, organs, stations of the cross, "religious paraphernalia of all sorts... at moderate prices" (pp. 92–93; BN n.a. 24633, fol. 478). Letterhead: "The Ateliers catholiques undertakes to fabricate all kinds of religious paintings at the lowest possible price and in a manner worthy of religion" (p. 93).
- Art studio supervised by **Joachim Issartier**, "a minor painter admired by Louis-Philippe, also from Cantal" (p. 93). Bloch compares Migne the "art dealer" to Arnoux in Flaubert's *Éducation sentimentale* (p. 93).
- Stations-of-the-cross sales copy: hand-painted oils, "frames in wood and oil-base gold leaf, a gilding that is not affected by humidity" (p. 93). Priced **by the square centimeter** — dimensions table (paintings + frames): 0.72m × 0.60m = 480 fr; 0.85 × 0.72 = 600; 0.96 × 0.79 = 750; ~1.0 × 0.99 = 940 fr, etc. (p. 94; BN n.a. 24633, fol. 478). Transport at buyer's expense "at a rate of 13 c. per 100 kilogr. for each 4 kilom." (p. 94).
- Migne's own formula: prices "at the last limit of the beautiful and the good at a good price [la dernière limite du beau et du bon à bon marché]" (p. 94).
- **Quality anecdote:** a painting commissioned by the Tribunal de Commerce de Saint-Flour displeased its buyers ("fairly good, but... certain defects" — Guillaume Delcros); Migne's retort: "Those who judge are neither artists nor amateurs. Christ is rendered according to the laws of true perspective" (p. 95; Fouilheron p. 394).
- N. 29 (p. 142): none of Migne's artwork has been positively identified; the *Moniteur de Cantal* suggests looking first in churches around Saint-Flour. Vernet (unattributed sources): the organ/pious-object commerce "does not appear to have had much financial success," and the great organs were shoddy — "all the parts sooner or later had to be replaced."

### 1e. "Mass Traffic" — the Mass-stipend commerce (pp. 95–98)

**Mechanics** (pp. 95–96; Bloch cautions that accounts are hard to come by "because of the illicit nature of the commerce"):
- Migne used his brochures to offer to "**centralize**" masses for dioceses of post-Revolutionary France lacking priests: he collected **1 fr 25 c per mass sung**, then either (a) paid the officiating priest **65 centimes** cash, supplying the remainder in books priced at full undiscounted retail — "Migne thus accruing approximately 30 percent of the remaining sixty centimes"; or (b) paid entirely in volumes of the Bibliothèque universelle (p. 95).
- The 1838 prospectus set the book-for-masses exchange rates: **half a Cours for 250 masses, a whole Cours for 500, both Cours for 1000**; or the 4 *Démonstrations*, Pallavicini and Sainte Thérèse for 240 masses; the *Summa* + *Perpétuité de la foi* for 260; all five works for 500 (p. 96; AN F18 1803). Beyond that, terms individually negotiable — "the administration would consent to do all in its power to take his wishes into consideration"; no referral too small or too large (p. 96).
- Migne's self-defense against the simony charge in the same prospectus: "It is painful for the editors to enter into such details, but who does not know that, in order to do good, one often has to resolve to conquer more than a little reluctance?"; the very openness of the offer is "a guarantee against the charge of simony"; "The masses... will be said, well said, promptly said" (pp. 96–97). N. 32–33 (p. 143): he answered a seminary superior's simony worry by repeating the offer ("The administration of the Cours complets accepts the 1000 masses...") and claimed episcopal channels: masses distributed only "through the proper channels of the secretariat of the bishopric or of the local seminary" (AN F18 1803).

**Exposure and sanction** (p. 97):
- **Police report, February 1854** (AN F18 333a): "He has engaged in a scandalous traffic, which consists of having masses for the dead said in Belgium at a cost 75c, and of having his dupes pay for them to the tune of 1 f. 50 and 2 francs. He has even offered bonuses consisting of the works of his bookstore."
- **The Goncourts** (Journal, Aug. 21, 1864): cures pay for books in "coupons for masses [bons de messes], which are signed off for by the bishop. It costs, all in all, 8 sous; he resells them for 40 sous in Belgium, where there aren't enough priests to fulfill all the endowments of masses established at the time of the Spanish domination. ... A purse full of masses!" (p. 97).
- **Suspension and Rome:** Migne "was suspended by the Archbishop of Paris in **1874** for the practice of 'centralizing' masses and was condemned a second time in **July 1875** by the authorities in Rome" (p. 97). ⚠️ Bloch names no archbishop here. **Note for our essay: it cannot be Darboy** (killed by the Commune, May 1871); the archbishop in 1874 was **Guibert**. Do not follow secondary accounts that put Darboy in this role; Bloch's text gives only "the Archbishop of Paris."
- Bloch's verdict withheld: "it is difficult if not impossible... to determine with certainty whether his activities were essentially altruistic or self-serving... an act of efficient charity or one of self-interested simony" (p. 97). "At worst Migne joined the sin of usury to that of simony; at best, he charitably harnessed his considerable organizational capacities to the present needs of mid-century Catholicism under siege" (p. 98).

### 1f. "Les Prêtres Prêteurs" — Migne's private bank (pp. 98–104)

- Malou's quip (requiring a deposit before lending Migne his Rufinus): "I believe that the man's interest in money is greater than memories or promises even" (p. 98).
- Migne solicited outright **loans from the lower clergy** and "a few enlightened, pious, and comfortable laymen" under prospectus titles like "A Licit, Advantageous, Dependable, Easy, and Worthy Means OF PLACING YOUR FUNDS" — "Providence has never permitted, not even once, that our appeal rest in vain" (pp. 98–99).
- Bloch's literary parallel: the Cointet frères of Balzac's *Illusions perdues*, printers who run an unlicensed bank (p. 99; Paris banking license then 500 fr/year).
- **The sleeping-money obsession** (pp. 99–100): Migne "could not tolerate the idea of money standing idle," was "haunted... by the notion of priests hoarding money in drawers throughout the countryside"; prospectus horror of "funds [that] would otherwise sleep sterilely in a corner of their desk" (AN F18 1803); lending as "an act of high religious devotion and of enlightened prudence" that brings, "even while they sleep, a material and spiritual good."
- **Loan terms** (p. 101): (1) free postage; (2) free pick-up of funds at the priest's residence; (3) interest at **5% in cash or 7% in bound volumes** of the lender's choice; (4) withdrawal on two months' notice; (5) interest sent annually unprompted, in bank notes or postal vouchers at Migne's expense; (6) honesty — "our priests will never see their rent clipped." Sweetener: "Each lender will be able to sleep on both ears, without worrying in the least about the fate of his savings" (p. 100). For lenders needing more: **6% cash or 8% in books**, justified by "our commercial strength" (p. 102).
- **The Pope as rate-setter** (p. 102): Migne pegs 5% to the Pontiff's own borrowings — "In lending or in borrowing at the rate of the Sovereign Pontiff neither party needs to be ashamed or to make any kind of restitution." N. 42 (p. 144): higher rates would signal "great need and the instability of the Institution"; anything above the legal rate strikes his readers as "canonically and civilly usurious, no matter by what made-up name one calls it" (Mély p. 225).
- **Claimed safety** (pp. 101–3): 29 years without one contested bank note, "not even during the financial crises following 'the republican explosion'" — a claim Bloch calls "hard to believe, and even harder to believe that Migne believed," given the disputes over his newspaper empire (p. 101); "notre signature est restée vierge" (p. 102); assets > liabilities — even sold "for their paper contents to the grocer, and... the plates sold for their lead contents to the dealer in metals, the sum raised would greatly surpass our Liabilities"; everything mortgage-free (pp. 102–3); and "everything is insured by **twenty Insurance Companies**, whose plaques appear on the entrance [frontispice] of the building" — "in no imaginable circumstance, not even that of a fire, of the most extreme revolution or our death, would their funds be exposed to risk" (p. 103). Company names: la Paternelle, la Fraternelle, le Monde, le Soleil, l'Aigle, la France, la Clémentine, la Confiance (p. 103). Bloch: "Migne... could not have been more perversely prophetic" (p. 103).
- **Bonnetty's 1866 valedictory** (pp. 103–4, *Annales de philosophie chrétienne*): "Here they are at last finished, these two Patrologies... the two most beautiful historical monuments to be found anywhere in the world!"; the plates are the Church's "titles of origin, of ownership and of nobility... monuments etched, if not in stone, at least in metal, and as a result... **henceforth indestructible**" (p. 104).
- **Delcros financing** (Ch. 5 nn. 1–5, pp. 139–40, from Fouilheron): the Maison Delcros-Migne of Saint-Flour backed him — "We [the Delcros] still have ten thousand francs at your disposal, you can make withdrawals without notification... and we will find still more resources if you need them"; and Migne begging in reverse: "If you can find some easy lender... right now my position is the same as yours after February 1848."

## 2. THE FIRE OF FEBRUARY 1868 ("Trial by Fire," pp. 104–112)

### 2a. The night of the fire (pp. 104–106)

- Migne — "his sleep in reality guaranteed by eighteen insurance companies" — was awakened **between one and two o'clock on the morning of February 12, 1868**: the Ateliers catholiques were in flames, "the fire having started somewhere in the print shop or between the print shop and the typesetters' room" (pp. 104–5).
- "Migne's first thought apparently was of his personal library. He began throwing books out of the window of his apartment before neighbors arrived to help save the furniture" (p. 105). Fire not contained until dawn; still smoldering in the morning; journalists arriving around seven found "the heat of the ground was still such that if paper fell upon it, it burst immediately into flame" (p. 105, *Le Monde illustré*, Feb. 22, 1868).
- *Le Monde illustré*: "Five hundred thousand plates, stacked in piles, melted in an instant; they are now enormous blocks, in the most bizarre forms... a river of molten metal." *Le Moniteur de Cantal* (Feb. 15, 1868): "this immense workshop... a pile of blackened papers, of twisted steel in the midst of which enormous lead lingots could still be seen" (p. 105).
- **The tally: 627,855 plates (clichés) and 582,722 kilos of lead**, "not counting an enormous organ, worth 30,000 francs and ready for delivery, which had melted so completely that... no trace remained" (Alfred d'Aunay, *Le Figaro*, Feb. 13, 1868; p. 105). Le Figaro's fuller quote (n. 49, p. 144): "In this factory without equal, twelve professions were practiced... A great organ worth thirty thousand francs, ready for delivery, was literally melted. At ten o'clock I looked in vain for its trace."
- **Survived:** only Migne's apartments and the accounts office (p. 105).
- **Migne's response:** "Ma comptabilité est intacte. Personne ne perdra rien" ("My accounts office is intact. No one will lose anything"), to *Le Figaro* — his "second thought... directed toward the interests of those who had lent him money for interest" (p. 105). To the *Chronique du journal général de l'imprimerie* (Feb. 15, 1868): after seeing "his steadfast work of thirty-five years perish in a few hours... he is not discouraged. 'I am very old, and I will finish my life's work before being myself finished'" (p. 105).
- **Bonnetty's phoenix piece** (pp. 105–6, *Annales*, 1868) — Bloch notes the irony that one of the insurers was **the Phénix**: "the irrepressible Migne, devastated... saying... *They are no more!* Devastated, we say, but not beaten, still on his two feet"; and, recalling his own "henceforth indestructible" boast of two years earlier, Bonnetty asks "how the good Lord could have permitted such a disaster?" and sees "the Abomination of the desolation of which the prophet speaks" (p. 106).
- **Cause — LEGEND FLAG:** "The origin of the fire... however suspicious, remains unknown." An 1875 letter of M. de Font-Réaulx says the abbé Ulysse Chevalier related that Migne thought it set by a **disgruntled worker**; the concierges of the quarter spoke of an *incendie volontaire*. Bloch: "Both reports are on the order of hearsay, and the second is especially unworthy of belief," since Migne's insurance was inadequate and he knew it (pp. 106–7).

### 2b. Insurance shortfall and the delayed trial (p. 107)

- *L'Illustration* (Feb. 22, 1868): "One figure says it all. The abbé Migne estimates his losses to be **twelve million**, and the thirty-three companies [sic] whose names one used to see at the entrance to his establishment have only insured his creditors for **six million**."
- Corrections by Bloch: the advertised "twenty" companies "were really only **eighteen**"; Migne was covered for only **29/40** of the worth of his assets, **self-insured for 11/40** (p. 107).
- The eighteen companies balked at paying even the 29/40; negotiations "were further delayed by the **Franco-Prussian War** and by the events of the **Commune**." The case of the abbé Migne versus his insurers was finally heard in court only in **December 1871** (p. 107). (That is the whole of this chunk's Franco-Prussian War/Commune coverage — as a delay mechanism only; no account of the siege's effect on the shop itself.)

### 2c. The trial — what is a book worth? (pp. 107–112)

**Insurers' case** — argued by a **M. Scellos** ("whose very name summons the idea of misdeed (Lat. *scelus*)," p. 107):
1. Wear and tear of previous printings had reduced the plates' value (p. 107).
2. Sales were declining, so earning potential ≠ original earning power (p. 107). N. 56 (*Le Droit*, Dec. 22, 1871): the Series of Holy Writings and of Theology — the two biggest printings, **10,000 copies** — had fallen from ~550 copies/year to 150–200 and 100–150 respectively.
3. Some plates were worthless; movable type would reprint more efficiently "almost all the Latin Patrology, with the exception of Saint Ambroise, Rufinus, Saint Jerome, Saint Augustine, Saint Leon, Saint Denis le Petit, Saint Gregory of Tours, Saint Bernard, the indexes, etc." and "a great part of the Greek Patrology" (pp. 107–8).
4. An insurance policy must not be a source of profit: indemnified for slow sellers, Migne would print bestsellers first and pocket interest on the rest (p. 108; n. 58, *Le Droit*).
- Their exhibit, the *Dictionnaire de littérature*: 1,050 copies printed in 1851; 875 remained at the fire; 175 sold in eighteen years (~10/year); 375 destroyed, 510 [sic] surviving — "enough copies in the warehouse to meet the sales demands of the next fifty-one years!" (p. 108, *Le Droit*). Bloch: "Migne was in some real sense hoisted by the petards of his own propensity for intricate and precise reckoning" — his adversaries "had... invented a scholastics of compensation worthy of his own outlandish calculations" (p. 108).

**Migne's case** — expert witness **M. Firmin Didot** (pp. 108–10):
1. Unique character of the loss: "the greatest publishing enterprise since the invention of printing."
2. Restitutive nature: unlike paintings or jewels, "these **1,019 volumes** could be reconstituted, page by page, line by line, by the Insurance Companies."
3. Proximity to completion: "only several pages of the Patrologies, in **476 volumes**, still remained to be printed."
4. Value to Christianity: full indemnity "or, and this would be an even greater solace for Christians, the plates should be reconstituted."
5. Inseparability of the whole — an "ensemble sui generis": "even those [volumes] which seem from a purely commercial point of view less valuable than others are nonetheless necessary to them" (p. 109). Also compensation for delay: "delays which have already caused him such a great loss" (p. 110).

**How close to done** (pp. 109–10): The **Patrologia graeca lacked but one volume, set but not yet printed**; Bonnetty: "all would have been saved had the disaster occurred only two or three days later, since M. Migne only needs two days to print 1,000 copies of a volume." The **PL lacked only the last three sheets of the last of six index volumes** — the first compilation of the tables had taken two years. N. 62 (pp. 145–46, *Annales* 1868): the six volumes contained "240 general and specific tables," including an *Index rerum* and an *Index Scripturae sacrae* tracking patristic commentary "from the first verse of Genesis to the last of the Apocalypse"; the missing tome was "volume 223 of the whole collection"; the plates were destroyed and "one fears even that the proofs and the manuscript burned, which means that it will be necessary to begin again, as if nothing had yet been done."

**Rebuilding intent** (pp. 109–10): Bonnetty, quoting the *Semaine religieuse de Paris*: "he is resolved not to leave his life's work uncompleted. ... He is going to begin work anew, redo the tables of the Patrologie latine, reset the melted plates of the last volume of the Patrologie grecque and of the other publications." Bloch's caveat: Migne "was seventy-one at the time of the settlement" and "could not possibly have reconstituted in their entirety the plates of the Bibliothèque universelle"; and the insurers reasonably suspected the surplus would be turned to interest (p. 110).

**Bloch on the nature of the book** (pp. 110–11, his interpretive set-piece): the insurers valued only "la valeur vénale" — paper and lead, fungible like "wheat, flour, cotton, wool"; Firmin Didot's formula — a material, reproducible object "nonetheless invested with something of the sacred" — "resembles nothing so much as the idea of the **sacrament**" (p. 111). Capped by Migne's own credo: "private property is 'the most sacred thing in the world after religion'" (p. 111).

**Verdict** (pp. 111–12): the third expert, **M. Daguin**, pronounced for Migne. The court ruled:
1. Indemnity must correspond to **cost of replacement**;
2. Market value of individual works and what Migne would do with the money were irrelevant;
3. Plates and industrial goods: insured for **3,800,000 fr**; adjusters' pre-fire estimate **3,890,135 fr 91 c**; clean-up cost **340,457 fr 05**; **loss set at 3,529,678 fr 90** (pp. 111–12).
- Applying proportionality and deducting the self-insured 11/40: **the companies owed 2,512,141 fr 80** — plus **13,402 fr 03** for buildings; **7,250 fr** for his private library; **411,699 fr 95** for books stored in the warehouses; **224 fr 74** for "the Stations of the Cross, paintings, organs and harmoniums"; **10,875 fr** for the big organ; **626 fr 18** for personal possessions (p. 112).
- The court **refused interest** on money dormant "for the almost four years during which the Ateliers catholiques failed to print a single book" (p. 112).

## 3. FINAL YEARS, DEATH, AFTERMATH

This chunk is thin here — Bloch closes the chapter in two sentences and buries the Garnier sale in a footnote:

## 4. THE CONCLUSION'S ARGUMENT ("Le Bon Marché and the Ateliers Catholiques," pp. 113–128)

### 4a. Balzac frame (pp. 113–114)
- "There is almost no aspect of Migne's publicist or publishing activity that is not contained in Balzac's... *Illusions perdues*." Bloch confesses wondering "more than once if Migne, before launching the Bibliothèque universelle du clergé in 1838, had read the first part of *Illusions perdues*, which appeared in February 1837" — then dismisses it (Migne, claiming "not even one hour of recreation a year," lacked leisure for a 700-page novel; and his own journalism of 1833 could as easily have informed Balzac). **The real claim:** both men were "part of a moment of mighty change"; Migne is "an embodiment of the industrial revolution of the Restoration and July Monarchy, and... a paradigm of the print capitalists" of *La Comédie humaine* — "the pure product of the times Balzac chronicles" (p. 114). "One answer to the question... of how he did it is purely technical" (p. 114).

### 4b. The technical answer (pp. 114–119)
- **Paper:** the rag-shortage problem (Balzac's David Séchard theme) resolved only in 1846 by paper from defibrated sawdust chemically bonded ("the acidified paper whose disintegration is currently of such worry" — Bloch's aside, p. 115); Louis Robert's continuous-paper patent 1798, first prototype in England 1803; post-1814 French machines produced 1,000 kg/day vs. 100 by hand (p. 115).
- **Presses:** wooden hand presses essentially unchanged since Gutenberg; Haas's semi-metal press (1770s–80s); Didot screw press design stolen by Anisson-Duperron; Pierres's improvements; Dearborn's wheel press (1785); Prosser's spring press (1794); the all-metal **Stanhope press (1804)** introduced to France by Ambroise Firmin Didot (its absence marks the archaic provinces in the first sentence of *Illusions perdues*); **Koenig & Bauer's steam-driven cylinder machine** — by 1823 the *Bulletin des lois* had an Applegath two-cylinder; ~twenty Koenig machines sold in France 1828–30; Gaveaux's machine for *Le National* (1831); **Marinoni's four-feeder cylinder for *La Presse* (1854)** (pp. 115–16).
- **Inking and typesetting:** Cowper (1818) and Applegath (1823) mechanical inking rollers; Gannal and Les Marne of Paris — "printers of religious books alongside Migne" — used gelatinous sugar-adhesive rollers from 1819; stereotype-plate patent of Herhan, Firmin Didot, and Gatteaux (1797–98); Delcambre's pianotype typesetter (1855) "probably arrived on the scene too late for Migne" (p. 117).
- **Print-industry statistics** (pp. 117–18): print shops grew 150% between 1811 and 1840 (twice population growth), slowing to 108% in the 1840s and 98% in the 1850s under Second Empire surveillance; titles/year: <2,000 pre-Revolution → 2,547 (1814) → 8,237 (1826) → 5,530 (1847) → 12,269 (1869); Parisian booksellers 310 (1804) → 434 (1826).
- **The religious-books boom:** Restoration = Catholic restoration = more religious titles; Balzac's Cointet brothers, "like a cynical version of Migne," court the clergy and "brought out reprints of books of devotion as soon as they came into demand. Thus they took the lead in a lucrative side-line" (p. 118).
- **Serial complete editions:** Restoration vogue for *oeuvres complètes* "libraries" (Desoer's 12-vol. Voltaire, 1816; Lefèvre's 73-vol. French classics), sold sequentially by subscription — "the director of the Ateliers catholiques **invented less than he assimilated** the marketing trends of the time" (p. 119).

### 4c. Shrinking national space (pp. 119–121)
- Paris grew from **547,000 (1800) to 1,000,000 (1850)**; Migne both rode the wave and "drew others to Montrouge" (pp. 119–20).
- Industrial takeoff (Crouzet, Lévy-Leboyer, Markovitch, Pinkney): production indices climbing from 1841, peaking 1853 (p. 120); rural agricultural growth 1820–70 meant rising purchasing power of the *curés de campagne* — "as attested by Migne's appeals for investment of their surplus cash and by his horror of money lying unproductively throughout the countryside" (p. 120).
- Steam engines: ~150–200 (1816) → 1,447 (1835) → 2,591 (1840) → 4,900 (1850); combined industrial horsepower 37,000 → 166,000 (1840–60) (p. 121).
- Railroads: 1842 law; <1,000 km before the 1840s; 1,830 km (1848); >8,000 km (1860); canals tripled 1815–48; transport costs more than halved; **the first telegraph line came "in the very year of the first volume of the patrology (1844)"** (p. 121).

### 4d. "Wholesale Drygoods and the Fathers of the Church" — the Bon Marché thesis (pp. 121–128)
- Faster movement of goods and information dissolved the Ancien Régime guild system into **wholesale**: bulk purchase, mass manufacture of standardized objects, volume sales at the lowest price. "And Migne was about nothing, if not about wholesale" — his creed "du bon, à bon marché" captures "the mid-century trend toward a mass market, bureaucratization, and rationalization of economic exchange" (p. 122).
- Bloch's guiding question: how did a priest "trained in the provinces in the ways of the Lord" learn "so quickly the ways of the world"? Answer steered "in the direction of that other incarnation of the mass market — **the Bon Marché**, the department store whose innovative product line, publicity, and sales methods resonate uncannily with the very concept of and the modes of marketing of the Cours complets" (pp. 122–23).
- **Chronology of the grands magasins** (p. 123): earliest *magasins de nouveautés* appeared, "like Migne, in the Paris of the 1830s" (Le Bon Marché, Petit Saint-Thomas); 1840s Ville de Paris; 1850s Louvre, Bazar de l'Hôtel de Ville; 1860s Printemps; 1870s Samaritaine; 1890s Galeries Lafayette.
- **Point-for-point analogy** (pp. 123–24): full product line (the whole of Christian tradition *in toto* vs. single-author editions); diversified services — "The Ateliers catholiques was not simply a book shop, but contained as well a library, a bookstore, a chapel, a bindery, a foundry, a warehouse, an artist's atelier, a 'museum,' and a bank" (p. 123); elaborate publicity — the Petit Saint-Thomas issued the first department-store catalogue in **1844, "the year, again, of the first volume of the patrologies"**, with discounts, mail-order, and special sales; Migne as "the 'Napoleon of the prospectus'"; both planted paid articles in the supposedly independent press (pp. 123–24); mass standardization — the ready-made suit :: the uniform volume; **5,000 letters of consultation dispatched, 50,000 letters of praise "supposedly received"** (p. 124 — note Bloch's "supposedly"); the war with Paris's small booksellers mirrors the specialty shops vs. grands magasins (p. 124).
- **The drygoods punchline** (pp. 124–25): "And here we have it: the answer to the questions of how Migne could have printed as much as he did and how he could have known as much about marketing what he printed is, simply put, **drygoods**." The grands magasins grew out of drapery/drygoods stores; "Migne too came from drygoods, the **Maison Delcros-Migne of Saint-Flour**," which supplied him financial assistance, "all his clothing and table linen," and sold Cours volumes in the Auvergne (pp. 124–25). Bloch immediately qualifies: not that he got specific marketing ideas from home, nor that he studied the 1830s magasins (his 1838 prospectus was *ahead* of them, p. 125); rather (1) he learned the techniques from Paris newspaper journalism, and (2) both phenomena were "the syllogistic conclusion to the logic of demographic, economic, and technological takeoff of mid-century... a world, not unlike that of our own information revolution, made faster and smaller by technological progress" (pp. 125–26).
- **Final assessment** (pp. 126–28): mass production/marketing became "not only a form of religion in its own right, but a particular quality of modernism itself" (p. 126). The department store as "internal work community"/House with institutionalized paternalism (Miller) parallels the Ateliers "which conceived itself along the lines of the monastery" (p. 126); Zola's *Au Bonheur des dames*: "a cathedral of modern commerce, solid and light, made for a people of clients" (p. 127). **Boucicaut offered a guided tour of the Bon Marché daily at 3 p.m.; Migne, "some decades earlier," printed a standing invitation to visit on his stationery and in the prospectus "Curieux détails"** — "Both conceived of the enterprise at whose head they stood as, respectively, a commercial and an industrial shrine to which pilgrimage was encouraged" (p. 127).
- Coda: Apollinaire's "Zone" ("Only religion is still new... You read the prospectuses the catalogues the public notices that sing out / Here's the morning's poetry and for prose we have newspapers") — Migne had "had a hand in creating... the transformation of the press into a religion revealed in the advertising apparitions of the prospectus, the catalogue, the newspaper, and the affiche" (p. 127); mid-century's shrinking time-space culminates in cubism, relativity, psychoanalysis, Proust (p. 128). The book's last line returns to Migne quoting Tertullian: "In this world anxious for Progress, we offer the Tradition of the past in order to march forward. *Traditio tibi praetendetur auctrix* (Tert., de Coron. milit., cap. 4)" (p. 128).

## 8. BLOCH'S INTERPRETIVE CLAIMS, LEGENDS FLAGGED, TEXTURE

### Interpretive claims (Bloch's, not the sources')
- **"Fantasmatic figuring / fantasmatics of figures"** — Migne's pricing and cost calculations mirror each other; his real innovation is "a fundamental rethinking of the means of such production which assumes the possibility of a precise conversion between time, labor, and price" (pp. 84, 87–88).
- **The prepay price as disguised loan** — the 8,300-fr figure was computed so advance cash plus interest equals retail (p. 82). This is Bloch's arithmetic reconstruction, not documented in Migne's papers.
- **"Nineteenth-century version of a pyramid scheme"** — Bloch's label for the referral requirements (p. 82).
- **Publication as purification** — proofreading assimilated to the refinement of precious metal; kitsch art priced by the square centimeter as the aesthetic twin of the standardized volume (pp. 88–94).
- **Usury inverted** — Migne turns the Church's classic anti-usury arguments "upon their head," his horror of sleeping money a theological work-ethic transposed to capital (pp. 99–100). "At worst Migne joined the sin of usury to that of simony" (p. 98).
- **The trial as a debate on the ontology of the book** — commodity (valeur vénale) vs. fetishized cultural object vs. Firmin Didot's third way, which "resembles nothing so much as the idea of the sacrament" (pp. 110–11). One of Bloch's signature moves.
- **Scellos/scelus** — Bloch's own pun on the insurance lawyer's name (p. 107).
- **The Balzac thesis** — Migne as "pure product" of the print-capitalism Balzac chronicled; the how-did-he-do-it answer is "purely technical" plus market assimilation: he "invented less than he assimilated" (pp. 113–19).
- **The Bon Marché / drygoods thesis** — the book's culminating argument: Migne's methods = the department store's (full line, catalogue, mail order, fixed low prices, standardization, publicity, the House-as-shrine), both "the syllogistic conclusion" of mid-century takeoff; the biographical clincher that Migne "came from drygoods" via Maison Delcros-Migne (pp. 121–27). Note Bloch's own hedges: no direct influence in either direction (p. 125).
- **Migne and modernism** — the compression of time and space in his rhetoric anticipates Apollinaire, cubism, relativity, Proust (pp. 127–28). Final assessment: neither hagiography nor exposé; Migne left suspended between "efficient charity" and "self-interested simony" (p. 97), his death rendered as that of "some Balzacian character whose task had been completed, whose energy had been transferred into the object of his obsession" (p. 112).

### Legends / dubious claims flagged
- The **monk-minute equivalence** ("may or may not be accurate," p. 85); attributed to newspapers Migne may have written himself (n. 37, p. 131 — "Historical reality was rarely the ultimate criterion of his rhetorical solicitations").
- **50,000 letters of praise** — "supposedly received" (p. 124).
- **Arson rumors** — disgruntled worker (Chevalier via Font-Réaulx) and the concierges' *incendie volontaire*: "both reports are on the order of hearsay," the second "especially unworthy of belief" given the underinsurance (pp. 106–7).
- **"Never a contested bank note in 29 years"** — "a claim that is hard to believe, and even harder to believe that Migne believed" (p. 101).
- **Insurance-company counts** — twenty advertised, thirty-three reported by L'Illustration [sic], really eighteen (pp. 103, 107).
- **Brother's name** — Etienne (p. 91) vs. Victor (n. 24, p. 142).
- **Did Migne read Illusions perdues?** — raised and dismissed by Bloch himself (p. 113).
- **Quality of the artwork** — none positively identified; organs allegedly needed all parts replaced (n. 29, pp. 142–43).
- **The Darboy problem (ours, not Bloch's):** Bloch never names Darboy in the 1874 suspension; any draft naming him should be corrected to Guibert or left as "the Archbishop of Paris."

### Texture (vivid details for the essay)
- The insurance plaques by the door — la Paternelle, la Fraternelle, le Monde, le Soleil, l'Aigle, la France, la Clémentine, la Confiance — "like the frontispiece to one of the volumes of the patrologies" (p. 103); one of the insurers was **the Phénix** (pp. 105–6).
- Migne awakened at 1–2 a.m. throwing his own books out the window (p. 105); ground so hot at 7 a.m. that falling paper ignited (p. 105); plates fused into "enormous blocks, in the most bizarre forms," "a river of molten metal," "enormous lead lingots" (p. 105); the 30,000-fr organ vanished without trace (p. 105).
- "Twelve professions were practiced" in the factory (Le Figaro, n. 49 p. 144); the Ateliers as library-bookstore-chapel-bindery-foundry-warehouse-atelier-museum-bank (p. 123).
- Migne blind between the fire and the settlement (p. 112); volume 223 — the last index tome, two years of typographic work — feared lost down to proofs and manuscript (n. 62, pp. 145–46).
- Garnier selling Migne's envelopes to stamp collectors — why so few letters *to* Migne survive (n. 50, p. 144).
- Migne dunning the French government for 190 fr owed by a runaway curé believed to have "made it to California" (Ch. 1 n. 7, p. 129, AN F19 5842).
- The full Latin title of the Patrologia is itself an advertisement, praising its own "pretii exiguitas" (low price) (pp. 80, and translation in Ch. 5 n. 8, p. 140).
- Paper of the prospectus offered as a sample of the paper of the books, "like a traveling salesman" (n. 20, p. 142).

## 9. SOURCE-MAP FROM THE ENDNOTES (for our further-reading section)

The notes printed in this chunk cover the whole book (Chs. 1–5 + Conclusion), pp. 129–148. The most load-bearing primary and secondary sources:

### Archives (Bloch's documentary spine)
- **Archives nationales (AN) F18 1803** — the 1838 prospectus dossier and print-license file: pricing schemes, six advantages, referral bounties, mass-for-books exchange rates, typographic self-description. The single most-cited source in Ch. 5.
- **AN F18 369** — the long dossier on the *Journal des faits* ownership quarrel; also prospectus material (monk-in-a-minute; the 7,000-fr prepay offer; favored-nation shipping; the "six advantages" extension to other series).
