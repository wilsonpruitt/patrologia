## F1 — Direction 1 (Migne's own defect, faithfully carried into the English). Col. 0760C.

**Lemma (Job 3:7, as Migne prints it):** `« Nec laude digna. »` — English: `« Nor worthy of praise. »`

**Latin as printed (0760B–C):**
> « Nec laude digna. » Homo cum substratus, idola coluit, in quibus daemonia quasi tenebras noctis
> [0760C] laudavit: sed jam idolis reprobatis, **nox est laude digna.**

**English as it stands:**
> « Nor worthy of praise. » Man, when laid low, worshipped idols, in which he praised demons as the
> darkness of night; [0760C] but now that the idols have been rejected, **the night is worthy of praise.**

**The contradiction:** the gloss is expounding a lemma that says the night is *not* worthy of praise, and
the gloss's own argument runs the same way — men once praised demons as night's darkness; *now the idols
are rejected*, so the praise has stopped. The concluding clause as printed asserts the opposite of both
its lemma and its own premise.

**Diagnosis:** a dropped `non` in Migne (`nox non est laude digna`), or a lost interrogative. Not our
defect — the English renders `est` with *is*, exactly as printed. Flagging so the crux can be checked
against the plate; if confirmed it wants a marker, since as it stands the sentence silently reverses the
verse it glosses.

**What the sense requires:** "…but now that the idols have been rejected, the night is **not** worthy of praise."

## S1 — soft, Direction 2 candidate (possible relocation of the negation by us). Col. 0753C.

**Latin:** `Non ergo vires Job, sed quemque verius adversis probare sciens, tentandum expetit.`

**English:** `Therefore, knowing not the strength of Job, but that each man is more truly proved by
adversities, he demands that he be tempted.`

The Latin puts `non … sed` on the objects of `probare`: he knows how to prove *not Job's strength [by
prosperity]* but *each man, more truly, by adversities*. The English is most naturally read as *he does
not know Job's strength* — a claim about the devil's ignorance that the Latin does not make, and one that
sits oddly with the immediately preceding sentence, where the devil reasons confidently about how Job
would behave in adversity. Not a dropped particle, and defensible as an English `not X but Y` contrast,
so I rate it low-confidence; but the negation has moved from the object to the participle, which is the
class of change this pass exists to catch.

**Suggested:** "Therefore, knowing that it is not Job's strength, but each man more truly, that adversities
prove, he demands that he be tempted."

## S2 — soft, Direction 1 candidate (lemma vs. gloss). Col. 0756B.

**Latin:** `« Dominus dedit. » Judaea dum venturum credidit, abstulit, cum ipsa venientem contempsit.`

**English:** `« The Lord gave. » Judaea, while it believed that he was to come, took [him] away when she
herself despised him at his coming.`

The lemma quoted is `Dominus dedit`; the gloss as printed has no verb answering it, and hands `abstulit`
to *Judaea* rather than to the *Dominus* of the verse (Job 1:21 `Dominus dedit, Dominus abstulit`), so the
gloss on "the Lord gave" says only that Judaea took away. The expected shape is `[Dedit] dum Judaea
venturum credidit; abstulit cum ipsa venientem contempsit` — He gave while she believed he would come; He
took away when she despised him come. The English is faithful to the printed Latin (including the bracketed
`[him]`, which is the translator flagging the missing object), so this is Migne's, not ours — but it is a
gloss that does not say what its lemma says, and is worth a plate check.

## Rejected candidates (looked, decided not a finding)

- **0000, 0751A** `Post omnia mala, ob duplicia recepit` → "he received on account of double things".
  Odd Latin (one expects `omnia duplicia recepit`), but the following clause immediately supplies the
  sense (`quia Ecclesia … duplicia accipit`), so nothing is reversed; the English is literal.
- **0000, 0750D** `quia per veritatem fidei victus est, sed per tumorem superbiae displicet` → "because by
  the truth of faith he is overcome". Reads as a *non sequitur* on first pass, but Elihu is *won over*
  by true faith and displeases only by pride, which is exactly the paragraph's argument. No reversal.
- **0000, 0749A** `Gentilitas, quae, dum legem custodit, auditores legis redarguit` — sounds paradoxical
  next to "Blush, Sidon", but it is the Rom. 2 commonplace (the Gentile who keeps the law refutes the
  law's hearers). Faithful and coherent.
- **0002, 0754D** `Puer sanus redit, quia vera dixisse prophetiam claruit zelus apprehendit populum
  ineruditum.` Latin is syntactically broken (two clauses fused without a conjunction); the English is
  broken in the same place and in the same way. A text defect, not a polarity defect — no negation and
  no reversal — so I have not logged it as a site.
- **0003, 0757C** `Quid simpliciter aeterna petis et super his male gemis, excidens (aeterna despice) et
  mala praesentia, vel moriens evade.` Garbled in Migne, including the intrusive parenthesis; the English
  reproduces the garble without smoothing it. Deliberate carriage, not a hidden repair.
- **0003, 0757C** `« Benedic Domino et morere. »` — Migne's lemma against the Vulgate's `Benedic Deo`;
  a lemma divergence, explicitly out of scope, and the gloss beside it does not contradict it.
- **0004, 0760A** `Quod si ita punita est. « Non requirat. » In judicio ut puniat; et non illustrabit.`
  Sentence-boundary damage in Migne; the English follows it exactly, and both negations survive.
- Unbalanced `«` at the `VERS. n.--` lemmata throughout all five chunks, mid-sentence `?`, missing
  terminal stops, `Nunquid`/`coelum`/`Exspectet` orthography — all excluded by the brief, none logged.
