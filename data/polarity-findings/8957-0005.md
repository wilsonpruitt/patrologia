## Finding 1 — chunk 0005, col. 0763B (VERS. 25, gloss on « Quia timor »)

**Direction 2 — OUR defect.** A negation doubled in the English, reversing the clause.

Latin as printed:
> unde necesse est ut in omnibus timeatur; in prosperis, **ut non sint sibi data ad majorem excaecationem, non ad consolationem**, ut bonis solet fieri, ne sit in adversis initium poenae.

English as it stands:
> whence it is needful that he be feared in all things: in prosperity, **lest the things given him be not to a greater blinding, and not to consolation**, as is wont to befall the good; lest in adversity there be the beginning of punishment.

The Latin has one negative in the first limb (`ut non sint … data ad majorem excaecationem` — a negative purpose/fear clause equivalent to `ne`), and `non ad consolationem` is its contrastive second limb: the fear is that prosperity was given *for blinding, not for consolation*. The English renders `ut` as "lest" **and** `non` as "not", so the first limb carries two negatives and now says the opposite — a fear that the things were *not* for blinding; and the intrusive "and" turns the contrastive limb into a second parallel fear. The gloss's whole point (prosperity is to be feared because it may be a blinding rather than the consolation the good ordinarily receive) is lost.

That the translator handles the genuine `ne … non` two sentences earlier correctly — « et ne pia percussio **non** sit disciplinae, sed vindictae » → "and lest the godly stroke be **not** of discipline, but of vengeance" — is what makes this one look like a slip rather than a house convention.

**Suggested English:** "…in prosperity, lest the things be given him for a greater blinding, not for consolation, as is wont to befall the good; in adversity, lest there be the beginning of punishment."

## Finding 2 — chunk 0008, col. 0770C (VERS. 19, gloss on « In sex tribulationibus »)

**Direction 1 — Migne's own defect, faithfully carried.** Report for the crux file; the English is not at fault.

Latin as printed:
> VERS. 19.-- « In sex tribulationibus. » … **in septima ergo tribulatione liberat, ne in septima malum attingat**: quia hic paterne erudiens atterit, sed in die judicii a verbere abscondit.

English as it stands:
> VERS. 19.-- « In six tribulations. » … **in the seventh tribulation therefore he delivers, lest in the seventh evil touch him**: because here, chastening as a father, he wears down, but in the day of judgment he hides from the stroke.

This is the shape the pass was hunting: the gloss contradicts its own lemma one line above, and contradicts itself within the sentence (delivers *in* the seventh, so that evil not touch *in* the seventh). Job 5:19 and Gregory both give the six/seven pairing: `in sex ergo tribulationibus liberat, ne in septima malum attingat` — he delivers in the six (the labours of the present life, signified by the senary just expounded: "By the number six, which a seventh follows…"), so that in the seventh (the rest, the day of judgment) evil not touch. The first `septima` is an intrusion for `sex`.

**English is correct as it stands** (it renders Migne). If the Latin is emended, the English should follow to "in the six tribulations therefore he delivers, lest in the seventh evil touch him."

## Candidates considered and rejected

- **0006, col. 0763D**, « Sed conceptum »: `Perfecti nec in corde perversa concipiunt quae silentio compescunt` → "The perfect do not even in their heart conceive perverse things, which they check by silence." Reads as self-contradictory (they do not conceive them, yet check them by silence), and Migne has compressed Gregory's three-tier scheme. But `quae silentio compescunt` construes as "the perverse things which [others] restrain by silence", which the next sentence supplies. Compression, not polarity; the English is faithful either way. Not a finding.
- **0008, col. 0768A**, « Et non erit qui eruat »: `Quia illos ibi veritas eripit, quos hic per disciplinam premit` → "Because the truth snatches away there those whom here it presses down through discipline." Lemma says none will deliver; gloss says truth delivers. Rejected: this is the ordinary *a contrario* gloss — the wicked, unpressed here, are the ones with no deliverer. English faithful.
- **0006, col. 0764D**, `Hi non (ut ait) pereunt, sed felicius hic justis vivunt` → "These do not (as he says) perish, but live here more happily than the just." Flags on a naive negation count against Eliphaz's thesis, but the whole paragraph is the glossator refuting Eliphaz. Faithful; not a finding.
- **0006, col. 0764B**, the boldness/fear chiasmus (`audacia fortitudinem, timor debilitatem parit … audacia debilitatem, timor Dei fortitudinem`). Checked limb by limb; the English preserves the crossing exactly.
- **0008, col. 0768B**, `Diabolus ut inermis vincitur … sed armatus vincit` → "The devil is overcome as unarmed … but he conquers armed". Verified: the two verbs are not swapped.
- **0008, col. 0769B**, `et quod sine ejus voluntate geritur, ejus voluntati contrarium non sit` → "and what is done without his will is not contrary to his will." Verified against the Augustinian dictum; the negation is where it belongs.
- **0009, col. 0771B**, `Non pacatae sed pacificae, quia non pacem habent, sed pacem faciunt` → "Not pacified but peacemaking, because they do not have peace, but make peace." Verified; both limbs intact.
- Unbalanced `«` at the several `VERS. n.--` lemmata (0006 VERS. 16 and VERS. 20; 0008 VERS. 22; 0009 VERS. 26), the `[sic: *e s*]` at 0770D, the `[var:]` at 0766C, and lemmata diverging from the Vulgate (e.g. 5:6 `et de humo non egredietur` for `non oritur dolor`) — all excluded by the brief as non-defects.
