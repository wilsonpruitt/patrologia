## 2. Findings, ranked

### 2.1 ⛔ WORST — 0022, col 0415A: the negation is parked in the bracket and the sentence goes affirmative (Pattern 7a⁗)

- **Latin:** *Illa quae Dominus scit non intelligentibus esse proverbia: illi usque adeo
  **nobis** intelligunt, ut nec saltem se non intelligere intelligant.*
- **English as shipped:** "Those things which the Lord knows to be proverbs to men who do
  not understand: they to such a degree **understand for us** [cj: *nobis*; read *non*,
  "do not understand"], that they do not even understand that they do not understand."
- **What is wrong:** this is the measured 7a⁗ class exactly. The conjecture is right —
  Augustine, *Tract. in Ioh.* 103 reads *illi usque adeo **non** intelligunt, ut nec
  saltem se non intelligere intelligant* — but **the running English asserts that they
  DO understand**, and the negation survives only inside the editorial bracket. Delete
  the bracket, as 7a⁗ instructs, and the clause reads "they to such a degree understand
  for us" — the opposite of the sentence, and flatly contradicted by its own second half
  seven words later ("they do not even understand that they do not understand") and by
  the clause before it ("proverbs to men who do **not** understand"). Three contradicting
  neighbours on one line.
- **Direction: OURS (2).** The plate's defect was seen and correctly diagnosed; the
  rendering is what failed. The repair is to carry the force outside the marker, e.g.
  "they to such a degree do not [cj: *nobis*; read *non*] understand, that …".
- **Why worst:** it is our defect, it inverts the sentence, and `verify-english` cannot
  see it because a bracket containing the word "not" makes the paragraph *look* negative
  to any mechanical check — including my own tally, which scored 0022 clean.

### 2.2 0021, col 0412B: *Patrem* re-bound to the participle, and the paragraph loses its conclusion

- **Latin:** *Et si oderunt veritatem, oderunt eum de quo nata est veritas, et ita
  **Patrem** nescientes de eo natam veritatem oderunt.*
- **English as shipped:** "And if they hate the truth, they hate him of whom the truth
  was born; and so, **not knowing the Father**, they hate the truth born of him."
- **What is wrong:** *nescientes* here governs an accusative + infinitive — `de eo natam
  [esse] veritatem`, "that the truth was born of him" — exactly as it does eleven words
  earlier in the same paragraph (*nescientes eum esse veritatem*, correctly rendered
  "not knowing that he is the truth"). That leaves **`Patrem` as the object of
  `oderunt`**: "and so they hate **the Father**, not knowing that the truth was born of
  him." The English instead attaches `Patrem` to `nescientes` — the contextually easier
  binding — and gives `oderunt` "the truth born of him" as its object, which merely
  repeats the previous clause and makes the sentence add nothing.
- **What it costs:** the paragraph is a chain (hate the punishment → hate the truth →
  hate him of whom truth is born) whose whole point is the conclusion **they hate the
  Father**. That is the conclusion the Gloss then reuses at 0412C ("Now, seeing the
  works, they hate gratis both me **and the Father**") and at 0412A ("he who hates me
  hates my Father also"). The English deletes the conclusion its own neighbours depend on.
- **Direction: OURS (2).** Mechanism 5, the dominant class.

### 2.3 0023, col 0416A: *ex Patre* where the argument needs *ex parte*

- **Latin:** *Ut cognoscant te. Quod hic est **ex Patre**, in futuro erit plene.*
- **English as shipped:** "*That they may know thee.* What is here **from the Father**,
  in the world to come will be fully."
- **What is wrong:** the contrasting term is `plene`, and the pair *ex parte … plene* is
  1 Cor 13:9–12, the standard tag for knowledge here-and-hereafter, which is precisely
  what this gloss on *haec est vita aeterna ut cognoscant te* is about ("he who advances
  in knowledge tends the more to live; but when knowledge shall be full…"). *ex Patre*
  leaves `plene` with nothing to contrast and produces a sentence with no predicate
  sense at all ("what is here from the Father … will be fully").
- **Direction: MIGNE (1) — the English is right to print it and needs a crux.**
  ⛔ **This is a conjecture against a plate I cannot see.** *Patre*/*parte* is a
  four-letter difference and the work is saturated with genuine *a Patre* / *ex Patre*
  formulas, so a compositor's eye-skip to the ambient phrase is the likeliest story —
  but I have not seen the plate, and a defect correctly seen is not a defect correctly
  attributed. The crux should record the printed *ex Patre*, the proposed *ex parte*,
  and the 1 Cor 13 evidence.
- **Why it ranks here:** in a chapter arguing continuously about *a Patre*/*ex Patre*,
  an English sentence that says knowledge "here" is "from the Father" and later "will be
  fully" is the kind of thing a reader will quote.

### 2.4 0021, col 0412C: the English says the Son proceeds from the Father

- **Latin:** *Spiritus procedit a Patre et Filio, sed **Filius dicitur a Patre
  procedere**, quia ad Patrem solet referre, et quod ipsius est de quo ipse est.*
- **English as shipped:** "The Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son, but **the Son
  is said to proceed from the Father**, because he is wont to refer to the Father even
  what is his own, from whom he himself is."
- **What is wrong:** the lemma is *Qui a Patre procedit* (Jo 15:26) and the gloss exists
  to answer why John says the Spirit proceeds *from the Father* and not *from me*: the
  Son is accustomed to refer to the Father even what is his own (Augustine, *Tract.* 99).
  The subject of *procedere* must be the **Spirit**; the printed clause instead has the
  **Son** proceeding — which contradicts its own first clause (the Son is one of the two
  from whom the Spirit proceeds) and its own last clause ("from him he has that the
  Spirit proceeds from him"), and, standing in English, asserts a heresy.
- **Direction: MIGNE (1) — literal rendering is correct; a crux is missing.**
  ⛔ **Conjecture against an unseen plate.** Plausible originals include *sed [Spiritus]
  a Patre dicitur procedere* (a dropped subject) or *sed Filius **dicit** a Patre
  procedere* ("but the Son says [that the Spirit] proceeds from the Father"), the second
  being one letter from the print and giving *quia ad Patrem solet referre* its subject
  for free. I am not confident which; the crux should log the print and both options.
- Secondary, same paragraph: "From whom therefore he has that he is, from him he has
  that the Spirit proceeds **from him**" — the two "him"s have different referents (the
  Father, then the Son), and a reader who binds both to the Father loses the filioque
  point the whole gloss was built to make. Worth a bracketed disambiguation.

### 2.5 0027, col 0425A: *nec* for *ne* — a genuine polarity defect on the plate, confirmed against Augustine

- **Latin:** *Productior enim poena est quam culpa, **nec** parva videretur culpa, si cum
  illa auferretur poena.*
- **English as shipped:** "For the punishment is drawn out longer than the fault, and the
  fault would seem **no small thing** if the punishment were taken away along with it."
- **What is wrong:** the argument is that the penalty outlasts the guilt *precisely so
  that* the guilt should not be thought trivial — i.e. **`ne` parva videretur culpa**,
  "lest the fault should seem small, if the punishment were taken away along with it."
  Augustine, *Tract. in Ioh.* 124.5, has it in so many words: *ne parva putaretur esse
  culpa, si cum illa finiretur et poena.* As printed with `nec`, the sentence says the
  fault would NOT seem small if the punishment were removed — which makes the preceding
  clause (the punishment is drawn out longer) pointless.
- **Direction: MIGNE (1).** The English is literal and therefore right; the crux is
  missing. Note this one is **not** a bare conjecture — the source is quoted above — but
  the reading of the plate's own type is still unverified by me.

### 2.6 0026, col 0422D: *Noli peccare* where the neighbour says the tears are what is forbidden

- **Latin:** *Quid ploras? Quasi dicat: **Noli peccare.** Prohibent lacrymas quodammodo
  nuntiantes gaudium.*
- **English as shipped:** "*Why weepest thou?* As if he should say: *Sin not.* They forbid
  her **tears**, in a manner announcing joy."
- **What is wrong:** the gloss glosses *Quid ploras?* and then immediately says what the
  angels forbid: tears. The expansion must be *Noli **plorare*** ("Do not weep"), one
  letter-cluster from the print and the only reading the next sentence licenses. "Sin
  not" is contradicted by its own immediate neighbour, which is the tell 7a always gives.
- **Direction: MIGNE (1) — rendered literally, correctly; crux missing.**
  ⛔ **Conjecture against an unseen plate.**

### 2.7 0023, col 0416B: *habet in homine* generalized into a maxim about mankind

- **Latin:** (on *Dedisti mihi*) *In quo notatur gratia. **Nihil enim habet in homine quod
  non accepit.***
- **English as shipped:** "Wherein grace is noted. For **man has nothing** which he has
  not received."
- **What is wrong:** the subject of *habet* is the speaker of the lemma — Christ — and
  *in homine* is "in [his] manhood": "For he has nothing in the man which he did not
  receive." The gloss is about the human nature of the Son receiving everything from the
  Father (that is what "grace is noted" means here, three words earlier, and it is the
  same distinction the chunk works throughout — *secundum quod est homo* / *secundum quod
  Deus*). The English promotes the ablative `in homine` to subject and converts a
  Christological claim into 1 Cor 4:7 as a general maxim about human beings.
- **Direction: OURS (2).** Mechanism 5 again — the easier noun took the reference.

### 2.8 0026, col 0422B: the Church enters after itself

- **Latin:** *Joannes significat Synagogam, quae prior venit ad monumentum sed non
  intravit … Petrus Ecclesiam, quae cognovit carne mortuum, viventem credidit Deum,
  **post quem et in Judaea in fine intrabit**.*
- **English as shipped:** "…Peter signifies the Church, which knew him dead in the flesh
  and believed him living God; **after whom she too will enter, in Judaea, at the end.**"
- **What is wrong:** the subject of *intrabit* cannot be the Church, which has just
  entered — as shipped, the English has the Church entering after the Church. The
  subject is the **Synagogue** (Judaea), the topic of the first half: she came first and
  did not enter, and she will enter **after** Peter/the Church, at the end (the standard
  Rom 11 reading of the footrace at the tomb, Gregory/Bede). The whole eschatological
  point — the Jewish people entering last — is lost, and the English sentence is
  self-contradicting.
- **Direction: OURS (2)** for the binding. ⚠ A secondary Migne question rides along:
  `et in Judaea` reads as though `in` were intrusive (`post quem et Judaea in fine
  intrabit` gives the subject outright). ⛔ **That part is a conjecture against a plate I
  cannot see** and should be logged as such, not silently adopted; the binding finding
  stands with or without it.

### 2.9 0020, col 0409B: a "distinction" whose two halves are identical

- **Latin:** *Audito quod **mundus modo videbit eum, sed ipsi videbunt**, pro omnibus
  quaesiturus quaerit Judas causam **hujus discretionis**.*
- **English as shipped:** "Having heard that **the world will presently see him, but that
  they themselves shall see**, Judas, about to ask on behalf of all, asks the cause of
  **this distinction**."
- **What is wrong:** as shipped there is no distinction in the pair at all — both halves
  say someone will see — and the next clause names it "this distinction". Judas's
  question at Jo 14:22 is *quid factum est quia manifestaturus es nobis teipsum et non
  mundo?*, and the gloss on VERS. 19 four lines earlier is explicit: *mundus qui modo
  videt oculis carnis, post modicum **non** videbit*. Either the plate has lost a `non`
  (*mundus modo non videbit eum*), or `modo` is doing restrictive work ("the world will
  see him **only for the present**") that the rendering "presently" does not carry.
- **Direction:** most likely **MIGNE (1)**, needing a crux; but ⛔ **this is a conjecture
  against an unseen plate**, and the second reading needs no emendation at all. Either
  way the English as shipped is contradictory on its face and should not stand unmarked.

### 2.10 0022, col 0413C: *non videbo* against a following *videbor*

- **Latin:** *Vel parvum tempus est, id est triduum quo clausus **non videbo**. Et iterum
  parvum, id est quadraginta dies, quo resuscitatus **videbor**.*
- **English as shipped:** "Or it is a small time, that is, the three days in which, shut
  in, **I shall not see**. And again a small [time], that is, forty days, in which, raised
  up, **I shall be seen**."
- **What is wrong:** the two clauses are built as an exact pair (*clausus* / *resuscitatus*,
  *triduum* / *quadraginta dies*) glossing *modicum et jam non videtis me, et iterum
  modicum et videbitis me* — so the first verb must be **`non videbor`**, "I shall not be
  seen". As printed, Christ in the tomb is said not to *see*, which the parallel refutes
  one line later.
- **Direction: MIGNE (1) — literal rendering correct, crux missing.** ⛔ Conjecture
  against an unseen plate; a single dropped `-r`.

### 2.12 0025, col 0420C: *occulta potentia* bound to the verb, dissolving a *potentia/patientia* antithesis

- **Latin:** *Ecce implentur quae de se praedixerat Jesus **occulta potentia**, commendatur
  **patientia** qua informantur martyres.*
- **English as shipped:** "Behold, there are fulfilled the things which Jesus had
  foretold of himself **by his hidden power**; his patience is commended, by which the
  martyrs are formed."
- **What is wrong (probably):** the sentence is built on the standard scourging-scene
  antithesis — the **power** is hidden, the **patience** is displayed. That wants
  `occulta potentia` as an ablative absolute governing the second half ("his power being
  hidden, his patience is commended"), not an instrumental with `praedixerat`. The
  English took the easier attachment and the antithesis disappears.
- **Direction: OURS (2), low-to-moderate confidence.** The instrumental reading is
  grammatically available; I flag it because the rhetorical shape is so standard.

### 2.13 0021, col 0411D: one Latin subject rendered as two English ones

- **Latin:** *Si malus punit scelus mali, ex parte odit mundus quod suum est; **dum punit
  ex parte amat**, quia malus favet malis.*
- **English as shipped:** "…in part the world hates what is its own; while **he** punishes,
  in part **it** loves, because an evil man favours the evil."
- **What is wrong:** `punit` and `amat` share one subject, `mundus`, carried over from the
  clause before ("in part the world hates … while [it] punishes, in part it loves"). The
  English splits them between the evil man and the world, and the *ex parte … ex parte*
  balance — which is the sentence's only argument — stops being about one subject.
- **Direction: OURS (2), minor.**

### 2.14 0019, col 0406A: passive *cogitur* made active, and an `et` dropped

- **Latin:** *…cum urgens causa **et** zizania a tritico ante messem **cogitur** separare.*
- **English as shipped:** "…when an urgent cause **compels the cockle to be separated**
  from the wheat before the harvest."
- **What is wrong:** `cogitur` is passive with `causa` as its subject: "when an urgent
  cause **is compelled** to separate **even** the cockle from the wheat before the
  harvest." The English inverts the voice and drops `et` ("even"), which is the word
  carrying the reluctance the whole gloss is about (the spiritual are troubled *out of
  charity* at having to separate before the harvest, against Mt 13:29–30).
- **Direction: OURS (2), minor.**

## 3. Rejected candidates — sites looked at hard and cleared

1. **0023 @0416D, *in natura sua*** — the site the translating stint flagged as the place
   where a mis-binding would make the creature consubstantial with God. **Clean.** "That
   they may be one **in their own nature**, as we also **in ours** are one … because they
   are **not** of the same nature with God, as the Son … is one with the Father." Both
   possessives bound right, the `non` present, the *nobiscum*-denial intact.
2. **0024 @0418A, *in quem …*** — `quem` could take *hortum* or *torrentem*; English binds
   it to the garden, which Jo 18:1 (*ubi erat hortus, in quem introivit ipse et discipuli
   eius*) confirms. Cleared.
3. **0020 @0409D, *cum non possint esse non simul*** — a triple negative ("we do not say
   them at the same time, since they cannot be **not** at the same time"). Reads as an
   error and is not one: all three particles are in the Latin and all three are in the
   English. Cleared.
4. **0023 @0417D, *quamvis unum essent natura, dissentiendo tamen ab uno non erant unum***
   — looks like it contradicts 0416D's *non sunt ejusdem naturae cum Deo*. It does not:
   0416D denies unity of nature **with God**, this denies unity **among themselves**
   despite a shared nature. Rendered correctly. Cleared.
5. **0021 @0410C, *licet non de suo, nec per ipsos, sed per meipsum*** — `suo` is singular
   and could be reflexive to `qui` (Christ), which would contradict *sed per meipsum*.
   The parallel with `ipsos` (the branches, accusative plural) settles it for "out of what
   is their own", which is what shipped. Cleared, but it was the closest call in 0021.
6. **0025 @0422A, *quae in sorte Dei gratia commendatur*** — Augustine's own tag is *in
   sorte non meritum sed Dei gratia commendatur*, which would make `gratia` nominative
   ("in the lot the grace of God is commended") rather than the shipped ablative ("which
   in the lot is commended by the grace of God"). But Migne prints `quae`, and `quae` has
   to be the subject of `commendatur`; the shipped reading is the one the printed text
   licenses. Cleared under Pattern 7.
7. **0023 @0415D, *cognitione hominem clarificari Pater*** — `hominem` is accusative where
   the sense wants *hominum*; English prints "in the knowledge of man", i.e. reads it as
   a genitive singular. A silent normalization of a case, but not a sense change and not
   a non-word. Noted, not raised.
8. **0026 @0422D, *Et ille ex passione sua erat nuntiandus*** — reads as corrupt (the
   head/feet, God-before-the-ages / man-at-the-end scheme has no room for "from his
   passion"), but I could not reconstruct a candidate original I would defend, and
   Pattern 7 says render and log, not guess. Recorded here as an observation only.
9. **0026 @0423A, *nec suis oculis, nec oculis illorum duorum credentium putavit*** —
   `putavit` where the datives want `credidit`; the English's "she gave no thought either
   to … or to …" keeps both negatives and does not read `credidit`. Cleared.
10. **0022 @0414C–0415A, the two inline `[var: …]` notes** (1 Cor 2:14 printed as *I Joan.
    I*; *loqueris* → *loquitur*) — checked specifically against 7a⁗: in both cases the
    running English keeps its own verb and its own polarity, and the bracket carries only
    the collation. Cleared. **The third bracket in the same chunk did not survive that
    check — see §2.1.**
11. **0027 @0426A–B, the closing `non`/`nec` pile-up** (*Non est intelligendum quod Joannes
    non sit mortuus* … *Non enim magnum erat dare dilecto non mori* … *Nec ipsum arbitror
    mundum* … *Non dicit spatio non posse capi*) — every particle the Latin prints is in
    the English, including the awkward double negatives. The work's last sentence lands on
    *excedere fidem per hyperbolen* intact. Cleared.
12. **0019 @0406D, *in proximo enim non nisi Deus diligitur*** — `non nisi` rendered
    "nothing is loved but God"; the exclusive force is kept. Cleared.
13. **0020 @0408D, *Non ita dabitur Spiritus pro me, ut ego etiam non sim vobiscum*** — two
    negatives, both present, in a sentence where dropping either produces a christological
    error. Cleared.
14. **0023 @0417C, *unum tamen cum Deo esse non possumus, quia non unius substantiae*** —
    the heresy-shaped sentence in the High Priestly Prayer material; both negatives present
    and correctly bound. Cleared.

### 4b. Known site, but the finding is new

- **§2.1 — 0415A, the `[cj: nobis → non]`.** The site is the one `[cj:]` in the range and
  is thoroughly documented — including the plate read that would settle it (candidate
  #3). **But the 7a⁗ question was never asked of it.** The crux's own summary says the
  marker "is additive … and the printed word keeps its English beside it," which is
  exactly the decision that leaves the running clause affirmative. And the range's
  Pattern 7/7a self-check — *"every `non`, `nec`, `nisi`, `nullus`, `nihil` the Latin
  prints is in the English"* — is **structurally blind here**, because the Latin does not
  print `non`; it prints `nobis`. The check passes and the sentence still asserts the
  opposite of itself. **This is the finding, and it is new: not that the conjecture is
  wrong (it is right), but that the force was parked inside the bracket.**
- **§2.9 — 0409B, *mundus modo videbit eum*.** Logged, and called "the most interesting
  decline in the range"; the `[cj:]` was declined **on the express ground that *modo* can
  be construed as "only"** — "the world will see him only [for a while]" — which restores
  the distinction. **But the shipped English reads "will presently see him"**, i.e. it
  prints the construal the crux *rejected* (*modo* = "now"), so the decline's own
  justification is not in the text it justifies. Either the English should carry "only",
  or the decline loses its ground. **New: the gap between the crux and the shipped line.**
- **§2.5 — 0425A, *nec parva videretur culpa*.** Logged, and explicitly ruled **"not a
  defect … recorded so a later sweep does not 'repair' it."** ⚠ **I disagree, and the
  entry's own reasoning is why.** It states Augustine's point as *"a fault whose
  punishment vanished with it would look trivial"* — which is the sense of **`ne` parva
  videretur** ("**lest** the fault seem small"), not of the `nec` it then defends, whose
  English ("would seem **no** small thing") says the fault would *not* look trivial. The
  rationale and the rendering point opposite ways. Augustine, *Tract. in Ioh.* 124.5:
  ***ne** parva putaretur esse culpa, si cum illa finiretur et poena.* **New as a
  disagreement with a closed entry** — this is precisely the sweep the entry warns off,
  so it should be re-opened deliberately rather than by a later mechanical pass.
- **§2.4 — 0412C, "the Son is said to proceed from the Father".** The binding is settled
  in `cruces-0020.md` §d.3 and **their argument is better than mine**: *Filius* is
  nominative, *dicitur* passive, so the Son is the subject, and the sentence is Jo 8:42
  (*ego ex Deo processi*) setting up the conclusion. I withdraw the "mis-binding" half.
  **What is new is only the smaller question they did not raise:** whether *dicitur* is
  itself a one-letter defect for *dicit* (*"but the Son **says** [the Spirit] proceeds
  from the Father"*), which would give *quia ad Patrem solet referre* its subject for
  free. ⛔ Conjecture against an unseen plate; worth adding to the plate-read list at
  most. My secondary point — that the closing "from him … from him" has two different
  referents and needs disambiguating — stands and is not logged anywhere.
