Abbo of Fleury

Abbo Floriacensisc. 945 – 13 November 1004

Monk and abbot of Fleury (Saint-Benoît-sur-Loire), educated at Paris and Reims. He spent 985–987 at Ramsey Abbey assisting Archbishop Oswald of York's monastic reforms, then returned to Fleury as abbot in 988, writing on computus, logic, canon law, and hagiography. He was killed at La Réole in Gascony in 1004, speared while trying to quell a fight between two factions of monks, and died in the arms of his disciple Aimoin, who later wrote his life.

  1. Canones PL 139

Alcuin of York

Alcuinusc. 735 – 19 May 804

Northumbrian deacon, poet, and master of the cathedral school of York, whom Charlemagne drew to the Continent around 782 to lead the palace school at Aachen and direct the Carolingian revival of learning. He spent his last years as abbot of Saint-Martin of Tours. This compendious commentary on the Song of Songs is among his biblical works.

Anselm of Laon

Anselmus Laudunensisc. 1050 – 1117

Theologian and master of the cathedral school of Laon, whose teaching on the sacraments and on the ordered reading of Scripture shaped the twelfth-century schools. He and his circle are traditionally associated with the origins of the Glossa ordinaria on the Bible.

Hugh of Saint Victor

Hugo de S. Victorec. 1096 – 11 February 1141

Theologian and head of the school of the abbey of Saint-Victor in Paris, and the founding master of the Victorine tradition of learning and contemplation. His work ranges across the liberal arts (the Didascalicon), sacramental theology (De sacramentis christianae fidei), and mystical writing. The present piece is a short exposition of the Magnificat, the canticle of the Blessed Virgin Mary.

Justus of Urgell

Justus Urgellensisfl. 527–546

First bishop of Urgell in the Pyrenees (in present-day Catalonia), a signatory of the Second Council of Toledo (527) and the First Council of Lérida (546). His Explanatio in Cantica canticorum — which Migne prints as Explicatio — is dedicated to Sergius, archbishop of Tarragona, and is the earliest surviving Latin commentary on the Song of Songs.

  1. Explicatio in Cantica canticorum PL 67

Richard of Saint Victor

Richardus S. Victorisd. 1173

Canon regular of the Abbey of Saint Victor in Paris — of British, probably Scottish, origin — and its prior from 1162. After Hugh, whose pupil he was, he was the leading theologian of the Victorine school: his De Trinitate reasoned toward the persons of God from the nature of perfect love, and his Benjamin minor and Benjamin major mapped the ascent to contemplation for generations of later readers.

Robert of Tombelaine

Robertus de Tumbalenac. 1010 – after 1084

Monk of Mont-Saint-Michel, later abbot of Saint-Vigor of Bayeux (1066–1084). He wrote this two-book commentary on the Song of Songs at the request of a fellow monk, Anastasius, prefacing it with a covering letter to a monk named Ansfrid.

  1. Commentaria in Cantica canticorum PL 150

Vincent of Lérins

Vincentius Lirinensisd. before 450

Monk of the island monastery of Lérins, off the coast of Provence, and one of the sharpest Latin minds of the semi-Pelagian milieu of southern Gaul. Around 434, three years after the Council of Ephesus, he composed the Commonitorium under the pen-name Peregrinus, 'the Pilgrim' — a handbook for telling catholic truth from heresy by the test of what has been believed everywhere, always, and by all. Migne prints the surviving first book together with the recapitulation that is all that remains of the lost second.

  1. Commonitoria PL 50

William of Saint-Thierry

Guillelmus abbasc. 1085 – 1148

Benedictine abbot of Saint-Thierry near Reims, who later laid down his abbacy to become a Cistercian monk at Signy; a close friend of Bernard of Clairvaux and the author of the first book of his life. He wrote his own exposition of the Song of Songs; the present work is an anthology he wove together from Bernard's sermons on the same book — hence 'ex Bernardo contexta,' compiled out of Bernard.

  1. Commentatio in Cantica canticorum ex Bernardo contexta PL 184
