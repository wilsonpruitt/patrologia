These are the edition's working notes, published as they were written. They record, work by work, where Migne's plate is defective or the reading uncertain: the printed form, what the English does with it, and why. They are not a groomed apparatus. Where a note argues with itself, withdraws an earlier decision, or leaves a question open, that is the record of how the reading was actually reached, and it is left standing — a conjecture that is visible can be checked, and one that has been tidied away cannot.

The English never silently repairs the page. A construable but wrong reading is rendered literally and the conjecture is recorded here, never applied to the text. How the English is produced and checked →

Chunk 0001 (dc-001)

  • 1177 · …our holy father and teacher Maximus the Confessor.* — T. Migne's heading reads «Of our holy father and teacher Maximus the Confessor, Various chapters on theology and on the economy, and on virtue and vice»; the machine reading lost the two middle lines (after «ΔΙΔΑΣΚΑΛΟΥ»), and the English gives the full title. The Latin heads leaked into the Greek («DIVERSA CAPITA …», «CENTENA PRIMA CAPITA») are left out.
  • 1180 · …beginning, and superessential, and without parts, and undivided; — T. The facing Latin has «simplexque et superessentialis», with a «simple» the Greek here does not carry; logged as a doubt for the plate.
  • 1181 · …participated in the mode which he himself knows, — T. In our only scan the left edge of this column is clipped: the first letters of every line are missing; they are supplied from the myriobiblos transcription and the facing Latin.
  • 1181 · …them by the virtues; and he shows himself — T. The opening of this line is lost and the second witness offers nothing for it; only a final «-ς» survives before «ὅσον». The English follows the facing Latin, «seipsum manifestat».
  • 1181 · …and today, the same, and unto the ages*, — S. Heb 13:8.
  • 1181 · …her who bore him, a mother, a virgin — T. The «μη-» at the line end and the second witness give «μητέρα τὴν τεκοῦσαν», "her who bore him, a mother"; the facing Latin «puerperam magis virginem» may have read «μᾶλλον», "more a virgin". The plate decides.
  • 1181 · …make the child-bearing an unbreakable bond of virginity. — T. Migne prints «ἀπεργάζεσθαι [ἀπεργάσασθαι]»: the present infinitive, with the aorist set after it in brackets as a variant. The English, "to make", is the same for either.
  • 1184 · …he exulted, revelling in the corruption of nature: — S. cf. Gen 3:5.
  • 1185 · …altogether in divine pleasure and gladness without end. — S. cf. Gen 3:6, 16.
  • 1188 · …lance, made the thief to be within paradise. — S. cf. Gen 2:21–22; John 19:34; Luke 23:43.
  • 1189 · …be present, the enjoying of it as present. — S. cf. 1 Cor 13:13.
  • 1189 · …become gods, receiving from God our being gods. — S. cf. Gen 1:26–27; Ps 81:6 LXX (82:6 Heb.).

Chunk 0003 (dc-003)

  • 1204 · …tunic, is filled with the stench of passions. — T. The chunk opens mid-sentence: «the soul» is the object of a participle in the lines before (dc-002). The facing Latin: pravorum motuum atque operum memoria, animum secundum conscientiam formans; quam is velut tunicam … videns. The relative ἣν (the habit) is the object of ὁρῶσα; the English turns it as «seeing this».
  • 1204 · …image beside the divine. 62. A firm assurance — T. πίστις here in the sense «assurance», «pledge» (Latin fiducia), not the virtue of faith. ἐκθέωσις (here and ch. 74) is rendered «deification», as θέωσις.
  • 1204 · …he himself, for man's sake, brought himself down. — T. Kraken prints a lone «]» at three line ends in this chapter (after ἐλπίδος, after ἀναβι-, after ἑαυτὸν), with no opening bracket and no word enclosed: nothing is supplied or deleted, and the facing Latin shows no trace. Read as marks of the plate, not as Migne's brackets; the English is unaffected. Logged for the plate check.
  • 1204 · …grace supervenes: leading reason away from natural distinction — T. διάκρισις is «distinction» where it is logical or ontological, as here, and «discernment» where it is the virtue.
  • 1204 · …instead the loss that friendship toward them brings; — T. Migne prints μεταμαθοῦσαν [μεταμαθούσης]: the bracketed genitive is a variant set after the printed accusative, the first agreeing with «the soul» (τὴν ψυχήν), the second with αὐτῆς («its»). Both give the same sense, and the English renders it once. The Latin: ubi is … didicerit.
  • 1205 · …having learned from the great Gregory of Nyssa, — P. Gregory of Nyssa (c. 335–c. 395), Cappadocian Father, brother of Basil the Great.
  • 1205 · …onto the more irrational part of the nature; — S. cf. Gregory of Nyssa, De hominis opificio 18.
  • 1205 · …of heavenly things: for instance, when we make — T. Migne prints ἐργασόμεθα; the sense needs the subjunctive ἐργασώμεθα, as μεταχειριζώμεθα before it.
  • 1205 · …law of the first Covenant, through practical philosophy, — T. «Philosophy» here, as throughout, is the ascetic life; «practical philosophy» is the practice of the commandments.
  • 1205 · …partaker of the wisdom spoken among the perfect. — T. κἂν οὔπω … ἐκτήσατο, καὶ … γέγονε μέτοχος: the οὔπω is read as governing both verbs («has not yet acquired … and [has not yet] become a partaker»), as the facing Latin does (tametsi necdum … nactus est, nec … factus est particeps).
  • 1208 · …any of the goods laid upon his rank, — T. Migne sets an asterisk call after τῷ (τῷ βαθμῷ, «his rank»), and his foot note reads «Male edita, εἶναι τῷ, quod sequitur Picus»: the earlier edition's faulty reading εἶναι τῷ, followed by the old Latin version. The English follows the printed text.
  • 1208 · …imitation of the image of the divine beauty. — T. ἀγάπην is kept (the second witness has ἀγάπης): the accusative agrees with φέρουσαν, so that it is the love that bears the imitation of the image; the facing Latin construes the same way (quae … imaginem … ferat, divinam charitatem … complexi).
  • 1208 · …some manner essentially inborn in God toward creation, — T. οὐσιωδῶς ἐμπέφυκέ πως τῷ Θεῷ πρὸς τὴν κτίσιν: rendered literally. The facing Latin: velut essentialiter Deo erga creaturam insitus est.
  • 1208 · …rightly has no lack, being perfect in love, — S. cf. 1 John 4:18.
  • 1208 · …that endures to the age of the age*, — S. Ps 18:10 LXX (19:9 Heb.).
  • 1208 · …there is no lack to him at all. — S. Ps 33:10 LXX (34:9 Heb.); the same words stand earlier in the chapter («has no lack»).
  • 1209 · …is a believer, has a heart without intelligence, — S. cf. Rom 1:21.
  • 1209 · …reaches out for the salvation of all men — S. cf. 1 Tim 2:4.
  • 1209 · …withers their conceit like the barren fig tree, — S. cf. Matt 21:18–20; Mark 11:12–14, 20–21.
  • 1209 · …the befalling of what is contrary to nature. — T. πάσχειν is rendered «suffer» throughout this sequence (chs. 75–77), to keep it with πάθος («passion») in ch. 63 and ch. 77. σύμβασις = «befalling», what comes upon one (Latin casus ac eventus).
  • 1212 · …but that contrary to nature is without existence, — T. Migne prints ἀν-ύπαρκτον («without existence»), divided at the line end, as read on the page; the facing Latin agrees (nec vere est), and the argument requires it (what is inert is without existence, as what is efficacious is unbounded). The second witness has ἐνύπαρκτον.
  • 1212 · …practical mind, like holy Peter, held by Herod, — P. Herod: Herod Agrippa I, who imprisoned Peter (Acts 12), read here as the «law of skin», the flesh.
  • 1212 · …is the way of thinking of the flesh — S. cf. Rom 8:6.
  • 1212 · …up under two guards and one iron gate, — S. cf. Acts 12:4–10.
  • 1213 · …we have sinned; and the book about Job — P. Job: the righteous man of the book of Job, tried by the devil with God's leave.

Picus did not express it well in saying 'it is referred to no cause at all', since a thing is also said to be referred to a cause when it traces its origin to it in some way, even if it is not moved toward it; as in the Godhead the Son is referred to the Father as to the cause (as the Greeks are accustomed to say), yet he is not moved toward him, nor has motion, as toward a cause in the sense of an end, in the way Catholics speak of any of them: for he is not so for the sake of an end, but with the Father and the Holy Spirit is the one end of all, as there is one goodness, as there is one nature. Hence he is called by Gregory the Theologian and by Maximus 'without cause' (anaitios), having no cause or end, in so far as he is for himself, but 'through a cause' (di' aitian), on account of the cause, in so far as he became man for our sake. One must therefore speak with more care in theology, lest any obscurity, still less any error, creep into the theology of Maximus, the most careful of theologians.

Prima ratio est cur Deus zelotes, quod vi maxima boni amoris erga res præditus sit : est enim zelus, amoris excessus. Erudita Maximo omnia — keyed to ἑαυτοῦ — working translation (a first reading by the apparatus reader, not yet reviewed): 'Unforthcoming from himself' (ἀνεκφοίτητος ἑαυτοῦ). Read thus entirely, in place of what stood (ἀνεκφοίτητον): a word Picus wrongly renders actively and refers to power, 'and never deserting', whereas it refers to God himself. Nothing is more usual in theology than that the divine processions are to be understood as 'not going forth' (ἀνεκφοιτήτως): so that through them the persons (or the Trinity itself in the processions outward) do not leave themselves by producing, even though they truly exist in what is produced, whether persons or things; yet otherwise persons in persons, otherwise the Trinity in things, of which Maximus here speaks: 'the abundant love of the good toward beings' (Picus ineptly: 'the highest love of good toward creatures'). The first reason why God is a jealous one is that he is endowed with the greatest force of good love toward things: for jealousy (zelus) is excess of love. Maximus has [all] learning on these [matters] [the note continues in the right column].

Quod sic auguste Maximus, Ἡ ἐρωτικὴ κίνησις τοῦ ἀγαθοῦ προϋπάρχουσα ἐν τῷ ἀγαθῷ : Amoris motus erga bonum ipse prior exsistens in bono ; in Deo scilicet quasi in exemplari, qui est ipsum bonum amans, seque adeo bonum amans, ex quo omnis origo amoris boni. Illepide Picus : Motus qui est in primis amans boni, etc. — keyed to ἀκίνητος — working translation (a first reading by the apparatus reader, not yet reviewed): 'Simple and unmoved.' I read what is found in the scholia to Dionysius and in Dionysius himself: αὐτοκίνητος ('self-moved'), for what is explained is the divine motion of love in himself and toward himself, whose likeness is our ever-moving (ἀεικίνητος) yearning toward him, a constant motion of desire. This Maximus says grandly: 'the erotic motion of the good, pre-existing in the good': the motion of love toward the good, itself prior and existing in the good; in God, that is, as in the exemplar, who is himself the good loving, and so loving the good, from which is every origin of love of the good. Picus is inelegant: 'the motion which is in the first place loving the good', etc. (The editor would read αὐτοκίνητος in place of the printed ἀκίνητος.)

Immobilis firmitas et sedes fundati in honesto situs sui, quod maxime justitiæ convenit ; quæ velut terra in hoc universo, suis undique librata momentis, rei ipsum medium immobiliter attendat, ac cuique, quod reipsa illius est, tribuat. Immovable firmness and the seat of what is founded in the honourable placing of itself, which fits justice above all; which, like earth in this universe, poised on all sides by its own moments, attends to the very middle of the thing unmovably, and gives to each what truly is his. What stood in the text, μετάθετος ('removable'), is badly wrong, and, as they say, contradicts itself in the adjective [i.e. the editor restores the privative: ἀμετάθετος]. Quod deerat vox υἱόν, nihil commodum reddere potuit Picus : ἀπροαίρετον γένεσιν καὶ ὑλικήν, ortum nativitatis non ex libera voluntate et terrenum, quo naturaliter propagamur, ubique Maximus post Greg. Since the word υἱόν ('son') was missing [in the text], Picus could not render it suitably: 'a not-chosen and material genesis', the origin of a birth not from free will and earthly, by which we are naturally propagated; Maximus everywhere, after Gregory of Nyssa and other Greek theologians, teaches that it grew upon us from the transgression of the first parent, there being another, more spiritual, manner of multiplying the human race, not unknown to God, with the parents' virginity intact; which Augustine confesses he does not know. As for ἀπροαίρετον, which Picus renders 'unwilling' ('invitam'), it is not apt enough: that word sounds like a contrary and resisting will, whereas the Greek word says only the absence of free will, in opposition to the spiritual generation 'by choice in the Spirit': that which is from free will in the Spirit, by which we are born of God, so as to be his sons and gods like God. (The note supplies υἱόν, the missing word, in the text.)

Cap. sequenti περιστατικὴν ἀνάγκην vocat Maximus necessitatem ex ipsis vitæ mortalis rationibus, ejusque variis casibus ; quam levare artis præceptis studet humana solertia : hæc nempe indigentia, quam idem excludit a primi status felicitate, Καὶ ἀπροσδεὴς ὑπάρχων τῆς περὶ τέχνας περιστατικῆς ἐλεύθερος ἦν. Ubi male Picus, Καὶ προσδεὴς ὑπάρχων· Et cum egeret : quæ ratio est non absolvens ab artium cura, sed potius ei addicens. Alia pridem in aliis non bonis Maximi interpretibus mihi sugillata, unius litteræ seu syllabæ elisione, proclivi lapsu festinantis manus scribæ, decoquentibus quod palam Scripturæ adversetur, ac cujus contrarium Maximo serio adnotetur. — keyed to περιστάσεως — working translation (a first reading by the apparatus reader, not yet reviewed): 'From circumstance.' In the following chapter Maximus calls 'circumstantial necessity' (περιστατικὴν ἀνάγκην) the necessity arising from the very conditions of mortal life and its various chances, which human skill strives to relieve by the precepts of art: namely that indigence which he excludes from the happiness of the first state, 'And being without need (ἀπροσδεής), he was free of the circumstantial need concerning the arts.' Where Picus is wrong with 'Καὶ προσδεὴς ὑπάρχων', 'And when he was in need', a reading that does not release from the care of the arts but rather binds him to it. I have already pointed out similar [slips] in other not-good interpreters of Maximus: the dropping of one letter or syllable, by the easy lapse of a hasty scribe's hand, making [the passage] contradict Scripture openly, whereas the opposite is seriously noted by Maximus.
