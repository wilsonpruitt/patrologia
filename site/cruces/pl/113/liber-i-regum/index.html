2a · 0542A · dilatavum — a NON-WORD, and the chunk's own lemma settles it. NO MARKER.

The Clementine has dilatatum, and — this is the decisive part and it is internal — the lemma at the head of this very chunk, eleven lines above, is Dilatatum est. (1Rg 2:1), correctly formed. A t read as a v is the ordinary shape of a digitization misread.

Rendered "our heart is enlarged", and NO [sic:] was fired. The 2026-08-23 addendum measured non-word suspicions at six for six as our corruption rather than Migne's, and a [sic:] is a public claim that Migne's plate is defective. ⚑ Plate candidate — PL 113 col. 0542A, and the suspect form is a NON-WORD. 2b · 0546A · Judic um — a SPLIT NON-WORD. NO MARKER.

Dan interpretatur Judic um vel judicans. The split run is Jerome's Onomasticon etymology of Dan, iudicium sive iudicans — and the second half, judicans, stands unbroken in the same clause, which is what makes the reconstruction safe. 2c · 0539C · quia fuit a de Ramatha civitate Ephraim — two prepositions stacked. NO MARKER.

Both a and de mean "from" and English has one word for the pair, so there is nothing for the English to show; the rendering is "because he was from Ramatha, a city of Ephraim". 2d · 0542B · Non dicit nisi Dominus: Sed nullus est sicut Dominus — which reading was taken

Two construals both parse:

  1. "He does not say except the Lord" — nisi Dominus as the glossator's own qualification;
  2. "He does not say 'except the Lord'" — nisi Dominus quoted as a form of words, contrasted with the italic Sed nullus est sicut Dominus that follows the colon.

Reading 2 was taken, and rendered "He saith not, save the Lord: But none is as the Lord, holy and sanctifying". Why reading 1 lost: the Sed is inside Migne's italic span, so it belongs to the quoted matter, not to his own sentence, and a sed inside a quotation with nothing before it to contrast against is exactly what a "he does not say X, but Y" frame produces. 4a · The charitas/dilectio pair — the sharpest of the declines, and it runs BOTH ways

At 0556D–0557A Migne quotes Rom 13:10 as Plenitudo legis charitas est where the Clementine has dilectio, and eleven lines later quotes Eph 4:2 as Sufferentes invicem in dilectione where the Clementine has caritate. He has each of the two words exactly where the Vulgate has the other.

[var:] was weighed on both and declined on both. The reason is not that the difference is invisible — it is that caritas and dilectio are the standard Latin pair for one thing, the exposition here is about the fulness of the law and not about which Latin word names love, and the form Migne prints for Rom 13:10 is the ordinary patristic citation form of that verse. A [var:] that fired here would have to fire on every Latin father who cites Rom 13:10, which is the definition of noise. What is worth recording is the chiasm, and that is what this entry is for; the divergence is now findable by anyone who wants it.

8.1 · ⛔⛔ 0561C — the one site where the reader overturned a decision this file had already made

Phaltiel, id est evadens a Deo dicitur: quia custodivit eum ne tangeret eam, ne fieret transgressor legis.

§6's table resolved the subject as Phalti — "he kept himself" — on the ground that "the paragraph names him five times." The reader disputed it, and the reader is right. Migne prints eum, an accusative object. A Latin sentence whose subject and object are the same man requires se; eum forecloses that reading outright. The unexpressed subject is God, named two words earlier in the very etymology the clause exists to explain — Phaltiel = evadens a Deo — and under the old rendering the -el did no work at all.

Now "because God kept him from touching her." The supply of the name is recorded here as a supply.

⭐ The reason this one matters beyond itself: the losing argument was FREQUENCY, and the winning one was STRUCTURE. That is the same split the batch's plate reads produced — corpus frequency decisive for non-words, worthless against a real form — and the same trap test 2a names: the test validates the mapping you propose, not the mapping the Latin makes. Counting how often a man is named tells you nothing about which case ending stands in front of you. ⚠ Flagged to Wilson as the one genuinely contested call in this batch; both readings are stated here so the rival can be weighed.

8.2 · 0543B — a verb annexed from the verse, leaving "the same" with no referent (repaired)

Lemma 1 Rg 2:7, Dominus pauperem facit et ditat. The gloss substitutes Jesum for the verse's pauperem and then resumes the verse's own second verb: Jesum, qui propter nos pauper factus est, et ditat eumdem gloria resurrectionis. The subject of ditat is the verse's Dominus; eumdem is Jesus, its object.

The English had attached ditat to the relative qui — "Jesus, who … was made poor, and enriches the same" — which makes Jesus the enricher and leaves eumdem pointing at nothing. ⚑ The tell is that the finished English does not construe, and it shipped anyway: containment checks cannot see a dangling referent. Now "…and the Lord enriches the same with the glory of the resurrection", which restores the verse's own shape — the Lord makes poor (the incarnation) and enriches (the resurrection). The supplied subject is recorded as a supply.

8.3 · 0549D — a word of the Latin unrendered (repaired)

Agnus oblatus pro populo Dei victoriam praestat… was Englished "The lamb offered for the people affords victory". Dei appeared on neither construal. Now "for the people of God" — populus Dei being this gloss's standing phrase, which recurs unmodified six lines later (coram Israel, id est populo Dei). ⚠ The rival construal, Dei victoriam, "God's victory", is possible and is recorded here; it loses to the work's own usage, and either way the word had to reappear.

8.4 · 0553D — one of Migne's own, previously unlogged: non for noti (no change)

…sic jam non eramus ut in ipso essemus electi ante mundi constitutionem. Augustine's clause (the Quaesivit sibi extract) reads sic iam noti eramus, ut in illo essemus electi — "we were already so known that we were chosen in him". Migne's non for noti inverts the sentence against its own sed ipsi quoque Unigenito, qui venit quaerere quod perierat.

The English renders him literally, which is correct and stays. It is recorded here only so the ledger carries it: a defect of Migne's, reproduced deliberately, not one of ours.
