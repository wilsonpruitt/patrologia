⚖ THE BOUNDARY OF THE MIRROR RULE — ruled here, and reversible if Wilson disagrees

The third stint capitalized two words the plate sets lower-case — faciente domino (0661C, in the etymology of Asabias) and In regum (0657D) — and flagged the departure rather than hiding it, asking whether the mirror should be absolute.

Ruled: it should not, and the plate itself is the argument. In the very chunk containing In regum ita, Migne also prints In Regum ita — both casings of the same phrase, a few lines apart. His case is not carrying a distinction there, so mirroring it would transcribe an accident as though it were a signal.

So the rule has a stated scope: mirror the plate where the case is SEMANTIC IN ENGLISH — Ecclesia/ecclesia is the founding case, and it qualifies because "the Church" and "the church" are two different claims a reader can tell apart. Do not mirror where English has no lower-case option for the referent — the divine name, a book title. Rendering faciente domino as "the lord doing" would not be fidelity; it would be a misspelling wearing fidelity's clothes.

⚑ Ecclesia itself was mirrored word for word throughout all three stints, and the lower-case sites were verified at the source, not taken on trust — including 0011's ecclesiarum → "the churches of God".

§T · WHAT THE FOURTH TEST FOUND, AND WHY IT IS THE ONE THAT MATTERS HERE

Every containment check passed on this book from the start. Three conformations were caught only by reading the finished English back against the Latin asking whether it asserts what he asserts — and all three were the familiar English Bible arriving over the plate:

Absalom → Absalon · Judas Iscariot → Judas Scarioth · In Hebron → In Hebrun.

⛔ Nothing mechanical sees these. The name is present, spelled like a name, in the right place, and it reads better than the truth. They are the exact defect this edition exists to catch, and a genealogy is where they are easiest and least visible — which is why the first stint was warned about them and the third still found three.

The same test produced the reading repairs no count would show: fidei taken as an accusative object (0656B), sibi as "to him" (0657D), and a Douay-supplied object in Osee 6:5.

§V · THE DIVERGENCES THAT CARRY AN ARGUMENT

Thirty-four is a high count, and the reason is that this book reasons from its lemmata. Four are worth naming because in each the divergence is not incidental to the gloss but load-bearing:

  • ⭐⭐ 0657A — an arithmetic argument with a wrong operand. The gloss quotes 2 Reg. 24:9 at 80,000 and 50,000 where the Clementine reads 800,000 and 500,000, and then at 0657C reasons from the difference between the two books' numbers. The marker is what lets a reader see that the argument is being run on figures the received text does not have.
  • ⭐⭐ 0636A — qui dividebat dimidium requietionum for the Clementine's videbat (1 Par. 2:52). The gloss underneath uses videre four times and dividere not once.
  • ⭐ 0639C — Migne negates an apostolic sentence. He prints scientia non inflat; 1 Cor. 8:1 has no negative at all. The negative was rendered on its printed host and the divergence marked — never silently repaired, which is exactly 7a.
  • ⭐ 0659C–D — the gloss does textual criticism on its own lemma and lands on ad torrendum, which is the Clementine's reading. torrentem and torrendum are held apart as "the parching" and "the roasting"; collapsing them would have erased the argument being made.

§2 · THE TWELVE [var:], AND WHY EACH EARNED A PUBLIC CLAIM

The gate applied throughout, stated so it can be checked: Pattern 14's own test — a divergence "in a way that changes the sense." A word substituted, added or dropped inside the italic span such that the span asserts something else fires. A pure clip, a dropped or added particle (autem, quoque, et, At, Propterea), a word-order change, a case adapted to the gloss's own syntax, a proper-name spelling, and an orthographic variant (caritas/charitas, coelo/caelo, i/j) do not fire and are all in §4. The witness prefix is Vulg., matching what 8965's shipped chunks print, so the two books read alike where they will stand side by side. Eleven are argued a–k below; the twelfth, on 2 Reg. 24:9 at 0657A, is argued at §3a because the reasoning about it is inseparable from the gloss's arithmetic.

### a. 0656A — Prov. 17:20, a tense and an added possessive Migne: Qui vertit linguam suam, incidit in malum. Clem. Prov. 17:20: qui vertit linguam incidet in malum. Future → present, and suam added. The gloss is about Christ extinguishing folly now, in his examining, so the tense is doing work.

### b. 0656C — Ps. 33:17, the Lord dropped out of the quoted span Migne prints, inside the italics, vultus est super facientes mala, with cujus standing outside them. Clem. Ps. 33:17: Vultus autem Domini super facientes mala. The subject of the verse — Domini — is not in the span at all; an est stands where it was, and the span has been re-hinged on a relative antecedent supplied by the gloss. A reader collating the span against the psalm meets a different sentence.

### c. 0656B — Phil. 2:13, nobis for vobis Migne: qui operatur in nobis velle et perficere. Clem.: qui operatur in vobis et velle, et perficere. First person for second. The gloss applies it to us, the faithful who are the crown, so the divergence is load-bearing and not a slip a reader should have to guess at.

### d. 0658B — Deut. 10:12, O Israel for Et nunc Israel, and ut for et Migne: O Israel quid Dominus Deus tuus petit a te? nisi ut timeas … ut ambules in viis ejus. Clem.: Et nunc Israel, quid Dominus Deus tuus petit a te, nisi ut timeas … et ambules. The vocative replaces the temporal frame, and the second clause is subordinated to the first instead of coordinate with it. ⚑ Migne's own ? mid-quotation is preserved exactly (Pattern 8); it falls on a direct question and needs no 8b recasting.

### e. 0659C–D — 1 Par. 23:29, and it is the most interesting lemma in the range Migne: Ad ferventem similam, et ad torrentem. Clem. 23:29: et ad similae sacrificium … et ad torrendum. Two substitutions in one short lemma. ⭐ The gloss then does textual criticism on its own lemma: some codices have Ad ferventem similam, others ad ferventem only, sed in Hebraeo habetur, ad torrendum — and ad torrendum is exactly what the Clementine prints. So Migne's page preserves the variant and the correction. ⛔ The rendering is where this could have been lost. torrentem (participle) and torrendum (gerund) are one root, and English has no clean exponent for the difference; rendering both "for the roasting" would have erased the very distinction the gloss is arguing. They are held apart as "for the parching" (ad torrentem, the lemma) and "for the roasting" (ad torrendum, the Hebrew reading), and ad ferventem similam / ad ferventem as "for the seething fine flour" / "for the seething." The four are distinct English phrases because they are four distinct Latin readings.

### f. 0660C — Act. 13:2, and Paulum for Saulum Migne: orantibus apostolis et jejunantibus, ait Spiritus sanctus: Segregate mihi et Paulum et Barnabam. Clem. Act. 13:2: Ministrantibus autem illis Domino, et jejunantibus, dixit illis Spiritus Sanctus : Segregate mihi Saulum et Barnabam. Four divergences, of which the name is decisive: the Spirit separates Saul, who is not yet Paul. orantibus apostolis for ministrantibus illis Domino is the second material one — Migne's span names the apostles and makes the act prayer rather than ministry, which is precisely what the gloss needs (a primis praedicatoribus ad praedicandum ordinati sunt).

### g. 0660C — 1 Cor. 9:19, omnes for plures Migne: ut omnes lucrifacerem. Clem.: ut plures lucrifacerem. "All" for "the more" — and the gloss's whole point is the universality of the condescension (Omnium servum me feci), so the substitution is the one that makes the proof-text prove it.

### h. 0660D — Matth. 24:12, a perfect turned future Migne: abundabit iniquitas, et refrigescet charitas multorum. Clem. Matth. 24:12: Et quoniam abundavit iniquitas, refrigescet caritas multorum. The gloss is expounding the western gate as finis mundi … persecutio Antichristi, and Migne's future puts the whole verse in the time to come where the Clementine has a perfect protasis. (charitas for caritas is orthography and is in §4.)

### i. 0661C — Isa. 26:12, in nobis for nobis Migne: Omnia opera nostra operatus es in nobis. Clem. Isa. 26:12: omnia enim opera nostra operatus es nobis. "Wrought in us" for "wrought for us" — and the gloss has just said nemo haec agit nisi dono Dei, so the indwelling reading is the one it wants. ⚑ Migne's note reads (Psal. XXVI); the words are Isaias'. Notes ride verbatim, so the marker names the true book and §6a carries the citation.

### j. 0662C–D — Isa. 66:2, an Old-Latin-shaped quotation Migne: Super quem requiescet Spiritus meus, nisi super humilem et quietum et trementem sermones meos? Clem. Isa. 66:2: ad quem autem respiciam, nisi ad pauperculum, et contritum spiritu, et trementem sermones meos? Three substitutions: the Spirit resting for the Lord looking, humilem for pauperculum, quietum for contritum spiritu. Only the last four words are common ground. This one has no [n:] at all — it is the resumption tail of an usque ad — so without the marker a reader has nothing to collate against.

### k. 0664D — Job 21:13, qui sibi videntur added Migne: Qui sibi videntur in bonis ducere dies suos, et in puncto ad inferna descendunt. Clem. Job 21:13: Ducunt in bonis dies suos, et in puncto ad inferna descendunt. Job's flat indicative becomes a self-deception. Standing as it does at the very end of the book, beside Ps. 54:24 on the bloody and deceitful, the added clause changes who is being described.

§3 · THIS RANGE'S CRUCES — the numbers, the names, and the words that will not construe

### ⭐ a. 0656A — In Regum libro ita gives 80,000 and 50,000 where Kings gives 800,000 and 500,000, in a gloss that is doing arithmetic between the two books Migne, at 1 Par. 21:5: In Regum libro ita. Quod inventa sunt de Israel octoginta millia virorum fortium et de Juda quinquaginta millia pugnatorum. Clem. 2 Reg. 24:9: octingenta millia virorum fortium … quingenta millia pugnatorum. Both of Migne's are real, well-formed numerals, so the non-word rule does not touch them; both are an order of magnitude short of the text they are explicitly quoting (In Regum libro ita). This earned a [var:]* — not because the numerals look wrong, but because the passage presents itself as the text of Kings, and because the gloss at 0657C then reasons from the difference between the two books' numbers (tot millia interfecta … quot millibus numerus in Paralipomenon scriptus, numerum qui in Regum est excedit*). An arithmetic argument with a wrong operand is exactly the class the launch brief calls the most valuable kind, and a reader who cannot see the operand cannot see the argument.

### ⭐ b. 0656A — non viginti et octo millia, and 0656A decem et octo with no millia Three numerals in one short gloss, all real words, all rendered as printed and none marked:

  1. In quibusdam autem codicibus invenitur David non viginti et octo millia in valle Salinarum occidisse, quod vitio scriptorum factum est. The lemma four lines above is Decem., clipping 18:12's decem et octo millia, and the gloss is about which number the codices carry. Repaired, four sites

  1. ⭐ 0637C — the conformation the translators' own final test missed. Migne prints David Absalonem filium suum fugerit; the English printed Absalom, the familiar English Bible spelling. The work's other occurrence (0662D, cum Absalon de nece David tractavit) was already correct. The fix had been applied at one site and not the other — which is exactly why a blind read is a different check from a self-audit. Repaired.
  2. ⛔⛔ 0648C — the serious one: a relative bound to the wrong antecedent made the saints incarnate. quia ad eum omnis sanctorum coetus pertinet qui descendens de coelis, et factus homo … morte sua interficiens in infernum demersit. Every one of those participles and verbs is singular and belongs to eum — Banaias, read as Christ, the subject of the surrounding chain of Hic-clauses. The English attached "who" to the nearest noun, the saints, and the sentence then attributes the Incarnation and the harrowing of hell to the company of the saints, leaving an unresolvable "his own death" inside a plural clause. ⚑ The English was internally incoherent where the Latin is not — the shape the fourth test exists to catch. Repaired with an em-dash and "he who", the least invasive way to rebind it.
  3. 0643B — an ablative agent moved off its verb, so a sentence about a marriage stopped naming who married her. a patre venalis ducta a Manasse: qui horreis praeerat cibariis patri datis, in conjugium … suscepta est — ducta already has its agent in a patre, so a Manasse governs suscepta est. The English gave ducta two agents and left suscepta est agentless. Second defect in the same sentence: cibariis patri datis is the girl's father, rendered "his father", pointing the reader at Manasse's. Both repaired.
  4. 0634B — an adjective rebound to make an impossible agreement. septem filios et tres filias praedictas uxor genuit: praedictas is accusative feminine plural and cannot modify uxor, a nominative singular. The English moved it to the noun that made the sentence read smoothly ("the aforesaid wife") and dropped it from the noun that carries it. Repaired to "the aforesaid three daughters". Low severity in sense, but a repair with no marker is the class that leaves no trace.

⬜ TWO THINGS LEFT FOR WILSON, deliberately not acted on

  • The lemma-expansion practice is inconsistent inside this work, and the inconsistency is real even though none of the three expansions is a defect. Achar qui. → "Achar who troubled Israel", Her pater. → "Her the father of Lecha", Iniit autem. → "And he took counsel" are all fragments, which convention 3 licenses. But Mesraim autem genuit. (0632A) is the identical shape — transitive verb, object elided, Vulgate continuation available — and stands as "And Mesraim begot." So do Filii autem., Jectan autem., Sem, Ar. ⚑ The standing ruling is to expand the stump rather than contract its twin, which would mean expanding these; that is a change to a shipping work's lemmata and is Wilson's call, not mine. ⚠ Note the cardinals here are all correct and bare (Viginti → "Twenty", Septuaginta millia virorum → "Seventy thousand men") — this work did not repeat the sister book's numeral confusion.
  • 0646C — a "reads better than its Latin" site the reader flagged and declined to assert. Videntes indocti … medicamina lavacrumque baptismi, ignorantes in Judaea magistros suos … victos: the English swaps the two participles' objects. The reader's reason for not calling it a defect is good — the alternative parse is the only one compatible with majorum ruina turbati six words later, since men "not knowing" cannot be "troubled by the ruin" — while Migne's comma slightly favours the other binding. Genuinely undecided; left as printed.
