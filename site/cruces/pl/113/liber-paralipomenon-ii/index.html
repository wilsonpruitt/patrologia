§M · THE MERGE — what was reconciled between the two stints, and what needed nothing

Two translators cannot see each other's work, and they diverge on terminology without knowing it (11551 shipped one half calling a column's members stylus/epistylium and the other "epistyle"/"shaft", which left the author's own etymological argument explaining nothing). So the merge is a check, not a concatenation. Both stints stated their vocabulary before it could be compared, and the comparison was then made against the English files themselves, not against the tables — a table records what a translator believed it did.

Checked across the seam, and consistent without intervention:

  • The number-names. senarius · centenarius · denarius · binarius · quadragenarius · duodenarius → the senary · the centenary · the denary · the binary · the quadragenary · the duodenary, never flattened to "six" or "a hundred", because in CAPUT IX–X the number-name is the argument. All 12 occurrences fall inside chunk 0006; the first stint's range contains none, so there is no seam here to fail at.
  • Ecclesia / ecclesia. Capitalization mirrors the plate, word for word. The second stint stated the rule; the first had followed it silently — its single lowercase "the church" (0003, in the atrium sacerdotum gloss) stands opposite the single lowercase ecclesia its Latin twin prints, against three capitalized forms in the same chunk. Verified at the site, not taken on trust.
  • doctores → teachers, never "doctors", in both stints and across nine of the eleven chunks (18 occurrences). praedicatores stays preachers, held apart.
  • pascha → pasch, in both (0001, 0009, 0010). No "Passover" anywhere in the work — which matters here, because 0010 is where Migne prints phase and Phase beside it and the second stint kept those form for form. Flattening either word to "Passover" would have destroyed a distinction the plate itself makes.
  • The abridgment formula, rendered as far as throughout, every occurrence inside its own italic span answering an italic usque ad in the Latin. Both stints decided usque per occurrence rather than by rule, which is what the brief requires: 50 tokens work-wide, of which 10 sit inside another word (utriusque, cujusque) and the remainder split 28 formula / 13 ordinary prepositional uses. The trap at 0680D — where usque ad is not the formula but 9:26's own words — was caught.
  • Pattern 17. thou = singular, you = plural — 21 second-person singular forms in the English (thou 7, thee 4, thy 10), each named in its stint's table with the Latin that fixes the number.
  • Proper names kept form for form as Migne prints them, in both — Philitshiim, Neopolis, Eroge, Amulcus, Ononimus, Zareas, Benennom, and Jesus where Migne writes it for Josue. None normalized toward a familiar English Bible spelling.

Held apart deliberately, and stated here because a later sweep would otherwise churn it: charitas is charity and dilectio is love, throughout. The pair is not decorative — the [var:] at 0668C exists because Migne's Rom. 13:10 lemma prints charitas where the Clementine prints dilectio, and an English that used one word for both would make that marker unreadable. The same applies to typice / figuraliter (typically / figuratively), scientia / sapientia (knowledge / wisdom), and significat / figurabat / designatur.

Nothing was rewritten in either stint's English at the merge. No drift was found that required it, and a merge that silently edits a translator's chunk destroys the only record of what that translator actually read.

One thing the merge deliberately does NOT do: reconcile the two lemma collations into one list. They cover disjoint column ranges, each was checked against clementine-flat.txt independently, and each names the lemmata it found to agree as well as those that diverge — roughly 200 spans between them. Flattening them would lose which reader checked what, which is exactly what a later blind read needs to know.

§2 · THIS RANGE'S CRUCES

2a · Centum. (0665C) and Ducenti. (0677C) are CLIPS and are expanded; Posteriora. is not

Both are one-word lemmata and both are clips whose own gloss proves the missing word. Centum. (1:17 equum centum quinquaginta) is glossed Qui sunt ter quinquageni — thrice fifty, which is 150, not 100. Ducenti. (8:10 ducenti quinquaginta) is glossed by taking the centenarius and then the quinquagenarius in turn — 2×100 + 50. Rendered "A hundred and fifty." and "Two hundred and fifty.", per Ruth §1.2: the fragment is rendered as the phrase it stands for. Rendered as bare "A hundred" and "Two hundred" each gloss would state a falsehood about its own lemma.

⚑ And the control's class, per the brief. Posteriora. (0671C) and Sacerdotes. (0676A) are complete one-word lemmata with nothing elided, and are rendered as they stand. Contrasting them with Centum. would manufacture an inconsistency that is not there.

2b · 0665B · doctorum testamentum praedicatione — will not construe. RENDERED AS PRINTED, NO MARKER.

Quia doctorum testamentum praedicatione ad cognoscendam fidei veritatem per praedicatores in usum veri Salomonis homines adducuntur. The clause is passive (adducuntur), so the nominative/accusative testamentum has nothing to govern it and nothing to be governed by; testamenti, with praedicatione, is the construction the words want, and the same paragraph's per utrumque testamentum two lines below shows the phrase is live in his mind. The conjecture is deliberately NOT adopted: nothing here is broken type (Pattern 12), nothing is lost from the digitization (Pattern 13), and this corpus has no marker meaning "Migne prints a word that will not construe" — the Ruth 0534A precedent (societate fieri) is exactly this and took none. Rendered "Because by preaching, the testament of the teachers, men are brought…", which leaves the stranded word visible where the plate leaves it.

E1a · 0684A · reperetur — a non-word with TWO candidate reconstructions, and the choice is recorded

et tanto durius reperetur ad poenam, quanto securius viveret in culpa (the tail of the Gregory abridgment). reperetur is a form of no Latin verb; measured against the whole corpus of src/latin/, it occurs exactly once — here — while reperiretur's family runs to hundreds of well-formed tokens and reservare to twenty-six.   2. 0686B — a supplied pronoun made Athalia a man, in a sentence that names her in its own middle clause: "Not that he then had sons … Athalia endeavoured … he calls his sons". molita est is feminine, and the lemma's ejus is hers (2 Par 24:7).   3. 0689D — a participle bound to the wrong noun. §Z2 · THE LEMMA-EXPANSION FINDING — RAISED, ACTED ON, AND THEN WITHDRAWN

⛔⛔ THE CORRECTION STANDS FIRST, because a false contraction shipped for part of this session and the reasoning that produced it was inverted. The reader flagged five lemmata printing longer in English than in the Latin twin, each addition exactly the Vulgate continuation. Two of them — Centum. and Ducenti. — were contracted to "A hundred." and "Two hundred." That was wrong, and both have been reverted.

What the plate did and did not settle. Gallica bpt6k5505319w (map f = (column + 1) / 2, calibrated against the printed corner numbers) shows f333 a bare Centum. and f339 a bare Ducenti.. That answered the reader's honest caveat — the words are not dropped from our Latin, our Latin is right — and it answered nothing else. Whether a bare lemma should be expanded in English is a question about convention 3, and no plate can decide it.

⛔ And convention 3 decides it the other way. Both lemmata are clips of two-word numerals: 2 Par 1:17 reads equum centum quinquaginta and 2 Par 8:10 ducenti quinquaginta. A lemma whose governed word is missing is a fragment, and the Ruth pilot's convention 3 renders a fragment as the phrase it stands for, not as a word-for-word stump. The settled ruling on a work that treats the same bare lemma two ways is explicit: resolve it by EXPANDING the stump, not by contracting its twin.

⛔⛔ The gloss argument was backwards, and it is the part worth remembering. The contraction was justified on the ground that Migne's gloss supplies the figure itself — Qui sunt ter quinquageni under Centum., Centenarius … Quinquagenarius vero under Ducenti. — so that an expanded English "pre-empts the gloss's own arithmetic." It does the opposite. Ter quinquageni is thrice fifty, which is 150, not 100: the gloss is not withholding the figure, it is confirming it, and it is the proof that Migne's Centum. means centum quinquaginta. Contracting the English left the lemma saying one hundred on a page whose gloss immediately says one hundred and fifty.

So all five were licensed expansions and none was a defect. Carduus qui, etc. (carrying Migne's own etc.), Obturaverunt cunctos., Ad quam Deus. — the second stint's deliberate, stated applications of convention 3 — and the two numerals with them.

⚑ What survives as a real observation is smaller and duller: the two stints of this work state different rules for a bare cardinal. The second stint ruled that an English cardinal stands as a substantive so nothing is elided (Sexcenta. "Six hundred.", Quadraginta. "Forty."), which is right for a complete numeral and does not reach a clipped one. Migne's Sexcenta. is the whole of 2 Par 1:17's six hundred; his Centum. is half of its hundred and fifty. Same-looking lemma, different class — which is the brief's own warning that a control must be the same class as the thing it controls. No further change was made on either account.

⚑ The general lesson, and the reason this is written out rather than quietly reverted: a blind reader's finding can be right about the FACT and wrong about the RULE. The five English lemmata really did print words the Latin twin does not — that observation was correct and worth having. What it could not supply is whether the convention licenses them, and acting on the fact without checking the rule turned three sound renderings into candidates and two into errors.
