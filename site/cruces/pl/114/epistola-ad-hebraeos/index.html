§2 · THE THREE [cj:] — and the second-best reading in each case

2.1 · Confessus / confessus for consessus — 0644B and 0646A, TWICE

0644B Sic et per dexteram, non Deum deformavit, sed similitudinem honoris demonstravit. Confessus enim nihil demonstrat aliud nisi honoris aequalitatem. 0646A VERS. 13.-- … Sede. Victori Filio confessus offertur a Patre.

Both sentences are about Christ's session at the Father's right hand — the first follows per dexteram in the same breath, the second glosses the lemma Sede. Both slots demand a fourth-declension noun as subject (offertur; demonstrat), and confessus supplies none: it is a deponent perfect participle, so what the page says is "he that hath confessed", which is not what either sentence is arguing. Consessus, -us ("a sitting together, a session") fills both slots and both arguments exactly.

§4 · THE SCOPE QUESTION AT 0650C — where nunquam attaches, and why I left it open

Trigger condition for test 2a fires here (a negative with a quantifier/correlative adjacent):

…requiem praestitisset nunquam, id est nullo tempore; posthac, id est in sequenti tempore; loqueretur de alia die, id est tempore gratiae, quo deberet dari. Vel, nunquam post loqueretur de alia, scilicet requie, praeter terram promissionis.

In the Clementine (Heb 4:8) numquam governs the apodosis — numquam de alia loqueretur, "he would never speak of another". Migne's serial gloss cuts it onto praestitisset and glosses it nullo tempore, which reads it with the protasis; then his own Vel clause puts it back on loqueretur. Migne prints both scopes and adjudicates neither.

So neither do I. I rendered the serial as a serial — "had given rest, never, that is, at no time; thereafter, … would he speak of another day, …" — with the comma before "never" holding it loose, and then rendered the Vel clause with the negative squarely on "speak". Supplying a scope in the first half would have been the 2a defect exactly: a negative correctly present, landing in the wrong place in English, passing every count. ⭐ Test 2a's own warning applies: it validates the mapping I propose, not the one the Latin makes. Here the sentence's structure (Vel) is what proves there are two mappings, so the honest English is the one that carries both.

2. The three markers, and why each rival lost

2a. [var:] at 0656C — fixit Deus for Vulg. fixit Dominus

Migne prints the divine name Deus at Hbr 8:2 where the Clementine prints Dominus, and he prints it twice: once in the closing-quotation span at 0656B (Deus et non homo.) and once as the glossed lemma at 0656C (fixit Deus,). The gloss then keeps Deus a third time in its own prose (quia et vetus posuit Deus, sed per ministrum hominem).

  • Why it is marked and not merely cruxed: it is a substituted divine name in a proof-text, the class Pattern 14 exists for, and the three-fold internal agreement makes a one-off transcription slip in our source implausible.
  • The rival, and why it lost: the rival was crux-only, on the ground that Deus/Dominus changes no argument in this gloss. It lost because the reader of our English is entitled to know that the words on Migne's page are not the words of the Vulgate at a verse the whole chapter is expounding; the sense-neutrality argument would suppress the divergence rather than record it.
  • One marker, not three. It stands at 0656C, on the span Migne actually glosses.
  • ⚠ Plate gate: this asserts what Migne's page reads. Col. 0656B–C needs a plate read before ship.

2b. [var:] at 0658A — Tabernaculum enim sanctum for Vulg. Tabernaculum enim factum est primum

The strongest lemma divergence in the stint. Hbr 9:2 opens Tabernaculum enim factum est primum; Migne's lemma is Tabernaculum enim sanctum. — three words replaced by one, and the substituted word (sanctum) is the very word the preceding verse's lemmata have just been glossing (Sanctum, … Saeculare. at 9:1, and the verse itself closes quae dicitur Sancta).

  • Rival 1 (declined): read it as our source's loss and mark [ed:]. Rejected — [ed:] requires a demonstrated hole, and there is no hole here: the lemma is a complete, well-formed phrase and the gloss that follows attaches to it.
  • Rival 2 (declined): crux only. Rejected because the divergence is material: a reader taking the lemma as the verse would carry away a "holy tabernacle" that Hbr 9:2 does not contain.
  • Rendered as printed, "For the holy tabernacle."; the [var:] stands beside it and replaces nothing.
  • ⚠ Plate gate: col. 0658A needs a plate read before ship. If the plate reads factum est primum, the marker is withdrawn and this becomes a Corpus Corporum patch.

2c. [cj:] at 0659A — Ac operibus mortuis for Ab operibus mortuis

Hbr 9:14 reads emundabit conscientiam nostram ab operibus mortuis, ad serviendum Deo viventi. Migne's lemma sequence for that verse runs Obtulit semetipsum → Immaculatum → Ac operibus mortuis → Viventi, i.e. it walks the verse word by word; the lemma stands exactly where ab stands.

  • Ac is a real word, so Pattern 12 has nothing to wrap and addendum A forbids a [sic:] on a corpus argument. Pattern 18 is the right instrument: the printed word is real and the faithful English misleads, because ac makes the fragment a conjunction where ab governs the ablative that immediately follows it.
  • The rival, and why it lost: the rival was crux-only, on the ground that the fragment reads tolerably either way and the gloss (id est peccatis) treats operibus mortuis as a substantive regardless. It lost because [cj:] is additive — Migne's Ac keeps its English ("And by dead works") and the conjecture stands beside it — so nothing is concealed and the reader is not left to reconstruct why a preposition became a conjunction. The a/b–a/c confusion is also the cheapest possible type error, which is what makes the conjecture worth printing rather than burying.

4. [cj: …] — 0661D vetata

VERS. 20.-- Per velamen. Fides nostra est vetata, quia fides non habet meritum, cui humana ratio praebet experimentum.

vetata is a perfectly good participle of veto — "forbidden" — so Pattern 12 has nothing to wrap and Pattern 7 renders it. And the rendered page then says "Our faith is forbidden", which is not what the gloss asserts. This is Pattern 18's founding shape exactly (munde / mundo at 11081 @0030B).

  • The conjecture is velata, "veiled". The lemma being glossed is Per velamen, and the clause that follows is Gregory's commonplace that faith has no merit where human reason supplies proof — i.e. faith is veiled, and is meritorious precisely because it is.
  • The rival, and why it lost. vetata read as "forbidden" was tested against a possible sense "our faith is fenced off / made inviolable" — it fails, because veto takes a person or an action as its object, not a thing held back from view, and because that reading leaves Per velamen doing no work in its own gloss.
  • The gloss is additive (Pattern 18): Migne's word keeps its English and the conjecture stands beside it. Corpus frequency (vetata 1 · velata 0 · velamen/velamine 51) was not used to fire this — addendum A rules frequency worthless for real words. The argument is the sentence's own.
