§2 · THE DIVERGENCES DECLINED, each with its reason

Twelve spans diverge from the Clementine and took no [var:]. A blind reader will look here, so each is argued rather than listed.

  1. 0601C sicut justum est sentire, — Vulg. 7 sicut est mihi justum hoc sentire. The glossator has just quoted the same clause's opening as its own lemma (Sicut est mihi) and is now re-quoting the remainder inside his own sentence, dropping the pronoun he has already used. Word order and one dropped demonstrative, no sense at stake. Pattern 14's bar is a divergence that changes the sense.
  2. 0602A Pro vobis gratias ago: Confido. Vos autem volo scire, — not a citation at all but the glossator's précis of the epistle's movement so far, stitching vv. 3, 6 and 12. Marking a précis as a variant would misdescribe what is on the page. ⚑ But note what it proves: his own précis prints Vos autem volo scire, WITH the pronoun that his lemma at the head of the same paragraph drops. See §4.
  3. 0602C Christus mihi vivere, — the glossator's re-quotation of v. 21, reordered so that it can sit inside quia …. Same class as (1).
  4. 0603B Superiores, — agrees; the note is about the ENGLISH. The Vulgate clause is superiores sibi invicem arbitrantes and the fragment-expansion convention would give "esteeming others better than themselves." It is rendered "Superior," because the gloss beneath it expounds the WORD, twice (quo superior sit, quo videmur superiores esse), and an expansion into the familiar clause would leave the gloss with no hook — the Song-of-Songs ruling's case 2 (stylus/epistylium, murenulae/ferculum): where the gloss is about the word, the word stands.
  5. 0604B Deus enim. — Vulg. 13 Deus est enim. A two-word incipit that has lost the copula. Nothing in the gloss (an Augustinian excerpt on perseverance) turns on it, and the English "For it is God." supplies no word the Latin lacks: it renders Deus enim as the clause it opens.
  6. 0604D Qui Spiritu Dei, vel, Deo, — Vulg. 3 qui spiritu servimus Deo. This is a real divergence and it took no marker because Migne prints the received reading himself, in the same breath, as his own alternative (vel, Deo,). The [cj:] decline-class "anything the edition already glosses" is written for conjectures, but its argument is general: a reading the reader already meets does not need ours beside it.** Logged here so the decline is visible; a later editor who disagrees will find it named.
  7. 0605A Quanquam et ego, — Vulg. 4 quamquam ego habeam confidentiam et in carne. The et has moved from "also in the flesh" to "I also." A real shift of emphasis — but the gloss (Ne videatur Apostolus contemnere in aliis quod non habet) rests on neither position, and Migne re-quotes the lemma two lines later as the bare Quanquam. Declined as positional; named because it is the closest call in this section.
  8. 0607B modestia nota sit omnibus — vestra dropped and the quotation cut before hominibus so that the glossator can finish it his own way (fidelibus et infidelibus). The truncation is the gloss's method, not a variant text.
  9. 0607D superat omnem sensum: — the simplex for the Vulgate's compound exsuperat, in the glossator's own second, alternative construal. See §4 for how the English keeps the two verbs apart without importing the familiar English of either.
  10. 0607D quaecunque sunt amabilia, — sunt supplied, which the Vulgate carries only on the first member of the eight-fold series. A compositor's regularization at worst; no sense at stake.
  11. 0608C Qui recipit justum vel prophetam, and in nomine justi vel prophetae. — Matt. X, 41 is a doubled saying (prophet … prophet, just man … just man) and the glossator explicitly collapses it into one in order to make his point about datum versus fructus: non ait simpliciter … sed addidit. He is analysing the verse, not quoting it. A [var:] here would report as a textual variant what is an act of exegesis.
  12. 0608D In odorem. — Vulg. 18 odorem suavitatis, in apposition, without in. The preposition changes nothing the gloss uses (placent Deo ut odor suavissimus).

Also declined, and it is not a lemma: the Augustine excerpt at 0603C–D opens Exinanivit autem se formam servi accipiens against the Vulgate's sed semetipsum exinanivit, formam servi accipiens. Pattern 14 is for scripture quoted as scripture; this is Augustine's own sentence in the Enchiridion, reported by Migne as an excerpt. Rendered as printed, no marker.

The two places where the hymn's ENGLISH could have gone wrong, and what was done

(a) Non rapinam. — where the negation attaches. non governs rapinam, not arbitratus est: he did not judge it robbery. The English is "He thought it not robbery." — negative sitting on "robbery," which is where Migne prints it. The fragment is expanded to its clause (convention 3) and the expansion coincides with the traditional English because the printed Latin coincides with the Vulgate, which the register expressly permits; it was not reached for.

⚠ The gloss then offers a second construal in which the negation moves: Vel, non est arbitratus se aequalem Deo esse secundum humanitatem — here non is on the verb ("he did not judge himself equal to God according to his humanity"). (b) Factus. … qui erat infectus … — infectus is in-factus, "unmade", played against the lemma factus. It is a privative and it is carried: "he who was unmade according to the form of God." Rendering it "uncreated" would be the theologically expected word and would lose the play on the lemma; rendering it "tainted" (the other infectus) would be a different word altogether. §4 · THE CRUCES PROPER

⭐⭐⭐ 0607A (VERS. 1) · Gaudium, et consolatio — THE LEMMA IS DEFECTIVE AND ITS OWN GLOSS PROVES IT

Migne prints VERS. 1.-- Gaudium, et consolatio. Id est per quos in praesenti laetificor et in futuro coronabor. Discipulis enim in agone victoribus, dignus erit corona magister.

Phil. IV, 1 reads gaudium meum, et corona mea. Migne's lemma has consolatio where the Vulgate has corona — and the two sentences of gloss beneath it expound corona and nothing else: the master will be crowned, the master is worthy of the crown. The lemma word has no purchase on its own gloss.

Rendered as printed — "My joy, and my consolation." — with [var: Vulg. gaudium meum, et corona mea, "my joy and my crown"]. This is the single most valuable finding in the work and it is exactly the shape 7a″ describes in reverse: not a translator conforming a lemma to the familiar English, but a plate whose lemma has drifted off the verse its own gloss is written for.

⚠ No [cj:]. The bar is that the printed word makes the sentence assert something the author argues against, and consolatio asserts nothing false — the gloss simply answers a word that is not there. Pattern 14 is the right home, and Pattern 18's own boundary rule settles it: [var:] inside a quoted scripture, [cj:] in the author's own prose.

⭐⭐ 0602A (VERS. 12) · Scire autem volo — A DROPPED vos, CONTRADICTED SIX WORDS LATER

Vulg. I, 12 is Scire autem vos volo fratres. Without vos the lemma says "I wish to know," not "I wish you to know" — the subject of the infinitive changes, which is as material as a divergence gets in three words.

And the same paragraph carries the correction. The glossator's own précis two lines below prints Vos autem volo scire, with the pronoun. So the plate contains both readings, and the lemma is the defective one.

Rendered as printed ("But I wish to know, etc.") with [var: Vulg. Scire autem vos volo, "I wish you to know"]. The précis is rendered with its own pronoun ("But I wish you to know,"), because that is what Migne prints there. ⚠ The two English readings differ on the page and that is not an inconsistency — it is the plate.

⭐⭐ 0602B (VERS. 17) · Non puro animo for non sincere

Vulg. I, 17 is Christum annuntiant non sincere. Migne's lemma is Non puro animo, "not with a pure mind" — a different phrase, not a different form of the same word, and quite possibly an Old Latin reading. 0605D · resurrectionis Christi Et in hac vita — A FULL STOP MIGNE DOES NOT PRINT

The reading is odd on the page and it is odd on the plate's terms, not ours.

The clause it sits in is itself unconstruable as printed — quae nostra resurrectio est virtus et efficacia resurrectionis Christi ("which our resurrection is the power and efficacy of Christ's resurrection"), with quae and nostra resurrectio competing for the same slot. Rendered as closely as the words allow; no emendation, no marker — nothing here is a non-word, and the defect is syntactic, which Pattern 18a explicitly leaves declined.

  • The stop is Migne's and stays (Pattern 8).
  • The question mark is Migne's and stays; it is one of the five in the work.
  • The likely intention is quid de aliis? ("what of others?"), with me qui an eye-skip from the preceding de me. Declined for [cj:] because Pattern 18's bar is an English that asserts something false, and this English asserts nothing at all — 18a's own ruling puts anomalies that merely fail to construe outside the class, and this is not a negation or a correlative. 0607D · Exsuperat vs superat — TWO VERBS, TWO ENGLISH WORDS, NEITHER OF THEM THE FAMILIAR ONE

The lemma is Exsuperat omnem sensum (Vulgate); the glossator's alternative construal re-quotes it as the simplex superat omnem sensum. English has one obvious word for both, and the one obvious word ("passeth all understanding") is the Authorized Version's, which would conform the lemma AND erase the difference in a single stroke.

Taken: "It surpasseth all understanding." for the compound, "surmounts all understanding:" for the simplex. Neither is the received English phrase; the pair keeps Migne's two verbs distinguishable in the English as they are in the Latin. Declined: rendering both "surpasses" (the difference would vanish), and rendering the second "passeth" (the AV import, on the weaker of the two verbs).

⭐⭐⭐ Repair 1 · 0605D (VERS. 11) · Occurram — A VULGATE CONFORMATION THAT SEVERED THE GLOSS'S OWN HINGE

Was: "I may attain." — Douay's rendering of Phil. III, 11. Now: "I may meet."

The whole gloss exists to expound occurram in its literal sense: occurram Christo in judicio · occurrent Domino in aera deportati ab angelis — the running-out-to-meet of 1 Thess. IV, 17. I rendered that same verb "meet" twice within four lines and the lemma itself "attain", so an English reader could not see that the "meet" of the exposition was the lemma's own word.

⚠ Aggravated by a collision I had not noticed: "attain" was also doing duty for assequi at 0606C (illam non assequitur). Two Latin verbs on one English word — and the one that lost its identity was the one being glossed. That third move required changing ad quod pervenimus from "whereunto we have attained" to "whereunto we have come" in all three places.

⛔ This is 7a″ exactly, and it landed where I was not watching. §1 records the lemma as "agrees" with the Vulgate — which is true, and which is precisely why the guard was down: a lemma that agrees with the Vulgate still has to be TRANSLATED from the Latin. 7a″ is usually described as conforming a divergent lemma to the familiar English; this is the other half of the class, where the Latin agrees and the familiar English is still the wrong word because the gloss is built on the Latin verb's own sense. ⚠ [var: Douay "I may attain"] was offered and is DECLINED. ⭐ THE TWO-READING SITE, DECIDED · 0606D · ut omnes idem de illo sapiamus

de illo is masculine or neuter — the form does not decide it, and a flag is not a decision. Decided: NEUTER. The English now reads "that we all be of the same mind concerning that same thing whereunto we have come by faith and knowledge" (was: "concerning him").

For the neuter:

  1. The relative that follows is neuter and immediately adjacent — de illo, ad quod pervenimus. Read as one referent the sentence is seamless; read as two it has a masculine antecedent and a neuter relative side by side with nothing between them.
  2. The subject of the whole gloss is a thing, not a person — the regula disciplinae, and quod commune sit et modestum in veritate evangelica, three lines above; and the lemma it closes on is et in eadem permaneamus regula.
  3. No person has been named for several lines, and the nearest masculine noun (ad cultum Dei) is a genitive inside another clause, reached across an intervening subdit.
  4. The paragraph before it has live neuter-ish deixis in istam … illam for the two righteousnesses, so ille is already working as a pointer rather than as a person.

Against it, honestly: the paragraph's frame is ad cultum Dei excogitaveritis, so "of one mind concerning him" construes and is not strained; and if the Glossa meant the neuter, de eo or de eodem would have been the ordinary choice. The reading was taken on (1) — the adjacency of ad quod — which is textual rather than thematic, and a later editor who prefers the masculine has the case for it above, in full, rather than a bare flag.
