These are the edition's working notes, published as they were written. They record, work by work, where Migne's plate is defective or the reading uncertain: the printed form, what the English does with it, and why. They are not a groomed apparatus. Where a note argues with itself, withdraws an earlier decision, or leaves a question open, that is the record of how the reading was actually reached, and it is left standing — a conjecture that is visible can be checked, and one that has been tidied away cannot.

The English never silently repairs the page. A construable but wrong reading is rendered literally and the conjecture is recorded here, never applied to the text. How the English is produced and checked →

1d · The one scripture divergence that is neither Migne's nor the Clementine's

0675C · Prov. 24:16. Migne prints Septies in die cadet justus et resurget; the Clementine has Septies enim cadet justus, et resurget. The added in die comes from Luke 17:4 (septies in die), and the gloss depends on it: the next clause reads sicut quotidiana est offensio, ita quotidiana est medela. Rendered as printed ("Seven times in the day"). No [var:], and the reason is a judgment, not an oversight: this is not Migne's compositor slipping but the glossator quoting a conflated form he then argues from, and a marker saying "Vulg. lacks in die" would invite the reader to discount the very word the argument turns on. The divergence is recorded here instead, where the argument can be given. ⚑ Note also that the gloss's closing sed impii corruunt in malum echoes the same verse's impii autem corruent in malum in the present tense, outside any italic lemma.

§3 · THE [cj: …] — 0678A, a dropped negative in the gloss on Propter quod

Migne prints, on Jac. 4:2 (propter quod non postulatis):

Propter haec, scilicet quod Deum digne postulatis; si enim illum pia intercessione postularetis etiam temporalia ad usum necessaria, et non solum sempiterna daret.

Rendered as printed, the gloss says the readers do ask God worthily, which is the opposite of the verse it glosses and is contradicted by its own next clause: si … postularetis … daret is contrary to fact, so they are not asking. A non has fallen out of the setting.

[cj: postulatis; read non postulatis, "you ask not"]

Which word, and why the rival lost. The alternative was to gloss digne (read non digne, "not worthily"), which also construes. It is rejected because the lemma itself is Propter quod, whose verse reads propter quod non postulatis: the word that fell out is the verse's own non, standing where the verse stands it, before the verb. Glossing digne would put our conjecture on a word the verse does not carry. The marker opens on postulatis, which is verbatim in the Latin twin, so the convention's guard is satisfied — the 7561 @0648 precedent for a dropped word exactly.

Bar check. Pattern 18a: a lost negation is inside the class the extension admits, and incoherence is not a defence there.

0675C · alii male docendo, et aliis male vivendo

Se illis … alii male docendo, et aliis male vivendo, et aliis modis — the second member wants the nominative alii to answer the first, and prints the dative/ablative aliis, which then sits oddly beside the genuine ablative aliis modis three words later. Rendered for the sense the parallel requires ("some by teaching ill, and others by living ill, and in other ways") and not marked: nothing is broken type, and Pattern 18 is scoped away from case/number slips that merely fail to construe (18a's declined list). Logged so a plate reader can settle it.

0676B · concinantur — read as concinnantur, and the rival named

…cuncta fere facinora, aut concinantur, ut latrocinia, stupra: aut patrantur … aut defenduntur… Corpus control: concinantur occurs once, here; concinn- forms occur nine times (concinnant, concinnare, concinnatum, concinnabat, concinnandas…), and single-n concin- forms in the corpus are all from concino, "to sing together" (concinamus, concinat). Rendered "are contrived", i.e. read as concinnantur with the medieval simplification of the geminate, which is a Pattern 9 orthographic convention and not an emendation. ⛔ The rival was to render concino literally — "almost all crimes are sung together, as robberies, ravishments" — and gloss it [cj:]. It is rejected because the triad concinantur / patrantur / defenduntur is a contrive-commit-defend sequence in which only concinnare has a slot, and because "sung" is not a sense concino carries of a crime in any Latinity. Stated here rather than hidden, because the reader meets an English word whose Latin looks like a different verb.

0672B · nec tamen omnino desistit

Negation on desistit, scoped by omnino, with the sed correlative answering (sed sedet in occultis). English keeps the scope: "nor yet does he altogether desist" — not "does not desist at all", which would deny the sed clause. Checked against the 8960 @0734B failure shape and cleared.

0672B · Sicut qui tentatus superat, praemia vitae

The first limb of the comparison has no verb at all in Migne; the second supplies incurrit. Rendered with a bracketed English copula, "[has] the rewards of life", rather than promoting a verb into the running text.

0678C · sic aerugo vestras carnes

The same shape: sic aerugo vestras carnes has no verb. Rendered "so rust [consumes] your flesh."

0680A · quid ergo multi fideles multis orationibus?

The usque ad tail ends on a verbless question. Not completed — rendered elliptically, "what then of many faithful men with many prayers?", keeping Migne's question mark. The abridgment removes words; it does not license us to supply a verb (the ferias lesson).

0678A · Quasi dicat, lex non fecit

Two readings construe: "the law did not do so" (i.e. did not forbid detraction, which is what the detractor implicitly asserts) and "the law did it not [well]". The first is taken, on the strength of the immediately preceding Quae detractionem prohibuit, which supplies the verb's content. The other is not excluded and nothing else in the paragraph turns on it.

§7 · RENDERING DECISIONS THAT A READER MIGHT QUERY

7a · The three bare Nolite lemmata — Ruth §1.2 applied, with one stated exception

  • 0673C Nolite. Quasi dicat: (Jac. 2:1). The italic span in Migne swallows the glossator's own formula Quasi dicat:; the span is preserved exactly as printed, so the English italic reads Do not hold the faith in respect of persons. As if he should say: The bare Nolite is expanded to the phrase it opens (Jac. 2:1 nolite in personarum acceptione habere fidem), on Ruth's Non te → "Let no man see thee" precedent: "Do not." is not English.
  • 0675B Nolite. and Nolite plures. (Jac. 3:1) are rendered minimally, "Be ye not." and "Be ye not many.", and this is the exception to the rule just applied. The reason: Migne glosses the bare prohibition first and plures second, so expanding both to the verse ("Be ye not many masters") would collapse two distinct lemmata into one identical English and destroy the distinction the double lemma exists to make. The rule bends where obeying it would erase what it is protecting.
  • 0674C Superexaltat autem. is rendered "But mercy exalteth itself." — the subject misericordia supplied from Jac. 2:13, the verb's own extent kept. The fuller "above judgment" was declined as more supply than the fragment reaches.

7b · 0673B · Per saeculum intelligit mundum — the one added italic span

The gloss defines saeculum by mundus, and English has one word for both, so a faithful all-English rendering ("By the world he understands the world") explains nothing. The glossed word is therefore carried in italic Latin: "By saeculum he understands the world, or all the things which are in the world." This is the stylus/epistylium (11551) and murenulae/ferculum (10804) precedent — where the gloss is the word, the word stands in Latin. ⚑ It is the only place in this work where the English carries an italic span the Latin does not, and the band check at 0673B reports it (LA 4 / EN 5); that delta is this and nothing else.

7c · 0679A · Sufferentiam Job rendered "the patience of Job"

sufferentia is "endurance", and the familiar English is exactly the pressure 7a″ warns about. The traditional wording is nevertheless allowed here because Migne's Latin is verbatim Clementine (Jac. 5:11 Sufferentiam Job audistis), which is the register's own scripture-echo licence and Ruth's Aethiopia praeveniet precedent. Nothing in the gloss turns on patience against endurance; what it turns on is sufferentiam against finem, and that contrast is carried.

7d · 0676A–B, 0675C · magisterium fixed as "mastership"

magisteria / magisterium occurs four times (0675B ×1 plural, 0676B ×2, and the argument's mandatione magistrorum). One English is used throughout — mastership, with magistrorum as "masters" — because the chapter's whole argument is about the one office and elegant variation would break it.

7e · 0671B · A perfectioribus incipit de extrinsecis tentationibus — a call declined

a perfectioribus may be "from the more perfect [men]" or "from the more perfect [matters]". Rendered literally, "He begins from the more perfect, concerning outward temptations", which carries both. Declined rather than resolved: nothing later in the work disambiguates it, and the sequence it announces (outward temptations first, inward at VERS. 13 Hactenus de tentationibus exterioribus) works under either reading.

7f · Column anchors that had to be split away from their Latin word

Three anchors fall at a point English word order cannot reproduce; each is placed inside the same phrase, as near its Latin neighbour as the syntax allows, per 2b. Recorded so a reader who compares the columns knows it was a choice: 0671D temporalia [0671D] sunt → "are the temporal [0671D] things"; 0677B bonis [0677B] consentiens → "consenting to good [0677B] things"; 0677C quasi semente [0677C] aspergit → "as with [0677C] seed" (the verb aspergit stands after the anchor in Latin and before the phrase in English, so no placement can put the anchor before it). All other anchors stand immediately beside the English word that renders the Latin word they precede, checked one at a time.

### ⭐ BATCH RULING — lemma expansion is NOT the defect; CONFORMATION is This class was raised independently by three of the four blind readers in this batch (here, in 1 Peter at 0687C, and as a six-site cluster in Ecclesiastes), each proposing that expansions be contracted to the printed catchword. The boundary was already settled last batch, tested from both sides — one reader flagged expansions as repairs, another flagged a non-expansion as a defect, both declined — and it stands: the defect is never expansion or its absence, it is CONFORMATION. Convention 3 licenses the expansion; what is forbidden is following the familiar English Bible where Migne's own plate diverges from it. None of the sites raised in this batch is that. ⚑ Worth noting for the next brief: the readers' "control" is often not a control.
