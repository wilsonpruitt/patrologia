1d · Fragment expansion — where the boundary was drawn, and why

Brief convention 3 expands a fragment only where the stump is not English. Ruth's shipped practice is the control: it renders Elevata. as "She lifted up." and Quocunque. as "Whithersoever." — minimal — and expands only Non te., which is unrenderable. Applied here:

  • Rendered minimally (renderable as they stand): Quia non. → That it was not. · Neque. → Neither. · Ideo. → Therefore. · Pervenit. → It came. · Eos. → Them. · Fidelis. → Faithful. · probate, → prove,. ⛔ Quia non. was not expanded to "That it was not vain": inanis is not in the lemma, and the gloss immediately supplies the idea itself (quae inanis). Supplying it in the lemma would have put a Vulgate word on the page Migne did not print there.
  • Expanded, because the stump is not English: Spiritum nolite. → Extinguish not the Spirit. ("The Spirit do not." is not a sentence in any register.) The expansion is exactly the words of the second, whole lemma at 0620B (Spiritum nolite exstinguere), so the two lemmata read alike on the page, which is what the Latin means.
  • Verbs supplied to fragments on the Ruth model: Vocavit, → He called, · Sed impedivit. → But he hindered. (subject Satanas, 2:18 — see §3) · Vos enim imitatores, → For you became imitators, · Descendet. → He shall descend.
  • Comma / etc. carried as printed on every fragment that has one: Quales fuerimus, · Possemus vobis, · Vos enim imitatores, · Ore, aspectu, · Quae est, · Quam enim gratiarum, · a nobis, · sic et ambuletis, · Qui etiam dedit, · Rogamus autem vos fratres, · Induti loricam, · In omnes, · probate, · Non sumus noctis neque tenebrarum, · Spiritum nolite exstinguere, · Ipse autem Deus pacis sanctificet vos per omnia, · Quomodo vos oporteat ambulare,.

1e · One rendering fixed across the work

querela = "complaint", never "blame." It is the lemma at 0617C (3:13) and again at 0620C (5:23), and the second passage is an argument about the word: Aliud est esse sine peccato … Aliud est esse sine querela … de quo … justa querela esse non possit. Douay's "without blame" at 3:13 would have broken the link between the two columns and left the 0620C argument glossing a word that no longer stands above it. Both lemmata read Without complaint.

§2 · NEGATION AUDIT — 49 particles carried, none invented

Every non/nec/neque/nisi/nunquam/nullus/nihil/ne/noli(te)/nedum/nequimus Migne prints was located in the Latin and matched to its English. The two lemmata that carry negation on their face are exact: Non quasi hominibus placentes. → Not as pleasing men. (2:4, agrees with the Vulgate word for word) and Non sumus noctis neque tenebrarum, → We are not of the night nor of darkness, (5:5, both negatives standing, and the neque is not collapsed into the first).

Sites where the English could easily have lost one, and did not:

  • 0616C Hoc nisi ipse operetur in nobis, vitare nequimus → "Unless he himself work this in us, we cannot avoid it." Two negations in six words, both carried.
  • 0616C amor non sentit, nisi qui ei bellum indixerit → "he perceives not, save he who has declared war upon it." nisi rendered "save", not folded into the non.
  • 0616C si cuiquam facile est laudem non cupere … difficile est tamen ea non delectari → "not to covet praise … not to be delighted with it." The sentence's whole point is the pair.
  • 0615D (2nd) Non dico in avaritiam, sed nec feci vel dixi → "I say not, in covetousness, but neither did I do or say."
  • 0615D (2nd) quibus nec facultas erat, nec pudor poscendi → "who had neither the means nor the shame of demanding."
  • 0619B qualis ante non fuit, nec post erit → "as was not before, nor shall be after."
  • 0619C nos qui non sumus noctis non debemus dormire → "we who are not of the night ought not to sleep."
  • 0620A Justus nunquam desinit orare, nisi desinat justus esse → "never ceases to pray, unless he cease to be just."
  • 0618B et non de iderabitis rem alterius, nedum rogetis vel tollatis → "and you will not desire … much less ask for it or take it away." The negation survives the [sic:] beside it — see §4 and 7a⁗.

⚑ One English "not" that answers no printed particle, declared: 0617A convertentur Judaei nonnulli → "not a few of the Jews will be converted." nonnulli is one word meaning "several, a good many"; "not a few" is its literal English and the negation is inside the lexeme, not a separate non on the plate. Flagged here so a particle-count check does not read it as an invention. "Some" was the alternative and was declined as less literal.

§4 · CARRIED TYPE — three [sic: …], and what each swallowed

Each entry states what the marker took out of the clause and what was put back beside it in plain words, per 7a⁗.

1. 0618B · et non de iderabitis rem alterius → and you will not desire [sic: de iderabitis] another's property

Pattern 10 split type for desiderabitis. The whole broken run is carried, spacing included. ⚠ 7a⁗ questions 1 and 2 both fire here: the marked run is the clause's only verb and it stands under a printed non. So the English carries "will not desire" in plain words beside the marker — the negation is outside the quarantine, where a reader meets it. Conjecture: desiderabitis (near-certain; the clause continues nedum rogetis vel tollatis, "much less ask for it or take it away", which only makes sense as a climax on desire).

2. 0619A (chunk 1, 5:1) · qua hora dici vel noctis → at what hour of the day [sic: dici] or of the night

dici is a real Latin form (present passive infinitive of dico) with no grammatical slot here: the phrase needs a genitive parallel to noctis. Pattern 12's first test — "it has no grammatical slot" — so it is carried, not silently read. Conjecture: diei. ⚑ Rendering it straight as "of the day" was the alternative and was declined: English would have shown the reader nothing at all, which is Pattern 12's second test (it renders only by hiding the defect). The sense "of the day" stands beside the marker so the phrase is not left with a hole where its noun was.

3. 0619D (chunk 1) · ita egestas dum satutari quaerit → so poverty, while it seeks to be satisfied [sic: satutari], turns aside from justice

satutari is not a word. Carried under Pattern 7's non-word rule. Conjecture: saturari, "to be filled/satisfied" — the antithesis is divitiae … egestas, riches breeding negligence and want being driven from justice by seeking to be filled, and the t/r slip is a single sort. ⚠ 7a⁗ q1: the marked run was the infinitive governed by quaerit, so the sense is supplied beside the marker rather than left inside it.

§5 · MIGNE'S OWN PRINTING — reproduced, not tidied

a · The out-of-sequence column anchor [0615D], and it is not the band-arithmetic false positive. Chunk 0's anchors run 0615B · 0615C · 0615D · 0616B · 0616C · 0615D · 0617A · …. The second [0615D] falls inside the VERS. 5 gloss of CAPUT II (qui gloriam non ad praesens, [0615D] sed in futuro quaerebat), between a 0616C and a 0617A anchor. Reproduced verbatim and in place. It is recorded, not reported as a defect: runbook false positive 1 covers recurring column numbers, and no text is broken mid-word or mid-construction across it — the sentence runs through the anchor intact in both languages. (Note also that 0616A and 0616D never appear; that is the same false-positive class and equally not reported.)

b · The verse numbering runs one behind the Clementine chapters in CAPUT IV, and it is his. VERS. 12 carries Nolumus autem (Clem. 4:13), VERS. 13 carries Per Jesum adducet cum eo (4:14), VERS. 14 carries Eos / Qui dormierunt (4:15), VERS. 15 carries Quoniam ipse Dominus and In tuba Dei (4:16), VERS. 16 carries In nubibus (4:17). A consistent −1 offset across five consecutive addresses is a versification, not five misprints — and CAPUT V is back in step (VERS. 13 = 5:13, VERS. 15 = 5:15, VERS. 23 = 5:23). Nothing renumbered; the addresses pass through verbatim by the settled convention. Recorded so a reader checking a lemma against a modern Bible in CAPUT IV knows to look one verse on.

c · A full stop breaking a relative clause — 0620B/C, and it was NOT merged. Migne prints Ut nihil agatis ejus ministerio. Unde aliquis conqueratur. The unde clause is plainly the continuation ("that you do nothing by its ministry whereof anyone may complain"), and joining the two would have produced smooth, readable English. ⛔ Pattern 8 forbids deleting a printed mark, and the 11542 @1041D and 11436 @0349A precedents are exactly this. The full stop is MIGNE'S and it was KEPT. Rendered as two sentences: "That you do nothing by its ministry. Whence anyone may complain." ⛔ Do not "fix" this later — joining the two sentences would delete a printed mark, and a blind reader independently confirmed the decision (§11). This is the site in the work where the English is deliberately least smooth, and it is the one a blind reader should check first for having been quietly repaired.

d · Migne's three question marks, all preserved, none supplied. 0617B Quanti est hoc ardoris? (inside the CHRYS. note) → "Of how great ardour is this?" · 0617C Si modo gloria, quid apud verum judicem? → "If glory now, what before the true judge?" · 0619C Quid enim prodest honorem sine fructu habere? → "For what does it profit to have honour without fruit?" All three are direct questions in the Latin, so Pattern 8b's colon recast was not needed anywhere in this work. Two indirect questions that Migne closes with a period keep the period and take no mark: an aestate, an hieme, an die vel nocte and qua hora … venturus sit Christus (both 5:1).

e · Person shifts left standing. 0615D Inde ita certi ac si jam videatis, et ita eos animat — second person plural, then third person plural, in eleven words. Rendered as printed. The clause also has no verb (certi with none), so the English supplies a bracketed one: "Thence [you are] so certain…". 0619C's singular-to-plural shift (qui dormit … traxerunt eos) is the same class and is at §3.

f · inter nos, not inter vos. 0616C deinde sua inter nos prudenti conversatione — "then his prudent conversation among us", in a gloss about how Paul behaved among the Thessalonians, where vos is what the argument wants. Rendered as printed. Declined for a [cj:]: the bar is that the English must assert something the author is arguing against, and inter nos is defensible on its own terms (the Glossa's "we" for the apostolic party, cf. a nobis, id est a me et Silvano at 0617D). It is a pronoun slip, not a negation or a correlative, and Pattern 18a's declined classes cover exactly this.

g · Orthography left alone under Pattern 9: caetero, acolythum, Tichicum (for Tychicus), charitas, incoepit, econtra, quandoque, Intantum, nonnulli, pythonem. Names are rendered as Migne spells them: Tichicus, Onesimus, Silvanus, Michael, Elias, Enoch.

§6 · TWO REFERENCES THE GLOSS GETS ODD, BOTH LEFT AS PRINTED

a · 0618C — unde et in alia scribitur Epistola: Nos qui vivimus. The words nos qui vivimus are in this epistle (4:15 and 4:17), not another one; and 4:15 is the very verse the next lemma (VERS. 14 Eos) glosses. Rendered literally: "whence also it is written in another Epistle: We who live." ⛔ Not repaired to "in this Epistle" or "elsewhere". Two innocent readings exist — alia as "another passage" loosely, or the compiler recalling 1 Cor 15:51–52, which is the resurrection locus with the same trumpet — and neither is certain enough to touch the text. Recorded for a reader who will notice.

b · 0619B — the Antichrist report attributed to Daniel and the Apocalypse. tribus annis et dimidio is Daniel's time-times-and-half; Elias et Enoch slain, and the tribulation formula, are the Apocalypse and Matthew respectively. The gloss's sicut in verbis Danielis invenitur … sicut et in Apocalypsi habetur distributes them loosely. Rendered as printed; no [var:], because neither is presented as a verbatim quotation of either book.

§7 · PATTERN 17 — one number shift, and it is in the Latin

0620B moves from plural to singular inside a single gloss: Vos subditi prophetias illorum nolite spernere … sed probate … noli prohibere loqui quod sentit. The plural imperatives address the congregation; noli is singular and addresses one man — whoever has the Spirit's word in his ear. Rendered "despise not … prove … forbid thou him not to speak what he perceives." Thou is the singular, and it recovers a distinction the plate makes and modern English cannot. Everything else in the work is plural vos and reads "you"; there is no second-person singular anywhere else in either chunk.

11a · The two that matter, stated as MECHANISMS — the unexpressed subject again

Both repairs below are the same class, and it is the class that dominates this whole series. The mechanism is worth more to the next translator than the two sites are.

⭐ MECHANISM 1 — a bare accusative participle takes the person of the LEMMA, not of the narration around it. 0620D prints Oro ut sanctificet, ut sanctificatos servet. Nothing in the sentence says who is sanctified. The surrounding gloss is third-person narration about God (sanctificet, servet, verus est), so third person is what the ear reaches for, and "that he may keep them sanctified" was written. It is wrong. The antecedent of a bare accusative in a gloss is almost always in the lemma above or in the verse the lemma comes from — here 5:23 sanctificet vos per omnia — and this sentence then confirms it in its own closing words, qui vocavit vos ad hoc ut sancti essetis. The tell is countable: the wrong reading made the gloss go 2nd → 3rd → 2nd person inside eleven words. A supplied pronoun that forces the sentence to change person and change back is wrong; look for one that does not. Repaired to "that he may keep you sanctified."

⭐ MECHANISM 2 — getting the referent right is only half the job; if the Latin cannot express it, the supply must be VISIBLE. 0618D prints suos ad bellum invitabit, ut expugnent adversarios, et effugiant a facie Domini atque mittantur in gehennam. All three subjunctives grammatically share suos, and Christ's own fleeing the Lord's face into gehenna is impossible, so the subject must shift to adversarios — which is accusative and cannot be a subject at all. The original English said "and that these may flee", which is the right sense but reads as though Migne had written a clean subject change. He had not: the construction is broken on the plate. Repaired to "and that [the adversaries] may flee", using the supplied -words bracket convention. The rule the two mechanisms make together: name the referent from the lemma, then ask whether the printed Latin can carry it — and if it cannot, bracket it, because otherwise the repair is invisible and the plate's defect is concealed.

11b · The four smaller repairs

  1. 0618D, colon restored at a correlative joint. Migne: convocabat populum Hebraeorum: sic haec tuba convocabit… The colon had been demoted to a comma. It is the hinge of a sicut … : sic … correlative and carries structure — the same shape as the lost ita … ut of the previous batch. ⚑ Position beats tally: a colon at a correlative joint is not interchangeable with a comma, whatever the corpus-wide punctuation rate is.
  2. 0618A, VERS. 4 Vas suum. Migne prints Id est uxorem, possideat dominando ei, et hoc…; the English had printed a colon for the first comma and a semicolon for the second. Both of Migne's commas restored: "That is, his wife, let him possess her by ruling her, and this, In sanctification,…" One of the two was a mark supplied where he prints a comma, which is Pattern 8's "never supply" and not a rate question.
  3. 0617A, VERS. 16 Pervenit. quia ira Dei pervenit ut excaecentur had been rendered "has come upon them". Migne's gloss prints no goal; the Vulgate (2:16 pervenit … super illos) does. The mildest form of the 7a″ conformation class, and still the class — a phrase carried into the gloss from the verse the gloss is not quoting. Repaired to "because the wrath of God has come, that they may be blinded."
  4. 0616A, sed in multa sollicitudine pro adversis had been rendered "amid adversities". pro is causal; "amid" converts a cause into a circumstance for no gain. Repaired to "on account of adversities."
