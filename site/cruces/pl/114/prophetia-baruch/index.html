§1 · WHAT THIS WORK IS — complete, not a stub

Migne prints the preface only for Baruch, and he says why himself, in the work's one note: Antiqua exemplaria Glossae ordinariae textum tantummodo Baruch exhibent, nulla comitante expositione — the old copies of the Glossa ordinaria give the text of Baruch alone, with no exposition accompanying it. There is no missing material. The work is complete as Migne printed it: a preface, and an editorial note stating that the gloss tradition carries nothing further on this book. Anyone reading this page should take the brevity as the Glossa's own testimony about Baruch, not as an abridgment of ours.

The parallel case in the corpus is 8948 (Esther) @0748C, where the same editorial voice records that the ancient copies carry no exposition from that point to the end of the book and that chapters 11–16 are therefore passed over. Same class of note, same handling (below). It is a hint, not an authority — but it is the same editor writing about the same manuscript tradition, and the two agree.

§2 · NO VERSE LEMMATA IN THIS WORK — the lemma pass had nothing to pass over

There is not one VERS. n.-- address in this work, and not one italic lemma. The whole text is Migne's own continuous prose preface. This is stated rather than left silent because a reader of a Glossa work legitimately expects a lemma pass and a divergence list:

  • Glossa conventions 1–3 (address passes through verbatim · lemma Englished · fragmentary lemma rendered as the phrase it stands for) do not fire anywhere in this work.
  • 7a″ — the named-lemma divergence list, normally the most valuable deliverable — has an empty domain here. No Vulgate collation was performed because no scripture is quoted: the preface talks about Baruch and the epistle of Jeremiah, it never quotes them. No [var:] is possible, and its absence is a real zero, not an unchecked field.
  • sources/vulgate/clementine-flat.txt was accordingly not consulted for a lemma. That is not a decline for want of a witness; there is nothing to collate.

§3 · THE [nt:] DECISION (Pattern 15) — and why it is not close

The single [n:] in the Latin twin reads, with Migne's own internal spacing:

[n: ( Antiqua exemplaria Glossae ordinariae textum tantummodo Baruch exhibent, nulla comitante expositione. )]

Converted to [nt:] and translated, at the same marker index, in the English chunk only. The Latin twin is untouched and keeps [n:].

Pattern 15 decides by content, not length: [n:] is reserved for citations — locators into a source corpus. This note contains no locator of any kind. No book, no chapter, no work title, no Vide, no tom., no cf. It is a finite verb making an assertion about the manuscript tradition — exemplaria … exhibent, with an ablative absolute stating what does not accompany the text. That is a scholion, and left in Latin it would leave an English reader a Latin sentence in the middle of the page carrying the one fact that explains why the page is short.

The rival reading, and why it lost. The note could be read as a see-under — an editorial cross-reference of the kind 8948 keeps as [n:] (Vide inter opera B. Hieronymi, tom. VIII.). It lost on two counts. First, that note names a place a reader can go (inter opera … tom. VIII); this one names none. Second, this note's content is precisely the proposition the reader needs in order to understand the work, and Pattern 15 exists for exactly that case. Judged by content, the call is unambiguous.

The [nt:] renders as .notecite.prose, and per Pattern 15 the indexer keeps prose notes out of fontes[]: this note is not a source and must not be indexed as one.

§4 · THE SCOPE CALL — in Hebraeo canone non habetur, sed tantum in Vulgata editione

This is the one sentence in the work with real doubt in it, and it is brief-test 2a's named population: a negative near a sed … tantum pivot.

Printed: Liber iste … in Hebraeo canone non habetur, sed tantum in Vulgata editione, similiter et epistola Jeremiae.

  • The reading taken: the non governs the locus — the prepositional phrase in Hebraeo canone — not the verb habetur. Migne is not denying that the book is had; he is saying where it is had and where it is not. The proof is structural, not stylistic: the second limb sed tantum in Vulgata editione has no verb of its own and must borrow habetur by ellipsis. A verb that the affirming limb borrows cannot be the thing the denying limb negates. The sed … tantum correlative pivots on the two ablative phrases, and the negative sits on the first of the pair.
  • The rival, and why it lost: reading non habetur as a flat denial of existence ("is not had at all, except in the Vulgate"). It construes, and in isolation it reads perfectly — which is what makes it dangerous. It loses because it makes tantum modify a bare exception rather than the phrase it stands in, and because it would make the second limb assert existence rather than restrict location, which the shared verb forbids.
  • How the English holds the scope: "is not contained in the Hebrew canon, but only in the Vulgate edition." The English negative stands before the verb, as the Latin's does, and the "but only" correlative fixes it on the phrase — the same construction the brief cites as handled correctly at 8960 @0740B (non affliguntur ex desperatione, sed…), where a non standing before the verb is shown by the sed limb to govern the phrase. The verb ellipsis in the second limb is carried into English as ellipsis, so nothing is supplied.
  • ⚠ What was NOT done: the sentence was not recast into "is found only in the Vulgate" or any other positive paraphrase. That would read better and would delete a printed negative (Pattern 7a).

§5 · THE UNEXPRESSED SUBJECT — hic scripta sunt

Propter notitiam autem legentium hic scripta sunt. The subject is unexpressed and the participle is neuter plural against a singular liber iste. Named: the subject is the two books just listed — liber iste (masc.) and epistola Jeremiae (fem.) — for which Latin takes the neuter plural as standard. This is what tells us the sentence is about both books, not about Baruch alone.

Rendered "they have been written here." English "they" supplies no gender and no number beyond what the Latin prints; naming the books explicitly ("these two books") was rejected as supplying text Migne does not print. The following indicant is plural and agrees, which confirms the antecedent.

§8 · CANDIDATES RAISED AND REJECTED

  • praenotatur — rendered "is headed with the name of Baruch." Not a coinage: the corpus is its own control, and the same ablative-of-name construction is already rendered "headed with" at 11061/0002 @0016C (auctorum suorum nominibus praenotatur → "headed with the names of its authors") and @0016D (auctoris sui titulo praenotatur). "Entitled" (8930/0002's rendering, with sub … nomine) is equally attested; "headed" was preferred because 11061's construction is the ablative without sub, exactly ours. No marker; not a defect of any kind.
  • Vulgata editione — "the Vulgate edition." Considered and rejected: lowercasing to "the common edition." Migne is naming Jerome's version as against the Hebrew canon, and the contrast is with a named canon, so the proper name stands.
  • novissimis temporibus — "the last times," the received English. Not "the newest times."
  • No suspiciously-tidy form (brief test 4). Fifty words, all of them ordinary ninth-to-nineteenth-century orthography, nothing rough anywhere in the chunk against which a tidy form would stand out. No plate read requested, and this is a checked negative, not an unexamined field — the page was already read at 300 dpi for §7 and the printed text agrees with our twin.
  • No em-dash was introduced; none was needed.
  • No [f:] tags. This is not a florilegium and Pattern 4 does not apply (runbook false positive 4).

Blind read (2026-08-23) — 1 site, ours, repaired

A fenced reader (no access to this file, no grep of src/english/) read the chunk against the Latin. One site, and it is ours.

col. 0064, in the [nt:] note. Migne prints Antiqua exemplaria Glossae ordinariae textum tantummodo Baruch exhibent, nulla comitante expositione. The English read "exhibit the text of Baruch only, with no accompanying exposition" and now reads "exhibit only the text of Baruch, with no accompanying exposition."

tantummodo sits second in its noun phrase and restricts textum … Baruch as a whole. The contrast set is fixed independently by the ablative absolute that follows — nulla comitante expositione names the thing excluded, and it is the exposition. English post-nominal "only" attaches by default to the phrase on its left, so "the text of Baruch only" asserts Baruch and no other book — a claim about which books the old copies contain, not about whether they carry a gloss. A reader can retro-fit the right scope from the following clause, but the English's default host was not the Latin's. Nothing was added and nothing dropped; every count passed.

⚑ Corpus control, class named: textum tantummodo Baruch matches the ordinary shape throughout src/latin/ — tertiam tantummodo partem, tres tantummodo cubitos, simplicem tantummodo historiam, sensum tantummodo moralem — particle in second position, restricting the whole phrase. The sed tantum in the body is offered only as corroboration, and it is the same class (a restrictive particle inside a phrase); the finding rests on the ablative absolute.

⛔⛔ The lesson, and it is the recorded one arriving again: this work's cruces contains a careful adjudication of the scope of non habetur … sed tantum in the body — correct, argued from the correlative, with the smoother positive paraphrase explicitly rejected for deleting a printed negative — and the SAME CLASS in the note went straight past, in the very note the translator was converting to [nt:]. Using the apparatus somewhere is not evidence it was used where it was needed. Ruth's four silent repairs were made by a translator marking [sic:] correctly two columns away; here the distance was two lines.

⚑ Also checked and found clean by the reader, worth recording as checked negatives: both printed negatives hosted correctly (non on the locative phrase, nulla on expositione); both supplied "they"s traced to the mixed-gender pair liber + epistola taking a neuter plural; the bare [0064] anchor verified in position against notitiam / "knowledge", not merely present; punctuation matched band by band; and novissimis temporibus rendered "the last times", NOT conformed to the familiar "the last days" — the 7a″ trap correctly avoided.
