1. Work-wide conventions (fixed at merge)

Migne prints an Old Latin / LXX-derived Song of Songs throughout, and Ambrose's exposition repeatedly argues from the divergent word. The controlling rule for this work is therefore: one English word per Latin word, held across all forty chunks, because the moment two Latin words share an English word the author's own argument — and our [var: …] apparatus, which exists to show Migne's Latin against the Vulgate's — stops meaning anything.

1.2 What the merge actually had to repair

Four divergences between agents were found and reconciled in the English chunks, not merely noted here. Each is recorded so a later sweep does not churn it back.

  1. proxima mea had three Englishes. "my neighbour" (0005, 0006, 0007, 0019), "my neighbor" (0011, 0012, 0023, 0024) and "my nearest one" (0025, three occurrences plus the gloss Nearest by charity). All now read neighbor, in the American spelling used by the style anchor (9741) and by the corpus at large. 0025's gloss now reads Neighbor by charity, a dove by simplicity, perfect by virtue, which is what Charitate proxima says. At 1864C the adjectival prose quae proxima eras was "thou who wast nearest" and now reads "thou who wast a neighbor", keeping the aliena / proxima antithesis on the same word as the lemma.
  2. formosa had collapsed into pulchra. 0011, 0012 and 0019 rendered formosa "beautiful" — the word already carrying pulchra — so 1870A's formosus … et quidem pulcher and 1905C/1907A's formosa against the Vulgate's pulchra both went silent. 0011/0012/0019 now read well-formed (nine occurrences), matching 0006's Behold, my cousin is well-formed, and indeed beautiful.
  3. speciosa had collapsed into pulchra and decora. 0004 and 0034 rendered speciosi/speciosa "beautiful"; 0011 "fair"; 0032 "beautiful"; 0033 "beautiful"; 0036 "comely". 1.3 The equivalence tables as the agents recorded them

1. Fixed equivalences adopted for this stint (merge-time reconciliation note)

Migne's text uses three different words for the Vulgate's dilectus, sometimes within one sentence, and the exposition sometimes turns on the kinship sense (1879C: ut non aliae, sed consobrina sola sibi ejus vindicaret adventum). One English word is fixed per Latin word and never interchanged:

  • frater meus → my brother
  • consobrinus meus → my cousin
  • fraternus meus (substantival, 1873C, 1873C–D) → my kinsman

Likewise: proxima mea → my neighbour (Vulg. amica mea, "my friend"); acclinatio / reclinatio → reclining-place (Vulg. lectulus, "little bed"); decora / speciosa / bona / pulchra / formosa are each kept distinct (comely / shapely / good / beautiful / well-formed), because §48 argues from the difference between bona es and decora es.

Agents taking later chunks of 6963 should follow this table; it is a within-work coherence requirement (translation-style.md, Song of Songs boundary note), not a cross-work harmonization.

Register decisions fixed for this batch

  • salire = "to leap (upon)", transilire = "to leap over", exsilire = "to leap forth". The contrast is load-bearing at 1879D (salit super Adam, transilit super Synagogam) and 1880B (et si transilit, ut te transitus ejus custodiat), so the three are never interchanged.
  • retia = "nets", laquei = "snares" throughout §§43–44, 57, where the pair is enumerated (Quot vitia, tot retia: quot peccata, tot laquei).
  • portio = "portion" (never "share" or "part") through §§64–66; pars in the Deut 10:9 quotation is "part", as printed.
  • proxima = "neighbor", the gloss word at 1883C; formosa = "beautiful"; columba = "dove". Fixed at 1882C and held.

2. ⛔ Plate divergences deliberately left UNHARMONIZED

These are not agent drift. Migne's text visibly sits on two Latin traditions at once, and that is the finding this edition exists to show. A tidy-minded pass that conforms any of them deletes it. Each is rendered as printed at every occurrence.

  1. amica mea (1901C) vs proxima mea (1905C, 1907A) for the same address. Migne prints both; the English prints "my love" and "my neighbor" respectively. Do not merge them.
  2. Cant 5:10 ex millibus (§§58–59, 1934A) vs ex decem millibus (§60, 1934C). Ambrose needs "ten thousands" for the Ephraim/David argument and quotes it that way two paragraphs after printing "thousands."
  3. Rom 13:10: plenitudo legis est Christus (1873B) vs …est charitas (1878D), and again plenitudo enim Legis charitas est (0035 §12). The work carries both predicates.
  4. Cant 5:1: manducavi panem meum (1909C, 1917D, 1919A, 1920B) vs Comedi favum (1918B); bibi vinum meum (1917D, 1920A) vs bibi potum meum (1919A). "my bread" / "the honeycomb", "my wine" / "my drink".
  5. Cant 6:3 in three forms within two columns — the LXX-derived Beneplacita es… speciosa sicut Hierusalem, admiratio sicut ordinata (1942B §6), then the Vulgate form Pulchra es, amica mea, suavis et decora in the very next paragraph (1942B §7), then Pulchra ut Hierusalem (1942C §7).
  6. Cant 7:4 Nares tuae (1948D §9) then Nasus tuus (1949A §10), one paragraph apart — plural/feminine against the Vulgate's singular; and Nares ejus again at 1949A §11.
  7. Cant 7:2 both readings four lines apart inside one paragraph (1948C §8) — the Vulgate's numquam indigens poculis and the LXX's non deficiens mixto, with Ambrose glossing the LXX one.
  8. Cant 8:13 masculine Qui sedes in hortis (1959C §28) vs the feminine Quae sedes that Ambrose reports from Symmachus and Aquila (§31). He is knowingly holding both, and §31 exists to say so.
  9. Cant 8:5 in three forms in two columns (1951D §1, 1952B §2, 1953B §6) — ascendit candida / ascendit dealbata / ascendit with no epithet: "white" / "made white" / bare.
  10. Cant 4:16 perfluunt (indicative) vs perfluant (subjunctive), 1916B, four lines apart — "and my spices flow forth" / "and let my spices flow forth".

3. SETTLED — 1893B–C, nex for nec, and the two sites that made it a class

⚠ The ruling below REVERSES the merge-pass ruling of earlier the same day. That first ruling — carry the type, let the English stand affirmative, log the consequence — was made on the printed page alone. The blind polarity read then brought corpus evidence the merge did not have, and it decides the question.

Ruling (2026-08-14, blind polarity read): carry the negation, keep the marker. The English now reads She who was speaking Christ, [sic: nex] was not stripped of her cloak.

The evidence that overturned it is inside the work and did not need an outside witness. §8 of this same passage prints et non exutus est pallio, "and he was not stripped of his cloak"; and chunk 0028 asks qui castae animae pallium tollunt? The crux Ambrose is worrying at, across thirty-seven columns, is that this soul kept her cloak. The affirmative English contradicted both its own paragraph and a passage far downstream. Note that this is exactly the alternative the translating agent proposed and did not take.

And it is not one case — it is a class, found the same afternoon by three independent readers in three chunks far apart:

  • 1893B — [sic: nex] swallowed the negative; the English asserted the opposite.
  • 1899A — [sic: car] swallowed cur; a reproach ("why does he not take away…?") became an assertion that he does, in a paragraph closing Non potest igitur haec dici justa misericordia. Now reads [sic: car] why does he not take away…
  • 1917A — [sic: eccidit] swallowed the clause's only verb, leaving "which … the prophets" with no predicate. Now reads which slays [sic: eccidit] the prophets.

The principle, now written into translation-style.md as 7a⁗: a [sic: …] quarantines the defective TYPE, never the FORCE that type governs. The marker isolates what the plate got wrong; it is not a place to park a negation, an interrogative, or a clause's only verb and let the English go silently affirmative. The reader still meets the broken type at the point of loss — nothing is concealed — but the sentence still says what the author is arguing.

This does not reopen Pattern 7. Supplying the force the marked run governs is not a conjectural emendation of the plate: the negation is in the printed run, which is why the run is marked at all. What was conjectural was the earlier reading, which had the English assert a thing the surrounding Latin twice denies.

The stint's own entry, in full:

###### 1893B–C — nex for nec (§7) ⚠ READ THIS ONE Migne prints: Quae Christum loquebatur, nex exuta est pallio, et quem quaerebat, invenit.

nex ("slaughter, violent death") is a real Latin word with no grammatical slot here — a bare nominative noun stranded between a relative clause and a finite passive verb (Pattern 12, test 1). Carried untranslated in italics.

Conjecture, near-certain: nec. Ambrose's own preceding sentence sets the question — qua ratione hic ei non tulerint pallium, haereo reperire ("by what reasoning they did not take away her cloak here, I am at a loss to find out") — so the clause must read nec exuta est pallio, "she was not stripped of her cloak," and §8's closing sentence repeats the point affirmatively: et non exutus est pallio, postea quam coepit esse perfectior.

⚠ Consequence for the English, stated plainly per Pattern 7a′. Because nex is not a negative particle but a noun, carrying it leaves no negation in the English clause, and our page therefore says that she was stripped of her cloak — the contrary of the argument the paragraph is conducting. This is not a repair hidden in a marker: the marker sits exactly where the negation belongs, and the reader sees the broken type at the point of loss. It is logged here in full because it is the one place in this stint where the plate's defect inverts the sense. If a later reader judges that the printed letters do carry the negation (i.e. that nex is simply nec with a broken c), the remedy is to render "was not stripped of her cloak" and keep the [sic:] — not to leave the affirmative standing silently.

The five that matter most, and why:

  1. 1861A, non est intuitus me sol. The Vulgate says the sun has discolored her; Migne prints that the sun has not looked upon her (LXX παρέβλεψέν με ὁ ἥλιος). The negative is the hinge of the whole of §§21–24 — "the sun of justice has forsaken me… I did not open the windows… when I shall have opened them, he will enlighten my eyes." On the Vulgate text the exposition is unintelligible. The non is carried everywhere it is printed.
  2. 1864A, quem dilexit anima mea. Migne's perfect against the Vulgate's present — and Ambrose's next sentence is Cur quem dilexit anima mea dicis, et non quem diligit? The commentary is an argument about the tense. A conformed lemma would have destroyed the paragraph.
  3. 1861B, Fusca sum et decora. §24 turns on the conjunction itself ("Ambrose Bassus" vs "Ambrose and Bassus"), and the Vulgate prints sed. The et is load-bearing type.
  4. 1859C / 1860D, cubiculum against cellaria. Ambrose adjudicates the divergence himself at 1860D: Graecus in promptuarium suum et in cellarium suum habet — the plate records the author noticing the very variant this apparatus tracks.
  5. 1865B, in calceamentis gregum. "In the sandals of the flocks", against the Vulgate's "after the footsteps of the flocks" — the Greek πτέρναις ("heels") read as footwear. §34's entire gloss is about unshod feet and Moses's loosed sandal, so the divergent word is the peg the exposition hangs on.

Every Latin interrogative is present in the English in both chunks. This check exists because it caught a dropped 46-word sentence in 11632; here it caught punctuation.

###### 1960B–C — Migne's Quae sedes against his own Qui sedes At 1959C–D the plate prints the masculine Qui sedes in hortis (speaker: the soul, addressee: Christ). At 1960B, reporting Symmachus and Aquila, it prints the feminine Quae sedes — and Ambrose's whole appeal to them is that reversal (speaker: Christ, addressee: the Church). English "Thou that sittest" cannot inflect, so the distinction is invisible on the page; the frame sentence ("Christ says to the Church") carries the reversal, but not its textual basis. No inline marker: [var:] names an outside witness, and this is Migne disagreeing with himself — the verifier correctly refused it. Recorded here instead.
