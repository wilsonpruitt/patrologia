The seam this work turns on

mens = mind · animus = spirit · anima = soul · spiritus = spirit. 11536 flattened mens and animus both to "mind" because in that work they never collided. Here they collide inside single clauses — chunk 0030's own chapter head is Quod quidquid animum per cogitationem pulsat, mentis devotionem excitare debeat — so the flattening was not available and the four words are kept three ways (animus and spiritus share "spirit").

animus and spiritus collide in exactly two sentences in the whole work, and both were handled deliberately, by opposite reasoning that reaches the same English:

  • 0005 @1244A — ex animi impetu beside ex spiritus sui impetu. Richard's eodem/eamdem assert these are the same impulse; rendering them differently would silently deny the identity his own correlatives assert.
  • 0034 @1309A — animum humanum Deo conjungit, et cum eo quasi unum spiritum efficit (1 Cor. 6:17). Here the force is conclusion, not identity: what is joined becomes one spirit, and rendering animus "mind" would leave unum spiritum efficit nothing to attach to.

⭐ simultas = pretense — how it was settled, since it overturns a classical dictionary sense

Not by ear. The metals allegory reads in auro charitas, in argento veritas, in aere simultas, in ferro crudelitas, in testa fictili fragilitas — a moral descent in which "enmity" sits oddly between truth and cruelty — and 1258D prints semetipsum in omnibus sine simultate accusat, accusing oneself without dissembling, which "without enmity" does not say. Two agents had rendered the word two ways (0018 "enmity", 0024 "pretense"); the second was right. ⚑ This also WITHDREW a Pattern 18 census candidate at 1269D. That site was filed as the range's strongest candidate on the reasoning that the plate glosses the slot with simulatio — but if simultas already carries that sense, the word is not a defect at all. One agent's crux was answered by another agent's vocabulary call, which is what the merge is for.

⚠ Two ratifications NARROWED at the merge, and it is worth saying why

Both were mine, and both were solving problems this work does not have:

  1. intellectus = "intellect". Ruled to keep it apart from intelligentia = "understanding". The two words never once occur in the same sentence — zero collisions in 58 chunks — so the seam needed no defending, and the cost was real: 0038 read "In the intellects of his hands he led them" (Ps. 77:72) and "shall declare intellect" (Sir. 34:9). Reverted to "understanding" there. The agent had followed the ruling and reported the cost rather than quietly reverting, which is how it came to light.
  2. scientia = "learning" vs cognitio = "knowledge". They collide in exactly one place in the work — CAPUT XVI's own head, Quibus gradibus mysteriorum cognitio proficit, et ad quantam scientiae perfectionem… — which is preserved distinct ("the knowledge of mysteries … a perfection of learning"). Elsewhere scientia keeps the natural "knowledge". A blanket sweep of 65 sites would have bought nothing.

The general rule both cases teach: a fixed-vocabulary pair earns its rigidity only where the two words actually meet. Check for collisions before ruling, not after.

Declared exception: retained Latin is italicized where the plate prints roman

Seven spans in 0052–0054 (insolitum / non solitum / insolentia; invidens / invidentia; rogare / derogare). These are etymological mentions — the Latin word is retained in English prose and glossed beside it — and the plate prints them roman because in a Latin text they needed no marking. In English a bare Latin word has no exponent at all, which is Pattern 12's own logic (normalize the language; mark the page). Kept, and declared here rather than done silently. ✅ RATIFIED BY WILSON, 2026-08-17. It is the only break from 1:1 italic structure in the work.

C. Vocabulary: the collisions this range was flagged for

C1. animus / spiritus at 1309A (0034) — the second of the work's two collisions

Printed: quanta sit virtus verae devotionis … quae animum humanum Deo conjungit, et cum eo quasi unum spiritum efficit. Qui enim adhaeret Deo, unus spiritus est. The same collision stands in the chapter head (quae animum humanum Deo conjungit).

What I did: rendered both "spirit" — "which joins the human spirit to God, and makes it with him as it were one spirit." The chapter head follows suit.

Why, and why my site differs from 0005's. The 0005 agent met ex animi impetu beside ex spiritus sui impetu and ruled both "spirit" because Richard's eodem/eamdem there assert the two are the same impulse, so differentiating would silently deny an identity the text states. Here the logic runs the other way: the two words name two different things — a human animus and the spiritus that results — which are joined into one. So the argument for one English word is not identity but the conclusion: the sentence's whole force is that what is joined becomes unus spiritus, and it is 1 Cor. 6:17 that licenses the claim. If animus were rendered "mind" here (as 11536 would have), the proof-text would be about spirit and the claim about mind, and the unum spiritum efficit would have nothing to attach to. The union is the point, and one English word is what carries it. ⚠ Note that the same sentence is where the plate has assimilated the proof-text to the claim (Deo for Domino, §A1 above). The two observations reinforce each other: this is a sentence in which quotation and gloss have been drawn together, and both moves are recorded rather than smoothed.

F. Vocabulary calls I am proposing for the whole work

These are new to this stretch of Daniel and will recur in 0050–0057 and in the peer ranges 0035–0044. They need to be one word across the work or the tree allegory loses its hooks.

  1. bestia = beast · fera = wild beast · animal = animal. Non-negotiable in this range: 1339C glosses bestialiter degens et feraliter agens as two distinct vices (carnality and cruelty), and 1340D–1341A turns on ferae preying on animalia while aves prey on aves. Collapsing any two is the testa/fictilis error in another key.
  2. ungues = claws (not "nails"). Carries both the birds of Dan. 4:30 and 1341D's dente et ungue in omnes saevit, "with tooth and claw", where "nails" would be unreadable.
  3. fenum = hay · herba = grass. Both are in the Dan. 4 lemma at once (fenum ut bos comedes beside in herbis foris / in herba terrae), so they must not share a word. "Hay" also carries omnis caro fenum … quasi flos feni at 1339D.
  4. germen radicum = the shoot of its roots. Used at 1335D and 1336B; the peer range 0040 (1323B–1324A) expounds germen at length as propositum correctionis, so whatever that agent chose must win if it differs — flagged for the merge pass.
  5. decor = comeliness · decorare = to make comely. Dan. 4:33's ad honorem regni mei decoremque perveni is expounded at 1344C–1345A as a matched pair with honor, so the two English words must stay distinct and stable.
  6. aula = hall · palatium = palace. Richard distinguishes them explicitly at 1337D–1338A (Forte palatium … humilitas vera against aula Babylonica humilitas ficta).
  7. gloriatio = glorying · glorificatio = glorification. CAPUT XLVII's head is built on the opposition and it recurs through 1344A.

F.1 ⭐⭐ simulatio vs dissimulatio — the ratified equivalence CANNOT hold in this chunk, and I want a work-wide ruling

Simulatio et dissimulatio est de occultis. Sed simulatio de occultis bonis, dissimulatio vero de occultis malis.
