These are the edition's working notes, published as they were written. They record, work by work, where Migne's plate is defective or the reading uncertain: the printed form, what the English does with it, and why. They are not a groomed apparatus. Where a note argues with itself, withdraws an earlier decision, or leaves a question open, that is the record of how the reading was actually reached, and it is left standing — a conjecture that is visible can be checked, and one that has been tidied away cannot.

The English never silently repairs the page. A construable but wrong reading is rendered literally and the conjecture is recorded here, never applied to the text. How the English is produced and checked →

§1 · WORK-WIDE CONVENTIONS — RULED AND APPLIED. DO NOT RE-DECIDE.

Set down with their reasons so a later sweep does not churn them, and so a reader can tell a considered choice from an accident.

The diminutive is load-bearing. lectulus = "little bed", lectus = "bed", everywhere, because the exposition argues the point outright twice — chunk 0002's "Et non in lecto, sed in lectulo, quia quies a talibus habita exigua est, et angusta" and 0038B's "nec tam lectus quam lectulus nominatur". An English that says "bed" for both makes Richard's argument say nothing. Held at all three sites the Latin prints it (0002, 0012, 0013), including "Solomon's little bed" at 0012, where the oddity in English is the argument. Same reasoning: adolescentulae = "young maidens".

Bride and Bridegroom. sponsa = the bride · sponsus = the Bridegroom (capitalized: he is Christ throughout) · dilectus = the beloved · amica mea = my love. Canticum canticorum = "the Song of Songs" against plain canticum = "canticle".

quies splits by sense, and the split is in the Latin. "rest" where the sense is eschatological (CAPUT X's rest of the blessed; 0521A's quiesce post laborem), "stillness" where it is the mind's contemplative quiet (0521A's In sinistra quievisti, eight words later). Two agents hit this seam independently before it was ruled — 0007 @0421C and CAPUT X — which is why it is a distinction and not drift. vacare = to be at leisure/free.

ferculum stands in italic Latin at every occurrence (0016 ×6, 0038 ×4, 0047). Migne's Cant. 3:9 matches the Vulgate exactly, so this was purely an English question — and Richard hangs two incompatible glosses on the word: quae pascit et reficit (the dish that feeds, which the first half of CAPUT XVI runs on) and ferculum a ferendo, quia fertur de loco ad locum (the litter that is carried). No English word keeps both hooks, so both reach the reader in his own words. Decided from this work's double gloss; 10804 was deliberately not followed as an authority (translation-style.md § The Song of Songs — each commentary stands on its own). Same reasoning: reclinatorium = "reclining-place" (the reclinat se et requiescit hook runs a full column), and the Dei lectio / Dei electio wordplay and pacatae / pacificatae contrast (0005 — Richard says He does not say pacatae … but pacificatae) stay in Latin, because the sentence is about the Latin words.

emissiones = "sendings-forth" (0034), never Douay's "emissions": Migne re-quotes it with the verb three times — Emittit enim cyprum, Emissiones quoque ejus, Hanc paradisum emittit hortus — so the hook must survive in English.

Two pomegranates, two words. malum punicum = "pomegranate" · malogranatum = "granate apple", which keeps the etymology Richard states (grana … whence also it is called a granate apple). Checked across 0024 and 0034: the mapping holds and is not drift.

⚑ candidatio is TRANSLATED — "whitening" — and this REVERSES a call made mid-flight. The round-1 ratification kept it in Latin on one agent's report; the merge overturned it on the evidence of all five sites. Every one stands beside candidus / candet / candidat (0035: candidatio … qui candidus est · 0045: nec tantum est candidus, sed etiam candidatio. Candidat enim), and English white / whiten / whitening shares that root — so §Song case 2's condition for keeping Latin (no English word shares the root the gloss needs) is simply not met. Swept at 0016, 0031, 0035, 0045; 0029 already had it. ⚠ Two of those sites had also added italics the plate does not print (0031, 0045) — fixed in the same edit.

Watchmen and keepers stay distinct — vigiles / custodes, because 0507B prints vigiles qui circumierunt where Richard re-quotes custodes on the same page. vigilare splits the way both agents independently made it split: "waketh" where it renders Cant. 5:2's lemma (Ego dormio, et cor meum vigilat), "keeps watch" in the surrounding prose.

§3 · SCRIPTURE: 46 [var:], AND THE RULE THAT DECIDES THEM

The threshold, ruled at the round-1 merge and applied work-wide: [var:] fires on a continuous quotation functioning as a proof-text or as the thing being glossed — whether or not italics or an [n:] mark it — when the divergence changes the sense or a gloss depends on it. It never fires on pronoun-equivalence, on spelling, or on an allusive echo. Worked pair: per noctem for per noctes fires (a chapter-long exposition of one night hangs on the singular); tenui illum for tenui eum does not (same referent, nothing hangs on it). Spelling is Pattern 9: Hierusalem, coelorum, genitricis, caprae/capreae — no markers. ⚑ Ps. 26:4 at 0489A was promoted at the merge (voluntatem for voluptatem): the reading changes the sense and Richard's gloss per cujus impletionem depends on it, and it was declined only for sitting in unitalicized prose — a precondition Pattern 14 does not contain. Its founding case (10083, Hosea 1:4) was scripture introduced as proof, not a formatted lemma.

The divergences that would have destroyed an argument if a remembered Bible had been supplied — this is what the per-range naming of lemma words bought:

  • The work's last lemma diverges (0522B): Quae est ista quae ascendit, et transiens deserta? against Vulg. 8:5's de deserto — with three forms now in evidence, since this work itself prints per desertum at 0010 for Cant. 3:6.

§5 · CAPUT XLII IS RHYMED VERSE, PRINTED AS PROSE

Not scouted, found in the text: the final chapter is a rhymed Marian laus — operta · portendit · referta, Holoferni · spernis · sternis, ferus · Assuerus — and Migne prints it as continuous prose with no lineation.

  • It is NOT lineated by us. The plate's layout governs, as everywhere else in this corpus. The rhyme is recorded here and belongs in the "On this text" note, not in a silent re-typesetting. ⬜ If verse in this corpus is ever to be lineated, that is a conventions decision above this work's level, and it would be a rebuild, not a re-translation.
  • The rhyme explains two things that would otherwise look like defects: the chapter's ellipses, and Ave Maria … tecum sit Deus for Dominus tecum — a rhyme-driven divergence, correctly declined as a [var:] because it is a rhyme line, not a proof-text.
  • ⚠ Two Pattern 8 defects of OURS were found here and fixed at the merge: a supplied ? and a supplied ! where Migne prints plain commas (mulier Hebraea, quid Judaeos Aman spernis, Esther est Judaea). Caught by verify-english's question-mark parity check — the same check that caught a vanished 46-word sentence in 11632. The work now has zero sentence-type punctuation deltas against its Latin twin across all 48 chunks.
  • ⬜ Papae (0522B) is left in italic Latin — a genuine open call for Wilson, since every English equivalent guesses at the poem's tone. One word, one site.
  • The coda reuses the commentary's own vocabulary (singulare ferculum, reclinatorium, turris eburnea). Recorded as weak: these are standard Marian-Song commonplaces and are not evidence about the attribution question.

The eight our-side defects, all fixed. Not one was visible to any automated check, and verify-english had already passed the work.

  1. 0502D — agency reversed. immaculatam, id est a peccatorum sordibus a se mundatam was "cleansed by him". a se is reflexive to the bride, the subject of the whole gloss-series; "by him" transferred the cleansing to Christ and conformed the gloss to expected doctrine.   2. 0428C — separation read as location. quibus se extenuari vel implicari a perficiendo was "entangled in the perfecting of herself" — the opposite motion, and it dissolved the reason she withdraws at all.
  3. 0439B — plural verb given a singular subject. quanto spiritualius eos expugnant was "it storms them", making faith the aggressor in a sentence whose point is that the enemy concentrates his fire on the shield of faith.
  4. 0491B — a [sic:] swallowed the clause's only verb. ad proprium exerescere left an English infinitive with no predicate between two translated infinitives. Now "to grow up [sic: exerescere]", the sense fixed by semper excrescit six lines later.

⚠ The lectus / lectulus pair — how this range renders it (0002, 0003)

Fixed per the work brief and held visibly distinct through all five chunks: \lectulus\ = "little bed" · \lectus\ = "bed", with no exceptions and no elegant variation. The exposition at 0410C is what forces it: Et non in lecto, sed in lectulo, quia quies a talibus habita exigua est, et angusta → "And not in the bed, but in the little bed, because the stillness had from such things is scant, and narrow." The argument is restated at 0411B (Quaerit autem hunc in lectulo et non in lecto) and is unintelligible unless both English terms are already in place, so the diminutive is carried even where Richard is not arguing from it (0410B in lectulo hunc quaerere debet, 0411A lectulus est in quo sponsa quiescit, 0411A ad lectulum quietis).

Where lectus appears on its own, it stays plain "bed" — and 0003 is where this matters. At 0412A Multo labore lectum sibi paraverat the bride prepares a bed (not a little bed), and at 0413D Job's stratus and the bride's lectus suae quietis / lecto turbato are all "bed": Richard is now comparing her lectus with Job's overturned couch, and using "little bed" there would silently import the CAPUT PRIMUM argument into a chapter that does not make it. Stratus (0413D) is rendered "bed" as well, distinguished only by context; there is no third English diminutive in play.

Nine other agents inherit this: "little bed" is never "couch", "small bed" or "pallet", and "bed" is never "little bed".

6. Where a gloss hangs on the printed word (Pattern 14 / §Song case 2)

6a. 0005 @0417B — pacatae vs pacificatae: the words are MENTIONED, so they stay Latin

Unde dicit beatus Job quod bestiae terrae (daemones videlicet) pacificatae fiunt bonis. Non dicit pacatae, quia pacem non habent, sed pacificatae, quia pacem eis faciunt dum eos impugnando affligunt.

Richard's argument is the choice of word, so the two words in the second sentence are mentioned, not used, and they stay in italic Latin — the treatment the dialogue register already prescribes for a word discussed as a word. In the first sentence the phrase is used, and there it is Englished as "are made peace-making toward the good", chosen so that the etymology the gloss depends on (pacem… faciunt) is visible in English too. Rendering both as "peaceable" would have made Richard's Non dicit… sed… say nothing — the lectulus failure in another dress.

Divergence recorded, no marker: Vulg. Job 5:23 reads bestiae terrae pacificae erunt tibi. Migne's pacificatae fiunt bonis is woven into an indirect statement governed by dicit beatus Job, i.e. it is not quoted as scripture, so Pattern 14 does not fire — but the divergence is precisely the one Richard's gloss exploits, and pacificatae (the past participle, "made peace-making") is not the Vulgate's adjective pacificae at all.

6b. 0007 @0421D — supplantator / videns Deum

Jacob enim supplantator dicitur… quia Israel videns Deum dicitur, with ut amplius vitia supplantet in between. The etymologies survive translation intact here, so the words are Englished in their printed italics — "Jacob is interpreted supplanter" / "Israel is interpreted seeing God" — and the hook the gloss hangs on (supplanter → supplant) is carried in English. No Latin needed to be kept, and none was.

  • propugnacula = "bulwarks" (0025, four occurrences). 1a. ⛔ 0522B — the work's last lemma, and it diverges. MARKED.

Printed: Quae est ista quae ascendit, et transiens deserta? [n: (Cant. III)]

Three forms of one question are now in evidence, and this work prints two of them:

witnessreadingsense
Migne here (0522B), cited as Cant. IIIquae ascendit, et transiens desertaand crossing the deserts — an active passage through, plural
Vulg. Cant. 3:6 — and this work at chunk 0010quae ascendit per desertum sicut virgula fumithrough the desert, singular
Vulg. Cant. 8:5quae ascendit de deserto, deliciis affluensout of the desert

What I did: rendered the printed participle — "and crossing the deserts" — and marked [var: Vulg. Cant. 8:5 de deserto, "out of the desert"; Cant. 3:6, which the note cites, per desertum, "through the desert"].

Why it fires (sense, not spelling, per ratified item 1): de deserto makes the desert the place she is leaving; the printed transiens deserta makes it the place she is passing through, and turns a prepositional phrase into a second finite-force participle governing a plural accusative. The note's own citation (Cant. III) matches neither reading exactly, so the divergence cannot be resolved by supposing Migne quoted the other verse.

⚠ And the reason it is printed this way is almost certainly the verse form, which the brief could not have known: CAPUT XLII is not prose — it is a rhymed Marian rhythmus, and deserta is a rhyme word: deserta · operta · referta (…et transiens deserta? / Quo tendit aut quid portendit floribus operta? / Papae, furem non offendit opibus referta). The rhyme scheme runs the whole chapter (Holoferni · spernis · sternis; Hebraea · Judaea · ea; Deus · Judaeus · Sadducaeus). So the divergence is metrically motivated, and the participle is load-bearing on the poem, not on an exposition. Per Pattern 14 I state the two readings and do not speculate in the marker — the reasoning lives here.

⚠ Note for whoever collates further: Richard does not gloss this lemma at all. CAPUT XLII never expounds transiens deserta; it simply proceeds into the litany. So the divergence turns on the sense of the lemma itself and on nothing downstream — which is why it is marked once, at the lemma, and nowhere else.

3. Declined [cj: …] — census candidates, recorded not glossed

The bar (ratified item 7) glosses only where the wrong word is a negation or a correlative. None of these is. All rendered literally per Pattern 7; all named here so the census has them.

  1. 0515A · cum Tobia pergunt ad indigenas — "they go with Tobias to the natives." Almost certainly indigentes, "the needy": the whole sentence is about dividing one's means unicuique … prout possunt, and Tobit's alms are to the poor, not to the indigenous. This is the range's largest single sense-shift from one letter-run, and it is still declined — not a negation, not a correlative. Rendered "to the natives."
  2. 0522A · qui hic experte cognovit — "who here knew by experience." The sentence is built on 1 Cor. 13:12 (ut cognoscat perfecte, et sicuti cognitus est), where the Vulgate's contrast to perfect knowing is ex parte, "in part" — so a fused ex parte → experte is the obvious reading, and fused word-division is a documented shape (Pattern 10, interra, vovistvirum). Declined, and it does not even reach Pattern 10: the corpus control shows experte is a real word in the PL, the ablative of expers (a ferina legumque experte vita, 6843; nationes experte sunt sevitiam, 9481), so it is not the non-word class, and medieval experte as an adverb of expertus is defensible in a chapter that elsewhere writes experientia docente (0520B). Rendered as the adverb.
  3. 0517C · et si tantum quantum Christus — see §4.1; a possible dropped negation, declined, and the reason it is declined rather than glossed is that the missing word is not printed wrong, it is absent, which [cj:] cannot address.
  4. 0518D · quia adhuc ex faece carnalitatis accessit — "because it has thus far drawn near out of the dregs of carnality." accessit where the argument wants a verb of not yet having departed (recessit). Declined; rendered literally.

4. Passages that will not construe — rendered as literally as the printed words allow

  1. 0517C · Exaltata est haec beata quasi cedrus, sublimata est sicut Christus, et si tantum quantum Christus, Christus minimus fuit in regno coelorum… Rendered "…has been made lofty like Christ, and if as much as Christ: Christ was the least in the kingdom of heaven…" The clause has no apodosis and, as printed, concedes what the paragraph goes on to deny — the chapter closes Sublimis igitur Maria, sed sublimior Christus, "Mary is lofty, but Christ loftier." A dropped non (et si non tantum quantum Christus, "though not as much as Christ") would make the whole paragraph construe and would make its last sentence follow. Per the negation-fidelity rule I did not supply it: the contradiction is the finding. ⚠ This is the crux in the range I would most want a second reader on.
  2. 0521D · Cujus passionis gustus amore mihi sapiunt, sed confectum ex illa redemptionis humanae aromate suavi me reficit, nec martyrum myrrha, nec confessorum aromata haec gaudia percepissent, si non mortis meae myrrha percepisset. Rendered literally, including all three negatives and the singular percepisset against plural percepissent — "neither the myrrh of the martyrs nor the spices of the confessors would have perceived these joys, if the myrrh of my death had not perceived them." confectum is taken as a neuter substantive ("that which is confected out of it"); gustus … sapiunt is plural against singular passionis gustus. Nothing emended.
  3. 0519B · Hic bonitas Dei tenuis gustus est in futuro satietas. Rendered "Here the goodness of God is a slender taste, in the future satiety." The nominative bonitas where the contrast wants a genitive (bonitatis Dei tenuis gustus, "a slender taste of the goodness of God"), and no punctuation between the halves. Rendered as printed; not marked (Pattern 9 — a case ending with no English exponent once the phrase is Englished).
  4. 0522C–D · haec est veri Salomonis singulare ferculum et turris eburnea, a toto refulgens aurea tota delectabilis, haec est in quo se reclinat rex… Rendered "…and the ivory tower, golden, shining from the whole, wholly delightful; this is she in whom the king reclines himself…" Two things printed here and neither emended: a toto (see §3.5), and in quo (masculine/neuter) referring to the feminine haec — a Pattern 9 gender mismatch with no English exponent, since English "whom" carries no gender. No marker, no crux churn.
  5. 0522C · Singularis ferus, rex est Assuerus, Esther magni nominis. Rendered as printed, asyndeton included. Singularis ferus is Ps. 79:14's boar, i.e. the devil, and belongs logically with the Aman/Mardochai lines before it rather than with Assuerus; the verse form licenses the ellipsis. Nothing supplied.
  6. 0523A · translator non sic ventilat, fides allegat ita quod regnet jam cum Deo! Rendered "the translator does not so winnow it, faith so alleges, that she now reigns with God!" translator in a chapter de assumptione is presumably the one who translates her (carries her over — cf. arca foederis … magno translata jubilo three lines earlier), and ventilat "winnows/fans" resists any reading I can defend. The negation is carried as printed. Not emended, not marked.
  7. 0523A–0524A · Ave, Maria, gratia plena; tecum sit Deus. Corruptionis nostrae quam somniat Judaeus. Tabescat in fallacia seductor Sadducaeus: nam vere surrexisti. Corruptionis nostrae is a hanging genitive governed by nothing printed; rendered as the fragment it is ("Of our corruption, which the Jew dreams"). The rhyme (Deus · Judaeus · Sadducaeus) is intact across it, so whatever the genitive depended on stood in the same line, not in a lost one. ⛔ I am NOT reporting the 0523A → 0524A band gap as a defect — that is known false positive #1 (band marks are positional, not four per column), and the only admissible signal, text broken across an anchor, is absent: the [0524A] anchor falls between Sadducaeus: and nam vere surrexisti, which construes cleanly across it.   8. 0518B–C · consolatio spiritualis, quae suaviter gustantes expertos afficiunt. Plural afficiunt on a compound subject (fine), but gustantes expertos is two accusative plural participles in asyndeton. Rendered "which sweetly affect those who taste and have experience of them." Nothing emended.
  9. 0518C · Si enim bonitatem Dei cogitando comprehendas, et ad saporem ejus pertingens, … praedicabis. Anacoluthon in the Latin (a subjunctive protasis, then a participle, then a future indicative apodosis). Carried through as printed rather than regularized.

5. Interjection kept in Latin

0522B · Papae (Papae, furem non offendit opibus referta). Kept as Papae! in italic Latin. It is the Greek-derived exclamation of astonishment; every English equivalent I could reach for ("Strange!", "Wonder of wonders!", "Ah!") is a guess about the poem's tone rather than a rendering of a word, and inventing tone inside a rhymed sequence is the kind of silent editorial hand Pattern 7 exists to forbid. Flagged for Wilson as a genuine open call — if the house prefers interjections Englished, this is a one-word change and the only site in the range.

If the front end ever sets verse as verse, this chapter is the corpus's candidate.
