These are the edition's working notes, published as they were written. They record, work by work, where Migne's plate is defective or the reading uncertain: the printed form, what the English does with it, and why. They are not a groomed apparatus. Where a note argues with itself, withdraws an earlier decision, or leaves a question open, that is the record of how the reading was actually reached, and it is left standing — a conjecture that is visible can be checked, and one that has been tidied away cannot.

The English never silently repairs the page. A construable but wrong reading is rendered literally and the conjecture is recorded here, never applied to the text. How the English is produced and checked →

Carried type (Pattern 10 + 12 — [sic: …] in the English)

  • col 0535C (chunk 0002) — "centum cubitos longitudinis, et centum cubitos latitudiuis". latitudiuis is not a word; near-certainly latitudinis (n/u inversion, a standard Migne compositor slip). Carried untranslated in italics inside [sic: latitudiuis].
  • col 0536A (chunk 0002) — "Eminebat itaque templum cum vestibulo suo a parte occident eratali, situmque in atrio exteriori". A split/interpolated run: the sense requires a parte occidentali ("on the western side"), and the intruding letters erat appear to be a displaced verb driven into the middle of occidentali. Both fragments are carried as one run per Pattern 10 ([sic: occident eratali]); rendering only occidentali would repair the plate silently.
  • col 0544C (chunk 0004) — "qninquaginta cubitorum longitudinem ejus, et latitudinem viginti quinque cubitorum". qninquaginta for quinquaginta (u/n inversion). Carried as [sic: qninquaginta]. The arithmetic confirms the intended figure: the three porches are 25 + 10 + 25, and Richard's own summary two sentences later says they "implebant cubitos sexaginta" — so the porch is 50 long × 25 wide, exactly as the parallel southern-gate lemma (col 0544D) prints quinquaginta correctly. The defect is orthographic only; no measurement is in doubt.

Readings rendered literally though they will not construe

  • col 0527B (chunk 0000) — "Supervacuum enim et inane quis possit solidum aliquid fundare, vel firmiter statuere?" The two accusatives have no governing preposition; the argument requires something like in supervacuo et inani ("upon the superfluous and the empty"). Rendered "For upon the superfluous and the empty, who could found anything solid…", which is as close as the printed words allow; the supplied "upon" is the minimum needed to put the accusatives into English at all and is flagged here rather than concealed.
  • col 0527B (chunk 0000) — "ubi ipsa sibi expugnat, vel nihil nisi frivolum resonat". expugnare ("to storm, take by assault") does not take a dative and does not construe with sibi; the expected verb is repugnat ("fights against itself"), which the whole clause requires. Rendered as printed ("where it assails itself"), not repaired: expugnat is a real word and takes no [sic: …] per Pattern 12's boundary.
  • col 0537B (chunk 0003) — "Adductus est autem in terram promissionis, haud dubium ante quod de terra Babylonis…". ante will not construe here; the expected reading is haud dubium autem quod ("but there is no doubt that"). The printed haud is carried ("there is no doubt") and ante is rendered "before", producing the awkward "there is no doubt before, that it was from the land of Babylon". This is deliberate: smoothing ante away would be exactly the silent repair Pattern 7a forbids.
  • col 0540B (chunk 0003) — "Sed dicitur: Ei ascendit per gradus ejus." Richard has quoted this same lemma nine lines earlier as "et ascendit per gradus ejus" (col 0540A), and the Vulgate (Ezek. 40:6) reads et. Ei is therefore a compositor's slip for Et. Because ei IS a Latin word, Pattern 12 forbids carrying it untranslated, so it is rendered literally — "To him he went up by its steps" — which is the closest English the printed words allow. The reader should know the sentence is Migne's, not the translator's. This is the one place in chunks 0000–0004 where the literal rendering is actively misleading, and it is the class Pattern 12 flags as still open (cf. 11081 col. 0030B).
  • col 0535A (chunk 0002) — "Sane si consulas tam rationem quam Scripturam, inventes unumquemque trium horum murorum tres portas habuisse." inventes for invenies. Treated as a Pattern 9 orthographic variant (e/i interchange is routine in medieval Latin) and translated normally, "you will find"; logged so a later sweep does not read it as a silent repair. If the volume editor prefers to class it as broken type, it would take [sic: inventes].

0553A–B (chunk 0007) — ut in superioribus where the argument demands non ut

This is the negation site. Migne prints:

Ecce limen hoc ut superiora portae limen dicitur, et tamen hic, ut in superioribus, nihil determinationis adjungitur, et ex eo ipso innuitur quod vere et proprie portae dicatur.

Richard has spent the two preceding columns proving the opposite: at 0551B and again at 0551D–0552A he insists that in the earlier cases a determination (juxta vestibulum portae intrinsecus) was appended, and that its presence is precisely what shows those thresholds were not properly the gate's. Here he argues that nothing is appended, and that the absence is what proves this threshold IS properly the gate's. The clause as printed therefore asserts the reverse of the sentence it sits inside, and of the chapter around it. Conjecture: non ut in superioribus (or ut non in superioribus) — a dropped non, the PL 196 defect class.

Rendered exactly as printed ("and yet here, as in the foregoing, nothing of determination is added"), so the English contradicts itself where the plate does. No repair, no marker — there is nothing carried, only a printed word doing the wrong work.

0551A — Quam autem vir ille … metitur … Scriptura innuit quod …: our clause binding may invert Richard's inference. NOT changed; recorded for a ruling.

Migne prints Quam autem vir ille qui portam ingreditur hoc limen primo loco, et ante thalamos metitur, patenter, ut arbitror, Scriptura innuit quod in primo praedictorum VII vestibulorum invenitur.

Our English makes the measuring-order the thing Scripture hints, and the location the reason for it ("…Scripture hints that that man … measures this threshold in the first place, and before the little chambers, in that it is found in the first of the aforesaid VII porches").

The reading the argument seems to want is the reverse: because he measures it first and before the chambers, Scripture plainly hints that it is found in the first porch — i.e. the quod clause is the content of innuit, not a causal tail. Two things support that: the location is what this stretch is out to establish, and Richard states it as settled at 0551C (Primo de limine dicit quod erat in primo vestibulo).

Left as printed, deliberately. Quam is unaccounted for on either parse, and cruces-0014.md records a comparable unresolvable Quam autem … at 0571D where the English supplies a bracketed word — so this is likely the same defective construction rather than a mis-parse of a sound one. Re-binding the clause would assert a reading of a sentence that does not fully construe, which is the emendation Pattern 7 forbids. No claim is reversed to its opposite — both propositions are Richard's — so this is not a polarity defect; it inverts the direction of an inference. Flagged for Wilson.

The numeral that propagates — 0560B (chunk 0010)

This is the finding the range was opened for. cruces-0005.md records that at 0559A — three lines before this chunk begins — Migne prints et fecit frontes per quadraginta cubitos where the same lemma is printed sexaginta at 0554C and LX at 0555C, and where Richard's own arithmetic (XXV + XXV + X = LX, 0554A–B) requires sixty.

The corrupt figure recurs at 0560B, inside Richard's own prose:

Per istud vestibulum viginti quinque cubitorum longitudinis facile probari poterit, quod mensura illa frontium quadraginta cubitorum intra parietes sumpta fuerit.

The inference is that the twenty-five-cubit porch proves the fronts-measure to be an interior measure. It works only with sixty: the front is composed of two porches of twenty-five and a middle span of ten, so 25 + 25 + 10 = 60, and the twenty-five being an inside figure makes the sixty an inside figure. With forty the composition is impossible — two porches of twenty-five will not fit inside forty at all, and the sentence destroys the argument it is offered as proving.

So the defect is not isolated to 0559A: it appears twice within two columns, once in a lemma and once in the commentary that reasons from it. That is worth recording, because a single occurrence looks like a compositor's slip and two consecutive ones raise the possibility of a corrupt exemplar behind this stint of the plate.

Rendered as printed — "that measure of the fronts of forty cubits was taken within the walls" — so that the figure and the arithmetic disagree on the page. No marker: quadraginta is a real word doing the wrong work (Pattern 12's boundary). Conjecture: sexaginta, effectively certain.
