Gilbertus Foliot — read the work →

These are the edition's working notes, published as they were written. They record, work by work, where Migne's plate is defective or the reading uncertain: the printed form, what the English does with it, and why. They are not a groomed apparatus. Where a note argues with itself, withdraws an earlier decision, or leaves a question open, that is the record of how the reading was actually reached, and it is left standing — a conjecture that is visible can be checked, and one that has been tidied away cannot.

The English never silently repairs the page. A construable but wrong reading is rendered literally and the conjecture is recorded here, never applied to the text. How the English is produced and checked →

What this file is. The working editorial record for the English of Foliot's exposition of the Song of Songs: every place in the work where Migne's plate is defective, where his text diverges from the received one, or where the reading is uncertain — given by column, with what he prints, what the English does with it, and why. Declined calls are kept here with their reasons, because a place a reader already examined and let stand is worth as much to the next reader as a place that was marked.

What kind of work this is, and why it shapes the notes. The Expositio is a lemma-and-gloss commentary. Migne prints a verse or half-verse of the Song in italic as a numbered lemma, and Foliot then expounds it for anything from ten lines to two columns, re-quoting his own lemma inside guillemets as he goes — often four or five times, and often not in the same words. Most of the textual interest in this work is in those re-quotations, not in the lemmata: every named Song lemma in Caput III, IV, VI and VII agrees with the Vulgate word for word, while the glosses beneath them print the same clause three and four ways. So an entry below will frequently record that a lemma is clean and that the plate diverges from it eight lines later.

How to read the entries. They run in one ascending sequence by column, 1147A to 1304D, grouped under the work's own chapters — not by the order in which they were written, and not in batches. A reader arriving from a work page with one column in hand should be able to find that column and stop. Each entry opens with its column and (where it helps) the book or lemma at issue, then states the printed reading, then what was done. The markers named in them are the edition's standing apparatus:

Work-wide conventions

Fixed once and held through all 53 chunks. Where two readers of different parts of the work reached different answers, the collision and its resolution are named — the resolution, not just the winner.

The Song's vocabulary of address and love

  • amica = "friend" — 26 instances, whole-work scope. This was the largest collision in the merge and it was decided by Foliot, not by us. Two ranges fixed "friend" from two independent passages neither could see: at 1196A the gloss turns on the etymology (illam verbo amicabili convenit … dicens, « Amica mea »), and at 1231C Foliot puns on the word himself (ut amica nominum positio totum leviget) and calls the Bride's friends amicos at 1221C in the same breath as the vocative. A third range fixed "my love" on the sound but different ground that amica must stay a THIRD word, distinct from sponsa (Bride) and dilectus (beloved), with the vocative carrying the Bride/Bridegroom gender English loses. "My love" is not wrong about the vocative; it is wrong about who decides. A rendering that cannot carry Foliot's own pun makes his gloss unreadable, and "friend" keeps the third term just as distinct. Merged to friend everywhere, vocatives included ("my friend" where Migne prints amica mea). The masculine twin settles with it: at 1281C talis est Dilectus meus, et ipse est amicus meus puts both words in one line, rendered "such is my Beloved, and he is my friend"; and the work's last lemma, 1303C amici auscultant, is glossed fidelis amicitiae, so the friendship sense is what Foliot expounds at the end as at the beginning. Never "bride."
  • sponsa = Bride · sponsus = Bridegroom, with Migne's own capitalization mirrored — he varies it, and the English varies with him (lowercase sponsa in a lemma reads "bride").
  • dilectus = "beloved" · capital Dilectus = "the Beloved", again mirroring Migne's own varying capitalization, occurrence by occurrence, and never normalizing it. He prints both cases of the same phrase within a page — 1205B's capital Dilectus against 1205C's lowercase in the identical clause, 1294A's lowercase re-quotation of a lemma he capitalized eight lines above. Foliot's ordinary participial dilecta is "beloved" without promotion.

The praise-words — four Latin adjectives, four English ones

This was the second collision, and it took three ranges and a freed word to resolve.

  • pulcher / pulchra = "beautiful", never "fair" — pulchritudo is glossed continuously for four columns (1207A–1210C), and "fair" would leave the gloss expounding a word not on the page. pulcherrima = "most beautiful", deliberately unharmonized where Migne's re-quotations drop the superlative (see 1194B). ⚠ One partial's vocabulary table (chunks 0010–0014) lists pulcher = "fair". No pulcher is rendered "fair" in the shipped English of those chunks — the two "fair"s there are ordinary prose — so the table is a stale entry, not a divergence in the text. Named so that a later sweep does not go hunting.
  • decora = "comely" · decor = "comeliness" — Douay's word, and the precedent inside this work is Foliot's own prose at 1194C. The gloss depends on it at 1283B (unde patientiae decorem obtinet) and again at 1292D.
  • speciosa = "fair" ("my fair one") — this is the resolution of the three-way collision. One range had fixed speciosa = "comely one" at 1236C; a later range needed "comely" for decora, which is glossed in three separate places and could not move. The word that freed the deadlock is species = "fairness" / speciosus = "fair", fixed at 1281A on 1210B's precedent (Haec forma, haec species, haec pulchritudo → "This is the form, this the fairness, this the beauty"). With speciosus = "fair", speciosa mea reads "my fair one" and all four adjectives stay distinct across the whole work. ⛔ This is a live repair, not a settled fact: the shipped English at 1236C still reads "my comely one." See the open items at the foot of this section.
  • formosa = "beautiful" — the word of Nigra sum, sed formosa (1183C) and its fourteen re-quotations. It shares its English with pulchra, which is a flattening; the two never meet in one clause (formosa belongs to Caput I, pulchra enters at 1194A and takes over), so nothing in the work asks the reader to tell them apart.

The two hands

  • laeva = "left hand" (a hand, answering dextera "right hand", which is what the gloss at 1221C–1222D argues about: Dominum laevam pariter habere et dexteram); sinistra = "on the left", the bare substantivized adjective with no "hand" supplied, because Migne prints no manus in the Proverbs III, 16 proof-text and Proverbs is describing what Wisdom holds on each side. Two Latin words stay visibly distinct without inventing a distinction of sense. dextera / dextra = "right hand" in both places, since Migne prints the same word in both. ⚠ The ruling was made at 1221C and the earlier columns predate it. At 1173A–1175A the two Latin pairs run through the argument about the two breasts and the two Testaments, and that range's own note records the decision it made instead — "English has one pair of words for two Latin pairs, and the alternation is recorded here rather than invented in the translation." So the work carries a declared flattening in Caput I and a declared distinction from Caput II onward. Not silently harmonized at the merge: the two passages argue different things, and no gloss in Caput I turns on sinistra against laeva. Named here because it is the kind of thing a careful reader will find.

The homograph the work turns on — mala

⛔ malus, -a, -um "evil" · malum "apple" · malus, -i (f.) "apple tree" · malum punicum / malum granatum "pomegranate" · pomum "apple". Every instance in the work was decided from case, gender and governing verb, never from context alone, and the decisions are tabulated range by range in the entries below. The distinction is not pedantry: at 1265B Foliot contrasts the two fruits (pomegranates = the martyrs, apples = the confessors), so a single wrong reading in that chain would destroy the argument and pass every automated check. The two senses collide inside one eleven-word clause at 1297D (ut sibi mala junguntur in malis, et nullis a te potuerunt malis averti, rendered apples–apples–evils) and Foliot slides across the homograph six times in four lines at 1298D–1299A. No apple occurs before 1220C; Caput I is clean, and every mala in it is "evil".

Other terms fixed across the work

  • ferculum = "litter" — reconciled across the 0034/0035 seam, and the two readers agreed independently. The exposition proper is at 1252B–1253B, and Foliot's gloss is functional, not etymological (Surgens enim e lecto Rex, ferculo se suscipit, ut de loco feratur ad locum), so nothing in his argument depends on the word's root and every clause construes with "litter" (Douay's word at Cant. 3:9). ⚑ This is deliberately different from 11550, where Richard etymologizes the word (ferculum a ferendo) and it therefore had to stay in italic Latin. Both are right as they stand; each commentary stands on its own.
  • capra = "goat" · caprea = "roe", kept distinct wherever they meet — the lemma at 1256B says greges caprarum (goats) and Foliot's gloss on it says capreae (roes). The slip from one to the other is his, and it is now visible instead of smoothed away.
  • mammae and ubera both = "breasts" — a DECLARED flattening, not a silent one. Migne prints both Latin words in one verse at 1263A and the gloss uses them as plain synonyms in a single sentence (« quam pulchrae sunt mammae tuae! » id est, doctrina uberum tuorum). "Paps" for ubera would split a word the work treats as one. Stated here because a reader collating the Latin will see two words and one English word.
  • custodes = "keepers" · vigiles = "watchmen" · custodiunt = "keep" · circumeunt = "go about". ✅ Checked at the merge and there is no collision: these are two different verses two chapters apart, and Migne agrees with the Vulgate in both — Cant. III, 3 (1247A) is vigiles qui custodiunt, Cant. V, 7 (1275D) is custodes qui circumeunt. They must stay visibly distinct because the Song says two different things and Foliot glosses them differently (at 1247A the vigiles are angels who teach the law of confession; at 1277A the custodes are the Church's prelates and the custodes murorum are the angels). The shipped English of 1247A reads "watchmen" throughout, as it must.
  • mater = "mother" · genitrix = "her that bore her" — Foliot argues the distinction twice (1284C Haec vero genitrix, eadem quae et ejus mater est: diversitate tamen verborum, diversa denotat; 1299A), so collapsing them would leave those sentences expounding a difference not on the page.
  • charitas = "charity", always — never "love", which belongs to amor and dilectio. The distinction is load-bearing at 1154C: Amor namque in Deum accensus, charitas est is only a statement if the two words differ. amor and dilectio are both "love": they are synonyms in this work, and inventing an English distinction Foliot does not make would be the mirror of harmonizing a lemma.
  • lectulus = "little bed" · lectus = "bed" — the two stand side by side at 1252B. virgula = "little rod" against virga "rod", because Foliot argues the diminutive (quae non virga, sed « virgula » recte dicitur, twice at 1249C); Douay's "pillar of smoke" would make the page read "not a pillar, but a pillar". areola = "bed" (of spices) and not "little bed", because there the diminutive is not argued.
  • racemus = "berry" · botrus = "cluster" (1284A, ex multis racemis botrus unus). species = "fairness", electus = "chosen", tornatilis = "turned", guttur = "throat" (never "palate" — the gloss apostrophizes O beatum guttur), suavis = "sweet" beside dulcis "sweet", pacificus = "the peaceable one", Sulamitis = "Sulamitess", sexaginta = "sixty" and octoginta = "eighty", never "threescore" — Foliot's gloss is arithmetical for a full column (sive sexies decem, aut decies sex), and "threescore queens" glossed by "the number sixty" would explain a word the page does not print. campus = "plain" against ager = "field", and villa / oppidum / civitas = "village" / "town" / "city", all three ranked in one sentence at 1295B.
  • Latin kept visible where the gloss IS the etymology (the stylus/epistylium precedent): murenulae (1199B), monilia with monendo (1289B), collum with colligando (1198D), imber against pluvia in the defining sentence only (1232D), aurora / hora aurea (1285B), ager / agi (1294D), femina vel femora (1289C), ubera from ubertas (1173A), serum gathered sero (1173C), theoria beside contemplatio (1152D), Acoluthus and Sirasirim (the epistle and 1150A), pomum beside malum at 1213D, and the Greek θεοτόκον at 1267B with Foliot's own Latin gloss translated after it, as he wrote it.
  • Second person is NUMBER, not reverence (Pattern 17), throughout. The corpus's cleanest worked case is in one sentence of the 1638 dedication — Amplissima Dominatio Vestra → "Your most Ample Lordship" (plural) against Amplitudini Tuae → "Thy Amplitude" (singular); Young switches and the English switches with him. The daughters of Jerusalem take "you"; the Bride, the reader and God take "thou". The number changes mid-quotation where the Latin changes (1258C, 1261A).
  • Heads. CAPUT PRIMUM. → CHAPTER ONE., CAPUT II.–CAPUT VIII. → CHAPTER II.– CHAPTER VIII., following the sibling Song commentary 11550/10804 and the corpus majority. Migne's numbered section heads pass through unchanged — including his two lemmata both numbered "5." in one chunk at 1290D and 1291D, which are reproduced and not renumbered. ⬜ STANDING ITEM 1 — THREE SENTENCES WITH NO APODOSIS. It is a class, not an accident.

1159D · 1269D · 1299C. Found by three readers who could not see one another, in three separate parts of the work, each rendering the sentence ending where Migne ends and each deliberately taking no marker. Two made it worth watching; three settles it: this plate drops limbs.

  • @1159D — et ut homo de manu Domini bonum sine fine meruit, cum a veritate recessit. The ut clause has no sic clause. The argument requires "as man deserved good without end …, so he deserved evil without end when he departed from the truth", and the second limb is simply not on the plate.
  • @1269D–1270A — Cui cum in corde vigilavit ad intelligentiam, qui sibi septem annorum futuram copiam; et septem annorum inediam declaravit. Two relatives (Cui … qui) and no principal verb: the limb that should say Joseph was the one who watched in heart for him is absent.
  • @1299C — Haec enim si cohaerentia sunt, cor deliberans, et brachium id quod corde deliberatur exsequens. The protasis is complete, the two participial limbs are complete, and there is no main clause. The very next sentence starts a fresh conditional (Quae si ambo memoriale … tenent … cohibebunt) which does have its apodosis.

Why none of them is marked. [ed: …] is the edition's voice for what our digitization lost, and nothing here is lost on our side: at 1299C the TEI, the Latin chunk and the plate all stop in the same place. There is no marker class for a lacuna the printer left, so the English simply stops where Migne stops and the argument visibly breaks off.

⬜ For Wilson. Three independent sightings is a measurement, not an impression, and the question is what a reader should meet. The options, none free: leave them silent (current behaviour — the argument stops and nothing says so); log all three here (done, and cheap and honest, but invisible to a reader of the text); or open a marker class for a printed lacuna as distinct from a digitization loss. ⚠ The last is a real extension of the apparatus and would need a sweep of the other 105 works, because nothing has ever counted them. Do not open it casually. Note also that all three were found by translating, not by any check — no verifier in the pipeline can see this class.

⬜ STANDING ITEM 2 — "MIGNE AGAINST HIMSELF", and what Pattern 14 cannot say

The work quotes itself constantly, and it diverges from itself when it does. The apparatus has no vocabulary for that. A [var: …] must open by naming an EXTERNAL witness (Vulg. | LXX | Gk | Heb. | Douay | Vet. Lat.) — the validator rejects anything else — and it must not carry content that appears verbatim in the Latin twin, which is exactly what a self-quotation guarantees. So every one of these sites lives here, and the reader of the page sees nothing.

Fourteen sites, and for each the question that actually decides it: would it earn a marker ON MATERIALITY? That distinction is the one the 1199D repair established, and it must not be skipped: "re-opening the content with Vulg. would have made the marker validate while asserting a materiality it doesn't have… A marker that validates is not thereby earned." Passing the validator is a floor, not a warrant — the same shape as "a flagged passage is not a checked passage."

⬜ STANDING ITEM 3 — THE PLATE-READ QUEUE (original entries, now answered above)

Four sites where our files cannot settle the question and one look at PL 202 can. In priority order.

  1. @1303C — Centum against the lemma's ducenti. The lemma prints ducenti, two hundred, and the gloss's first limb correctly renders it ("by the two hundred, who keep the fruits thereof") — and then the very next sentence divides them and speaks of « Centum vero qui supersunt », "the hundred that remain." Two hundred split into a first group and a hundred remaining. Deliberately unmarked: a number is neither broken type nor a negation nor a correlative, so no existing pattern reaches it, and [sic:] cannot wrap perfectly good type. The conjecture is that Centum stands for a second hundred, or that the plate has dropped a C. The cheapest decisive read in the work.
  2. @1202D — pallium suum for Gen. XLIX, 11's pullum suum. The colt vanishes and a cloak takes its place — and pallium has just been correctly glossed two lines above, from the other half of the same verse, as vestem exteriorem, so the printed reading makes the verse say pallium twice. The work gives no independent witness either way: Foliot's gloss (ad quem asina alligata est) covers only the she-ass and never the colt, so it is compatible with what is printed. A [var:] ships on the received reading; the plate decides whether Migne's compositor or Migne's copy is responsible.
  3. @1254B — quanam with no noun to agree with, and two good conjectures competing. quanam « Aaron » filiabus Sion consulitur egredi…? — a feminine ablative interrogative with nothing to agree with, and « Aaron », in Migne's own guillemets, standing where that noun should be, in a paragraph that has nothing to do with Aaron.   4. @1304D — the possibly-missing colophon.
