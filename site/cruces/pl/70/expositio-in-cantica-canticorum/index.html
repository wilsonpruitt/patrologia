These are the edition's working notes, published as they were written. They record, work by work, where Migne's plate is defective or the reading uncertain: the printed form, what the English does with it, and why. They are not a groomed apparatus. Where a note argues with itself, withdraws an earlier decision, or leaves a question open, that is the record of how the reading was actually reached, and it is left standing — a conjecture that is visible can be checked, and one that has been tidied away cannot.

The English never silently repairs the page. A construable but wrong reading is rendered literally and the conjecture is recorded here, never applied to the text. How the English is produced and checked →

The Song of Songs rule, and how it governed this work

Wilson's ruling, 2026-07-30: each commentary stands on its own; a lemma is never harmonized with another commentary. Sixteen PL commentaries expound this book, and imposing one English across them would be editorializing Migne. So every choice below was derived from THIS plate. Where the result differs from another Song commentary on the site, that is correct and must not be swept.

The rule's second case — "where a gloss depends on the word, the Latin stands" — did most of the work here, and the agents applied it four times to words nobody pre-set:

  • murenulae (pre-set) — Murenulae autem dicuntur a similitudine piscis marini, qui murena vocatur. Kept in italic Latin.
  • vermiculatas argento → "vermiculated with silver", because the next clause glosses it in modum vermium terrenorum; an English word without the verm- root breaks the gloss exactly as translating murenulae would.
  • elatae palmarum — glossed through the Greek ἐλάτη at 1085C. Italic Latin.
  • malum granatum / malum punicum — glossed etymologically at 1101A (granorum … Punica enim ipsa est Africa), so both stay Latin in that passage only; "pomegranate" everywhere else, matching the 12 instances in the peer chunks.

⚠ Site furniture is NOT a lemma. Vers. N. was unified to "Verse N." across the work (Migne prints the abbreviation uniformly; the English was split 76/40). That is apparatus, like the project's existing CAPUT → CHAPTER, and site-wide consistency there is right. The ruling governs the words of Scripture, not the furniture around them — do not read this sweep as licence to harmonize a lemma.

Work-wide conventions (reconciled at merge)

  • ferculum (Song 3:9, 1070D) → "litter", reached independently by two agents. This work does NOT gloss it etymologically, only allegorizes it (designatur per ferculum, sancta videlicet Ecclesia), so the Latin need not stand. "Dish" is impossible — nothing is served on it; "palanquin" loses the couch. "Litter" carries both the conveyance Solomon made and ascended and the bed the saints recline on ad epulas, and keeps media hujus ferculi constrata sunt construable. It also stays clear of lectus/"bed" and lectulus/"little bed", which this work uses heavily and which 3:9 explicitly equates with the ferculum. Bruno of Segni (10804) glosses the same word as a banqueting table and renders it accordingly. Both are right on their own plates. Do not reconcile them.
  • Pre-set and honoured throughout: dilectus/dilecta/dilecte → "beloved" · amica/amicus/amice → "friend" (both families occur here and must stay distinct) · sponsa/sponsus → "bride"/"bridegroom" · pulchra "beautiful" · formosa "comely" · speciosa "fair" · nigra "black" · fusca "swarthy" · columba "dove".
  • Swept at merge: doctores → "teachers" (the 0018–0022 agent drafted "doctors" ×8, diffed against the peers 7-to-1, and swept itself) · maligni spiritus → "malignant" (×3) · castrorum acies ordinata → "a battle-line of the camps set in order" (×2 back-references, conformed to the peers' expounded lemmata).

ferculum (Song 3:9) — DECISION: "litter"

ferculum does not occur in this batch (it enters at 0007, col. 1070D), but the decision is recorded here for propagation, per the batch brief.

Rendering: "litter." Reasoning, from this work's own framing and nothing else:

  1. The work does not gloss the word etymologically. It allegorizes it — Quod superius per lectulum Salomonis, sic nunc designatur per ferculum, sancta videlicet Ecclesia, in qua velut in lecto requiescunt sancti Dei, et velut in lecto discumbunt ad epulas. So Pattern-9 case 2 (gloss depends on the word) does not apply, and the Latin need not stand.
  2. The two things the exposition needs the object to do are be reclined on as a bed (velut in lecto requiescunt) and be reclined on at a banquet (discumbunt ad epulas). "Litter" — a portable couch — carries both, and carries them at once, which "palanquin" (conveyance only) and "bed" (already spent on lectus/lectulus) do not.
  3. lectulus is heavily used in this work (Song 1:15, 3:1, 3:7) and is rendered "little bed"; lectus is "bed." The exposition at 1070D turns on ferculum being the same thing under a different name as the lectulus Salomonis — so ferculum must be a word that is clearly distinct from those two and still recline-able. "Litter" is.
  4. Downstream at 3:10 the work reads media hujus ferculi constrata sunt — "the middle parts of this litter were spread/paved" — which "litter" supports without strain.

Merge note: if a later agent has already shipped a different word, this is a WITHIN-work consistency question (the boundary in translation-style.md §Song of Songs), so it must be reconciled to one term, not left to stand.

These are real words that are merely wrong or odd. Per Pattern 12 they are rendered as ordinary English — the word on our page is ours — and the conjecture lives here.

### 4. Itera for Iterat — 0006, col. 1068B Printed: Itera autem difficultatem inventionis, et dicit: quaesivi, et non inveni. Itera is a well-formed imperative ("repeat!"), but the sentence is third-person narration about the author's technique (et dicit), so Iterat ("he repeats") is near-certainly meant. Rendered as printed: "Repeat, however, the difficulty of the finding, and he says…". The English is as odd as the Latin, deliberately. ### 5. Missing first nec — 0006, col. 1069B ⚠ NEGATION Printed: Quanta laude et admiratione digna, quae circumcisione mundata, nec adhuc baptismo sanctificata jam currit ad Deum? The subject is Ecclesia de gentibus — the Church of the Gentiles, which by this work's own argument was neither circumcised nor yet baptized. Sense requires nec circumcisione mundata, nec adhuc baptismo sanctificata; the printed text has only the second nec, so it asserts that she was cleansed by circumcision. Rendered exactly as printed — "who, cleansed by circumcision, and not yet sanctified by baptism, already runs to God?" The printed nec is carried; no nec is supplied. This is a Pattern 7a case of the purest kind: the smooth reading would require inventing a negation Migne does not print, and the contradiction with the surrounding argument is precisely the evidence the edition exists to expose.

### 6. labores aeternos — 0003, col. 1062B Printed: Videns sponsam suam ad requiem festinare, et labores aeternos fugere, ac per quietem praesentis temporis ad aeternam beatitudinem tendere. Additional choices made here:

### ferculum (Song 3:9, col. 1070D) — RULED: "litter" The prompt flagged this as the known crux. This work does not gloss ferculum etymologically; it allegorizes it: sic nunc designatur per ferculum, sancta videlicet Ecclesia, in qua velut in lecto requiescunt sancti Dei, et velut in lecto discumbunt ad epulas. The framing is therefore (a) a conveyance Solomon made of Lebanon wood, with silver pillars, a golden reclining-place and a purple ascent — one climbs up into it — and (b) a thing one rests on as on a bed and reclines on to feast. "Dish" (the commonest lexical sense of ferculum) is impossible: nothing is served on it. "Palanquin" and "portable throne" carry the conveyance but not the couch. "Litter" carries both — an English litter is a couch that is carried and ascended — and it keeps the later media hujus ferculi constrata sunt ("the midst of this litter is paved") construable. Used consistently: 0007 ×4 (the lemma, the sic nunc designatur, the media…constrata, the hujus ferculi). Since the gloss is allegorical and not etymological, keeping the Latin was not required (Song-of-Songs rule, case 2 does not fire).

The finding that matters beyond this work

All seven ours-side defects were lemma conformations (5) or mis-bound references (2). Not one was a dropped negation. The same day's read of 11065, a homily collection, gave the opposite profile. The genre predicts the failure mode. In a verse-by-verse commentary the risk is not losing a non; it is the familiar English of a known verse arriving before the printed Latin is read. Written up as translation-style.md 7a″, with these cases as the worked examples.

Fixed (ours)

  • @1079C — Heb 12:14. Migne prints et castimoniam, sine qua nemo videbit Deum; we gave the Vulgate's "holiness" (sanctimoniam). Nine words earlier our own English renders castimonia as "chastity", and the text is adduced precisely for chastity of the flesh — the argument's keyword had vanished from its own proof-text. → "and chastity".
  • @1094A — Heb 11:10. Printed Exspectabat enim manentem civitatem; we gave "a city that hath foundations", importing a word the printed text does not contain. → "an abiding city".
  • @1103A — Matt 7:25. Printed fuit enim aedificata supra petram; we gave "founded", six words after rendering aedificavit as "built". → "built".
  • @1071D — Heb 2:9. Printed perfect Vidimus; we restored the Vulgate present "We see". → "We saw".
  • @1079B — Gen 18:27. Printed cinis et pulvis; we gave Douay's "dust and ashes", reversing an order the gloss turns on (cinnamon's cinericeum colorem). → "ashes and dust".
  • @1102A — speaker. Et bene arborem mali dicit rendered "does she say". The speaker of the lemma Sub arbore malo suscitavi te is the bridegroom (ipsius sponsus et dilectus), and our own next paragraph renders the following dicit as "he means". The subject does shift to the bride at comparavit — dilectus meus is hers — but that shift is the author's and belongs to the second verb only. → "does he say".
  • @1095D — contra smoothed. Contra Damascum ergo est haec turris: quia … semper … resistit rendered "is toward Damascus", so our own "because … always resists" no longer followed. → "is against Damascus". The lemma at 7:4 keeps "which looks toward Damascus" — respicit contra is idiomatic facing; the author's gloss exploits the adversative sense, and flattening the two would destroy the point he is making.
