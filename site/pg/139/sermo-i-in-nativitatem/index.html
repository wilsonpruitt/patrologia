Isidore Glabas

Isidorus Glabas, metropolita Thessalonicensis

1341/42 – 11 January 1396

Born John Glabas, he took the monastic name Isidore in 1375 and was named metropolitan of Thessalonica in 1380, holding the see — through a deposition, a reinstatement, and long absences in Constantinople — across the city's first Ottoman capture, until his death in 1396. A correspondent of Demetrios Kydones and a preacher of the high Palamite rhetorical school, he left homilies that include the four Marian sermons of PG 139 and the earliest recorded protest against the Ottoman devshirme, the levy of Christian boys.

12SERMON I. On the Nativity of the all-surpassingly pure Mother of God and our Lady, the ever-virgin Mary. Give the blessing, Father.

1. That Moses — before whom the sea gave way, and for whom the earth ministered fountains beyond expectation, nay, for whom heaven too yielded, sending down a strange fare — recounting the genesis of the universe, says: The earth was invisible, in the beginning, and unformed, and darkness was above the deep — until the genesis of light parted the darkness. But I would say that, even after men had entered upon life and had spent the great part of the years, another unseeing lay poured over the universe, until the light-beaming coming-forth of the all-pure and blessed Virgin shone out upon the earth; whose shining rose indeed from the things below, but has filled all creation, above and below alike, with light; and neither sun nor any other of the beautiful things ever sent forth rays sweeter, or more abundant, or more translucent, or holding more of profit, than those of the All-immaculate; nay rather, both the sun and as many of the created works as seemed to excel the rest, before the all-pure one came to be, were so far from being able to make any brightness shine upon the soul, that there are those who even underwent the heaviest blinding by casting their gaze upon them — since indeed they wrongly supposed those things gods — and lived with a life that stood in need of death, that they might truly live, as the great-resounding voice of Paul to the Colossians shows, saying: If then you died with Christ from the elements of the world, why, as though living in the world, do you submit to decrees? But that darkness, and that — so to speak — tomb-bound existence, the Virgin by her appearing has taken out of the way. Therefore she poured light even upon those of the creatures that are, no doubt, august and luminous, that those who attend to them might be able to see the truth about them, and might no longer fall into the night of error.

And yet — someone will perhaps inquire into this — the most profitable of works ought surely to have shone out together with the first making of the things that came to be, seeing that it had power to adorn the greatest things; and ought not to have suffered those who fell to come even near, at the first, the disease into which they fell. 13But I think: just as we do not allow those still unperfected and more childish to approach the loftier sort of works, until age too permits them to take hold of greater things — or, if you will, just as we do not allow those weaker in sight to cast their eyes against the sun's rays before sufficient strength has been added, so as to converse with the luminary as with a friend — in the same manner the Creator of all things did not think it needful that this newest of created works, the loftiest spectacle, the most light-formed disk — I mean the much-hymned Virgin — should come into being first, together with the rest. Since — men being then but newly wrought as babes — none of the ancients could have contemplated, as was fitting, this most magnificent beauty, this most radiant and far-famed outleaping, or borne the ray that came from it. And if one should say further that heaven, and earth, and sea, and all that came forth before the Virgin, hold the rank of matter, having subsisted beforehand for this — that they might be set before the Fashioner toward the completion of the new and unrivalled statue that was to be given form (I mean the all-pure one) — he does not, it seems to me, cast far from the truth; or that all those things answer to trees in travail with this fairest and wondrous fruit — should one grant this too, I hold that he would not miss the mark of what is right. And this, I think, the divine oracle also hints at, saying: The earth has given her fruit — by "earth" signifying, as it were, the things that have come to be, and by "fruit" the All-honored one; for it was not the concurrence of two of the works, or three, or ten times so many, but of far more still, that this most beautiful-seeming and divine treasure required for its full working-out.

3. But why do I say this? To one who looks into the depth it will appear that the blessed one needed none of the things that have come to be for her own genesis; rather, all creation stood in need of her, both for its bringing-forth and for its adornment thereafter.

For the Virgin has plainly been established, for all things, as the cause of the change toward the better; and had this not been to come, the Maker would not even have taken thought for that simple making — so that the things 16that existed beforehand subsisted through her; and so the things that had come to be stood, wondrously, in need of her who was yet to come to be, in order to come to be at all; and she alone, after a new fashion, bore her fathers before her: so far was the Virgin from needing any one of those who had received life before her.

4. And further, she stands apart from all things; for what among things that have come to be could ever be comparable to this strange work of art, by whose beauty even the Fashioner was captured, and bound it to himself in the greatest kinship? For — the most wondrous thing of all — he prepared her to become his own mother, as though judging it insufficient otherwise to display the majesty of the all-pure one, unless he should employ toward her the first of the intimacies of kinship — I mean that of the mother, than which we know nothing among men more intimate nor more venerable. And if the all-pure one also shared the same form with us, it was for nothing else, I would say, but altogether for this: that holiness and gravity might be given form in those with whom she has held communion; that, as we have become planted together with the likeness of that blessed and divine image (and the illustrious Paul would surely pardon me for using his words in this matter), so we might also become partakers of her graces. And so it is that we keep festival today because this work of might beyond nature, this statue of new-fashioned beauty, she who has attained to such a pitch of glory as to be wrought in gold, a dwelling-place meet for God, has been born; and we celebrate the rite of her birthday, announcing to all the faithful a mystery lofty and venerable, the nativity of the Virgin.

5. But let us now consider. God, having brought forth the universe, pronounces upon it that it is good exceedingly; and over all these visible and fairest things he sets man as ruler; and not this only, but makes him superior to death and corruption, and destined to enjoy the glory which, being envied, he failed of — I mean equality with God, so much as befitted one of clay — if only he should not leap away from the commandment given him.

But he, bounding away from the Fashioner, and handing over his subjection to the deceiver, lay exposed — naked, pitiable, captive, alas! — stripped of all his former nobility; and all those things good and 17exceedingly good by the unerring verdict of the Maker were remade, as it were, into dejection, having seen their proper master — man, I mean — pinioned elbow-bound by the enemy; for earth, and sea, and the circle of the stars, and the hills, and whatever else the prophet calls to the praise of the Creator, kept silence, not striking up the hymn, since the plectrum — I mean the tongue — was being forced to strike the stranger's lyre; nay, even the heavens, the heralds of the glory of God, kept holiday, and came to forgetfulness of applauding and recounting his splendors; and, in a word, everything that was beautiful laid aside its proper bloom, having nothing whereby to appear beautiful, since the interpreter — through whom they had been heard in that voice — had given his attention to other ways.

6. So then all that delightful creation lay voiceless, unlovely, and as it were dead. But since he who had furnished to himself and to the rest of creation the cause of the calamity — this great moulded work — was man, and it was needful that by the exceeding mercy of the good God he should return: what comes to pass? and what marvel is wrought for a wonder?

God chooses to tread the earth; and a man is sought out who shall receive him, that he may both loose the race from slavery and paint anew the beauty of creation. For though at one time and another many great men had appeared — such as Moses, himself the worker of spectacles beyond expectation, and Elias, the doorkeeper, so to speak, of heaven, and all that other great circle of those august men and prophets — not one was a counterweight to what was sought. 17. Moreover, the name of corruption is predicated not of bodies only, but of the soul as well. And of the body it is the dissolution of the order that holds it together, and the withdrawal into those things out of which it was composed; but for the soul this would be corruption: estrangement from God, and, against nature, attachment to the things it was not born for. And, so to speak, this is death for both: the disjoining of the harmony that suits them — though, set one beside the other, in another way, as one might perhaps say, the end has befallen each of them in unsuited fashion; since, as we said, for the body corruption is the resolution into its elemental and first beginning, whereas for the soul death is, on the contrary, the standing away from the first beginning that truly is. Yet according at least to the account of corruption just given, which is contemplated in the soul, the all-pure one, if any is, is incapable of corruption.
