Procopius of Gaza

Procopius Gazaeus

c. 465 – c. 528

DRAFT (Wilson to read). Rhetorician and head of the school of Gaza in the reign of Anastasius, teacher of Choricius, author of letters, declamations, descriptions of works of art, and of long catena-commentaries on the Octateuch, Kings, Proverbs, Song of Songs and Isaiah, which fill the rest of PG 87. The Panegyric on Anastasius, delivered at Gaza when the emperor's image arrived there, is his one surviving political speech; Villoison printed it from a Venice manuscript in 1781 with his own Latin version and notes, and Migne reprinted both. Migne also set under his name a description of Hagia Sophia that is in fact Procopius of Caesarea's (Buildings I.1), and a monody on the church's collapse by earthquake whose ascription to the Gazan is doubtful.

Βʹ.

Already, most mighty emperor, every city, proud in thee and glorying in thy trophies, and having learned by experience what happiness truly is, contriving by every means not to be seen lagging behind thy gifts, 2796is at a loss for a requital to contend with thy deeds; and hence admires its benefactor the more, since by the good he does he outstrips the requitals of those he has done good to. For this is an adornment that befits an emperor: to conquer, together with his enemies, his subjects too — the one by arms, the other by the abundance of the goods at his command, and both by his virtues. But our city, having received through his image the benefactor himself, like some passionate lover rises up to the sight and rouses every age: father stands beside child, old man comes with young, and points, and together they leap for joy at the sight. Now perhaps I shall seem, alone among the rest, to be venturing upon so vast a mass of deeds, and to have such boldness that I have stood up in the middle of the theatre and dare to say something, and will not stop though someone threaten. But it is not so: the whole city is moved to a just requital for the good it receives; and judging the time not enough for each man to speak in turn, all by common vote content themselves with the orator's voice; for he who is put forward to speak on the city's behalf gives utterance, with one tongue, to the minds of all. And from this it comes about, against all reason, that I am at once confident and afraid. That the matter, drawing in nothing low, lifts the speech to a height and sets the speaker in abundance — this is a great and splendid piece of good fortune for me; but I fear lest, struck dumb by the sheer excess and multitude of what is there, and not knowing what use to make of what lies before me, I turn the wealth into speechlessness, and stand there at a loss. As in a pleasant meadow that offers a varied sight, all is beautiful to look upon; but the viewer, since all alike delight him, not knowing what to prefer and pluck, stands holding off from all alike. Nevertheless, though this is how things stand, words, which dare everything, now too grow bold just the same, and leap about in disorder, and come forward as if revelling in delight, trusting to fortune rather than judgment, and reckoning nothing of what is to come. So we shall dare something even beyond our power, holding out our eagerness as plea enough for pardon.

2. For to thee fortune, as though divining the future well, furnished everything that befits the rule of the Romans; and first, as was right, she bestows a native city that surpasses the happiness of the others.

For Epidamnus the historians sing, and report cries out along with them: a city thrust out from the mainland 2797into the sea, as though inhabiting each of the two elements, and having the advantage in all that land and sea know how to bear; for there one may both live on an island, in part, and be mainlanders, and keep clear of what is troublesome in both, enjoying either one purely. For it lies out ahead as though holding out a hand to those coming from the open sea, and meeting them kindly; and a man sailing out over the Ionian leans out from the ship and turns toward it, and feasts his gaze with the pleasure of what he sees. Epidamnus, earth-born, came forth from here and bestows his name upon the city; and later moves Pythian Apollo too to love of it, and he adorned it with a colony of Greeks, as a seer, surely, who knew the future well: that the common benefactor of all land and sea must appear from here. So then, if thou hast marvelled to hear that the Athenians are earth-born, thou mayest vie with them; if thou admirest the Phaeacians' kindness to strangers, the city is from them. It has, besides, the stock of the Corinthians and the ambition of Sparta, and Heracles for an ancestor, and through him Zeus.

3. Having received these and the like as the first gifts from the Higher Power, thou didst not shame, by Zeus, a lineage so splendid and sung through all the world; rather thou didst eclipse those who went before; and they who conquered others by their virtues learn in thy case defeat instead, and are conquered with a most sweet victory, and one in which fathers would pray to be conquered. For, sprung from such forebears, and reared as befitted one who has so conquered in all things, and increasing thy virtues with the advance of time, and thinking it right that a fair foundation be laid beneath the best life, thou didst reckon that foundation to be the possession of piety; this thou didst add to thy longing, pursued it in practice, and pressed on to the summit of the matter, and very swiftly arrived. If this is boasting and the vanity of words, I have wandered far from the truth.

ON HAGIA SOPHIA

Men of the herd, once, and the swept-together rabble, rising up against the emperor Justinian in Byzantium, brought about the riot called Nika; which I have related, without concealment and with precision, in my books on the wars. And showing that it was not against the emperor alone, but no less against God, that, accursed as they were, they had raised their arms, they dared to set fire to the church of the Christians (the Byzantines call the church Sophia, having made the name most fitting for God). And God allowed them to accomplish the impiety, foreseeing into what beauty this sanctuary was to be changed. So the church then lay all burnt to charcoal; but the emperor Justinian, not long after, turned it out such that, if anyone had asked the Christians whether it was their wish that the church be destroyed and become such as this, showing them some model of what is now to be seen, they would, it seems to me, have prayed as quickly as could be to see the church suffer it, so that it might be changed for them into its present form. So the emperor, taking no thought for money of any kind, set himself with zeal to the building, and gathered all the craftsmen from every land.

And Anthemius of Tralles, most learned in what is called the science of the master-builder, not only of all those of his own time but by far of those who had lived before him, 2829served the emperor's zeal, ordering the works for those who were building, and preparing beforehand images of what was to be built. And with him was another master-builder, Isidore by name, a Milesian by birth, both intelligent in other respects and fit to serve the emperor Justinian. And this too, then, was part of God's honour toward the emperor, that He had set ready beforehand those who would be most useful to him for what was to be done; and one might fairly admire the mind of the emperor himself as well, for this very reason, that out of all mankind he was able to choose, for the most serious of his affairs, the most fitting men.

So the church has become a spectacle of surpassing beauty: to those who see it, extraordinary; to those who hear of it, altogether past belief. For it is lifted to a heavenly height, and, as though riding at anchor among the other buildings, it leans out over them, lying above the rest of the city; adorning the city, because it is part of it, and glorying in itself, because, being part of it and mounting above it, it stands so high that from it the city is viewed as from a watch-tower. Its breadth and its length have been so fittingly turned out that it may be found, not unreasonably, both very long and altogether broad. And it is made majestic by a beauty past telling; for it is refined both in its mass and in the harmony of its measure, having nothing either too much or too little; since it is both more stately than the buildings we are used to and much more seemly than the immoderate. And it abounds exceedingly in light and in the gleaming of the sun. You would say that the place is not lit from without by the sun, but that the radiance is born within it: such an abundance of light is poured round about this sanctuary.

And the face of the church (that would be the parts toward the rising sun, where they perform for God the ineffable rites) has been wrought in this way. A building rises from the ground, not made in a straight line, but drawn in little by little from the sides and receding at the middle, and, passing into the shape of a half-circle, which those skilled in such things call a half-cylinder, it stands up to a sheer height.

Of the arches which I mentioned just now (the master-builders call them loroi), one, the one which is toward the rising sun, had already risen on either side, but had not yet been wholly 2837completed in the middle, but was still waiting. And the piers, upon which the building was going up, not bearing the size of what lay upon them, somehow suddenly cracking, looked as though they would before long be broken up. So Anthemius and Isidore and those about them, terrified at what had befallen, brought the matter before the emperor, having come to despair of their art. At once the emperor, driven by what I do not know, but by God, I think (for he is no master-builder), bade them bring the curve of this arch round to its completion. "For this," he said, "resting upon itself, will no longer need the piers beneath it." And if the account were without witnesses, I know well that it would have seemed flattery and wholly past belief; but since there are many witnesses present of what was done at that time, we need not hesitate to go on to the rest of the account. So the craftsmen did what was commanded; and the whole arch hung aloft in safety, setting the seal of experience on the truth of the conception. That, then, was worked in this way. But with the other arches, which face the south and the north wind, this is what came about. The so-called loroi had been raised aloft; and everything beneath, weighed down by them, laboured; and the columns there threw off small flakes, as though scraped. And again the master-builders, disheartened by what had befallen, reported their plight to the emperor. And again the emperor devised this counter-measure. He ordered that the tops of these labouring parts, as many as touched the arches, be taken down at once, and set in again much later, once the damp of the masonry had well left them. And they did accordingly; and the structure has continued safe thereafter.

There was, then, a calamity greater than calamity, and suffering than suffering, and grief than grief, and wailing than wailing, and lament than lament, and more grievous too; and the other sufferings, all that books and memories record (the razing of cities, the desolation of temples, the ruin of houses, the falling of statues, deportations, abductions, captivities) we have both learned and know; and we have seen what we would not have hoped, would not have expected, would never have pictured in our minds; and that than which there was no greater evil either to see or to hear; and before which every man would have prayed to die. What is this? Our ornament, our heaven, our sun, our shelter, our refuge, our hope, our only delight, our only succour, our stay, our mercy-seat, the all-beautiful church, the incomparable house which Wisdom built in her own name; on which she set those seven pillars; for which too she slaughtered her own victims and mixed her bowl. O the suffering! O the calamity! O the greatness of the evils! O the Iliad of woes! Who would not bite through his tongue rather than speak?
