Maximus the Confessor

Maximus Confessor

c. 580 – 662

Monk, theologian and spiritual writer of the seventh century, the most searching Greek theologian of his age. He wrote on prayer, love and the ascetic life, on the difficulties in Gregory of Nazianzus and Dionysius, and on Christ's two natures and two wills against the Monothelite policy of the emperors and the patriarchs of Constantinople. Arrested and tried at Constantinople in 655 and again in 662, he was condemned, mutilated, and died in exile on the Black Sea coast. Migne prints his letters as Combefis edited them, in Greek with Combefis's Latin facing.

A spring flowing with the dew of ethical words Here thou shalt find, if thou go after it genuinely: The labours of Maximus the Confessor, friend. Embrace them, and thou shalt learn the gain.

1. It is incumbent on every Christian who believes rightly in God not to be without care, but always to look for and await temptation, so that when it comes he may not be taken aback nor troubled, but to endure with thanksgiving the toil of the affliction, and to consider what he says, singing psalms with the Prophet: Prove me, O Lord, and try me. Psal. xxv, 2. And he did not say, «Thy chastening has overthrown me», but, has set me upright to the end. Psal. xvii, 36.

2. The beginning of the good is the fear of God; and its end, the longing for him. The beginning of every good thing is a word put into practice, and a practice informed by the word. Therefore neither is practice good without the word, nor the word that goes forth without practice.

3. Practice is: of the body, fasting and vigil; of the mouth, psalmody; and better than psalmody, prayer; and silence, more precious than speech. And the practice of the hands is what is done by them without murmuring; and of the feet, what is accomplished through them at the first bidding. And the practice of the soul is self-control with simplicity, and simplicity made active by self-control. And of the mind, prayer in contemplation, and in prayer the contemplation of God.

4. Before all the virtues go mercy and truth, whose offspring is humility, and the discernment which, according to the Fathers, accrues from it. Without which neither of them will be able to see its own end.

6. The courageous soul, like a woman, keeping throughout her whole life two lamps burning, the practical and the contemplative, does what is due; but the contrary, again, she who is slackened by pleasures.

7. Voluntary hardship does not suffice the soul for perfect deliverance from vice, unless, fired through the involuntary as well, she is not dissolved.

8. The soul, like a sword, for perfect deliverance from vice, unless it passes through fire and water, that is, voluntary and involuntary labours, is not kept unbroken against the shocks of what befalls.

9. Just as of voluntary temptations there are three more general causes, health, wealth and good repute, so those of the involuntary are also three: losses, sicknesses and revilings. And these become for some unto upbuilding, and for others unto pulling down.

10. There subsist alongside, in the soul, desire and grief; in the body, pleasure and pain. And the cause of pain is pleasure; for, wishing to escape the painful sense that goes with pain, we flee for refuge to pleasure; and of grief, desire or infirmity.

11. The virtuous man possesses the good inward; the vainglorious, contrived. And evil the earnest man has on the surface; but the lover of pleasure, in depth.

12. Seldom and not with care is he who hates evil found turned toward it; but more often and more attentively he who still clings to its causes.

13. Those for whom repenting is not by intent, for them offending too is continual; but for those for whom sinning is against intent, repentance is fully assured, and its cause is not frequent.

15. It is not he who does not wrong his soul by deeds who has thereby also kept it undefiled by words; nor he who guards these who has thereby also not stained his thoughts. For he who sins does this in three ways.

16. Thou wilt not be able to behold the face of virtue while still gazing with pleasure on that of vice. And the second will appear hateful then, when thou desirest the taste of the first, and estrangest thy sight from so great a form.

17. It is through thoughts, not through things, that the demons principally make war on the soul. For things in themselves are necessary. And of things, hearing and sight are the cause; of thoughts, custom and the demons.

18. Threefold is the sinning part of the soul, being found in deeds and words and thoughts; and sixfold, the good of sinlessness. For one must keep the five senses and the uttered word without stumbling. In which he who does not stumble, it says, is a perfect man, able to bridle the members of the body too. Jac. iii, 2.

19. The irrational part of the soul is divided sixfold: into the five senses, and into the uttered word, which is divided without division together with what suffers, being passionless; but takes the impress of its vice when it is found passionate.

20. Neither can the body be cleansed without fasting and vigil, nor the soul without mercy and truth, nor yet the mind without contemplation and converse with God. For these pairings are most notable in these.

21. The soul, encircled by the virtues spoken of, renders its fortress unbattered by the din of temptations: which is patience. For in your patience possess your souls, Luc. xxi, 19. the Word says. But if things should hold otherwise, like some unwalled city it is wont to be shaken, even by noises far off, by the levers of cowardice.

22. Not as many as are prudent in the things of reason are prudent also in the things of notion; but as many as are so in the things of notion will be found so also in the things of sense outwardly. For even if sense has all men as tributaries, yet they do not render her like tributes. For out of artlessness most do not know how, in the things that belong to her, to honour her as she herself demands.

23. Prudence, though uncut by nature, is cut into different cuts.

On this text

DRAFT (Wilson to read). Two hundred and forty-three short chapters of Maximus the Confessor, which Migne prints (PG 90.1401–1461) from a Vatican manuscript, with a Latin version facing; his running head reads Alia ex Vatic. capita, “other chapters from the Vatican”. The collection opens with four dedicatory verses (“Here thou shalt find, if thou go after it genuinely: the labours of Maximus the Confessor, friend”), and three short verse couplets stand among the chapters, one of them on prayer joining the soul to the Bridegroom. The chapters are mostly a sentence or three long and keep to the ascetic life: the Christian is to expect temptation and meet it with thanksgiving, since the beginning of the good is the fear of God and its end the longing for him; practice is not good without the word, nor the word without practice; the soul is to be tried by voluntary hardship and by the involuntary, as a sword is passed through fire and water. They go on to the passions of the body and of the soul, to the thoughts and how they are met, to a long run on prayer (“prayer is the key of the kingdom of heaven”), and, toward the end, to the laws of nature seen as flowering meadows, the mind freed from material ties and going to heavenly things as to its own, and the senses that follow the palate. Many chapters read a scriptural image as a picture of the soul: the heifer under the yoke, the two lamps, the bridal chamber, the vineyard and the beasts at its hedge, the garments of skin.
