⟦q1.1|1⟧ [1177] *Of our holy father and teacher Maximus the Confessor.*

⟦q1.2|1⟧ VARIOUS CHAPTERS ON THEOLOGY AND THE ECONOMY

⟦q1.3|1⟧ *and on virtue and vice.*

⟦q1.4|1⟧ FIRST CENTURY

⟦q1.5|1⟧ 1. ⟦q1.5|2⟧ One is the good that is beyond beginning and beyond essence, the holy Monad of three hypostases, Father and Son and Holy Spirit: an infinite growing-together of three infinites, which has the principle of its being, and of how, and what, and of what sort it is, wholly inaccessible to the things that are. ⟦q1.5|3⟧ For it escapes every intellection of those who understand, in no way going out from its hidden inwardness according to nature, and it infinitely extends beyond every knowledge of all knowledges.

⟦q1.6|1⟧ 2. ⟦q1.6|2⟧ The good that properly is according to essence is that which has neither beginning, nor end, nor cause of being, nor any motion whatever, in respect of being, toward some cause. ⟦q1.6|3⟧ But what does not so hold is not properly being either, as having beginning and end and cause of being, and the motion in respect of being toward some cause. ⟦q1.6|4⟧ And what properly is not, even if it is said to be, both is and is said to be by participation, by the wish of what properly is.

⟦q1.7|1⟧ 3. ⟦q1.7|2⟧ If the Word leads the way before the coming-to-be of beings, there neither was, nor is, nor will be a word higher than the Word. ⟦q1.7|3⟧ And the Word is not mindless or without share in life, but endowed with mind and living, as having the begetting Mind [1180] essentially subsisting, the Father, and Life subsisting essentially, coexisting, the Holy Spirit.

⟦q1.8|1⟧ 4. ⟦q1.8|2⟧ One God, begetter of one Son, the Father, and source of one Holy Spirit: Monad unconfused, and Trinity undivided; Mind without beginning, alone essentially Begetter of the alone Word without beginning, and source of the alone eternal Life, that is, of the Holy Spirit.

⟦q1.9|1⟧ 5. ⟦q1.9|2⟧ One God, because one Godhead. ⟦q1.9|3⟧ Monad without beginning, and superessential, and without parts, and undivided; the same Monad and Trinity, and so on.

⟦q1.10|1⟧ 6. ⟦q1.10|2⟧ If every participation is conceived before those that participate, then plainly the cause of beings lies above all beings, incomparably and in every mode, pre-existing according to nature and conceived before beings: not as essence before accidents, since then the Divine will be shown to be composite, having the hypostasis of beings for the completion of its own existence; but as the superessential before essence. ⟦q1.10|3⟧ For if the arts devised the shapes of the things that come from them, and universal nature the species of the things under it, much more did God make the essences of beings subsist out of things that are not, as superessential, and still more as infinitely exalted beyond the positing of superessentiality: he who also yoked the sciences to the arts for the devising of shapes, and gave to nature the activity that produces the species, and made subsist the very being of essences, whatever it is.

⟦q1.11|1⟧ 7. ⟦q1.11|2⟧ He who is not participable by beings according to essence, but wishes to be participated in another mode by those who are able, in no way departs from what is hidden according to essence; since even the mode itself in which, willing, he is participated remains perpetually undisclosed to all. ⟦q1.11|3⟧ So then, as God, willing, is participated in the mode which he [1181] himself knows, so too, willing, he made the participants subsist according to the principle which he himself understands, through the surpassing power of goodness. ⟦q1.11|4⟧ So then, what has come to be by the volition of him who made it could never be co-eternal with him who willed it to come to be.

⟦q1.12|1+2+3⟧ 8. ⟦q1.12|1+2+3⟧ The Word of God, born once for all according to the flesh, is always born willingly according to the spirit, through love for man, in those who are willing; and he becomes a babe, forming himself in them by the virtues; and he shows himself[?] so far as he knows the one who receives him has room, not diminishing out of envy the manifestation of his own greatness, but weighing out by measure the capacity of those who long to see. ⟦q1.12|1+2+3⟧ Thus the Word of God, though always appearing in the modes of those who participate in him, always remains, by the excess of the mystery, unseen by all. ⟦q1.12|4⟧ Whence the divine Apostle, wisely examining the power of the mystery, says: *Jesus Christ, yesterday and today, the same, and unto the ages*, knowing the mystery to be ever new, and never grown old by comprehension.

⟦q1.13|1⟧ 9. ⟦q1.13|2⟧ Christ God is born, becoming man by the assumption of flesh having a noetic soul; he who granted to beings their coming-to-be out of things that are not; bearing whom above nature, the Virgin undid no token of her virginity. ⟦q1.13|3⟧ For as he himself became man without altering his nature or changing his power, so he makes her who bore him both mother, and keeps her virgin, at once interpreting wonder by wonder in the same respect, and hiding the one by the other. ⟦q1.13|4⟧ For God, of himself, is always according to essence a mystery, bringing himself out of his natural hiddenness so far as to make it, through the manifestation, still more hidden; and again making her who bore him, a mother, a virgin so far as to make the child-bearing an unbreakable bond of virginity.

⟦q1.14|1⟧ 10. ⟦q1.14|2⟧ Natures are innovated, and God becomes man: not only the divine nature, stable and unmoved, moving toward the nature that is moved and unstable, that it may stop it from being borne along; nor only the human, without seed, above nature, cultivating flesh brought to completion by the Word, that it may stand from being borne along; but also a star from the east, appearing by day and guiding the magi to the place of the Word's incarnation, [n: Matth. ii, 1 seqq.] to show mystically that the word in the Law and the Prophets surpasses sense, and guides the nations to the greatest light of full knowledge. ⟦q1.14|3⟧ For plainly the word of the Law and of the Prophets, understood piously like a star, guides to the full knowledge of the incarnate Word those called according to intent by the power of grace. [n: Rom. viii, 28.]

⟦q1.15|1⟧ 11. ⟦q1.15|2⟧ Since I, man, willingly transgressed the divine commandment, and the devil, luring the firmness of my nature with the hope of Godhead, dragged it down toward pleasure, [1184] by which, having made death subsist, he exulted, revelling in the corruption of nature: for this reason God becomes perfect man, passing over nothing of nature save sin, since that was not of nature at all; so that, luring with the bait of the flesh, he might provoke the insatiable dragon, gaping, to swallow the flesh, which would become poison to him, utterly destroying him by the power of the Godhead within it, but to the nature of men an antidote, recalling it to the grace that was from the beginning by the power of the Godhead within it. ⟦q1.15|3⟧ For as he, pouring the venom of his vice into the tree of knowledge, destroyed the nature that tasted it, so too he, wishing to devour the flesh of the Master, was destroyed by the power of the Godhead within it.

⟦q1.16|1⟧ 12. ⟦q1.16|2⟧ The great mystery of the divine becoming man always remains a mystery: not only because, being manifested in proportion to the power of those who are saved by it, it holds what is not yet seen greater than what has been manifested; but because even what has appeared still remains wholly hidden, known by no account as it is. ⟦q1.16|3⟧ And let no one think what is said a paradox. ⟦q1.16|4⟧ For God, being superessential, and having risen above all superessentiality, wishing to come into essence, was essentiated superessentially. ⟦q1.16|5⟧ Hence also, having for men's sake, as lover of man, truly become man out of the essence of men, he holds for ever undisclosed the mode of how he became man; for he became man beyond man.

⟦q1.17|1⟧ 13. ⟦q1.17|2⟧ Let us faithfully contemplate the mystery of the divine becoming man, and without curiosity let us only glorify him who was well pleased that this should come to be for our sake. ⟦q1.17|3⟧ For who, trusting in the power of logical demonstration, can declare how the conception of God the Word comes about; how the coming-to-be of flesh without seed; how birth without corruption; how she is mother who remained virgin even after childbirth; how he who is more than perfect advanced in stature; [n: Luc. ii, 52.] how the pure one was baptized; how he nourished, who hungered; how he who toiled bestowed strength; how he who suffered gave healings; how he who died gave life; and, to say last what is first, how God becomes man; and what is still more mysterious, how the Word, who according to essence exists hypostatically whole in the Father, is essentially in flesh according to hypostasis? ⟦q1.17|4⟧ How the same is both wholly God according to nature, and has become wholly man according to nature, having altogether denied no nature, neither the divine, according to which he is God, nor ours, according to which he has become man? ⟦q1.17|5⟧ These mysteries faith alone contains, being the hypostasis of things that are beyond mind and reason. [n: Hebr. xi, 1.]

⟦q1.18|1⟧ [1185] 14. ⟦q1.18|2⟧ Adam, having disobeyed, taught that the coming-to-be of nature begins from pleasure; the Lord, banishing this from nature, did not admit the conception that is from seed. ⟦q1.18|3⟧ The woman, having transgressed the commandment, showed that the birth of nature begins from pain; the Lord, shaking this off from nature, when he was born did not allow her who bore him to undergo corruption: so that at once he might take out of nature both the voluntary pleasure and the involuntary pain that is due to it, of which he was not the creator, becoming their destroyer; and might teach mystically, by inclination, to make a beginning of another life, which begins perhaps from pain and toils, but ends altogether in divine pleasure and gladness without end. ⟦q1.18|4⟧ For this reason he who made man becomes man, and is born as man, that he may save man, and, healing passions by passions, may be shown, being himself the passion of our passions, renewing above nature, in his love for man, our habits according to the spirit by his own privations according to the flesh.

⟦q1.19|1⟧ 15. ⟦q1.19|2⟧ He who by divine longing has conquered the soul's disposition toward the body has become uncircumscribed, even if he is in the body. ⟦q1.19|3⟧ For God, who draws the aspiration of him who longs, is incomparably higher than all, not allowing him who longs to nail his aspiration to any of the things after God. ⟦q1.19|4⟧ Let us then long for God with the whole power of our nature, and make our choice such as no bodily thing can hold, and become in disposition above all beings, sensible and intelligible; and in no way, as to inclination, shall we be harmed at all by our natural circumscription in respect of being with God, who is by nature uncircumscribed.

⟦q1.20|1⟧ 16. ⟦q1.20|2⟧ The hardship of the saints is a contest of envy and virtue: of the one, contending that it may prevail; of the other, bearing all things that it may remain unconquered; and of the one, struggling that vice may prosper, through the punishment of those who do right; of the other, that it may keep the good holding the advantage even in misfortunes.

⟦q1.21|1⟧ [1188] 17. ⟦q1.21|2⟧ The contest of virtue is to struggle in toils, victory bringing, as prize to those who endure, the passionlessness of the soul, according to which, united to God through love, it is separated in disposition from body and world. ⟦q1.21|3⟧ For to those who endure, the ill-treatment of the body is strength of soul.

⟦q1.22|1⟧ 18. ⟦q1.22|2⟧ Beguiled by the deceit of pleasure from the beginning, we preferred death to the life that truly is. ⟦q1.22|3⟧ Let us then gratefully bear its slayer, toil of the body, so that, the death of pleasure having made vanish with itself the death that came about through it, we may receive back, running back to us, the life that was sold for pleasure, bought by small toils of the flesh.

⟦q1.23|1⟧ 19. ⟦q1.23|2⟧ If, when the flesh fares well, the tone of sin is wont to grow fat, it is clear that when it suffers hardship the tone of virtue naturally grows young again. ⟦q1.23|3⟧ Let us then bravely bear the hardship of the flesh, which cleanses the defilements of the soul and procures the glory to come: *For the sufferings of the present time are not worthy of the glory to come, to be revealed to us.* [n: Rom. viii, 18.]

⟦q1.24|1⟧ 20. ⟦q1.24|2+3⟧ Neither do physicians, treating the body, apply one and the same medicine to all, nor does God, healing the diseases of souls, know only one mode of treatment fitting for all; but, assigning to each the suitable one, he works the healings. ⟦q1.24|4⟧ Let us then give thanks while we are being treated, even if what is done brings torment; for the end is blessed.

⟦q1.25|1⟧ 21. ⟦q1.25|2⟧ Nothing so tests the disposition of the soul as the uprisings of the flesh suffering hardship: if it gives way to them, it will appear to cherish the flesh more than God; but if it remains unshaken by their jolts, it will be shown to honour virtue more than the flesh; through which it will receive as indweller God, who for its sake endured to suffer as we do, crying, as once to the disciples: *Take courage, I have overcome the world.* [n: Joan. xvi, 33.]

⟦q1.26|1⟧ 22. ⟦q1.26|2⟧ If all the saints have become partakers of discipline, let us too give thanks, being disciplined with them, that we may be counted worthy to become partakers of their glory. ⟦q1.26|2⟧ *For whom the Lord loves he disciplines, and scourges every son whom he receives.* [n: Prov. iii, 11: Hebr. xii, 6.]

⟦q1.27|1⟧ 23. ⟦q1.27|2⟧ Adam, accepting the side that held out pleasure, puts the human outside paradise; but the Lord, having this side torn open through toil by the lance, made the thief to be within paradise. ⟦q1.27|3⟧ Let us therefore love the toil of the flesh, and hate its pleasure: for the one brings in and restores to good things; the other drives out and sets apart from good things.

⟦q1.28|1⟧ 24. ⟦q1.28|2⟧ If God suffers in the flesh, having become man, who that suffers does not rejoice, having God as fellow-partaker [1189] of the passion? ⟦q1.28|2⟧ For him, suffering with him becomes the procurer of the kingdom. ⟦q1.28|3⟧ For true is he who said: *If indeed we suffer with him, that we may also be glorified with him.* [n: Rom. viii, 17.]

⟦q1.29|1⟧ 25. ⟦q1.29|2⟧ If one must toil at all because of the pleasure mixed into nature through the forefather, let us bravely endure the temporary toils, which blunt for us the sting of pleasure, and free us from the everlasting punishments due to it.

⟦q1.30|1⟧ 26. ⟦q1.30|2⟧ Love is the end of all good things, since toward God, the highest of goods and cause of every good, it leads and brings near and gathers together those who walk in it, as faithful and unfailing and abiding. ⟦q1.30|3⟧ For faith is the foundation of those after it, I mean of hope and love, firmly establishing the true. ⟦q1.30|4⟧ And hope is the strength of the extremes, I mean of love and faith, revealing through itself what is to be believed and what is to be yearned for by both, and teaching to make through itself the course toward it. ⟦q1.30|5⟧ And love is the completion of these, embracing wholly and whole the last object of appetite, and providing for them rest from the motion toward it, introducing through itself, in place of believing that it is and hoping that it will be present, the enjoying of it as present.

⟦q1.31|1⟧ 27. ⟦q1.31|2⟧ The most perfect work of love, and the limit of the activity according to it, is, by a relational exchange of those joined by it, to make the idioms, and the names, befit one another; and to make man god, and God to be called and to appear man, because of the one and invariant wish and motion of both according to will.

⟦q1.32|1⟧ 28. ⟦q1.32|1⟧ If we are wholly according to the image of God, let us become our own and God's; or rather, of God alone and whole, and wholly, bearing nothing earthly in ourselves, that we may draw near to God and become gods, receiving from God our being gods. ⟦q1.32|2⟧ For thus the divine gifts are honoured, and the presence of the divine peace is welcomed.

⟦q1.33|1⟧ 29. ⟦q1.33|2⟧ Love is a great good, and of goods the first and choice good, inasmuch as God and men

⟦q2.1|1⟧ [1192] it joins through itself around him who has it, and prepares the maker of men to appear as man, because of the invariance toward God, in the good, of him who is deified, so far as is attainable for man; which invariance I take the saying *To love the Lord God with the whole heart, and soul, and strength; and one's neighbour as oneself* [n: Deut. vi, 5; Matth. xxii, 37.] to bring about.

⟦q2.2|1⟧ 30. ⟦q2.2|2⟧ One must know that the devil, having deceived us by a guile wickedly contrived through self-love, by the assault of pleasure, set us apart by inclination from God and from one another, twisting what is straight, and in this manner partitioning nature, and cutting it up into many opinions and imaginings.

⟦q2.3|1⟧ 31. ⟦q2.3|2⟧ There are three things that are the greatest and the sources of evil, and, to speak simply, begetters of all vice: ignorance, I say, and self-love and tyranny, dependent on one another and constituted through one another. ⟦q2.3|3⟧ For from ignorance about God comes self-love; and from this, tyranny toward the kindred. ⟦q2.3|4⟧ And no argument will gainsay that the evil one made these subsist in us by the mode of misuse of our own powers, reason and desire and anger.

⟦q2.4|1⟧ 32. ⟦q2.4|2⟧ It is needful, instead of ignorance, to be moved by reason through knowledge, by way of inquiry, toward God, the one most alone; and by desire, pure of the passion of self-love, to be driven by longing toward God alone; and by anger, separated from tyranny, to struggle to attain God alone; and to make, out of these, and for whose sake these are, the divine and blessed love, which joins to God and shows the lover of God to be god.

⟦q2.5|1⟧ 33. ⟦q2.5|2⟧ When self-love, as the beginning, as I said, and mother of evils, has been plucked out, all that comes from it and after it is wont to be plucked out with it; since, if it is not, no form or trace of vice whatever can in any way subsist at all.

⟦q2.6|1⟧ 34. ⟦q2.6|2⟧ It is needful to cleave to ourselves and to one another as far as Christ himself, going before, showed through himself, enduring to suffer for us.

⟦q2.7|1⟧ 35. ⟦q2.7|2⟧ Because of love all the saints stood against sin, making no account of the present life, and underwent the many-formed modes of death, that they might be gathered to themselves away from the world, and to God, and might unite in themselves the ruptures of nature. ⟦q2.7|3⟧ For this is the true and blameless divine wisdom of the faithful, whose end is the good and the truth: if indeed love for man is the good, and love for God according to faith the true, the marks of love; as it joins men to God and to one another, and for this reason has the permanence of good things unfailing.

⟦q2.8|1⟧ [1193] 36. ⟦q2.8|2⟧ The activity and demonstration of perfect love toward God is the genuine disposition toward one's neighbour through voluntary goodwill. ⟦q2.8|3⟧ *For he who does not love his brother, whom he has seen*, says the divine John, *cannot love God, whom he has not seen.* [n: I Joan. iv, 20.]

⟦q2.9|1⟧ 37. ⟦q2.9|2⟧ The way of truth is love; naming himself this, the Word of God presents those who journey in it, made pure of all things, to God and the Father. [n: Joan. xiv, 6.]

⟦q2.10|1⟧ 38. ⟦q2.10|2⟧ This is the door, through which he who enters comes into the Holy of holies, and is established as worthy to become a beholder of the inaccessible beauty of the holy and royal Trinity.

⟦q2.11|1⟧ 39. ⟦q2.11|2⟧ Fearful indeed, and beyond all condemnation, is it willingly to put to death, through love for things that perish, the life given us from God by the gift of the Holy Spirit; and they surely know this fear who have practised preferring the truth to self-love.

⟦q2.12|1⟧ 40. ⟦q2.12|2⟧ Let us use peace as we ought, and, setting aside the friendship that wickedly arose in us toward the world and the world-ruler, let us, though late, put an end to the war set up through the passions against God; and, making unbreakable treaties of peace with him, in the abolishing of the body of sin, [n: Rom. vi, 6.] let us cease from enmity toward him.

⟦q2.13|1⟧ 41. ⟦q2.13|2⟧ It is impossible for us to be made friends with God while we are at faction against him through the passions, and endure to pay tribute through vice to the wicked tyrant and slayer of souls, the devil, unless we have first been made wholly enemies of the wicked one. ⟦q2.13|3⟧ For until then we stand as enemies and foes of God, even if we wrap about ourselves the name of the faithful, so long as we wish to be slaves to passions of dishonour; and no profit will thereafter accrue to us from peace in the world, while the soul is ill disposed, and at faction against its own maker, and does not endure to come under his kingdom, being still sold to countless cruel masters, who press it on into vice, and prepare it deceitfully to prefer the road that leads to perdition to the one that saves.

⟦q2.14|1⟧ 42. ⟦q2.14|2⟧ For this God made us, that we might become sharers in the divine nature, and partakers of his eternity, and might appear like him by the deification that is from grace; [n: I Joan. iii, 2.] for whose sake is the whole constitution and permanence of beings, and the bringing forth and coming-to-be of things that are not.

⟦q2.15|1⟧ 43. ⟦q2.15|2⟧ If we aspire both to be named and to be of God, let us strive not to betray the Word to the passions, as Judas did, nor to deny him, as Peter did. ⟦q2.15|3⟧ For denial of the Word is the refusal, out of fear, to do the good; and betrayal is deliberate sin in act, and the impulse toward sin.

⟦q2.16|1⟧ [1196] 44. ⟦q2.16|2⟧ The end of every affliction is joy; and of every labour, rest; and of every dishonour, glory. ⟦q2.16|3⟧ And simply, the end of all the pains endured for virtue's sake is to come to be and to be with God for ever, and to enjoy the everlasting relief that has no end.

⟦q2.17|1⟧ 45. ⟦q2.17|2⟧ God, wishing to unite us to one another by inclination as well as by nature, and truly pushing the whole human toward this, engraved for us, in his love for man, the saving commandments, and for this reason simply legislated to show mercy and to be shown mercy.

⟦q2.18|1⟧ 46. ⟦q2.18|2⟧ The self-love and intelligence of men, having either thrust away or outwitted one another and the law, cut up the one nature into many portions; and, having introduced its insensibility that now prevails, armed nature itself against itself through inclination. ⟦q2.18|3⟧ For this reason, everyone who by sober thought and nobility of prudence has been able to dissolve this unevenness of nature has had mercy on himself before others, making his inclination accord with nature, and drawing near to God by inclination because of nature, and showing in himself what is the mode of the principle of «according to the image», and how God, having fittingly in the beginning made our nature like himself, and a manifest copy of his own goodness, established it identical with itself in all things, unwarring, peaceful, without faction, bound fast by love toward God and toward itself; according to which we cleave to God with aspiration, and to one another with fellow-feeling.

⟦q2.19|1⟧ 47. ⟦q2.19|2⟧ The God who loves man became man for this reason: that he might gather the nature of men to himself, and stop it from being borne along wickedly, at faction with itself, or rather against itself, and partitioned, and having no rest because of the unstable motion of inclination about each thing.

⟦q2.20|1⟧ 48. ⟦q2.20|2⟧ There is nothing among the things after God more honourable for those who have mind, or rather dearer to God, than perfect love, since it gathers into one those who are divided, and is [1197] able to make in the many, or in all, one identity without faction according to inclination. ⟦q2.20|3⟧ For it is proper to love to show forth as one the inclination of those who seek what is according to it.

⟦q2.21|1⟧ 49. ⟦q2.21|2⟧ If by nature the noble is unifying and holding-together of what is divided, evil, plainly, is divisive and corruptive of what is united. ⟦q2.21|3⟧ For evil by nature is scattered, and unstable, and many-formed, and divisive.

⟦q2.22|1⟧ 50. ⟦q2.22|2⟧ Deliverance from all evils, and a short road to salvation, is the true love of God in full knowledge, and the entire renunciation of the soul's affection toward the body and this world; by which, casting off the desire of pleasure and the fear of pain, we are freed from evil self-love, being raised up to the knowledge of the Creator; and, receiving in exchange for the wicked a good noetic self-love, separated from bodily affection, we do not cease worshipping God through this noble self-love, ever seeking from God the constitution of the soul. ⟦q2.22|3⟧ For this is true worship, and truly pleasing to God: the exact care of the soul through the virtues.

⟦q2.23|1⟧ 51. ⟦q2.23|2⟧ He who does not aspire to bodily pleasure, and does not fear pain at all, has become passionless. ⟦q2.23|3⟧ For with these, together with the self-love that bore them, he has killed with one accord all the passions that come through them and from them, together with ignorance, the most original of evils; and he has become wholly of the noble that stands and abides and is ever the same by nature, remaining utterly unmoved with it, and with unveiled face beholding as in a mirror the glory of God; from the light-like brilliance in it contemplating the divine and inaccessible glory.

⟦q2.24|1⟧ 52. ⟦q2.24|2⟧ Let us deny, so far as we can, the pleasure and the pain of the present life, and we shall be wholly delivered from every conception of passions and from demonic wickedness. ⟦q2.24|3⟧ For through pleasure we love the passions, and through pain we flee virtue.

⟦q2.25|1⟧ 53. ⟦q2.25|2⟧ Since every vice is naturally destroyed together with the modes that constitute it, man, finding through experience itself that pain is altogether the successor of every pleasure, had his whole impulse toward pleasure, and his whole flight from pain: fighting for the one with all his power, fighting down the other with all zeal; supposing, what was impossible, that by such scheming he would set these apart from one another, and have [1200] self-love joined to pleasure alone, wholly untried by pain; not knowing, under the passion, as it seems, that pleasure can never be without pain. ⟦q2.25|3⟧ For the toil of pain is blended into pleasure, even if it seems to escape those who have it, because of the prevailing of pleasure in the passion; since what prevails is naturally always visible, covering the sense of what lies beside it. ⟦q2.25|4⟧ So, laying claim to pleasure because of self-love, and again striving to flee pain for the same cause, we devise the unspeakable generations of corrupting passions.

⟦q2.26|1⟧ 54. ⟦q2.26|2⟧ One does not know the experience of sense in pleasure and pain when he binds, or rather glues, his mind, set free from bodily relation, to God, the truly beloved and yearned-for and aspired-to.

⟦q2.27|1⟧ 55. ⟦q2.27|2⟧ As one cannot otherwise worship God purely, unless he utterly cleanses the soul, so neither can he worship the creation, unless he cherishes the body; in respect of which body man, performing the worship that makes for corruption, and becoming self-loving in respect of it, had pleasure unceasingly, and pain at work, ever eating from the tree of disobedience, the tree of good and evil, which has, through experience, the knowledge mingled in the same respect according to sense. ⟦q2.27|3⟧ And perhaps one who says that the visible creation is *the tree of what is known of good and evil* will not miss the truth; for by nature it has a partaking that makes pleasure and pain.

⟦q2.28|1⟧ 56. ⟦q2.28|2⟧ Where reason does not rule, the rule according to sense naturally comes in; in it the power of sin is somehow mixed, drawing the soul down through pleasure to pity for the flesh kindred to it according to hypostasis; by which, having put into its hands as its natural work the passionate and pleasure-steeped care of the flesh, it leads it away from the life according to nature, and persuades it to become the creator of vice that has no hypostasis.

⟦q2.29|1⟧ 57. ⟦q2.29|2⟧ Vice of a noetic soul is the forgetting of the noble things according to nature, which comes upon it from the passionate relation to the flesh and the world; and this relation reason, as general, makes vanish according to spiritual science, searching out the coming-to-be and nature of the world and of the flesh, and driving the soul toward the kindred region of intelligibles; to which the law of sin makes no crossing, not having any longer, as a bridge carrying it over to the mind, sense, which has already been dissolved from the soul as to relation, and cast down among the sensible spectacles, whose relation and nature the mind has crossed beyond, and does not perceive at all.

⟦q2.30|1⟧ [1201] 58. ⟦q2.30|2⟧ As reason, ruling the passions, makes the senses an instrument of virtue, so too the passions, ruling reason, shape the senses toward sin. ⟦q2.30|3⟧ And one must soberly consider and meditate how the soul will fittingly make the noble reversal, using the things through which it formerly offended for the coming-to-be and hypostasis of virtues.

⟦q2.31|1⟧ 59. ⟦q2.31|2⟧ The holy Gospel introduces the denial of fleshly life, and the confession of spiritual life. ⟦q2.31|3+4⟧ And I speak of those who are always dying according to man, I mean the human life in the flesh in this age, but living according to God by the Spirit alone, like the divine Apostle and those about him, who in no way live their own life, but have Christ living in themselves according to the soul alone. [n: Galat. ii, 20.] Thus those who for God's sake are dead in the flesh in this age are judged, having many afflictions and torments and straits, and enduring with joy persecutions and countless kinds of temptations. [n: I Petr. iv, 6.]

⟦q2.32|1⟧ 60. ⟦q2.32|2⟧ Every passion is constituted by the interweaving of some sensible thing and sense, and a natural power: anger, say, or desire, or reason turned aside from what is according to nature. ⟦q2.32|3⟧ If then the mind, contemplating the end of these in their composition with one another, of the sensible, of sense, and of the natural power that bears upon it, is able, distinguishing each of them, to lead it back to its own principle by nature, and to contemplate the sensible by itself, without the relation of sense to it, and sense apart from the intimacy of the sensible to it, and desire, say, or some other of the powers according to nature, without the passionate disposition toward sense and the sensible, such as the qualified motion of the passion makes the contemplation become: then it has scattered and ground fine, like Israel's calf of old, the constitution of whatever passion occurs, and sown it under the water of knowledge, [n: Exod. xxxii, 20.] making wholly vanish even the bare imagination of the passions, through the restoration to themselves of the things that by nature make it up.

⟦q2.33|1⟧ 61. ⟦q2.33|2⟧ The life stained with many offences of the affections that come from the flesh is a soiled tunic. ⟦q2.33|3⟧ For each of men naturally shows through, as from a garment, by the conduct of his life, whether righteous or unrighteous: the one having a clean tunic, the virtuous life; the other possessing a life soiled with wicked works. ⟦q2.33|4⟧ Or rather, the tunic soiled by the flesh is that which, in the conscience, gives form through the memory of the

⟦q3.1|1⟧ [1204] … evil movements and effects from the flesh, to the soul, a habit and disposition; and the soul, seeing this continually about itself like a kind of tunic, is filled with the stench of passions. ⟦q3.1|2⟧ For as from the spirit, through the virtues woven together with one another according to reason, a tunic of incorruption comes to be for the soul, and having put it on the soul becomes beautiful and glorious; so also from the flesh, through the passions woven together with one another by correspondence, there comes to be a kind of tunic unclean and spotted, which of itself makes the soul known, setting in it another form and image beside the divine.

⟦q3.2|1⟧ 62. ⟦q3.2|2⟧ A firm assurance of the hope of deification for the nature of men is the becoming man of God, which makes man god as much as he himself became man. ⟦q3.2|3⟧ For he who became man without sin will deify the nature, clearly without its conversion into Godhead, and will raise it up for his own sake as much as he himself, for man's sake, brought himself down. ⟦q3.2|4⟧ Teaching this mystically, the great Apostle says that *in the ages to come* the surpassing riches of God's goodness toward us will be shown. [n: Ephes. ii, 7.]

⟦q3.3|1⟧ 63. ⟦q3.3|2⟧ Reason, presiding over anger and desire, makes the virtues; and the mind, applying itself to the principles, gathers the infallible knowledge of the things that have come to be. ⟦q3.3|3⟧ When, then, reason, after casting off what is opposed, finds what is by nature to be yearned for, and the mind, after passing through the things known, lays hold of the Cause of beings that is beyond essence and knowledge, then the passion of deification by grace supervenes: leading reason away from natural distinction, where there is nothing that is distinguished, and bringing the mind to rest from intellection according to nature, where there is nothing that is known, and making him who is counted worthy of the divine participation god, by the identity that belongs to rest.

⟦q3.4|1⟧ 64. ⟦q3.4|2⟧ Toil cleanses the soul that has been rusted with the filth of pleasure, and unnails altogether its relation to material things, the soul having learned instead the loss that friendship toward them brings; for which cause God permits the devil, by just judgement, to press men down with torments.

⟦q3.5|1⟧ 65. ⟦q3.5|2⟧ Pleasure and grief, desire and fear, and what follows on these, were not antecedently created together with the nature of men; since then they would also have contributed to the limit of the nature. ⟦q3.5|3⟧ I say this having learned from [1205] the great Gregory of Nyssa, that because of the falling-away from perfection these were brought in, grown onto the more irrational part of the nature; through which, in place of the blessed and divine image, at once with the transgression, the likeness of the irrational animals became transparent and manifest in man. ⟦q3.5|4⟧ For it was necessary, the dignity of reason having been veiled, that the nature of men should be justly punished by the marks of irrationality which it had drawn upon itself; God wisely arranging by economy that man should come to an awareness of his rational greatness of mind.

⟦q3.6|1⟧ 66. ⟦q3.6|2⟧ The passions too become good in the earnest, whenever, wisely withdrawing them from bodily things, we handle them for the acquisition of heavenly things: for instance, when we make desire the appetitive motion of the noetic aspiration toward divine things; pleasure, the unharmed gladness of the mind's enchanting activity over the divine gifts of grace; fear, the precautionary care against the punishment to come for offences; and grief, the corrective remorse over a present evil. ⟦q3.6|3⟧ And, to put it briefly, like the wise among physicians who, with the body of the viper, a destructive beast, take away the injury present or threatened, we use these passions for the destruction of vice present or expected, and for the acquisition and guarding of virtue and knowledge.

⟦q3.7|1⟧ 67. ⟦q3.7|2⟧ The law of the first Covenant, through practical philosophy, cleanses the nature of all defilement. ⟦q3.7|3⟧ That of the New, through contemplative mystagogy, raises the mind in the way of knowledge from bodily things to the kindred spectacles of intelligible things.

⟦q3.8|1⟧ 68. ⟦q3.8|2⟧ Divine Scripture calls *fearing* those who are beginners and are found somewhere at the gateway of the divine court of the virtues; those who have acquired a measured habit of the principles and modes of virtue it is wont to name *progressing*; and those who in it have already come, by way of knowledge, to the summit of the truth that reveals the virtues, it addresses as *perfect*. ⟦q3.8|3+4⟧ He, then, who has turned away from his old way of life in the passions, and through fear has given over his whole disposition to the divine commands, falls short of none of the goods befitting beginners, even though he has not yet acquired the habit in the virtues and become a partaker of the wisdom spoken among the perfect. [n: I Cor. ii, 6.] Yet neither does he who is progressing fall short of any of the goods laid [1208] upon his rank, even though he has not yet acquired the same knowledge of divine things that surpasses in the perfect. ⟦q3.8|5⟧ For the perfect, having already been mystically counted worthy of contemplative theology, and having made the mind pure of all material imagination, appear to have taken to their breast the divine love, which bears whole and without lack the imitation of the image of the divine beauty.

⟦q3.9|1⟧ 69. ⟦q3.9|2⟧ Fear is twofold: the one pure, the other not pure. ⟦q3.9|3⟧ For the fear that arises over offences in expectation of punishment, having sin as the cause of its own coming-to-be, is not pure, nor will it last for ever, vanishing together with sin through repentance. ⟦q3.9|3⟧ But that which always subsists without grief over offences, this is pure fear, [n: Psal. xviii, 10.] and it will never pass away; since it is in some manner essentially inborn in God toward creation, making manifest to all his natural venerableness, that of his pre-eminence above every kingship and power.

⟦q3.10|1⟧ 70. ⟦q3.10|2⟧ He who does not fear God as a judge, but reveres him for the surpassing pre-eminence of his infinite power, rightly has no lack, being perfect in love, loving God with reverence and the fitting veneration; and this is he who has acquired *the fear that endures to the age of the age*, and *there is no lack* to him at all.

⟦q3.11|1⟧ 71. ⟦q3.11|2⟧ From beings we come to know the cause of beings; and from the difference of beings we are taught the enhypostatic Wisdom of him who is; and from the natural motion of beings we learn the enhypostatic Life of him who is, the life-giving power of beings, the Holy Spirit.

⟦q3.12|1⟧ 72. ⟦q3.12|2⟧ The Holy Spirit is absent from none of beings, and above all from those that have in any way partaken of reason. ⟦q3.12|3⟧ For it holds together the knowledge of each, since, as God and Spirit of God, it passes through all things by power in the way of providence, and stirs up the reason in each according to nature, and through it brings him who perceives to an awareness of what has been done amiss against the ordinance of nature, and him who has his choice pliant to the reception of the right thoughts that come from nature. ⟦q3.12|4⟧ Hence we find many, even among utterly barbarous and nomadic men, laying claim to nobility of character, and setting aside the bestial laws that had prevailed among them from of old.

⟦q3.13|1+2⟧ 73. [1209] The Holy Spirit is in all simply, inasmuch as it holds all things together, provides for them, and stirs up the natural seeds. ⟦q3.13|3⟧ In a more determinate way it is also in all who are under the Law, inasmuch as it points out the transgression of the commandments and gives light concerning the promise foretold of Christ; and in all who are of Christ, in addition to what has been said, also as making sons by adoption. ⟦q3.13|4⟧ But as maker of wisdom it is simply in none of those mentioned, save those who understand and who, by their life in God, have made themselves worthy of its deifying indwelling. ⟦q3.13|5⟧ For everyone who does not do the divine wills, even if he is a believer, has a heart without intelligence, as a workshop of evil thoughts, and a body indebted to sins, as being held continually in the defilements of the passions.

⟦q3.14|1⟧ 74. ⟦q3.14|2⟧ God, who reaches out for the salvation of all men and hungers for their deification, curses and withers their conceit like the barren fig tree, [n: Matth. xxi, 19.] so that, preferring being just to seeming to be just, they may strip off the tunic of moral display worn in hypocrisy, and, pursuing without adulteration the virtuous life as the divine word wills, may pass their life in piety, displaying to God the disposition of the soul rather than to men the outward fashioning of their manners.

⟦q3.15|1⟧ 75. ⟦q3.15|2⟧ The principle of doing is one thing, and the principle of suffering another; and the principle of doing is the natural power for the activity of the virtues; that of suffering is either the grace of what is above nature or the befalling of what is contrary to nature. ⟦q3.15|3⟧ For as we have no natural power for what is beyond being, so neither have we by nature the power for what is not. ⟦q3.15|4⟧ We suffer deification, then, as being above nature, by grace, but we do not do it; for we do not have by nature a power receptive of deification. ⟦q3.15|5⟧ And again we suffer vice, as contrary to nature, by inclination, by way of befalling; for we have no natural power for the coming-to-be of vice. ⟦q3.15|6⟧ While we are here, then, we do the virtues by nature, having the natural power for doing them; but in what is to come we suffer deification, receiving the power to suffer it by the grace of a gift.

⟦q3.16|1+2⟧ 76. ⟦q3.16|1+2⟧ We do, inasmuch as we have by nature, in activity, the rational power productive of the virtues, and the noetic power receptive, without relation, of all knowledge, [1212] which passes through the whole nature of beings and of things known and puts all the ages behind itself; but we suffer when, having perfectly passed beyond the principles of the things that are from non-being, we come unknowingly to the Cause of beings, and make our own powers cease together with the things that are by nature finite, becoming that which is in no way an achievement of the power according to nature; since nature does not possess a power capable of grasping what is above nature. ⟦q3.16|1+2⟧ For nothing originated is by nature productive of deification, since it is not capable of grasping God either. ⟦q3.16|1+2⟧ For to the divine grace alone does this properly belong, to bestow deification on beings proportionately, as it makes nature resplendent with the light above nature and sets it above its own limits by the excess of glory.

⟦q3.17|1⟧ 77. ⟦q3.17|2⟧ Doing the virtues, we cease after this life; but deification by grace, in return for them, we do not cease suffering. ⟦q3.17|3⟧ For the passion above nature is unbounded, because it is also efficacious; but that contrary to nature is without existence, because it is also inert.

⟦q3.18|1⟧ 78. ⟦q3.18|2⟧ Types of the divine goods are both the modes according to virtue and the principles of beings; in which God continually becomes man, having the modes of the virtues as body, and as soul the principles of knowledge in the spirit; by which he deifies the worthy, giving an enhypostatic character of virtue and bestowing an enousial existence of unerring knowledge.

⟦q3.19|1⟧ 79. ⟦q3.19|2⟧ The faithful and practical mind, like holy Peter, held by Herod, the law of skin — for Herod is interpreted «of skin», which is the way of thinking of the flesh — is shut up under two guards and one iron gate, being warred upon both by the activity of the passions and by the assent of the understanding to the passions; and having passed through these, as through guards, that is, prisons, by the reason of practical philosophy as by an angel, it comes to the iron gate that leads into the city: I mean the relation of the senses to sensible things, firm and harsh and hard to overcome; and the reason of natural contemplation in the spirit, opening this gate so that it opens of itself, henceforth sends the mind on without fear to the kindred intelligible things, free of Herod's madness.

⟦q3.20|1+2⟧ 80. [1213] The devil is both enemy of God and avenger: enemy, when, as hating him, he seems somehow to have a destructive love toward us men, through the modes of the voluntary passions persuading our choice, by pleasure, to prefer temporal things to the everlasting goods; by which, stealing the whole aspiration of the soul, he removes us altogether from the divine love, making us voluntary enemies of him who made us; avenger, when, laying bare his hatred toward us, he demands the punishment against us, as of those who have already become subject to him through sin. ⟦q3.20|3⟧ For nothing is so dear to the devil as a man being punished. ⟦q3.20|4⟧ And when this has been permitted him, devising successive inflictions of involuntary passions, he falls harshly, like a whirlwind, upon those against whom he has received the authority by God's permission: not wishing to fulfil the divine command, but desiring to nourish his own passion of hatred against us; so that, under the great weight of painful calamities, the soul, sinking down through faintness, may cut off from itself the power of the divine hope, making the infliction of things that befall painfully a cause of godlessness instead of admonition.

⟦q3.21|1⟧ 81. ⟦q3.21|2⟧ When those who have received the practical habit and contemplative science pursue these for the glory of men, sketching the manners of the virtues in an outward mode, and only talking words of wisdom and knowledge without the works of justice, and displaying to others their arrogance, as over virtue and knowledge, they are with reason handed over to fitting toils, learning instead, through suffering, the humble-mindedness which, from their vain conceit, they had not known.

⟦q3.22|1⟧ 82. ⟦q3.22|2⟧ Each of the demons, according to the aptitude that inwardly underlies it, serves for the infliction of this or that temptation; for one is productive of one vice and another of another, and one is clearly fouler than another, and more apt for this form of vice rather than that.

⟦q3.23|1⟧ 83. ⟦q3.23|2⟧ Without the divine permission not even the demons themselves can in any way serve the devil. ⟦q3.23|3⟧ For God himself knows how, with the fitting providence that loves man and is good, he permits the devil, through his servants, to inflict the different punishments for the things in which we have sinned; and the book about Job shows this clearly, recording that the devil could not approach Job at all without the divine purpose. [n: Job i, 12.]

⟦q3.24|1⟧ 84. ⟦q3.24|2⟧ Enhypostatic faith is the active and practised faith, according to which the word of God is shown in those who practise, being embodied in the commandments,

⟦q4.1|1⟧ [1216] through which, as Word, he leads up those who do them to the Father, in whom he is by nature.

⟦q4.2|1⟧ 85. ⟦q4.2|2⟧ A change of life and angelic worship, and the soul's voluntary alienation toward the body, and the coming-to-be of the divine remaking in the spirit: these the mystery of the New Covenant proclaims. ⟦q4.2|3⟧ And the word is wont to call spiritual circumcision the cutting-away of the soul's impassioned relation toward the body.

⟦q4.3|1⟧ 86. ⟦q4.3|2⟧ God, being good and willing to pluck out of us altogether pleasure, the seed of vice, which has robbed the mind of the divine love, permits the devil to bring toils and punishments upon us: at the same time both scraping from the soul, through the toils, the rust of the pleasure that came before, and wishing to produce in us hatred and complete estrangement toward present things, which fawn on sense alone, as things that in use have won nothing more than punishment by way of profit; and to make that one's punishing and man-hating power a cause, by way of distress, of the return to virtue for those who have voluntarily slipped away from it.

⟦q4.4|1⟧ 87. ⟦q4.4|2⟧ It is fitting and just that those be punished by the devil who gladly received his evil suggestions of voluntary sins. ⟦q4.4|3⟧ For the devil is both the sower of pleasure through the voluntary passions, and the bringer of pain through the involuntary.

⟦q4.5|1⟧ 88. ⟦q4.5|2⟧ The contemplative mind, the mind of knowledge, is often handed over to the devil for punishment for this reason, he justly bringing toils and calamities upon it: that by suffering it may learn to philosophize about endurance and patience in toils, rather than to be proudly and idly vain about things that are not.

⟦q4.6|1⟧ 89. ⟦q4.6|2+3⟧ He who suffers on account of the grace of God that has been transgressed, if he recognizes the principle of the divine providence that is healing him, both receives the calamity thankfully, rejoicing, and corrects the fault for which he is being disciplined; but he who is insensible to this healing is both justly led away from the grace that was given and handed over to the confusion of the passions, being left to come to the doing of those things for which he inwardly had the aspiration.

⟦q4.7|1⟧ 90. ⟦q4.7|2⟧ Everyone who, out of awareness of what he has done, voluntarily endures to receive with the fitting thanksgiving the toilsome onsets of involuntary temptations, is not banished from the habit and grace according to virtue, as one who has voluntarily gone under the yoke of the king of Babylon, [n: Jerem. xxvii, 6 seqq.] and who accepts the onsets of torments as paying off a debt; but, remaining in them, he pays to the king of Babylon the violent toils that come from the passible part of the nature, and the assent of the understanding [1217] to them, as being their debtor because of the faults that went before; and to God, offering through true worship, I mean the lowly disposition, the correction of what was neglected.

⟦q4.8|1⟧ 91. ⟦q4.8|2⟧ He who does not receive with thanksgiving the calamity brought upon him for correction, by God's permission, through the involuntary temptations, and who does not, by changing his mind, put off the conceit of seeming to be just, as one who resists the divine decrees of the just judgements, and who does not consent to come voluntarily under the yoke of the king of Babylon according to the divine ordinance, is handed over into captivity, and to collars and bonds and pestilence and death and sword, and is altogether exiled from his own land. ⟦q4.8|3⟧ For all these things, and more than these, he suffers who is banished from the habit according to virtue and knowledge as from his own land; because out of pride and vain conceit he does not will, paying the penalties for the things in which he did amiss, to take pleasure in afflictions and necessities and distresses, according to the divine Apostle. ⟦q4.8|4⟧ For the great Apostle knew that the humility which comes about outwardly in the body through toils is protective of the divine treasures in the soul; and for this reason he endured contentedly, both for his own sake and for those to whom he had been set forth as a type of virtue and faith; so that, even if they suffer as answerable, like the Corinthian who was rebuked, [n: I Cor. v, 5.] they might have, for consolation and for imitation of patience, him who suffers without being answerable.

⟦q4.9|1⟧ 92. ⟦q4.9|2⟧ He who does not remain in the shapes of visible things because of sense, but seeks out their principles with the mind, contemplating them as types of intelligible things or principles of sensible creatures, is taught that nothing of what is seen is unclean; for all things are by nature *very good*. [n: Gen. i, 31.]

⟦q4.10|1⟧ 93. ⟦q4.10|2⟧ He who is not altered together with the motion of sensible things pursues the unadulterated practice of the virtues. ⟦q4.10|3⟧ He who has not imprinted his mind with their shapes has received the true opinion of beings. ⟦q4.10|4⟧ And he who with his understanding has run past even the very essence of beings, as an excellent theologian, has after it unknowingly drawn near to the Monad.

⟦q4.11|1⟧ 94. ⟦q4.11|2⟧ Every contemplative mind, having *the sword of the spirit, which is the word of God*, [n: Ephes. vi, 17.] having slain within itself the motion of the visible creation, has achieved virtue; and having cut off from itself the imagination of sensible shapes, has found the truth in the principles of [1220] beings, in which natural contemplation consists; and having come to be above the essence of beings, receives the illumination of the divine and unassailable Monad, in which the mystery of true theology consists.

⟦q4.12|1⟧ 95. ⟦q4.12|2⟧ To each, according to the opinion of God that underlies him, God appears: to those who in aspiration have passed beyond material composition, and possess the powers of the soul equal in power to one another according to one and the same ever-moving around God, he shows himself as Monad and Trinity, that he may both point out his own existence and mystically teach its mode. ⟦q4.12|3⟧ But to those who have their aspiration moving about material composition alone, and possess the powers of the soul unconnected with one another, he appears not as he is but as they are, showing that they have laid hold with both hands of the material dyad, by which the bodily world consists, as from matter and form.

⟦q4.13|1⟧ 96. ⟦q4.13|2+3⟧ The divine Apostle calls the different activities of the one Holy Spirit different gifts of grace, worked, clearly, by one and the same Spirit. [n: I Cor. xii, 4.] If, then, the manifestation of the Spirit is given according to the measure of the faith in each, in the participation of such and such a gift of grace, each of the faithful clearly receives, according to the proportion of his faith and of the disposition of soul that underlies him, the activity of the Spirit measured to it, bestowing on him the habit suited to the activity of this or that commandment.

⟦q4.14|1⟧ 97. ⟦q4.14|2⟧ As one receives *a word of wisdom*, another *a word of knowledge*, another *faith*, and another something else of the gifts of grace of the Spirit enumerated by the great Apostle, [n: ibid. 8-12.] so one receives through the Spirit the gift of grace of love perfect and immediate toward God and having nothing material, according to the proportion of faith; another, through the same Spirit, the gift of grace of perfect love toward the neighbour; and another something else, according to the same Spirit, as I said, each having his own gift of grace worked in him. ⟦q4.14|3⟧ For he says that every habit of a commandment that is worked is a gift of grace of the Spirit.

⟦q4.15|1⟧ 98. ⟦q4.15|2⟧ The baptism of the Lord is the complete mortification of our choice toward the sensible world; and the cup is the denial even of our present life itself for the sake of truth. [n: Marc. x, 38.]

⟦q4.16|1⟧ 99. ⟦q4.16|2⟧ The baptism of the Lord is a type of the toils, voluntary and our own, taken up by intent for the sake of virtue; through which, washing off the stains of conscience, we accept the voluntary death of our choice toward the things that appear; and the cup is a type of the involuntary temptations which, from distress and against our choice, rise up against us for the sake of the truth; [1221] through which, preferring the divine longing even to nature itself, we willingly undergo the death of nature that comes by distress.

⟦q4.17|1⟧ 100. ⟦q4.17|2⟧ The baptism has this difference from the cup: that the baptism, for the sake of virtue, makes the choice dead toward the pleasant things of life; while the cup persuades the pious to prefer the truth even to nature itself.

⟦q4.18|1⟧ SECOND CENTURY

⟦q4.19|1⟧ 1. ⟦q4.19|2⟧ He set the cup before the baptism, because virtue is for the sake of truth, but not truth for the sake of virtue. ⟦q4.19|3⟧ Hence he who does virtue for the sake of truth is not wounded by the arrows of vainglory; but he who cultivates truth for the sake of virtue has the conceit of vainglory living with him.

⟦q4.20|1⟧ 2. ⟦q4.20|2⟧ By truth he means the divine knowledge; by virtue, the struggles for truth of those who aspire to it. ⟦q4.20|3⟧ He, then, who for the sake of knowledge endures the toils of virtue is not vainglorious, knowing that truth is by nature not to be grasped by the toils; for what is first is not by nature circumscribed by what is second. ⟦q4.20|4⟧ But he who cultivates knowledge for the sake of the struggles on its behalf is altogether vainglorious, as thinking he has received the crowns before the sweat; not knowing that the toils are for the sake of the crowns, but not the crowns for the sake of the toils; for every method by nature falls out of exercise once that for which it exists has been accomplished, or seems to have been accomplished.

⟦q4.21|1⟧ 3. ⟦q4.21|2⟧ He who cultivates only the form of knowledge, which is the bare word, and he who cultivates the image of virtue, which is the bare manner, is himself also a Jew, puffed up with types of the truth.

⟦q4.22|1⟧ 4. ⟦q4.22|2⟧ He who sees the whole visible worship of the Law not according to sense, but has examined each of the visible symbols by the approaches of the mind, being taught the God-perfect Word hidden in each, finds God in the Law, groping well with the noetic power, as in a heap of sweepings, in the matter of the legal ordinances, whether he may somewhere find, hidden in the flesh of the Law, the pearl, the Word, which altogether escapes sense.

⟦q4.23|1⟧ 5. ⟦q4.23|2⟧ And he too who does not circumscribe the nature of visible things by sense alone, but wisely searches out with the mind [1224] the principle in each creature, finds God, being taught from the magnificence of beings set forth the cause of beings themselves. [n: Sap. xiii, 5.]

⟦q4.24|1⟧ 6. ⟦q4.24|2⟧ Since, then, distinction is proper to him who gropes, he who goes through the symbols of the Law in the way of knowledge, and contemplates the visible nature of beings scientifically, distinguishing Scripture and creation and himself — Scripture into letter and spirit; creation into principle and surface; himself into mind and sense — and taking, of Scripture, the spirit; of creation, the principle; of himself, the mind; and uniting them unbreakably with one another, has found God; as having recognized, as is fitting and as is possible, God who is in Mind and Word and Spirit; having been freed from all the things that lead astray and drag down into countless opinions, I mean letter and surface and sense, in which is the differing quantity, opposed to the Monad. ⟦q4.24|3⟧ But if anyone intertwines the letter of the Law and the surface of visible things and his own sense with one another and commixes them, he *is blind, short-sighted*, sick with ignorance of the Cause of beings.

⟦q4.25|1⟧ 7. ⟦q4.25|2+3⟧ The divine and great Apostle, defining what faith is, says: *Faith is the hypostasis of things hoped for, the proof of things not seen*. [n: Hebr. xi, 1.] And if anyone should define it also as an inward good, or as true knowledge demonstrative of the ineffable goods, he would not have missed the truth.

⟦q4.26|1⟧ 8. ⟦q4.26|2⟧ Faith is a relational power, or an efficacious relation, of the perfect, immediate union, above nature, of him who believes with the God in whom he believes.

⟦q4.27|1⟧ 9. ⟦q4.27|2⟧ Since, then, man, being of soul and body, is tossed by two laws, I mean of flesh and of spirit; and the law of the flesh has its activity according to sense, and that of the spirit according to the mind; and the law of the flesh is wont to bind to matter, being active according to sense, while that of the spirit, being active according to the mind, effects the union with God immediately: fittingly *he who is not two-minded in his heart* — that is, who has not distinguished in the mind, or cut asunder, the immediate union with God that has come about through faith — as passionless, or rather as one already become god through faith by the union, *will say to this mountain* to move, *and it will move*; [1225] by saying *this*, showing as with a pointing finger the way of thinking and the law of the flesh, which is truly heavy and hard to shift, and, as far as natural power goes, altogether immovable and unshakeable.

⟦q4.28|1⟧ 10. ⟦q4.28|2⟧ So deeply has the power of irrationality been rooted in the nature of men through sense, that the many think man to be nothing else than flesh, having sense as a power for the enjoyment of the present life.

⟦q4.29|1⟧ 11. ⟦q4.29|2⟧ *All things*, he says, *are possible to him who believes*, [n: Marc. ix, 22.] and is not two-minded, that is, who is not divided from the union with God that has come about for him in the mind through faith, because of the relation of the soul to the body according to sense: all things, that is, that estrange the mind from world and flesh, and make it akin to God, perfected by its achievements. ⟦q4.29|2⟧ For this must be understood in addition to the words *All things are possible to him who believes*.

⟦q4.30|1⟧ 12. ⟦q4.30|2⟧ Faith is knowledge without demonstration. ⟦q4.30|3⟧ And if it is knowledge without demonstration, then faith is a relation above nature, through which we are united to God unknowingly, and not demonstratively, by the union above intellection.

⟦q4.31|1⟧ 13. ⟦q4.31|2⟧ The mind that has received the immediate union with God has the power of thinking and being thought altogether idle. ⟦q4.31|3⟧ Whenever, then, it looses this, having thought of something of the things that come after God, it has become two-minded, cutting the union above intellection, according to which, so long as it is joined to God, as having become above nature and god by participation, it removes from itself, like an immovable mountain, the law of nature.

⟦q4.32|1⟧ 14. ⟦q4.32|2⟧ He who is being introduced to piety, being taught about the works of justice, performs the practice alone, with all obedience and faith, as though eating, like flesh, the visible things of the virtues, that is, the moral instruction; but the principles of the commandments, in which the knowledge of the perfect consists, he yields to God through faith, being unable as yet to stretch himself out along with the length of knowledge.

⟦q4.33|1⟧ 15. ⟦q4.33|2⟧ The perfect man, having run through not only the rank of beginners but also that of those progressing, is not ignorant of the principles of the things done by him according to the commandment; but, having first drunk them through in spirit, he eats through works the whole flesh of the

⟦q5.1|1⟧ [1228] virtues, raising up to knowledge according to the mind the motion of the things that come to be according to sense.

⟦q5.2|1⟧ 16. ⟦q5.2|2⟧ The Lord, saying, *Seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness* [n: Matth. vi, 33.] — that is, before all things the full knowledge of the truth, and so the exercise of the fitting modes — clearly showed that those who believe must make their inquiry concerning divine knowledge alone, and the virtue that adorns it through works.

⟦q5.3|1⟧ 17. ⟦q5.3|2⟧ Since the things sought by those who believe toward the knowledge of God and virtue happen to be many — release from passions, endurance of temptations, principles of virtues, modes of activities, the unnailing of the soul's disposition toward the flesh, the estrangement of sense's relation to sensible things, the mind's complete withdrawal from all things that have come to be. ⟦q5.3|3⟧ And, simply, there are countless others, those that make for abstinence from vice and ignorance, and for the achievement of knowledge and virtue: fittingly the Lord said, *All things whatsoever you ask believing, you shall receive*; [n: Matth. xxi, 22.] saying that the pious must both seek and ask, with science and faith, simply all things — only those that tend toward the full knowledge of God and virtue. ⟦q5.3|4⟧ For all these are profitable, and the Lord certainly gives them to those who ask.

⟦q5.4|1⟧ 18. ⟦q5.4|2⟧ He, then, who for the sake of faith alone, that is, the immediate union with God, seeks all the things that make for the union, will certainly receive; but he who, without this cause, seeks either other things or the things aforesaid, will not receive. ⟦q5.4|3⟧ For he does not believe, but as an unbeliever he trades on the divine things for his own glory.

⟦q5.5|1⟧ 19. ⟦q5.5|2⟧ He who by inclination keeps pure of the corruption that is according to sin corrupts the corruption of the things whose nature it is to corrupt. ⟦q5.5|3⟧ For the incorruption of choice is of a nature to keep the corruption of nature incorrupt, according to providence, through the grace of the Spirit that is in it, not permitting it to be corrupted by the contrary qualities.

⟦q5.6|1⟧ 20. ⟦q5.6|2⟧ Since there is not one and the same principle of nature and of grace, it is not worth puzzling over how some of the saints at times rose above passions and at times rather fell under passions; since we know that the wonder was of grace, and the passion of nature.

⟦q5.7|1⟧ 21. ⟦q5.7|2⟧ He who through imitation holds the memory of the way of life of the saints puts off the deadness of the passions and takes up the life of the virtues.

⟦q5.8|1⟧ 22. ⟦q5.8|2⟧ In the manner that he wills, God, who marked out the life of each before the ages, leads each to the end proper to his life, whether just or unjust.

⟦q5.9|1⟧ 23. ⟦q5.9|2⟧ The gloomy storm that befell the blessed Paul I judge to be the weight of involuntary temptations; the island, the firm habit of [1229] divine hope, and unshakeable; the fire, the habit of knowledge; the sticks, the nature of visible things, which he gathered with his hand — I mean with the mind's tactile power according to contemplation — nourishing with the concepts drawn from it the habit of knowledge, which heals the dejection rubbed onto the understanding by the storm of temptations; the viper, the wicked and destructive power secretly hidden in the nature of sensible things, which bit the hand, that is, the tactile activity of contemplation according to the mind, but did not harm the clear-sighted mind, which at once destroyed by the light of knowledge, as in fire, the destructive power fastened onto the practical movement of the mind from the contemplation of sensible things.

⟦q5.10|1⟧ 24. ⟦q5.10|2⟧ The Apostle was a savour from life unto life, as preparing the faithful, by the example of himself, to be moved through practice toward the fragrance of the virtues; or as a herald carrying over those persuaded by the word of grace from the life according to sense into the life in the spirit. ⟦q5.10|3⟧ And a savour of death unto death to those who from the death of ignorance had driven on into the death of unbelief, giving them a sense of the condemnation that awaits them. ⟦q5.10|4⟧ Or again, a savour from life unto life to those who are led up from practice to contemplation; and a savour of death unto death to those who, from making dead, by idleness as to sin, *the members that are upon the earth*, [n: Coloss. iii, 5.] pass over into the praised deadness of passionate concepts and imaginations.

⟦q5.11|1⟧ 25. ⟦q5.11|2⟧ Three are the powers of the soul: reason, anger, and desire. ⟦q5.11|3⟧ By reason we seek; by desire we long for the good that was sought; by anger we contend on its behalf. ⟦q5.11|4⟧ According to these powers, then, those who love God, persevering in the divine word of virtue and of knowledge — by the one seeking, by the other longing, by the other contending on its behalf — receive food incorruptible and fattening the mind: the knowledge of the things that have come to be.

⟦q5.12|1⟧ 26. ⟦q5.12|2⟧ The nature emptied of the knowledge that had been given it, the Word of God, having become man, filled again with knowledge; and, tempering it to unchangeableness, not by nature but by quality, he deified it, stamping it without deficiency with his own Spirit, as one who blends water with the quality of wine for strength. ⟦q5.12|3⟧ For it is for this that he truly becomes man, that by grace he may make us gods.

⟦q5.13|1⟧ 27. ⟦q5.13|2⟧ God, who created the nature of men, together with giving it being by his wish, fitted to it also a power productive of what is fitting; and by power I mean the motion that is essentially sown [1232] in the nature toward the activity of the virtues, but by inclination is manifested, in use, according to the wish of its possessor.

⟦q5.14|1⟧ 28. ⟦q5.14|2⟧ We have a natural criterion, the law according to nature, which teaches us that before the wisdom in all things our aspiration ought to be moved toward the mystagogy of the maker of all things.

⟦q5.15|1⟧ 29. ⟦q5.15|2⟧ The well of Jacob is Scripture; the water is the knowledge in Scripture; the depth, the hard-to-penetrate setting of the enigmas of Scripture; the bucket is the learning, through the letters, of the divine word, which the Lord did not have, being the Word himself, and giving to those who believe not the knowledge that comes from learning and study, but bestowing on the worthy the ever-flowing wisdom that comes from spiritual grace, which never ceases. ⟦q5.15|3⟧ For the bucket, that is, learning, taking a very small part of knowledge, leaves the whole, grasped in no way. ⟦q5.15|4⟧ But the knowledge according to grace has, even without study, the whole of the wisdom attainable by men, welling up variously according to the needs.

⟦q5.16|1⟧ 30. ⟦q5.16|2⟧ The tree of life and that which is not such have, from this alone — that the one was named tree of life, and the other not of life, but only of knowledge of good and evil — a great and unspeakable difference. ⟦q5.16|3⟧ For the tree of life is certainly also productive of life; but the tree not of life is clearly productive of death. ⟦q5.16|4⟧ For what is not productive of life, from not having been called tree of life, would clearly be productive of death; for nothing else is divided over against life by opposition.

⟦q5.17|1⟧ 31. ⟦q5.17|2⟧ The tree of life, also as wisdom, has the greatest difference from the tree of knowing good and evil, which neither is wisdom nor is so named. ⟦q5.17|3⟧ For proper to wisdom are mind and reason; but proper to the habit set over against wisdom as its contrary are irrationality and sense.

⟦q5.18|1⟧ 32. ⟦q5.18|2⟧ Since man came into coming-to-be composed of a noetic soul and a sentient body, let the tree of life be, by one approach, the mind of the soul, in which the very thing that wisdom is resides; and the tree of knowledge of good and evil, the sense of the body, in which clearly resides the motion of irrationality, [1233] which man, having received the divine commandment not to touch it by way of experience through activity, did not keep. [n: Gen. ii, 17.]

⟦q5.19|1⟧ 33. ⟦q5.19|2⟧ Both trees according to Scripture, that is, the mind and sense, are able to distinguish certain things: for example, the mind has a power that distinguishes intelligibles and sensibles, temporal things and everlasting; or rather, being a distinguishing power of the soul, it persuades her to cling to the one and to rise above the other. ⟦q5.19|3⟧ And sense has a power that distinguishes pleasure and pain of bodies; or rather, being a power of ensouled and sentient bodies, it persuades them to draw pleasure to themselves and to send pain away.

⟦q5.20|1⟧ 34. ⟦q5.20|2⟧ Man, if he gives himself to the sensory distinction of bodies alone, according to pleasure and pain, transgressing the divine commandment, eats of the tree of knowledge of good and evil, that is, the irrationality according to sense; having only the distinction that sustains bodies, by which he clings to pleasure as good and holds off from pain as evil. ⟦q5.20|3⟧ But if he gives himself wholly to the noetic distinction alone, which distinguishes temporal things from the everlasting, keeping the divine commandment, he eats of the tree of life — I mean the wisdom that subsists according to the mind; having only the distinction that sustains the soul, by which he clings to the glory of everlasting things as good and holds off from the corruption of temporal things as evil.

⟦q5.21|1⟧ 35. ⟦q5.21|2⟧ The good of the mind is the passionless disposition toward the spirit; its evil, the passionate relation to sense. ⟦q5.21|2⟧ And the good of sense is the passionate motion toward the body according to pleasure; its evil, the disposition that supervenes by privation of this.

⟦q5.22|1⟧ 36. ⟦q5.22|2⟧ He who has persuaded his conscience to hold the worst, when it is done, as good by nature, he, stretching out his practical part like a hand, took blameworthily of the tree of life, thinking the worst immortal by nature. ⟦q5.22|3⟧ Wherefore God, having naturally placed in man the accusation of evil according to the conscience, set him apart from life, since he had become evil by choice; so that, doing evil, he might not be able to persuade his own conscience that the worst is good by nature.

⟦q5.23|1⟧ 37. ⟦q5.23|1⟧ The vine makes wine; wine, drunkenness; drunkenness, ecstasy. ⟦q5.23|2⟧ Therefore the active word, which is the vine, cultivated by the virtues, begets knowledge; and knowledge begets the good ecstasy, which puts the mind out of its relation to sense.

⟦q5.24|1⟧ 38. ⟦q5.24|2⟧ To the concepts of sensible things the evil one is wont knavishly to join the forms and shapes of sensible things, through which the passions are of a nature to be made about the surfaces of visible things, while, through the sense that lies between, the [1236] crossing of the rational activity within us toward intelligible things comes to rest. ⟦q5.24|3⟧ Wherefore he also prevails to plunder the soul and to drag it down into the confusion of the passions.

⟦q5.25|1⟧ 39. ⟦q5.25|2⟧ The word of God is a lamp and, at the same time, likewise a light: [n: Psal. cxviii, 105; Prov. vi, 23.] both as illumining the thoughts of the faithful that are according to nature, and as burning those contrary to nature; and as dissolving the gloom of the life according to sense for those who press on through the commandments toward the life that is hoped for, and as punishing with the burning of judgement those who by inclination, through love of the flesh, cling to this dark night of life.

⟦q5.26|1⟧ 40. ⟦q5.26|2⟧ He who has not first, he says, been led up to himself through the casting-off of the passions contrary to nature will not be led up to his own cause, that is, God, through the acquisition in grace of the goods above nature. ⟦q5.26|3⟧ For he who is truly gathered to God must be separated in understanding from the things that have been made.

⟦q5.27|1⟧ 41. ⟦q5.27|2⟧ The work of the written law is release from the passions; of the natural law, equality of honour toward all men according to equality of right; and the perfection of the spiritual law, assimilation to God, as far as is possible for man.

⟦q5.28|1⟧ 42. ⟦q5.28|2⟧ The power of the mind is by nature receptive of the knowledge of bodies and of bodiless things; but of that of the holy Trinity it receives the reflections by grace alone, believing only that it is, but not presuming as to what it is according to essence, as the demonic mind does. ⟦q5.28|3⟧ For he who has no share of knowledge in no way knows the mode, according to virtue, that cleanses vice.

⟦q5.29|1⟧ 43. ⟦q5.29|2⟧ He who loves the lie is handed over to it for destruction, so that he may know by suffering what he willingly cherished, and, coming to experience, learn that unawares he was embracing death instead of life.

⟦q5.30|1⟧ 44. ⟦q5.30|2⟧ God has the knowledge of the good alone, as being according to essence both the nature and the knowledge of the good; but of evil, ignorance, as having incapacity for it; for of the things of which he naturally has the power, of these he essentially possesses the knowledge.

⟦q5.31|1⟧ 45. ⟦q5.31|2⟧ The breast in Leviticus signifies the better [1237] and lofty contemplation; and the arm, practice, that is, the habit and activity of the discursive part, or knowledge and virtue: since knowledge brings the mind immediately to God himself, and virtue, according to practice, withdraws it from all the coming-to-be of beings; which things the word set apart for the priests, who through all things have acquired God alone as an inheritance and possess nothing whatever earthly.

⟦q5.32|1⟧ 46. ⟦q5.32|2⟧ Since those who are wholly qualified in the spirit by knowledge and virtue make the hearts of others, through the word of teaching, receptive of piety and faith, and, withdrawing their practical habit and power from the pursuits that concern the corruptible nature, transfer them to the activity of the incorruptible goods above nature: fittingly the Word commanded that, of the things brought for a sacrifice to God, the breast of the wave-offering — that is, the heart of those who are brought — and the arm — that is, clearly, their practice — be consecrated to the priests.

⟦q5.33|1⟧ 47. ⟦q5.33|2⟧ All justice here, compared with that to come, holds the role of a mirror, having the image of the archetypal realities, but not the realities themselves subsisting in form; and all knowledge here of lofty things, compared with that to come, is an enigma, having a reflection of the truth, but not having, subsisting, the very truth that is to be made manifest.

⟦q5.34|1⟧ 48. ⟦q5.34|2⟧ Since divine things are held together by virtue and knowledge, the mirror is indicative of the prototypes according to virtue, and the enigma is manifestive of the archetypes according to knowledge.

⟦q5.35|1⟧ 49. ⟦q5.35|2⟧ He who has pleased God through practice clearly transfers his mind through contemplation to the region of intelligible things, [n: Gen. v, 24; Hebr. xi, 5.] so that through some imagination he may not behold, according to sense, the death that is in the passions, since he is not found at all by any of those who wish to seize him.

⟦q5.36|1⟧ 50. ⟦q5.36|2⟧ He who, with the pure eye of faith, has seen the comeliness of the good things to come obeys readily to go out from land and kindred and father's house, [n: Gen. xii, 1.] leaving behind the relation and the passionate attachment to flesh and sense and sensible things; and, being higher than nature in the time of temptation and of contests, preferring

⟦q6.1|1⟧ [1240] the cause of nature, as the great Abraham preferred God to Isaac. [n: Gen. xxii, 1 seqq.]

⟦q6.2|1⟧ 51. ⟦q6.2|2⟧ He who makes it his practice to pursue virtue, or the study of the divine words, neither for the sake of glory, nor on the pretext of greed, nor for the sake of flattery, and of man-pleasing and display, but who both does and says and thinks all things for God's sake — this man walks in the way of truth in knowledge. ⟦q6.2|3⟧ For the divine word is not of a nature to linger in paths not straight, [n: Isa. xl, 3.] even if it finds the way ready in some.

⟦q6.3|1⟧ 52. ⟦q6.3|2⟧ One fasts, and abstains from the diet that kindles the passions, and does the other things that can contribute to release from vice: this man has prepared the way spoken of; [n: ibid. 4; Luc. iii, 5.] but for vainglory, greed, or flattery, or man-pleasing, or for the sake of some other cause, apart from the divine good pleasure, he practises such manners: this man has not made the paths of God straight; and he endured the toil of preparing the way, but did not have God walking about in his paths.

⟦q6.4|1⟧ 53. ⟦q6.4|2⟧ *Every valley shall be filled*: [n: ibid.] not simply every one, nor of all; for not of those who have not prepared the way of the Lord and made his paths straight. ⟦q6.4|3⟧ When, then, the valley — that is, the flesh or soul — of those who, as I said, have prepared the way of the Lord and made his paths straight is filled with knowledge and virtue, through the presence of God the Word who walks about in them through the commandments, all the spirits of *the knowledge falsely so called* and of vice are brought low, the Word treading them down and subjecting them, and casting down the evil might that exalts itself against human nature, and as it were digging down the greatness and the height of the mountains and the hills, and bringing them to the filling of the valleys. ⟦q6.4|4⟧ For the casting-off of the passions contrary to nature, and the putting-on of the virtues according to nature, fills up the soul that has been made a valley and brings low the stirred-up dominion of the evil spirits.

⟦q6.5|1⟧ 54. ⟦q6.5|2⟧ *The rough ways* (that is, the onsets of involuntary temptations) *shall become smooth ways*, especially when the mind, rejoicing and glad, takes pleasure in weaknesses and afflictions and necessities, removing through involuntary toils the whole dominion of the voluntary passions. ⟦q6.5|3⟧ For it has called *rough ways* the occurrences of involuntary temptations, which change into smooth ways through endurance with thanksgiving.

⟦q6.6|1⟧ 55. ⟦q6.6|2⟧ He who aspires to true life, knowing that every toil, whether voluntary or involuntary, becomes the death of pleasure, the mother of death, will receive with gladness, rejoicing, all the rough [1241] onsets of involuntary temptations; making the afflictions, through endurance, easy and smooth ways, which escort him unerringly to *the prize of the high calling* [n: Philipp. iii, 14.] as he piously runs the divine course in them. ⟦q6.6|3⟧ For indeed the mother of death is pleasure; and the death of pleasure is toil, both the elective and that which is against choice.

⟦q6.7|1⟧ 56. ⟦q6.7|2⟧ Everyone, therefore, who by self-control has untied the many-coiled and many-twisted pleasure, interwoven in many ways with all sensible things together, *has made the crooked straight*; and he who through endurance has trodden down the hard-to-pass and rough onset of toils *has made the rough ways smooth*. ⟦q6.7|3⟧ Hence, as a prize of virtue and of the labours undertaken for it, as one who has contended well and lawfully, and conquered pleasure by longing for virtue, and trodden down pain by yearning for knowledge, and through both nobly carried through the divine contests, *he shall see*, as it is written, *the salvation of God*. [n: Isa. xl, 5.]

⟦q6.8|1⟧ 57. ⟦q6.8|2⟧ He who loves virtue willingly makes the furnace of pleasures wither; and he who is qualified in mind by the knowledge of the truth is not held back by involuntary toils, since the ever-moving according to aspiration bears him toward God.

⟦q6.9|1⟧ 58. ⟦q6.9|2⟧ He who through self-control has straightened the crooked things of the voluntary passions, that is, the movements of pleasure, and through endurance has levelled the rough things, the misfortunes of involuntary temptations, that is, the modes of pain, and has set them as smooth ways — such a man shall fittingly see the salvation of God, having become pure in heart; in which, through the virtues and pious objects of contemplation, he sees God at the end of the contests, according to *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*, [n: Ibid. 9.] having received in return for the toils for virtue the grace of passionlessness, than which nothing more manifests God to those who have it.

⟦q6.10|1⟧ 59. ⟦q6.10|2⟧ Scripture calls cisterns the hearts receptive of the heavenly gifts of grace of holy knowledge, which are hewn out by the firm word of the commandments, and cast off, like callosities, both the love of pleasure toward the passions and the relation of nature to sensible things, and are filled with the knowledge borne from above, that cleanses the passions and gives life to, and as it were nourishes, the virtues in the spirit.

⟦q6.11|1⟧ [1244] 60. ⟦q6.11|1⟧ The Lord hews cisterns in the desert — I mean in the world and in the nature of men — digging out the hearts of the worthy, and cleansing them of the material weight and way of thinking, and making them spacious for the reception of the divine rains of wisdom and of knowledge, so that they may water the cattle of Christ — I mean those who, through infancy of soul, need moral teaching.

⟦q6.12|1⟧ 61. ⟦q6.12|2⟧ Scripture calls hill country the lofty contemplation of nature in the spirit, which those cultivate who have died away from the imaginations of sensible things and have crossed over, through the virtues in between, to their intelligible principles.

⟦q6.13|1⟧ 62. ⟦q6.13|2⟧ The mind, so long as it has the memory of God living within itself, seeks out the Lord through contemplation; and not simply, but *in the fear of the Lord*, that is, in the practice of the commandments. ⟦q6.13|3⟧ For he who seeks out the Lord through contemplation without practice does not find him, because he did not seek out the Lord in the fear of the Lord. ⟦q6.13|4⟧ *And the Lord prospered him*; for the Lord prospers everyone who acts with knowledge, both teaching the modes of the commandments and revealing the true principles of beings.

⟦q6.14|1⟧ 63. ⟦q6.14|2⟧ The lofty word concerning Godhead is a tower in the soul, fortified by the activities of the commandments. ⟦q6.14|3⟧ And this is what it says: *Uzziah* *built towers in Jerusalem*. ⟦q6.14|4⟧ For he who is well prospered in the seeking out of the Lord through contemplation, with the fear that befits it — that is, the practice of the commandments — builds towers in Jerusalem, that is, in the simple and quiescent state of the soul, raising high the words concerning Godhead.

⟦q6.15|1⟧ 64. ⟦q6.15|2⟧ The principles of particular things, coming over to the universal, make the unions of the things divided; because the more universal comprehend uniformly the principles of the more particular, to which the particulars naturally have their reference. ⟦q6.15|3⟧ But also of mind to sense, and of heaven to earth, and of sensibles to intelligibles, and of nature to principle — of these too there is a certain relational principle in the spirit, giving them union with one another.

⟦q6.16|1⟧ 65. ⟦q6.16|2⟧ He who has been able to unnail the senses from the passions, and has separated the soul from the relation of the senses, has prevailed to wall off the entry that the devil had made toward the mind through the senses that lie between; and for this reason in the desert — I mean natural contemplation — he has built, as it were secure towers, the pious opinions concerning beings, in which he who takes refuge does not fear the demons who rob in this desert — I mean the nature of visible things — and lead the mind astray through sense and drag it down to the gloom of ignorance. ⟦q6.16|3⟧ For he who has acquired the pious opinion concerning each thing does not dread the demons who lead men astray through the things that appear.

⟦q6.17|1⟧ [1245] 66. ⟦q6.17|2⟧ Every mind that has strength for contemplation is a true husbandman, keeping by its own zeal and care the divine seeds of the good things pure of tares, so long as it has the memory of God preserving it. ⟦q6.17|3⟧ For it says: *And he was seeking out the Lord in the days of Zechariah, in the fear of the Lord*. ⟦q6.17|4⟧ And the word knows that Zechariah, translated into the Greek tongue, is "memory of God". ⟦q6.17|5⟧ Therefore let us always beseech the Lord that his saving memory be guarded for us, lest the very thing that is rightly achieved destroy the soul, lifted up to a height and having dared things above nature, like Uzziah.

⟦q6.18|1⟧ 67. ⟦q6.18|2⟧ Unerring contemplation of beings needs a soul released from passions, which is called Jerusalem because of its perfect virtue and its immaterial knowledge; which comes about not only through privation of passions but also of sensible imaginations.

⟦q6.19|1⟧ 68. ⟦q6.19|2⟧ Without faith and hope and love, nothing either of evil things is wholly abolished, or of good things achieved at all. ⟦q6.19|3⟧ For faith persuades the mind under attack to come over to God, becoming for it an encouragement toward confidence consisting in every equipment of spiritual arms. ⟦q6.19|4⟧ And hope becomes for it a most unlying guarantor of divine help, promising the overthrow of the contrary powers. ⟦q6.19|5⟧ And love makes it hard to move, or rather wholly unmoved, from divine affection even when under attack, nailing to the divine longing the whole power of its aspiration.

⟦q6.20|1⟧ 69. ⟦q6.20|2⟧ Faith encourages the mind under attack, strengthened by the hope of help; hope, bringing before its sight the help that was believed, beats back the onrush of the adversaries; and love makes dead, for the God-loving mind, the assault of the enemies, wholly dimmed by the aspiration toward God.

⟦q6.21|1⟧ 70. ⟦q6.21|2⟧ Of the truly divine portion, that is, of true knowledge, the son, first and only, is the word, according to faith, of the divine resurrection within us, with the due economy according to inclination, that is, discernment, nobly bearing the uprisings of both voluntary and involuntary temptations. ⟦q6.21|3⟧ For the first resurrection within us of God, who has been put to death in us through ignorance, is faith, well ordered by the works of the commandments.

⟦q6.22|1⟧ 71. ⟦q6.22|2⟧ Conversion to God clearly shows through itself the fullest principle of divine hope, without which no assent toward God is of a nature to come about for anyone, in any way, in any respect whatever; since it is proper to hope to bring things to come before sight as present, and to persuade that God the defender is by no [1248] means absent from those warred against by the adverse power, for whose sake and because of whom the saints have their war. ⟦q6.22|3⟧ For without some expectation, either hard or easy, conversion toward the good is never of a nature to come about for anyone.

⟦q6.23|1⟧ 72. ⟦q6.23|2⟧ Nothing truly so gathers those who are scattered as love does, and creates for them one inclination held fast by concord, whose character is the beauty of equality of honour. ⟦q6.23|3⟧ Therefore love is the offspring of the gathering into one, around divine things, and the union of the psychic powers — that is, the rational, the incensive and the desiring; by which those who have already received equality of honour with God through grace, inscribing in memory the beauty of the divine comeliness, hold unforgotten the aspiration of divine love, which records and imprints the undefiled beauty on the ruling part of the soul.

⟦q6.24|1⟧ 73. ⟦q6.24|2⟧ Every mind girded with divine strength possesses, as certain elders and rulers, the rational power, from which the faith of knowledge is of a nature to be born, by which it is ineffably taught that God is always present, and through hope it consorts with things to come as present; and the desiring power, by which divine love is constituted, through which, having of its own will nailed itself to the longing for the undefiled Godhead, it holds unloosed its aspiration for what is longed for; and further also the incensive power, by which it clings tenaciously to divine peace, drawing tight the motion of desire toward the divine yearning. ⟦q6.24|3⟧ Every mind has these powers working with it toward the overthrow of vice and the establishment and preservation of virtue.

⟦q6.25|1⟧ 74. ⟦q6.25|2⟧ Without the rational power there is no scientific knowledge; and without knowledge faith does not subsist, from which hope proceeds as its fair offspring, by which the faithful man consorts with things to come as present. ⟦q6.25|3⟧ And without the power according to desire, longing does not subsist, whose end is love; for to yearn for something is proper to desire. ⟦q6.25|4⟧ And without the incensive power, bracing desire toward union with what is sweet, peace is in no way of a nature to come about; since peace truly is the untroubled and complete possession of what is desired.

⟦q6.26|1⟧ 75. ⟦q6.26|2⟧ He who has not first been cleansed of the passions must not touch natural contemplation, because of the images of sensible things, which are able to stamp toward passion the mind of him who has not been perfectly released from passions. ⟦q6.26|3⟧ For the mind that through sense lingers in imagination on the surfaces of sensible things becomes a creator of unclean passions, not being able to cross over through contemplation to the kindred intelligibles.

⟦q6.27|1⟧ 76. ⟦q6.27|2⟧ He who in the time of the uprising of the passions

⟦q7.1|1⟧ [1249] having nobly shut the senses, and having utterly thrust away the imagination and memory of sensible things, and having wholly contracted the natural motions of the mind about the search into external things, put to shame, conquering through the divine hand, the wicked and tyrannical dominion that had risen against him.

⟦q7.2|1⟧ 77. ⟦q7.2|2⟧ When reason becomes mindless, and anger rash, and desire irrational, and ignorance and tyranny and licentiousness rule the soul, then the habit of vice is of a nature to become active in deed, entwined with the varied pleasure of the senses.

⟦q7.3|1⟧ 78. ⟦q7.3|2⟧ The mind that knows how, in the manner of knowledge, to escape the invisible grapplings must neither pursue natural contemplation, nor do anything else at the time of the assault of the wicked powers than only pray, and subdue the body with toils, and with all zeal make the destruction of the earthly way of thinking, and guard the walls of the city: I mean the virtues that guard the soul, or the methods that guard the virtues; I mean self-control and endurance; lest he who gives the soul a turbid overthrow to drink, [n: Habac. ii, 15.] deceiving through the things on the right hand, should steal the aspiration and turn it away from God, and through things reckoned good should draw down to worse things the understanding that seeks good things.

⟦q7.4|1⟧ 79. ⟦q7.4|2⟧ He who through reasoned and comprehending self-control and endurance has nobly shut the senses, and through the powers of the soul has walled off the entrances of sensible figures to the mind, easily destroys the wicked devices of the devil, turning him back with shame by the way that he came. ⟦q7.4|3⟧ And the way by which the devil comes is the material things that seem to be for the constitution of the body.

⟦q7.5|1⟧ 80. ⟦q7.5|2⟧ The mind that has joined sense to itself according to nature through reason as a middle term gathers the true knowledge that comes from natural contemplation.

⟦q7.6|1⟧ 81. ⟦q7.6|2⟧ There are springs outside the city, that is, outside the soul, which Hezekiah stops up: all sensible things; and the waters of these springs are the concepts of sensible things; and the river dividing through the middle of the city is the knowledge gathered by natural contemplation from the concepts [1252] of sensible things, passing through the middle of the soul, as being the boundary of mind and sense. ⟦q7.6|3⟧ For the knowledge of sensible things is neither altogether estranged from the noetic power, nor wholly allotted to the activity according to sense; but being, as it were, in the middle of the concourse of the mind with sense and of sense with the mind, it makes through itself their conjunction with one another: by sense, being imprinted according to form with the figures of sensible things; by the mind, transferring the impressions of the figures into principles. ⟦q7.6|4⟧ Therefore the knowledge of visible things was fittingly called a river dividing through the middle of the city, as being the border-ground of the extremes, I mean of mind and sense.

⟦q7.7|1⟧ 82. ⟦q7.7|2⟧ He who at the time of the uprising of temptations abstains from natural contemplation, but holds fast to prayer according to the contraction of the mind from all things toward itself and God, kills the habit that produces vice, and turns back with shame the devil who suggests the said habit, trusting in which he came with his own boastfulness to the soul, exalting himself against the truth through proud thoughts. ⟦q7.7|2⟧ This perhaps the great David both knew and suffered and did, he who of all men had most experience of the array of intelligible wars: *When the sinner stood against me*, he says, *I was deafened and humbled, and kept silent from good things*; [n: Psal. xxxviii, 2.] and after him the divine Jeremiah, commanding the people not to go out of the city because of the sword of the enemies that sojourns round about. [n: Jerem. vi, 25.]

⟦q7.8|1⟧ 83. ⟦q7.8|2⟧ And so the blessed Abel too, if he had kept guard and not gone out with Cain into the plain, that is, into the breadth of natural contemplation, before passionlessness, the law of the flesh, which both is and is called Cain, would not have risen up and killed him with guile, [n: Gen. iv, 8.] stealing upon him by the things on the right hand in the contemplation of beings before the perfect habit.

⟦q7.9|1⟧ 84. ⟦q7.9|2⟧ Likewise Dinah too, the daughter of the great Jacob, had she not gone out with the daughters of the natives, that is, with the sensory imaginations, Shechem the son of Hamor would not have risen up and humbled her. [n: Gen. xxxiv, 1 seqq.]

⟦q7.10|1⟧ 85. ⟦q7.10|2⟧ It is good for us, before the perfect habit, not to touch natural contemplation, lest, seeking spiritual principles from the visible creatures, we unawares gather passions. ⟦q7.10|3⟧ For in the imperfect the apparent figures of visible things prevail more upon sense than the principles of the things that have come to be, hidden in the figures, prevail upon the soul. ⟦q7.10|4⟧ Those, however, who have bound their understanding to the letter alone in Jewish fashion expect in this age [1253] the promises of the undefiled goods, ignorant of the goods of the soul according to nature.

⟦q7.11|1⟧ 86. ⟦q7.11|2⟧ He who has borne the image of the heavenly hastens to follow through all things the spirit of the holy Scripture, in which, through virtue and knowledge, lies the preservation of the soul; but he who bears the image of the earthy cherishes the letter only, in which consists the worship according to sense directed toward the body, making the passions.

⟦q7.12|1⟧ 87. ⟦q7.12|2⟧ The might of God is the virtue that destroys the passions and guards the pious thoughts, which the practice of the commandments begets, through which, by the co-operation of God, or rather by the power of God alone, we bring to ruin the wicked powers opposed to the good. ⟦q7.12|3+4⟧ And the height of God is the knowledge of the truth, which the toil of the contemplation of the things that have come to be begets, and the sweats over the practice of the virtues, which become fathers of toil; through which we utterly make to vanish the power of falsehood opposed to the truth, humbling and casting down every height of the wicked spirits that exalt themselves against the knowledge of God. [n: ibid.] For just as practice begets virtue, so contemplation begets knowledge.

⟦q7.13|1⟧ 88. ⟦q7.13|2⟧ Unforgetting knowledge, having about the divine infinity an unbounded motion according to mind beyond intellection, images through its unboundedness the more-than-infinite glory of the truth; and the self-chosen imitation of the wise goodness that is according to providence brings as its honour the assimilation of the mind to God, most manifest according to disposition, as far as is possible.

⟦q7.14|1⟧ 89. ⟦q7.14|2⟧ The tongue is a symbol of the soul's activity of knowledge; and the throat a token of the natural self-love toward the body. ⟦q7.14|3⟧ He, then, who has blameworthily glued these to one another cannot remember the peaceful habit according to virtue and knowledge, taking pleasure through zeal in the confusion of the bodily passions.

⟦q7.15|1⟧ 90. ⟦q7.15|2⟧ The appetites and pleasures according to nature, bringing no blame upon those who possess them, stand as a necessary consequence of natural appetite. ⟦q7.15|3⟧ For pleasure according to nature is produced, even when we do not wish it, by any chance food consoling a want that went before; and by drink, repelling the annoyance of thirst; and by sleep, renewing the power spent by waking; and [1256] by whatever others of our natural things there are, necessary for the constitution of nature, and useful to the earnest for the acquisition of virtue. ⟦q7.15|4⟧ These go out together with every mind that flees the confusion of sin, lest through them it remain held fast in slavery to the passions that are in our power, reprehensible and contrary to nature, which have no other origin in us than the motion of the passions according to nature, which are not even of a nature to pass over with us to the immortal and long-lived life.

⟦q7.16|1⟧ 91. ⟦q7.16|2⟧ The words of God, spoken barely, in utterance alone, are not heard, not having as their voice the practice of those who speak them. ⟦q7.16|3⟧ But if they are voiced by the practice of the commandments, they have both the demons melting away at such a voice, and men, through the progress of the works of justice, readily building the divine temple of the heart.

⟦q7.17|1⟧ 92. ⟦q7.17|2⟧ As God according to essence does not fall under knowledge, so neither is his word comprehended by the knowledge that is ours. ⟦q7.17|3⟧ For the word of the holy Scripture, even if according to the letter it admits of circumscription, ceasing together with the times of the things narrated, yet according to the spirit, in the contemplations of the things understood, it remains forever uncircumscribed.

⟦q7.18|1⟧ 93. ⟦q7.18|2⟧ He who receives the holy Scripture in the manner of knowledge, for the soul, according to Christ, must be exercised laboriously also in the interpretation of the names, being able from it to make clear the whole understanding of what is written, if he cares for the exact understanding of what is written; and not, in Jewish fashion, drag down the height of the spirit to body and earth, and circumscribe the divine and undefiled promises of the intelligible goods by the corruption of the things that pass away.

⟦q7.19|1⟧ 94. ⟦q7.19|2⟧ As a vow is a promise of goods brought by men to God by way of promise, so prayer, by the fitting account, will clearly be the request for the goods supplied by God to men for salvation, bearing a return for the good disposition of the things that went before. ⟦q7.19|3⟧ And a cry is the increase and augmentation, at the time of the uprising of the wicked demons, of the virtuous modes according to practice and of the objects of contemplation of knowledge according to contemplation; which God hears naturally, not least of all things, [hearing] instead of a great voice the disposition of those who care for virtue and knowledge.

⟦q7.20|1⟧ 95. ⟦q7.20|2⟧ The wicked and destructive kingdom [1257] of the devil, typified by the kingdom of the Assyrians, gathering war against virtue and against knowledge, contrives to rout the soul through its innate powers: first rousing desire to an appetite for the things contrary to nature, and persuading it to prefer the sensible things to the intelligible; and stirring up anger to fight on behalf of the sensible thing chosen by desire; and teaching the rational part to devise the modes of the pleasures according to sense.

⟦q7.21|1⟧ 96. ⟦q7.21|2⟧ It is proper to the utmost goodness not only to establish the divine and incorporeal essences of the intelligibles as images of the ineffable and divine glory, receiving in proportion to themselves, as is lawful, the whole inconceivable comeliness of the unapproachable beauty; but also to mingle into sensible things too, which fall far short even of the intelligible essences, echoes of its own majesty, able to ferry the human mind that rides upon them unwaveringly to God, as it comes to be above all visible things, as one that has mounted to the summit of blessedness.

⟦q7.22|1⟧ 97. ⟦q7.22|2⟧ Every mind crowned with virtue and knowledge, as having been allotted, like the great Hezekiah, to reign over Jerusalem, that is, over the habit that sees peace alone, or the state deprived of passions of every kind (for Jerusalem is interpreted "vision of peace"), has the whole creation under its hand: [creation] through the species that complete it bringing to God through him, as gifts, the spiritual principles of knowledge that are in it, and providing to him, as presents, the modes toward virtue present in it according to the natural law, and through both welcoming him who is able to be held in highest repute in both; I mean the philosophic mind perfected in reason and life through practice and contemplation.

⟦q7.23|1⟧ 98. ⟦q7.23|2⟧ He who through practice and contemplation has accomplished virtue and knowledge to the utmost is fittingly lifted above all, having come above the fleshly and reprehensible passions according to practice, and above the so-called natural bodies, I mean those that are under coming-to-be and corruption, and, simply, to speak summarily, above all the species under sense according to contemplation, having passed through in the manner of knowledge all the principles in them; such a one has led up the mind to the kindred and divine things.

⟦q7.24|1⟧ 99. ⟦q7.24|2⟧ He who through the toils of practice has been allotted to dwell in passionlessness as in Jerusalem, and is freed from all the annoyance that comes by sin, and both doing and speaking, and hearing and reasoning, peace alone; after receiving through natural contemplation the nature of visible things, bringing [1260] as gifts to the Lord through him the diviner principles in it, and bearing to him, as to a king, as presents, the laws in it, is lifted up before the eyes of all the nations, coming to be above all, namely both the passions according to the flesh through practice, and the natural bodies and all the species under sense through contemplation, having passed over the spiritual principles and modes in them.

⟦q7.25|1⟧ 100. ⟦q7.25|2⟧ Practical philosophy makes the practical man above the passions; and contemplation sets the man of knowledge above the visible things, having raised up the mind to the kindred intelligibles.

⟦q7.26|1⟧ THIRD CENTURY

⟦q7.27|1⟧ 1. ⟦q7.27|2⟧ He who knows with practice, and practises with knowledge, is the throne and footstool of God: a throne, because of knowledge; a footstool, because of practice. ⟦q7.27|3⟧ And if one should say that the human mind too is heaven, cleansed of all material imagination and occupied with, or rather adorned by, the divine principles of the intelligibles, he has not, it seems to me, stepped outside the truth.

⟦q7.28|1⟧ 2. ⟦q7.28|2⟧ Every philosopher, who is clearly also pious, guarded by virtue and knowledge, or by practice and contemplation, when he sees the wicked power rising up against him through the passions, as the king of the Assyrians against Hezekiah, has one help toward the release from evils, God, whom he propitiates crying without utterance through the greater intensity in virtue and knowledge; and he receives for alliance, or rather for salvation, an angel, that is, a greater word of wisdom and knowledge, *crushing every mighty man and warrior and ruler and general in the camp*.

⟦q7.29|1⟧ 3. ⟦q7.29|2⟧ The congenial sensible thing is of a nature to be the beginning of every passion. ⟦q7.29|3⟧ For without some subject that moves the powers of the soul toward itself through some intermediate sense, a passion would never subsist. ⟦q7.29|4⟧ For without a sensible object a passion does not subsist. ⟦q7.29|5⟧ For if there is no woman, there is no fornication; and

⟦q8.1|1⟧ [1261] if there are no foods, there will be no gluttony; and if there is no gold, there will be no avarice. ⟦q8.1|2⟧ Therefore of every passionate motion of the natural powers in us the sensible thing is the beginning, or the demon who through it provokes the soul to sin.

⟦q8.2|1⟧ 4. ⟦q8.2|2⟧ Rubbing makes the activity vanish; but crushing out makes vanish even the very pondering of vice. ⟦q8.2|3⟧ For rubbing is the abolition of the passionate action in activity alone; but crushing out is the complete vanishing even of the wicked movements in the understanding.

⟦q8.3|1⟧ 5. ⟦q8.3|2⟧ Midway between God and men are the sensible and the intelligible things; above which the human mind comes as it advances toward God: not enslaved to the sensible things according to practice, and in no way held fast by the intelligible things according to contemplation.

⟦q8.4|1⟧ 6. ⟦q8.4|2⟧ Creation becomes the accuser of impious men: through the principles in it proclaiming its own Maker; and through the natural laws in it according to each species, educating man toward virtue. ⟦q8.4|3⟧ The principles, then, are known in the holding-together of the permanence of each species; and the laws appear in the identity of the natural activity of each species; and not applying ourselves to these by the noetic power in us, we have both been ignorant of the cause of beings and have clung fast to all the passions contrary to nature.

⟦q8.5|1+2⟧ 7. ⟦q8.5|1+2⟧ The word bids that gifts be offered by us to God, so that it may set forth the infinity of the divine goodness, which, as though it had brought in nothing beforehand, receives from us as gifts the presents, reckoning the whole contribution as ours, showing God's goodness toward us to be great and unspeakable, who receives his own things, offered to him from us, as ours, and confesses the debt for them as for things belonging to another.

⟦q8.6|1⟧ 8. ⟦q8.6|2⟧ Man, considering the spiritual principles of visible things, is taught that there is a Maker of the things that appear; leaving the notion of what he is like unexamined, as unattainable. ⟦q8.6|3⟧ For creation, being seen, clearly furnishes the apprehension that he is Maker, but not of what sort the Maker is.

⟦q8.7|1⟧ 9. ⟦q8.7|2⟧ The wrath of God is the painful sense of those who are being educated. ⟦q8.7|3⟧ And painful sense is the bringing on of involuntary toils, through which God often leads the mind puffed up over virtue and knowledge to contraction and humility, granting it to become aware of itself and conscious of its own [1264] weakness; perceiving which, it lays aside the vain swelling of the heart.

⟦q8.8|1⟧ 10. ⟦q8.8|2⟧ The wrath of the Lord is the suspension of the supply of the divine gifts of grace; which comes profitably upon every mind that is lofty and high-flown, and that boasts of the good things given to it from God as of its own achievements.

⟦q8.9|1⟧ 11. ⟦q8.9|2⟧ Every mind of knowledge and philosophy has both Judah and Jerusalem: Judah as practical philosophy, Jerusalem as contemplative mystagogy. ⟦q8.9|3⟧ Whenever, then, through divine grace, the God-loving mind, by both the practical and the contemplative philosophy, having beaten off every power opposed to virtue and knowledge, perfectly binds on itself the might over the spirits of wickedness, and does not render the due thanksgiving to God, the cause of the victory, but is lifted up in heart, reckoning itself the cause of the whole achievement, then, as not having rendered to God according to the recompense which he rendered to him, it receives the wrath of abandonment coming upon it, not only itself, but also Judah and Jerusalem, that is, the habit of practice and of contemplation: passions of dishonour at once, by God's permission, rising up against the practice and defiling the hitherto pure conscience; and false notions being entwined with the contemplation of beings and perverting the hitherto right opinion of knowledge. ⟦q8.9|4⟧ For dishonour of passions succeeds him who is exalted over practice; and him who is lifted up over knowledge just judgement permits to stumble regarding the true contemplation.

⟦q8.10|1⟧ 12. ⟦q8.10|2⟧ A divine limit and law truly exists in beings according to Providence, permitting those who have appeared ungrateful over better things to be educated through contraries toward gratitude, and to make the experience of contraries a full knowledge of the divine power that accomplishes good things; so that, being permitted by Providence to keep altogether undemolished our conceit over better things, we may not slip down into the God-opposing disposition of pride, thinking the possession of virtue and knowledge to be ours by nature and not acquired by grace; and be found using the good for the coming-to-be of evil, and, through the very things by which the full knowledge of the divine ought to remain unshaken in us, bound the more tightly, through those, as we ought not, falling sick with ignorance of it.

⟦q8.11|1⟧ [1265] 13. ⟦q8.11|2⟧ We know as a divine limit and law existing in beings the providence that holds beings together, which by just judgement educates toward gratitude, by scarcity of goods, those who over the abundance of goods have appeared ungrateful toward their supplier, leading them through contraries to an awareness of the discernment of him who graciously gives the good things. ⟦q8.11|3⟧ For conceit over virtue, remaining uneducated, is of a nature to beget the disease of pride, which bears the disposition opposed to God.

⟦q8.12|1⟧ 14. ⟦q8.12|2⟧ He who thinks he has grasped the end of virtue will in no way seek any longer the fountain-like cause of good things, having circumscribed the power of aspiration to himself alone, and suffering by his own doing the loss of the very limit of salvation, I mean God. ⟦q8.12|3⟧ But he who is aware of his own natural poverty regarding good things does not cease running headlong to him who is able to give the filling of the want.

⟦q8.13|1⟧ 15. ⟦q8.13|2⟧ He who has discerned the infinity of virtue never ceases from the course according to it, lest he suffer the loss of the very beginning and end of virtue, I mean God, by fixing the motion of aspiration about himself; and lest he unawares, thinking he has laid hold of perfection, undergo the falling-away from what truly is, toward which every motion of the earnest man presses.

⟦q8.14|1⟧ 16. ⟦q8.14|2⟧ Wrath justly comes upon the high-minded mind: that is, abandonment, as has been said; or the permission for it to be troubled by demons both in practice and in contemplation; so that it may take awareness of its own natural weakness, and the full knowledge of the divine power and grace that shelters it and accomplishes the whole of the good things; and be humbled, putting far from itself altogether the alien height that is contrary to nature; so that the other wrath, the taking away of the gifts given, may not come upon it, once it has been humbled and has come to awareness of him who provides the good things.

⟦q8.15|1⟧ 17. ⟦q8.15|2⟧ He who has not been chastened by the first species of wrath, that is, of abandonment, so as to come to humility, reckoning it the teacher of fair judgement, clearly receives the other wrath coming upon him, which takes away from him the activity of the gifts of grace and renders him bereft of the power that hitherto guarded him. ⟦q8.15|3⟧ *For I will take away*, he says (God speaking of ungrateful Israel), *its hedge, and it shall be for plunder; and I will pull down its wall, and it shall be for trampling; and I will leave my vineyard, and it shall not be pruned nor dug; and thorn shall come up into it as into waste ground, and I will command the clouds not to rain upon it.* [n: Isa. v, 5, 6.]

⟦q8.16|1⟧ [1268] 18. ⟦q8.16|2⟧ Further, steep is the road to impiety: the insensibility regarding the loss of the virtues. ⟦q8.16|3⟧ For he who has accustomed himself, for the sake of the pleasures of the flesh, to disobey God will deny God himself too when occasion calls, preferring to God the life of the flesh, the pleasures of which alone he has held to be stronger than the divine wills.

⟦q8.17|1⟧ 19. ⟦q8.17|2⟧ Whenever we suppose that the mind has suffered something in any way whatever, we shall certainly believe that, with such a mind, its practical and contemplative power has suffered with it according to their natural principles. ⟦q8.17|3⟧ For it is not possible for the subject to suffer while the things in the subject do not suffer; and I call the mind the subject, as receptive of virtue and knowledge; and the things in the subject, practice and contemplation, which hold toward the mind the rank of accidents. ⟦q8.17|4⟧ Therefore in every way they also suffer with it when it suffers, having its qualified motion as the beginning of their own alteration.

⟦q8.18|1⟧ 20. ⟦q8.18|2⟧ Every God-loving and virtuous man, like Hezekiah, having girded on in the manner of knowledge the might against the demons, if there comes upon him an assault of wicked spirits, invisibly entwining the war against him in the mind, and through prayer he receives an angel sent to him from God, I mean a greater word of wisdom, and, having crushed out the whole phalanx of the devil, scatters it; and does not ascribe to God as the cause of such victory and salvation, but credits the whole victory to himself, such a one *has not rendered to God according to his recompense*, not having made the multitude of his thanksgiving equal to the greatness of the salvation, nor having measured his own disposition in return against the benefaction of him who saved.

⟦q8.19|1⟧ 21. ⟦q8.19|2⟧ Let us illumine the mind with divine concepts; and let us brighten the body with the modes of the diviner principles that have been understood, making it a rational workshop of virtue by the casting off of the passions. ⟦q8.19|2⟧ For the innate passions of the body, governed by reason, have no blame; but moved without it, they bring blame. ⟦q8.19|3⟧ Of these, then, he says, there must be a casting off: of those whose motion is innate, but whose use, not governed by reason, often becomes contrary to nature.

⟦q8.20|1⟧ 22. ⟦q8.20|2⟧ Everyone who has lifted up his heart over the gifts of grace he received, thinking great things as though he had not received them, justly receives the wrath coming upon him, God permitting the devil to grapple with him intelligibly, and to shake the modes of virtue according to practice, and to muddy the clear principles of knowledge according to contemplation; so that, having learned his own weakness, he may recognize the only power, that which wrestles down the passions in us, and be humbled, repenting, casting off the bulk of his conceit, and propitiate God, and turn away the wrath that comes upon those who do not repent, which takes away [1269] the grace that guards the soul and leaves the ungrateful mind bereft.

⟦q8.21|1⟧ 23. ⟦q8.21|2⟧ Saving wrath is the divine permission for the high-minded mind to be warred against with the passions by the demons; so that, suffering dishonourably while it boasts over virtues, it may know who is the giver of these; or may become bare of the things not its own, which it thought it had as though it had not received them.

⟦q8.22|1⟧ 24. ⟦q8.22|2⟧ Blessed truly is that mind which has praiseworthily died to all beings: to the sensible, by the putting away of the activity according to sense; to the intelligible, by the ceasing of noetic motion. ⟦q8.22|3⟧ Note that he calls the praised death of the mind the passing-away, by inclination, from all beings, after which it is of a nature to receive the life that is by divine grace, having received inconceivably, instead of beings, the cause of beings.

⟦q8.23|1⟧ 25. ⟦q8.23|2⟧ Blessed is he who has joined the practical part to the good according to nature, and the contemplative part to the truth according to nature. ⟦q8.23|3⟧ For every practice is of a nature to come about for the sake of the good; and every contemplation seeks knowledge for the sake of the truth alone; and when these have been accomplished, there will be nothing at all that strikes the practical part of the soul, nor indeed its contemplative part, stinging it through foreign objects of contemplation; since the soul has come to be beyond everything that is and that is understood, and has entered into God himself, the only good and true, who is beyond all essence and intellection.

⟦q8.24|1⟧ 26. ⟦q8.24|2⟧ They say that the end of practical virtue is the good. ⟦q8.24|3⟧ And this is the completion of the divine activity, to which the rational part of the soul leads, using anger and desire according to nature; in which the beauty according to the likeness is of a nature to shine forth. ⟦q8.24|3⟧ And they say that the end of contemplative philosophy is the truth; which is the knowledge, without parts and uniform, of all the things about God, toward which the pure mind is borne, having altogether quenched its own judgement according to sense; in which (in knowledge, that is) the dignity of the divine image is shown unadulterated.

⟦q8.25|1⟧ 27. ⟦q8.25|2⟧ No one can truly bless God [1272] unless he has sanctified the body with the virtues and illumined the soul with knowledges. ⟦q8.25|3⟧ For the disposition according to virtue is the face of the contemplative mind, lifted up as to heaven toward the height of true knowledge.

⟦q8.26|1⟧ 28. ⟦q8.26|2⟧ Blessed is he who knows in truth that God accomplishes in us as in instruments all practice and contemplation, virtue and knowledge, victory and wisdom, goodness and truth, we contributing nothing at all except the disposition that wills the good things; having which, the great Zerubbabel, in addition to the things already said, says, addressing God: *Blessed art thou, who gavest me wisdom, and to thee I confess, Master of the fathers; from thee is the victory, and from thee the wisdom, and thine is the glory, and I am thy servant.* ⟦q8.26|2⟧ As a truly grateful servant, he referred all things to God, who had given all things, from whom he had received and held the wisdom, confessing to him as Master of the fathers the power of the goods graciously given, which are the union, as has been said, of victory and wisdom, of virtue and knowledge, of practice and contemplation, of goodness and truth; for these, united with one another, flash forth one glory and radiance of God.

⟦q8.27|1⟧ 29. ⟦q8.27|2⟧ All the achievements of the saints were manifestly gifts of grace from God; no one having anything at all except the good given, measured, as from God the Master, in proportion to the gratitude and goodwill of the receiver; and each possessing those things alone which he presents to the Master who gives.

⟦q8.28|1⟧ 30. ⟦q8.28|2⟧ The mind that presides over virtue and knowledge, and has willed that the soul be deprived of the evil slavery of the passions, says: *Women prevail, and truth conquers above all*; calling women the deifying virtues, from which the unifying love of men toward God and toward one another consists; snatching the soul out of all things under coming-to-be and corruption, and out of the intelligible essences above them, and entwining it with God himself by a certain commingling of yearning, as far as is possible to human nature, and mystically making the undefiled and divine life-together. ⟦q8.28|3⟧ And saying truth, he means the only and one cause of beings, and beginning and kingdom and power and glory, from which and for the sake of which all things have come to be and come to be, and are held together toward being by it and through it; and for the sake of which is all the zeal and motion of the God-loving.

⟦q8.29|1⟧ 31. ⟦q8.29|2⟧ Through the women he indicated the end of the virtues, love; which is the

⟦q9.1|1⟧ [1273] … according to aspiration of the naturally good, for those who participate, an unfailing pleasure and an undivided union. ⟦q9.1|2⟧ And by «truth» he signified the end of all knowledges, and of the things themselves that are known; into which, as into the beginning and end of all beings, the natural motions are drawn together by a certain generic principle; since truth, as truth, the beginning and cause of beings, conquers all things according to nature, and draws to itself the motion of the things that have come to be.

⟦q9.2|1⟧ 32. ⟦q9.2|2⟧ By the privation of the many that is by way of eminence, truth is of a nature to appear as one and only, covering the knowing powers of all things that are able to understand or to be understood, as being, by a superessential existence, above both the things that understand and the things that are understood; and, by an infinite power circumscribing the extremities of beings at their beginning and their end, it draws together to itself every motion of all things: to some providing a manifest knowledge of the grace of which they were deprived; to others granting, by an ineffable sense, in participation, a manifest full knowledge of the goodness of which they had the aspiration.

⟦q9.3|1⟧ 33. ⟦q9.3|2⟧ The mind, he says, is the instrument of wisdom; reason, of knowledge; the natural full assurance according to both, of the faith constituted according to both; and of the gift of healings, natural love for man. ⟦q9.3|3⟧ For every divine gift has in us an apt and connatural receptive instrument, such as a power, or a habit, or a disposition. ⟦q9.3|4⟧ For example, he who has made his mind pure of every imagination of sensible things receives wisdom; he who has established reason as master of the innate passions, I mean anger and desire, receives knowledge; he who has the unshaken full assurance about divine things according to mind and reason receives the faith that can do all things; and he who has achieved natural love for man after the perfect destruction of self-love receives gifts of healings.

⟦q9.4|1⟧ 34. ⟦q9.4|2+3⟧ Each of us, according to the proportion of the faith in him, possesses the activity of the Spirit made manifest. [n: Rom. xii, 6.] So each is the steward of his own grace; and no one who thinks rightly would ever envy another who wins repute in the gifts of grace, since the disposition receptive of the divine goods lies with himself.

⟦q9.5|1⟧ 35. ⟦q9.5|2⟧ He says that the cause of the distribution of the divine goods is the measure of each man's faith. ⟦q9.5|3⟧ For as we believe, so too we have the increase of eagerness for doing. ⟦q9.5|4⟧ He then who does shows, in proportion to his action, the measure of his faith, receiving, as he believed, the measure of grace; and he who does not do shows, in proportion to his inaction, the measure of his unbelief, receiving, as he disbelieved, the privation of grace. ⟦q9.5|5⟧ So the envious man does ill to begrudge those who do rightly, since there lies plainly with himself, not with another, [1276] the selection both of believing and of doing, and of receiving the grace that comes according to the measure of faith.

⟦q9.6|1⟧ 36. ⟦q9.6|2⟧ The lover of noble things is urged on willingly, according to providence, by the principles of wisdom toward the grace of deification; but he who has no yearning for them is drawn away from vice unwillingly, according to the just judgement, by the modes of correction: the one, as a lover of God, being deified through providence; the other, through judgement, as a lover of matter, not being permitted to come into condemnation. ⟦q9.6|3⟧ For God, as good, tends with the principles of wisdom those who are willing, and with the modes of correction heals those who are slow to move toward virtue.

⟦q9.7|1⟧ 37. ⟦q9.7|2⟧ Faith that truly is, is truth both holding together and establishing, having no falsehood; and good conscience carries the power of love, as having no transgression of a commandment.

⟦q9.8|1⟧ 38. ⟦q9.8|2+3⟧ *And there shall rest upon him*, he says, seven spirits: *the spirit of wisdom, the spirit of intelligence, the spirit of knowledge, the spirit of science, the spirit of counsel, the spirit of strength, the spirit of the fear of God.* [n: Isa. xi, 2, 3.] And the property of these spiritual gifts is: of fear, abstention from evils; of strength, the practice of good things; of counsel, the distinction of opposites; of science, the unadulterated knowing of what is fitting; of knowledge, the comprehension in act of the divine principles in the virtues; of intelligence, the co-disposition of the whole soul toward what has been known; of wisdom, the indiscernible union with God, by which for the worthy the aspiration becomes enjoyment, making the participant god by participation, and establishing him an interpreter of the divine blessedness, according to the ever-flowing, unostentatious putting-forth and issue of the divine mysteries toward those who need them.

⟦q9.9|1⟧ 39. ⟦q9.9|2⟧ The spirit of the fear of God is abstention from evils in act; the spirit of strength, the eager impulse and motion toward the activity and practice of the commandments; the spirit of counsel, the habit of distinction, by which we perform the divine commandments with reason, and divide the worse from the better; the spirit of science, the unfalling knowing of the modes of practice according to virtue, by which, in acting, we in no way fall from the right judgement of reason; the spirit of knowledge, the comprehension of the commandments and of the principles in them, according to which the modes of the virtues are constituted; and the spirit of intelligence is the assent, or to speak more properly, the transformation, toward the modes and the principles of the virtues, by which there comes about a blending of the natural [1277] powers with the modes and the principles of the commandments; and the spirit of wisdom is the taking-up and union toward the cause of the more spiritual principles in the commandments, by which, being initiated unknowingly and simply, as far as is lawful for men, into the principles of beings that are in God, we offer to other men in varied ways, as from a welling spring of the heart, the truth that is in all things.

⟦q9.10|1⟧ 40. ⟦q9.10|2⟧ From the last things from God, but near to us, we ascend by a way and an order to the first things, far from us but near to God. ⟦q9.10|3⟧ For from idleness in evils through fear we come to the practice of the virtues through strength; and from the practice of the virtues to the distinction of counsel; and from distinction to the habit of the virtues, that is, science; and from the habit of the virtues to the knowledge of the principles in the virtues themselves; and from this to the habit that transforms toward the known principles of the virtues, I mean intelligence; and from this to the simple and exact contemplation of the truth in all things; setting out from which, we shall render, from the wise contemplation of the essences of beings, both sensible and intelligible, many and varied pious accounts concerning the truth.

⟦q9.11|1⟧ 41. ⟦q9.11|2⟧ The good that is first toward us in act, which is fear, the word of Scripture enumerated last, as the beginning of wisdom; moving from which, we ascend to the end of wisdom, intelligence, after which we become near to God himself, having wisdom alone mediating the union with him. ⟦q9.11|3⟧ For it is not possible to lay hold of wisdom for one who has not first, through fear and the remaining gifts in between, wholly shaken off from himself both the rheum of ignorance and the dust of vice. ⟦q9.11|4⟧ For this reason the scriptural order has set wisdom near to God, and fear near to us, that we may learn the limit and law of good order.

⟦q9.12|1⟧ 42. ⟦q9.12|2⟧ Ascending, then, through these eyes of faith, that is, illuminations, we are gathered together toward the divine monad of wisdom; gathering the division of the gifts that came about for our sake toward the cause of the gifts, by the particular ascents of the virtues, omitting, by the co-working of God, nothing of what has been said, lest, neglecting little by little, we render our faith blind and eyeless, not having the illuminations of the Spirit through works, and be justly punished for [1280] endless ages, as having blinded in ourselves, as far as lies in us, the divine eyes according to faith.

⟦q9.13|1⟧ 43. ⟦q9.13|2⟧ Everyone who has dug out in himself such eyes of faith through idleness in the commandments is wholly condemned, no longer having God looking upon him. ⟦q9.13|3⟧ For if, he says, the word calls the activities of the Spirit eyes of the Lord, he who does not open these by the practice of the commandments does not have God looking upon him. ⟦q9.13|4⟧ For God is not of a nature, as is likely, to oversee those on earth through other eyes, if indeed our illumination according to virtue is a ray of the divine vision.

⟦q9.14|1⟧ 44. ⟦q9.14|2⟧ Wisdom is a monad, contemplated without cutting in the different virtues that are from it; and conceived uniformly in their activities; and shown again to be a simple monad by the restorations toward it of the virtues that are from it; when we, for whose sake it makes its procession according to the coming-to-be of each virtue, are gathered upwardly through each virtue toward it.

⟦q9.15|1⟧ 45. ⟦q9.15|2⟧ He has a blind faith who does not work the divine precepts according to faith. ⟦q9.15|3⟧ For if the precepts of God are light, [n: Prov. vi, 23.] it is plain that he who does not do the divine precepts is without divine light, and carries about the divine name bare, but not true.

⟦q9.16|1⟧ 46. ⟦q9.16|2⟧ No one who sins can have the weakness of the flesh as advocate of his sin. ⟦q9.16|3⟧ For the union with God the Word strengthened the whole nature by the loosing of the curse, making the passionate attachment of the inclination to the passions without excuse for us. ⟦q9.16|4⟧ For the Godhead of the Word, always present by grace with those who believe in him, withers the law of sin in the flesh.

⟦q9.17|1⟧ 47. ⟦q9.17|2⟧ He who, through faith and love toward God, has conquered the irrational desires, or motions, of the passions contrary to nature, comes to be outside even the law according to nature itself, and is wholly transferred to the land of the intelligibles, and casts out together with himself, from the bondage alien to him, what is of one tribe with him according to nature, with the things that came to it.

⟦q9.18|1⟧ 48. ⟦q9.18|2⟧ Knowledge not bridled by divine fear in practice works arrogance, persuading the one puffed up by it to put forward as his own what is borrowed, making the contribution of the word for his own praise. ⟦q9.18|3⟧ But practice, increasing together with divine longing, not taking the knowledge that is beyond what is to be done, makes the practical man humble-minded, drawn in upon himself by the words that are beyond his own power.

⟦q9.19|1⟧ [1281] 49. ⟦q9.19|2⟧ A heavenly dwelling is the passionless habit according to virtue, and knowledge that has no concept of error warring against it.

⟦q9.20|1⟧ 50. ⟦q9.20|2⟧ As the end of the monad set in motion is the myriad, and the beginning of the myriad not set in motion is the monad — for the beginning of every end is plainly the motionlessness according to it, and the end of every beginning is the completion of the motion according to it — so also faith, being the beginning of virtues according to nature, has as its end the completion of the good through them; and the good according to nature, as the end of virtues, having faith as its beginning, is gathered inwardly toward it. ⟦q9.20|3⟧ For faith is an inward good, and the good is faith activated. ⟦q9.20|4⟧ And God is by nature faithful and good: the former as first good, the latter as last object of appetite. ⟦q9.20|5⟧ And these stand the same as each other in every mode, divided from one another by no principle at all save that according to conception, on account of the motion of the things that begin from him and end in him. ⟦q9.20|6⟧ So the myriad, bearing the type of the last object of appetite, circumscribes the perfect aspiration of the things moved toward it; and the monad, bearing the symbol of the first good, brings on the perfect base of the things moved from it.

⟦q9.21|1⟧ 51. ⟦q9.21|2⟧ The first passionlessness is the complete abstention from evils in act, contemplated in beginners; the second, the complete rejection, in the understanding, of thoughts concerning assent to evils, coming about in those who pursue virtue with reason; the third, the complete motionlessness of desire concerning the passions, in those who behold intelligibly, through the figures, the principles of the things seen; and the fourth passionlessness is the complete purification even of bare imagination itself, constituted in those who through knowledge and contemplation have made their ruling faculty a pure and transparent mirror of God. ⟦q9.21|3⟧ He then who has purified himself of the activity of the passions, and been freed from assent to them in the understanding, and taken rest from the motion of desire concerning them, and established his mind undefiled by their bare imagination, having the four generic passionlessnesses, goes out from matter and material things, and hastens toward the noetic and divine and peaceful lot of the intelligibles.

⟦q9.22|1⟧ 52. ⟦q9.22|2⟧ He calls first passionlessness the motion of the body in act that does not touch upon sin; the second, the perfect rejection in the soul of passionate thoughts, through which the motion of the passions according to the first passionlessness withers, not having the passionate thoughts kindling it toward act; the third, the perfect motionlessness of desire concerning the passions, through which the second too is of a nature to come about, being constituted in the purity of thoughts; and the fourth passionlessness he calls the perfect putting-off, in the understanding, of all imaginations of sensible things, according to which the third received its coming-to-be, not having the

⟦q10.2|1⟧ 53. ⟦q10.2|2⟧ For every practical man, reason and understanding labour after the manner of a male servant and a maidservant, devising and making the modes of practice according to virtue, and having, as it were, all their own power arrayed against the spirits of wickedness opposed to practice; but having fulfilled practical philosophy, which the sixth number of the years indicated — for it has been said that the number six signifies practical philosophy — they are released free toward the spiritual, plainly, returning to the contemplation of the kindred principles in beings, both reason and understanding. [n: Exod. xxi, 2 seqq.]

⟦q10.3|1⟧ 54. ⟦q10.3|2⟧ The foreign male servant and maidservant are anger and desire, whom the contemplative mind, through courage and temperance, yokes continually under the mastery of reason for the service of the virtues, not giving them at all the release to freedom until the law of nature is wholly swallowed up by the law of the spirit, as the death of wretched flesh by infinite life, and the whole image of the kingdom without beginning is shown purely, having through imitation the whole form of the archetype; having come to be according to which, the contemplative mind makes both anger and desire free: converting the one to the pure pleasure of divine yearning and the undefiled allure, and transferring the other to spiritual fervour and fiery ever-moving and sober madness.

⟦q10.4|1⟧ 55. ⟦q10.4|2⟧ The image of the kingdom without beginning is the mind's unchangeableness concerning true knowledge, and the sense's incorruption concerning virtue; the soul and the body being bound to one another by the divine law of the spirit alone, according to the transformation in the spirit of sense toward the mind, by which they have the activity of reason ever-moved and living and penetrating throughout; in which every unlikeness at all toward the Divine is absent.

⟦q10.5|1⟧ 56. ⟦q10.5|2⟧ They say that pleasure is desire activated, if indeed by its definition it is a present good; and that desire is pleasure not activated, if indeed by its definition it is a future good; and anger a rehearsed motion of madness, and madness anger activated. ⟦q10.5|3⟧ He then who has subjected these powers to reason will find desire [1285] become pleasure for him, according to the undefiled intertwining in grace of the soul with the Divine; and anger, pure fervour, guarding the pleasure concerning the Divine, and sober madness, carrying the soul's alluring power according to aspiration wholly out of beings. ⟦q10.5|4⟧ So, as long as the world lives in us, and the soul's voluntary relation to material things, one must not give freedom to these powers, lest, mingled with sensible things as with those of one tribe, they make war on the soul and take it, become a captive of war to the passions, as once the Babylonians took Jerusalem. ⟦q10.5|5⟧ For by the age in which the law commanded the foreign servants to serve, the word signified the soul's relation of inclination toward this world, that is, the present life, showing through the things narrated the things understood.

⟦q10.6|1⟧ 57. ⟦q10.6|2⟧ He calls evil begun; for it has as its beginning our motion contrary to nature; and the good not begun; for before every age and time the good is by nature. ⟦q10.6|3⟧ He calls the good intelligible, which alone one must understand; and evil not intelligible, which alone one must not understand; and he calls the good speakable, for it alone must be spoken. ⟦q10.6|4⟧ And he calls the good coming-to-be: for being by nature unbegotten, by grace, through love for man, it consents to come to be by us, for the deification of us who do and speak it, which alone of all must come to be; and evil not made, which alone must not come to be. ⟦q10.6|5⟧ He calls evil corruptible; for corruption is the nature of evil, having in no way, in anything, existence; and the good incorruptible, as always being, and never ceasing from being, and guarding all things in which it comes to be. ⟦q10.6|6⟧ This then we seek with the rational power; long for with the desiring power; guard inviolate with the incensive power; with the sensory power, according to science, we distinguish it unmixed from its contraries; with the vocal power, speaking it, we make it manifest to those who do not know; and with the generative power we multiply it; or rather, to speak truly, we are multiplied according to it.

⟦q10.7|1⟧ 58. ⟦q10.7|2⟧ The contemplative mind, reigning over the concepts and spectacles in beings and over its own movements, must have a habit barren of vice; that is, a vice that neither conceives at all nor gives birth; upon which it must be borne as it is moved concerning contemplation, lest, while making a spiritual examination of beings, it unawares fall into one of the wicked spirits that are of a nature to corrupt, through some one of sensible things, the pure [1288] examination of the heart.

⟦q10.8|1⟧ 59. ⟦q10.8|2⟧ He who, wounded by some vainglory on account of virtue or knowledge, vainly nourishes the hair of conceit, as Absalom did, displays his moral way of life artfully contrived and mixed, like a mule, for the deceit of those who behold it; borne aloft upon which, he thinks to overpower the father who begot him through the teaching of the word, wishing, as an arrogant man, to draw tyrannically to himself all the glory of virtue and knowledge that belongs to the father from God. ⟦q10.8|3⟧ But such a one, having gone out into the breadth of natural contemplation in the spirit, to the rational war on behalf of truth, because of his living sense is held fast by the hair in the thicket of the oak of material spectacles, having that very empty conceit binding him to death, which hangs him between heaven and earth. ⟦q10.8|4⟧ For the vainglorious man has neither knowledge, like heaven, drawing him up from the conceit that drags him down; nor again earth, I mean the base of practice in humility, drawing him down from the inflation that pulls him up; whom, even dead, the teacher who begot him mourns through love for man, as a lover of God, in imitation of God, not wishing *the death of the sinner, so much as that he turn and live*.

⟦q10.9|1⟧ 60. ⟦q10.9|2⟧ Wisdom is the beginning and end of each man's salvation: as it begins, first making fear, and as it is perfected, later establishing longing; or rather, itself becoming fear at the beginning for our sake by way of economy, that it may stop the lover of vice; and later being found longing, naturally, for its own sake, at the end, that it may fill with intelligible laughter those who have taken its companionship in exchange for all beings.

⟦q10.10|1⟧ 61. ⟦q10.10|2⟧ Wisdom is both fear, being a privation that comes about by way of flight for those who have no appetite for it; and it is longing, being found a habit of enjoying activity for those who love it. ⟦q10.10|3⟧ For it, delivering from passions by the expectation of punishment, both makes fear; and works longing, accustoming the mind by the acquisition of the virtues to look at the things to come.

⟦q10.11|1⟧ 62. ⟦q10.11|2⟧ Every confession humbles the soul: the one, teaching it that it has been justified by the grace of God; the other, that it is held liable to charges by the sloth of its own inclination.

⟦q10.12|1⟧ 63. ⟦q10.12|2⟧ Twofold is the account of confession: one coming about in thanksgiving for the goods given; the other spoken in reproof and examination of things done badly. ⟦q10.12|3⟧ For confession is said both of the enumeration, with thanksgiving, of the divine benefactions by those who have been well treated, and of the declaration of things done badly by those who are culpable; and both are productive of humility. ⟦q10.12|4⟧ For both he who gives thanks for goods [1289] at once, and he who is examined on charges, is humbled: the one judging himself unworthy of the good things given; the other begging to receive remission of his offences.

⟦q10.13|1⟧ 64. ⟦q10.13|2⟧ The passion of pride is constituted from two ignorances. ⟦q10.13|3⟧ And two ignorances, coming together into union, produce one confused prudence. ⟦q10.13|4⟧ For he alone is proud who is ignorant both of the divine help and of human weakness. ⟦q10.13|5⟧ So pride is privation of divine and of human knowledge. ⟦q10.13|6⟧ For by the negation of the true extremes, there is one, and false, affirmation.

⟦q10.14|1⟧ 65. ⟦q10.14|2⟧ Vainglory is a departure from the goal according to God, and a passing over to another goal besides the divine. ⟦q10.14|3⟧ For he is vainglorious who practises virtue for the sake of his own, but not of God's, glory, and with his own labours purchases the insubstantial praises of men.

⟦q10.15|1⟧ 66. ⟦q10.15|2⟧ The man-pleaser cares only for the outward manners, and indeed for flattering speech, that by the former he may win over to himself the sight, and by the latter the hearing, of those who are pleased by, or even struck with wonder at, only what is seen and heard, and who circumscribe virtue by sense alone. ⟦q10.15|3⟧ So we call man-pleasing the display of manners and words made, as if for virtue, for men's sake.

⟦q10.16|1⟧ 67. ⟦q10.16|2⟧ Hypocrisy is a pretence of friendship; or hatred covered with the form of friendship; or enmity activated through goodwill; or envy imitating the character of love; or a life having its comeliness by the fiction, but not the reality, of virtue; or a pretence of justice, preserved by the seeming to be; or deceit having the form of truth: which those practise who imitate the serpent by the crookedness of their manners.

⟦q10.17|1⟧ 68. ⟦q10.17|2⟧ God is the cause of beings, and of the goods in beings. ⟦q10.17|3⟧ He then who is exalted concerning virtue or knowledge, and does not extend, together with the measure of virtue, or of progress, in grace, the fair judgement of his own weakness, has not escaped the evil of pride. ⟦q10.17|4⟧ And he who practises the noble for his own glory has preferred himself to God, transfixed by the nail of vainglory. ⟦q10.17|5⟧ And he who does or speaks virtue *to be seen by men* has set human acceptance far above the divine, sick with the passion of man-pleasing; and he who has maliciously coloured his manners for deceit with the solemnity of virtue alone, and covers the wicked disposition of his inclination with the apparent form of reverence, buys virtue with the guile of hypocrisy. ⟦q10.17|6⟧ So such a one has led away his goal toward other things, besides the cause, in each of these.

⟦q10.18|1⟧ 69. ⟦q10.18|2⟧ None of the wicked demons [1292] ever hinders the eagerness of the virtuous man; but rather, having craftily cut off the deficiencies of the virtues, it suggests the intensifications, joining in the eagerness of the contestants; that it may make wholly its own the notion of the one practising asceticism, which, having lost the equal balance of the mean, travels unawares toward another lodging than the one it seems.

⟦q10.19|1⟧ 70. ⟦q10.19|2⟧ The demons neither hate temperance nor loathe fasting; not distribution of money, not hospitality, not psalmody, not leisure for reading, not stillness, not the loftiest of learnings, not sleeping on the ground, not vigil, not all the rest through which the life according to God is characterized, so long as the goal and the cause of what is done incline toward them.

⟦q10.20|1⟧ 71. ⟦q10.20|2⟧ The other demons the ascetic, perhaps detecting them quickly, easily escapes, and the harm from them; but those that seem to work together with the course of virtue, and as it were to wish to build together with him the temple to the Lord, what mind even of the very lofty could detect, without the word *that passes through* all things, *active and living, and penetrating to the division of soul and spirit*, that is, discerning which of works or concepts are psychic, that is, natural forms or movements of virtue, and which happen to be spiritual, that is, being above nature and characterizing God, but given to nature by grace; knowing, *of joints and marrow*, the fitting or unfitting convergence, wholly, of the modes according to virtue toward the spiritual principles; and *judging the ponderings and the notions of hearts*, that is, the hidden relations in the depth to the things said, and the invisible causes of these in the soul; to whom *no creature is hidden*, in us who seem to escape notice; *but all things are naked and laid bare*, [n: ibid. 13.] not only what has come to be and been conceived, but already also what will come to be from us and be conceived.

⟦q10.21|1⟧ 72. ⟦q10.21|2⟧ He calls *division of soul and spirit* the difference between the virtues from nature, of which we have the principles naturally, and those from the spirit, of which we receive the grace as a free gift. ⟦q10.21|3⟧ For the word, judging, makes the demarcation of these distinctly.

⟦q10.22|1⟧ 73. ⟦q10.22|2⟧ The ponderings and the notions, of which the word is judge, he says are the soul's relations to the divine principles and to the thoughts, and, plainly, the causes of the relations themselves. ⟦q10.22|3⟧ For pondering moves memory, to which relation is proper; and notion looks toward an end, which characterizes a cause.

⟦q10.23|1⟧ 74. ⟦q10.23|2⟧ If God is knowledge according to essence, and mind plainly precedes all knowledge, under which it is of a nature to fall, then God is above it, because [1293] he stands super-infinitely above every mind too, under which knowledge in any way is of a nature to fall.

⟦q10.24|1+2⟧ 75. ⟦q10.24|1+2⟧ Who, not having the divine word dwelling in the depth of the heart, will be able, having slipped aside from the hidden wiles of the hypocrisy of the demons against us, to stand by himself quite alone, without any mixing, and to build the temple of the Lord, like the great Zorobabel and Jesus and the leaders of the families, saying expressly with a loud voice to the deceiving spirits of pride and vainglory and man-pleasing and hypocrisy: *It is not for us and you to build the house to the Lord our God; for we alone will build to the Lord of Israel*; knowing that the mixing of these works the corruption and pulling-down of the whole building, and robs the grace of the comeliness of the divine offerings?

⟦q10.25|1⟧ 76. ⟦q10.25|2⟧ No one who has any of the demons mentioned accompanying his virtue can build it to the Lord; for he does not have God as the end of what is done, beholding whom he goes through it, but the passion that he establishes through it.

⟦q10.26|1⟧ 77. ⟦q10.26|2⟧ Those who war by deficiency of virtue are the demons that teach fornication and drunkenness, avarice and envy; and those who war by excess are those that teach conceit and vainglory and pride, and secretly pierce through the things on the right with the things on the left.

⟦q10.27|1⟧ 78. ⟦q10.27|2⟧ Would that we too might always say to the spirits of wickedness that attack invisibly in the fiction of spiritual friendship, and wish through the good to work out secretly the death of sin, and say: *We will build with you the temple to your Lord*: *It is not for us and you to build the house to the Lord our God; for we alone will build to the Lord of Israel.* ⟦q10.27|3⟧ Alone, because, having been freed from the spirits that war by deficiency of virtue, which we have also escaped, we do not wish, lifted up through you by the excesses, to be transfixed again and fall a fall as much harder than the former as for the former there was an easy hope of return, for those pardoned on account of weakness; but for the latter there either is none, or it will be difficult, for those hated on account of pride, and making for themselves something more right than the right. ⟦q10.27|4⟧ But again not alone, because we have the holy angels as helpers in good things; or rather God himself, manifesting himself to us through the works of justice, and building us a holy temple for himself, and free of every passion.

⟦q10.28|1⟧ 79. ⟦q10.28|2⟧ The limit of virtue is the union, by full knowledge, of human weakness with the divine power. ⟦q10.28|3⟧ He then who circumscribes himself by the weakness of nature has not come to the limit of virtue; and through

⟦q11.1|1⟧ [1296] this does wrong, not yet having received the power that strengthens what is weak. ⟦q11.1|2⟧ But he who self-willedly puts forward his own weakness as strength against the divine power has passed beyond the limit of virtue; and for this reason, doing wrong, he does not recognize that with him what is superior is coming to stand behind; for he thinks the wrong to be virtue. ⟦q11.1|3⟧ More pardonable, then, is he who has circumscribed himself by his weakness according to nature, having suffered the falling-away from virtue rather out of sloth, than he who puts forward his own weakness as strength against the divine power for the doing of what is fitting, having wrought the falling-away from virtue rather out of self-will.

⟦q11.2|1⟧ 80. ⟦q11.2|2⟧ Since it has been said, *The supplication of a righteous man, made active, avails much*, [n: Jac. v, 16.] I know this to be made active in two modes: in one, when he who prays makes the offering of his supplication to God together with the works according to the commandment, so that the supplication does not lie idle and without hypostasis, falling from the tongue in bare word and an empty echo of the voice, but active and living, ensouled by the works of the commandments. ⟦q11.2|3⟧ For the hypostasis of prayer and supplication is manifestly the fulfilment of the commandments through the virtues, by which the righteous man has his supplication strong and able for all things, made active by the commandments. ⟦q11.2|4⟧ In the other mode, when he who needs the prayer of the righteous man does the works of the prayer, both correcting his former life and making the supplication of the righteous man strong, empowered through his own good conduct.

⟦q11.3|1⟧ 81. ⟦q11.3|2⟧ There is no benefit from the supplication of the righteous man when he who needs it takes more pleasure in offences than in virtues. ⟦q11.3|3⟧ For once the great Samuel too mourned for Saul in his offending; but he could not propitiate God, not having received as the helper of his mourning the fitting correction of the offender. ⟦q11.3|4⟧ Wherefore God, making his servant cease from the unprofitable mourning, says to him, *How long dost thou mourn for Saul, and I have set him at naught from reigning over Israel?* [n: I Reg. xvi, 1.]

⟦q11.4|1⟧ 82. ⟦q11.4|2⟧ And again Jeremiah, most compassionate, praying for the people of the Jews, who had gone mad about the error [1297] of the demons, is not heard, not having, for the power of his prayer, the turning of the godless Jews from their error. ⟦q11.4|3⟧ Whence God, leading him too away from praying in vain, says, *And do not thou pray for this people, and do not ask that they be shown mercy, and do not make prayer, and do not come to me concerning them any more, for I will not hear.* [n: Jerem. vii, 16.]

⟦q11.5|1⟧ 83. ⟦q11.5|2⟧ It is truly of much foolishness, not to say derangement, for one who in disposition takes pleasure in ruinous things to seek salvation through the prayer of the righteous, and to ask forgiveness for those things in which he glories, stained in act by his intent; whereas he who needs the supplication of the righteous man ought, if indeed he truly hates wicked things, not to let it become idle and unmoved, but to make it active and strong, winged by his own virtues, and reaching him who is able to give forgiveness of the offences.

⟦q11.6|1⟧ 84. ⟦q11.6|2⟧ The supplication of the righteous man avails much, made active either by the righteous man who makes it or by him who asks the righteous man that it be made. ⟦q11.6|3⟧ For made active by the righteous man, it gives him boldness toward him who is able to grant the requests of the righteous; and by him who asks the righteous man for it, it draws him away from his former wickedness, converting his disposition to virtue.

⟦q11.7|1⟧ 85. ⟦q11.7|2⟧ Since the Apostle has said, *Wherein you rejoice for a little while now, if need be, that you be grieved in manifold temptations*, [n: I Petr. i, 6.] how can one who is grieved in temptations rejoice in that in which he is grieved?

⟦q11.8|1⟧ 86. ⟦q11.8|2⟧ The word of truth knows grief to be twofold: the one formed invisibly according to the soul, the other visibly according to sense; and the one embracing the whole depth of the soul, tormented by the scourge of conscience, the other circumscribing the whole of sense, contracted by the weight of painful things from its natural diffusion; and the one being the end of pleasure according to sense, the other of gladness according to the soul; or rather, the one the conclusion of the passions of inclination according to sense, the other of the passions according to it that are contrary to inclination.

⟦q11.9|1⟧ 87. ⟦q11.9|2⟧ Grief is, to speak for myself, a disposition deprived of pleasures; and deprivation of pleasures is an infliction of toils; and toil is plainly a deficiency or retreat of a natural habit; and the deficiency of a natural habit is a passion of the power that underlies the habit according to nature. ⟦q11.9|3⟧ And the passion of the power that underlies the habit according to nature is the mode according to the misuse of the natural activity; and the misuse of the mode according to activity is the motion of the power towards what is not according to nature and not subsisting.

⟦q11.10|1⟧ [1300] 88. ⟦q11.10|1⟧ He says that grief according to the soul is the end of pleasure according to sense; for out of this the grief of the soul is formed; just as, then, the end of pleasure according to the soul is grief according to the flesh; for the gladness of the soul becomes punishment of the flesh.

⟦q11.11|1⟧ 89. ⟦q11.11|2⟧ He speaks of grief as twofold: the one about sense, formed by the deprivation of bodily pleasures; the other about the mind, coming to be by the deprivation of the goods of the soul. ⟦q11.11|3+4⟧ And he speaks of temptations too as twofold, the voluntary and the involuntary; and the voluntary, he says, are the fathers of bodily pleasure according to sense, and the begetters of grief according to the soul (for sin alone, once committed, grieves the soul); and the involuntary, which show themselves in the toils contrary to inclination, are the fathers of pleasure according to the soul and the begetters of bodily grief according to sense.

⟦q11.12|1⟧ 90. ⟦q11.12|2⟧ Just as the word knows grief to be twofold, as I said, so it understands the mode of temptations also to be twofold: the one according to inclination, the other contrary to inclination; and the one the creator of voluntary pleasures, the other the bringer of involuntary pains. ⟦q11.12|3+4⟧ For the temptation according to inclination plainly forms the voluntary pleasures according to choice; but that contrary to inclination manifestly brings on the involuntary toils contrary to choice; and the one is the cause of grief according to the soul, the other of grief according to sense.

⟦q11.13|1⟧ 91. ⟦q11.13|2⟧ The temptation according to inclination forms grief according to the soul, and plainly creates pleasure according to sense; but that contrary to inclination makes to subsist the pleasure of the soul and the grief of the flesh.

⟦q11.14|1⟧ 92. ⟦q11.14|2⟧ I think that our Lord and God, teaching his own disciples how they must pray, says, with a view to praying away the form of temptations according to inclination, *And lead us not into temptation*; [n: Matth. vi, 13.] that is, to pray not to be abandoned to take trial of the pleasurable temptations, those of inclination and voluntary; and that the great James, called the brother of the Lord, teaching those who contend for the truth not to shrink, with a view to the form of the involuntary temptations, says, *Count it all joy, my brothers, when you fall into manifold temptations*, [n: Jac. i, 2.] that is, the involuntary temptations, contrary to inclination and productive of toils. ⟦q11.14|3⟧ And they show it plainly: there the Lord, adding, *But deliver us from the evil*; [n: Matth. vi, 13.] here the great James, *Knowing that the testing of your faith works endurance; and let endurance have its perfect work, that you may be perfect and entire, lacking in nothing.* [n: Jac. i, 3, 4.]

⟦q11.15|1⟧ [1301] 93. ⟦q11.15|2⟧ The Lord teaches us to pray away the voluntary temptations, as productive of pleasure of the flesh and of pain of the soul; but the great James exhorts us to rejoice at the involuntary temptations, as taking away pleasure of the flesh and pain of the soul.

⟦q11.16|1⟧ 94. ⟦q11.16|2⟧ He is perfect who fights the voluntary temptations through self-control, and perseveres through endurance in the involuntary temptations; and he is entire who carries through both practice with knowledge and contemplation not without practice.

⟦q11.17|1⟧ 95. ⟦q11.17|2⟧ Since both grief and pleasure are divided into soul and sense, he who acquires the pleasure of the soul and accepts with endurance the grief of sense becomes approved and perfect and entire: approved, through the trial of the contraries according to sense; perfect, as fighting unyieldingly through self-control and endurance against pleasure and grief according to sense; entire, as keeping unharmed, in the steadfastness of identity according to reason, the habits that fight against the dispositions according to sense opposed to one another: I mean practice and contemplation, held together with one another and neither disjoined from the other, but practice showing forth through its manners the knowledge of contemplation, and contemplation armed, no less than with reason, with the virtue of practice.

⟦q11.18|1⟧ 96. ⟦q11.18|2⟧ He who has taken trial of grief and pleasure of the flesh may be called approved, as having made trial of the ease and the difficulty of the affairs of the flesh. ⟦q11.18|3⟧ Perfect is he who has wrestled down the pleasure and the pain of the flesh by the power of reason; and entire, he who has kept the habits according to practice and contemplation unchangeable by the intensity of the divine longing.

⟦q11.19|1⟧ 97. ⟦q11.19|2⟧ Of grief according to the soul the mode is twofold: the one formed at one's own offences, the other at those of others. ⟦q11.19|3⟧ And the cause of such grief is plainly the pleasure according to sense of him who grieves, or of those on whose account he grieves. ⟦q11.19|4⟧ For by the exact account there is hardly any sin at all among men that does not have, as the beginning of its own coming-to-be, the soul's unreasoning relation to sense for the sake of pleasure. ⟦q11.19|5⟧ And the cause of pleasure according to the soul is manifestly the grief according to sense of him who takes pleasure and rejoices at his own or at others' virtues. ⟦q11.19|6⟧ For by the exact account there is hardly any virtue among men that does not have, as the beginning of its own coming-to-be, the soul's reasoned detachment from sense.

⟦q11.20|1⟧ 98. ⟦q11.20|2⟧ Without the soul's passionate relation to sense there is no sin at all among men; and of all grief formed in the soul, pleasure of the flesh goes before.

⟦q11.21|1⟧ 99. ⟦q11.21|2⟧ The true coming-to-be of virtue is the soul's voluntary alienation from the flesh; and he gladdens [1304] the soul spiritually who tames the flesh with voluntary toils.

⟦q11.22|1⟧ 100. ⟦q11.22|2⟧ When the soul has acquired, for the sake of virtue, detachment from sense, of necessity sense will be in toils, not having the soul's power that is inventive of pleasant things joined to it according to the relation of inclination; but on the contrary having it manfully slapping back through self-control the uprising of its own natural pleasures; and, towards the infliction of the toils contrary to nature and involuntary, remaining through endurance altogether implacable; and not departing from the worth and glory befitting God according to virtue for the sake of pleasure without hypostasis; and, towards the taking-up of toils, not falling away from the height of the virtues out of sparing the flesh because of the sense in pain. ⟦q11.22|2⟧ The cause of grief according to sense is the soul's complete occupation with the things according to nature. ⟦q11.22|3⟧ And pleasure according to sense is manifestly made to subsist by the activity of the soul contrary to nature, which cannot have any other beginning of its constitution than the soul's putting-off of the things according to nature.

⟦q11.23|1⟧ FOURTH CENTURY

⟦q11.24|1⟧ 1. ⟦q11.24|2⟧ He knows as inventive the noetic power of the soul; which, when it is separated from the relation according to sense, leaves the flesh bereft of provision for pleasure, according to the relation in the inclination, not even bearing to console the pain of the flesh, because of its whole leisure, in the relation of the inclination, for divine things.

⟦q11.25|1⟧ 2. ⟦q11.25|2⟧ Mind and sense have their natural activity opposed to each other, because of the extreme difference and otherness of the things that underlie them. ⟦q11.25|3⟧ For the one has as its subjects the intelligible and incorporeal essences, which it is its nature to apprehend incorporeally; the other the sensible and bodily natures, which it too apprehends naturally.

⟦q11.26|1⟧ 3. ⟦q11.26|2⟧ The soul's putting-off of the things according to nature is wont to become the beginning of pleasure according to sense. ⟦q11.26|3⟧ For when the soul toils about the goods according to nature, there is not the power that discovers the mode of pleasure according to sense.

⟦q11.27|1⟧ 4. ⟦q11.27|2⟧ Where reason leads sense in the contemplation of visible things, the flesh is deprived of all pleasure according to nature, not having sense let loose and freed from the bonds of reason for the service of its own pleasures. ⟦q11.27|3⟧ Whence also, of necessity, when the reason in us prevails, the flesh enslaved to it for virtue is tormented.

⟦q11.28|1⟧ [1305] 5. ⟦q11.28|2⟧ As soon as the mind holds sense to be its own power according to nature, entangled with the surfaces of sensible things, it devises the fleshly pleasures, not being able to pass beyond the nature of visible things, held fast by its passionate relation to sense.

⟦q11.29|1⟧ 6. ⟦q11.29|2⟧ If it is not possible for the mind to pass over to the kindred intelligibles without the contemplation of the sensibles set forth in between, and it is altogether impossible for this to come about without sense, which is joined to the mind and kindred by nature to the sensibles, fittingly, if on encountering them it is held fast by the surfaces of visible things, thinking the sense joined to it to be its natural activity, it has fallen from the intelligibles according to nature, and has seized with both hands, as the saying goes, the bodies contrary to nature; by which being activated contrary to reason, because of the sense that has conquered it, it is established as begetter of grief according to the soul, tormented by frequent scourges of conscience, and becomes the manifest maker of pleasure according to sense, fattened by the conceptions of the modes that procure for the flesh. ⟦q11.29|3⟧ But if, at the very encounter with the things seen, cutting through the surface that faces sense, it beholds the spiritual principles of beings pure of the shapes upon them, it has wrought the pleasure of the soul, which is held fast by none of the sensibles contemplated, and has formed the grief of sense, deprived by nature of all sensibles.

⟦q11.30|1⟧ 7. ⟦q11.30|2⟧ Since pleasure according to sense forms the grief of the soul, that is, toil (for both are the same as each other), and pleasure according to the soul is wont to make the grief of sense, that is, toil, fittingly he who aspires to the life in hope of our God and Saviour Jesus Christ, *through the resurrection of the dead unto an inheritance incorruptible and undefiled and unfading, reserved in the heavens*, [n: I Petr. i, 3.] has in the soul exultation, joy unspeakable, continually rejoicing in the hope of the goods to come; but in the flesh and sense, grief, that is, the toils that come upon it from the manifold temptations, [n: I Petr. i, 6.] and the pains at them. ⟦q11.30|3⟧ For pleasure and toil accompany every virtue: toil of the flesh, deprived of the gentle and smoother sense; pleasure of the soul, delighting in the principles in spirit, pure of everything sensible.

⟦q11.31|1⟧ 8. ⟦q11.31|2⟧ The mind must, in the present life (for this is how I understand «now»), grieved according to the flesh because of the many toils of the temptations brought upon it for virtue, always rejoice according to the soul and take pleasure, because of the hope of the everlasting goods, even if it has sense worn down. ⟦q11.31|3⟧ *For the sufferings of the present time are not worthy against the glory that is to be revealed unto us*, says the divine Apostle. [n: Rom. viii, 18.]

⟦q11.32|1⟧ 9. ⟦q11.32|2⟧ The flesh is the soul's, but not the soul the flesh's; for of the greater, he says, is the lesser, but not of the lesser

⟦q12.1|1⟧ [1308] the greater. ⟦q12.1|2⟧ Therefore, since through the transgression the law of sin, which is pleasure according to sense, was commixed with the flesh, on account of which the death of the flesh through toils was sentenced, devised for the destruction of the law of the flesh, he who has discerned that death entered in on account of sin for its destruction always has his soul rejoicing, as it beholds through manifold toils the law of sin passing away from his own flesh, for the reception of the blessed life to come in spirit, which it is not possible to attain otherwise, unless the law of sin has first been emptied, as from some vessel, out of the flesh in the present life, according to the relation of the inclination towards it.

⟦q12.2|1⟧ 10. ⟦q12.2|2⟧ He who in virtue is grieved according to the flesh because of the toils rejoices in virtue itself according to the soul, as beholding as present the comeliness of the things to come; for the sake of which, according to the great David, [n: Psal. xliii, 22.] by the passing-away of the flesh by inclination, he dies every day, he who is always renewed according to the coming-to-be of the soul in spirit, inasmuch as he has both the pleasure that saves and the grief that profits. ⟦q12.2|3⟧ For we call grief not that which is unreasonable and belongs to the many, tormenting the soul at the deprivation of passions or of material things, as of those who make their impulses contrary to nature towards what they should not, and their flights from what they should not; but that which is reasoned, and approved by those wise in divine things, and signifying the present evil. ⟦q12.2|3⟧ For they say that grief is a present evil, formed according to the soul when pleasure according to sense prevails over rational discernment, and subsisting according to sense when the course of the soul according to virtue is accomplished unhindered; this course bringing toils upon sense in the same measure as it produces pleasure and joy in the soul, which is brought near to God through the kindred illumination according to virtue and knowledge.

⟦q12.3|1⟧ 11. ⟦q12.3|2⟧ He calls saving pleasure the joy of the soul at virtue, and profitable grief the pain of the flesh for virtue. ⟦q12.3|3⟧ He, then, who clings to passions and things makes his impulses towards what he should not; and he who does not welcome the occurrences productive of the deprivation of passions and things makes his flights from what he should not.

⟦q12.4|1⟧ 12. ⟦q12.4|2⟧ Neither does divine grace work illuminations of knowledge if there is not that which receives the illumination according to natural power; nor does that which is receptive work the illumination of knowledge without the grace that furnishes it.

⟦q12.5|1⟧ 13. ⟦q12.5|2⟧ Neither does the grace of the all-holy Spirit work wisdom in the saints without the mind that receives it; nor knowledge without the receptive power of reason; nor faith without the full assurance, according to mind and reason, of the things to come and for the present unclear to all; nor gifts of healings without the love for man according to nature; nor any other of the remaining gifts of grace [1309] without the habit and power receptive of each; nor again will man acquire a single one of those enumerated by natural power, without the divine power that furnishes them. ⟦q12.5|3⟧ And all the saints show this plainly, seeking, after the revelations of divine things, the principles of what was revealed.

⟦q12.6|1⟧ 14. ⟦q12.6|2⟧ He who asks without passion receives the grace of being able to work the virtues in practice; and he who seeks passionlessly finds, in natural contemplation, the truth in beings; and he who knocks passionlessly at the door of knowledge will enter without hindrance into the hidden grace of mystical theology. [n: Matth. vii, 8.]

⟦q12.7|1⟧ 15. ⟦q12.7|2⟧ He who seeks divine things passionlessly will surely receive what is sought. ⟦q12.7|3⟧ But he who seeks with some passion will fail of what is sought. ⟦q12.7|4⟧ For it says, *You ask, and receive not, because you ask amiss.* [n: Jac. iv, 3.]

⟦q12.8|1⟧ 16. ⟦q12.8|2⟧ The Holy Spirit seeks in us the knowledge of beings, and searches; but he does not seek what is sought for himself, since he is God and beyond all knowledge, but for us who need knowledge; just as indeed the Word too becomes flesh, not for himself, but accomplishing for us the mystery through the flesh. ⟦q12.8|3⟧ For as without flesh noetically ensouled the Word did not operate, in a manner befitting God, the things of the flesh according to nature, so neither does the Holy Spirit work in the saints the knowledge of the mysteries without the power that according to nature seeks and searches for knowledge.

⟦q12.9|1⟧ 17. ⟦q12.9|2⟧ As it is not possible for the eye to apprehend sensible things without the light of the sun, so without spiritual light the human mind would never receive spiritual contemplation. ⟦q12.9|3⟧ For sensible light by nature illumines sense for the apprehension of bodies; but spiritual light shines upon the mind for contemplation, for the comprehension of the things beyond sense.

⟦q12.10|1⟧ 18. ⟦q12.10|2+3⟧ The seeking and searching powers for divine things human nature has essentially laid down in it by the Creator at its very passage into being; but the revelations of divine things the power of the all-holy Spirit, coming upon it, makes according to grace. ⟦q12.10|4⟧ But since in the beginning, through sin, the evil one nailed these powers to the nature of visible things, and *there was none that understood or sought out God*, [n: Psal. xiii, 2; lii, 3.] all who partook of the nature having the noetic and rational power circumscribed by the surface of sensible things, and possessing no notion of the things beyond sense, fittingly the grace of the all-holy Spirit, for those who had not come under the deceit by intent and inwardly, unnailing from material things the power that had been nailed, [1312] restored it; which, having received it back pure through grace, they first sought and searched, and so sought out and searched out, [n: I. Petr. i, 10.] through the same grace, namely, of the Spirit.

⟦q12.11|1⟧ 19. ⟦q12.11|2⟧ The salvation of souls, namely, is properly the end of faith. ⟦q12.11|3⟧ And the end of faith is the true revelation of what is believed. ⟦q12.11|4⟧ And the true revelation of what is believed is the ineffable interpenetration of what is believed, according to the analogy of the faith in each. ⟦q12.11|5⟧ And the interpenetration of what is believed is the return of those who have believed to the beginning according to the end. ⟦q12.11|6⟧ And the return of those who have believed to their own beginning according to the end is the fulfilment of aspiration. ⟦q12.11|7⟧ And the fulfilment of aspiration is the ever-moving rest of those who aspire around the object of aspiration. ⟦q12.11|8⟧ And the ever-moving rest of those who aspire around the object of aspiration is the continual and unintermittent enjoyment of the object of aspiration. ⟦q12.11|9⟧ And the continual and unintermittent enjoyment of the object of aspiration is the participation in the divine things above nature. ⟦q12.11|10⟧ And the participation in the divine things above nature is the likening of the participants to what is participated. ⟦q12.11|11⟧ And the likening of the participants to what is participated is the identity, possible through likeness, of the participants with what is participated itself, according to activity. ⟦q12.11|12⟧ And the identity of the participants, possible according to activity through likeness, is the deification, through likeness to what is participated, of those deemed worthy of deification.

⟦q12.12|1⟧ 20. ⟦q12.12|2⟧ Nature does not have the principles of the things above nature, just as it does not have the laws of the things contrary to nature. ⟦q12.12|3⟧ By «above nature» I mean the divine and inconceivable pleasure which God by nature is wont to produce when united according to grace to the worthy; by «contrary to nature», the unspeakable pain formed by the deprivation of it, which God by nature is accustomed to produce when united contrary to grace to the unworthy. ⟦q12.12|4⟧ For according to the quality of disposition underlying in each, God, being united to all, as he himself knows, provides to each the sense, according as each has been formed by himself for the reception of him who will surely be united to all at the end of the ages.

⟦q12.13|1⟧ [1313] 21. ⟦q12.13|2⟧ Those who seek out in spirit the salvation of souls, and search out the spiritual principles and modes of this salvation, the Holy Spirit guides to comprehension, not letting the power through which they are by nature apt to seek out divine things remain unmoved and without activity in them.

⟦q12.14|1+2+3⟧ 22. ⟦q12.14|1+2+3⟧ First one seeks to mortify sin to the choice, and the choice to sin; and so one searches how and in what mode one must mortify these to each other. ⟦q12.14|1+2+3⟧ And again, after their complete mortification to each other, one seeks the life of the choice according to virtue and of virtue according to choice; and so one searches how and in what mode one must create their life in each other. ⟦q12.14|4⟧ Seeking, then, is, to take it as in a definition, appetite for something desirable; and searching is a mode that accomplishes the appetite for the desirable.

⟦q12.15|1⟧ 23. ⟦q12.15|2⟧ He who is to be saved must truly not only mortify sin to the choice, but also the choice itself to sin; and not only raise up the choice to virtue, but also virtue to the choice; so that the choice, divided as dead from dead, whole from whole sin, may not perceive it, and, living, may perceive living virtue, whole the whole of it, by an unintermittent union. ⟦q12.15|3⟧ For he who has mortified the choice to sin *has been planted together in the likeness of the death* of Christ; and he who has raised it up to righteousness has been planted together also *of his resurrection*. [n: Rom. vi, 5.]

⟦q12.16|1⟧ 24. ⟦q12.16|2⟧ Sin and choice, mortified to each other, have a double insensibility towards each other. ⟦q12.16|3⟧ And justice and choice, having life to each other, have a double sense.

⟦q12.17|1⟧ 25. ⟦q12.17|2⟧ Christ, being God by nature and man, is inherited by us as God, above nature, by grace, according to the ineffable participation; and for our sake, in the form that is ours, appropriating us as man, he inherits himself with us according to the inconceivable condescension; whom the saints, having contemplated him beforehand mystically in the Spirit, were taught that the sufferings unto him for virtue's sake in the present must go before the glory to be manifested in Christ in the future because of virtue.

⟦q12.18|1⟧ 26. ⟦q12.18|2⟧ The mind, moving according to aspiration alone, unknowingly, towards the cause of beings, only seeks; but reason, going over in various ways the true principles in beings, searches.

⟦q12.19|1⟧ 27. ⟦q12.19|2⟧ Seeking is the first and simple motion of the mind towards its own cause with aspiration; searching is the first and simple discernment of reason about its own cause with some notion. ⟦q12.19|3⟧ Seeking-out, again, is the mind's motion of knowledge according to science towards its own cause with a fervent aspiration; searching-out is the discernment of reason according to the activity of the virtues about its own cause, [1316] coming about with some prudent and wise notion.

⟦q12.20|1⟧ 28. ⟦q12.20|2⟧ The holy and divine prophets, having sought out and searched out concerning the salvation of souls, had the motion of the mind according to aspiration towards God ardent and fervent with science and knowledge, and the discernment of reason concerning divine things according to activity prudent and wise; those who imitate them seek out the salvation of souls with knowledge and science, and, searching out with prudence and wisdom, pursue the discernment in the divine works.

⟦q12.21|1⟧ 29. ⟦q12.21|2⟧ The word knows the knowledge of divine things to be twofold: the one relational, as lying in word alone and concepts, and not having the sense, through experience, of what is known according to activity, by which we are governed in the present life; the other properly true, in experience alone, according to activity, without word and concepts, providing the whole sense of what is known by participation according to grace, by which in the lot to come we receive the deification above nature, activated unceasingly. ⟦q12.21|3⟧ And the relational, as lying in word and concepts, they say moves the aspiration towards the knowledge according to activity by participation; but that according to activity, providing through experience by participation the sense of what is known, takes away the knowledge lying in word and concepts.

⟦q12.22|1⟧ 30. ⟦q12.22|2⟧ He says that knowledge is twofold: the one lying in word and divine concepts, not having the sense of what has been understood present in form; the other having, only according to activity, without word and concepts, the enjoyment in form of the true things. ⟦q12.22|3⟧ For since the word through knowledge is wont to signify the knowable, it moves the aspiration of those moved through it towards the enjoyment of what is signified.

⟦q12.23|1⟧ 31. ⟦q12.23|2⟧ The wise say it is impossible for the word about God to coexist with the experience of God, or the intellection about him with the sense of God. ⟦q12.23|3⟧ By the word about God I mean the analogy, from beings, of the contemplation of knowledge about him; by sense, the experience in [1317] participation of the goods above nature; by intellection, the simple and unitary knowledge about him from beings. ⟦q12.23|3⟧ And perhaps this is recognized in the case of everything else too; if indeed the experience of this particular thing makes the word about it cease, and the sense of this thing makes the intellection about it idle. ⟦q12.23|4⟧ By experience I mean the very knowledge according to activity, which comes after all word; by sense, the very participation in what is known, which shines forth after all intellection. ⟦q12.23|5⟧ And perhaps the great Apostle, teaching this mystically, says, *Whether there be prophecies, they shall be done away; whether tongues, they shall cease; whether knowledge, it shall be done away*, [n: I Cor. xiii, 8.] speaking, evidently, of the knowledge lying in word and concepts.

⟦q12.24|1⟧ 32. ⟦q12.24|2⟧ Truly it was fitting that he who by nature is the creator of the essence of beings should also become according to grace the very worker of the deification of the things that have come to be; so that the giver of being might appear also as the bestower of ever-well-being. ⟦q12.24|3⟧ Since, then, none of the beings knows at all either itself or another, what it is according to essence, fittingly none of the beings has by nature the foreknowledge of any of the things that will be, except God who is beyond beings, who both knows himself, what he is according to essence, and foreknew the existence of all things made by him even before they came to be; and who is to bestow lavishly according to grace on beings the knowledge of themselves and of others, what they are according to essence, and to make manifest the principles of their coming-to-be that pre-exist in him uniformly.

⟦q12.25|1⟧ 33. ⟦q12.25|2⟧ The Word who created the nature of men did not create together with it, according to sense, either pleasure or pain; but he built into it a certain power according to the mind for pleasure, according to which it would be able to enjoy him ineffably. ⟦q12.25|3⟧ And this power, I mean the natural aspiration of the mind towards God, the first man, giving it to sense at the moment of coming to be, had, at its very first motion towards sensible things through the medium of sense, as pleasure activated contrary to nature; to which he who cares for our salvation affixed by providence pain, as a kind of avenging power; according to which the law of death was rooted with wisdom in the nature of the body, limiting the aspiration of the mind's madness moving contrary to nature towards sensible things.

⟦q12.26|1⟧ 34. ⟦q12.26|2⟧ Pleasure and pain were not created together with the nature of the flesh; but the transgression devised the one for the corruption of the choice, and sentenced the other for the dissolution of the nature; so that pleasure might work sin, the voluntary death of the soul; and pain, through the dissolution, might bring about the

⟦q13.1|1⟧ [1320] … passing-away of the flesh according to form. ⟦q13.1|2⟧ For by providence, for the punishment of the pleasure that is by choice, God has given to the nature the pain that is against choice, and the death that follows upon it.

⟦q13.2|1⟧ 35. ⟦q13.2|2⟧ On account of the pleasure that came upon the nature contrary to reason, the pain that is according to reason came in after it, through many sufferings, in which and from which is death, working the removal of the pleasure contrary to nature; but not indeed its complete destruction as well, which is what the grace of the divine pleasure according to mind is naturally apt to display.

⟦q13.3|1⟧ 36. ⟦q13.3|2⟧ The devising of voluntary labours, and the bringing-on of involuntary ones, remove pleasure, putting to rest its motion in activity; but they do not destroy the power for its coming-to-be that is laid in the nature like a law. ⟦q13.3|3⟧ For philosophy according to virtue is naturally apt to work passionlessness of inclination, but not of nature; according to which, namely the passionlessness of inclination, the grace of divine pleasure that is in the mind supervenes.

⟦q13.4|1⟧ 37. ⟦q13.4|2⟧ Every labour, as having as the cause of its own coming-to-be the pleasure that precedes it in activity, is clearly a debt, paid naturally, by way of cause, by all who have partaken of the nature. ⟦q13.4|3⟧ For upon pleasure contrary to nature labour by all means follows naturally in all those whose coming-to-be the law of pleasure has, without cause, gone before. ⟦q13.4|4⟧ And I call causeless the pleasure from the transgression, as having come into being, clearly, not as the successor of a preceding labour.

⟦q13.5|1⟧ 38. ⟦q13.5|2⟧ It was altogether impossible that the nature, having bowed under both the pleasure that is by choice and the pain that is against choice, should be recalled again to the life that was from the beginning, unless the Creator became man, receiving by choice the pain devised for the punishment of the nature's pleasure by choice, a pain not having preceding it the coming-to-be from pleasure; that he might free the nature from the birth that is from condemnation, accepting a birth that has no beginning from pleasure.

⟦q13.6|1⟧ 39. ⟦q13.6|2⟧ Since after the transgression all men had pleasure naturally preceding their own coming-to-be, and no one at all was naturally free of the passionate coming-to-be by pleasure, but all, as paying a natural debt, endured the labours and the death [1321] upon them; and the mode of freedom was altogether impossible for those tyrannized by unjust pleasure, and naturally held fast by the just labours and by the most just death upon them: there was need, for the destruction of the most unjust pleasure and of the most just labours on its account, by which man was pitiably torn apart in suffering, having the beginning of his coming-to-be from the corruption according to pleasure, and ending the end of his life in the corruption through death; and for the restoration of the suffering nature, that a labour and death be devised, unjust and at the same time causeless: causeless, as having in no way a preceding pleasure toward its coming-to-be; unjust, as being the successor of no passionate life at all; that, taken in the middle between unjust pleasure and most just labour and death, a most unjust labour and death might destroy entirely the most unjust beginning from pleasure, and the most just end of the nature through death on its account, and the race of men might become again free of pleasure and pain, the nature recovering its good estate from the beginning, defiled by none of the marks innate in the things that are under coming-to-be and corruption. ⟦q13.6|3⟧ For this reason the Word of God, being perfect God according to nature, becomes perfect man, constituted of a noetic soul and a body passible according to nature, like us, without sin alone; having in no way at all the pleasure from the disobedience preceding his birth in time from a woman; but having by volition, through love for man, accepted the pain on its account, which is the end of the nature: that, suffering unjustly, he might destroy the beginning of coming-to-be from unjust pleasure that tyrannizes the nature, a beginning not having the death of the Lord as a debt paid on its behalf, as with the other men, but rather cast forth against it; and that he might wipe out the just end of the nature through death, not having as the cause of his being the lawless pleasure on account of which [death] came in and which is justly punished by it.

⟦q13.7|1⟧ 40. ⟦q13.7|2⟧ It was needful, truly it was needful, that the Lord, being wise and just and powerful according to nature, as wise should not be ignorant of the mode of the healing; as just, should not make a tyrannical salvation of man, held fast by sin by inclination; and as able to do all things, should not fall short of the fulfilment of the healing.

⟦q13.8|1⟧ 41. ⟦q13.8|2⟧ The wisdom of God is shown in his becoming by nature truly man; his justice, in his having assumed, like us, the passible of the nature in coming-to-be; his power, in his having made, [1324] through sufferings and death, eternal life for the nature and unchangeable passionlessness.

⟦q13.9|1⟧ 42. ⟦q13.9|2⟧ The principle of wisdom the Lord made manifest in the mode of the healing, becoming man without change and any alteration whatever; and the equality of justice he showed in the greatness of the condescension, submitting by volition to the condemnation of the nature in the passible, and making that very thing a weapon for the destruction of sin and of the death on its account, that is, of pleasure and of the pain on its account, in which lay the power of sin and of death, both the tyranny of sin according to pleasure and the dominion of death on its account according to pain. ⟦q13.9|3⟧ For in the passible of the nature manifestly lies the power of pleasure and that of pain. ⟦q13.9|4⟧ For somehow, as the penalty of pain grows more intense according to nature, we strive to console it through pleasure; for wishing to flee the toilsome sense according to pain, we take refuge in pleasure, attempting to console the nature pressed by the torment of pain. ⟦q13.9|5⟧ But hastening through pleasure to blunt the movements of pain, we ratify the more its handwriting against ourselves, being unable to have pleasure released from pain and labours.

⟦q13.10|1⟧ 43. ⟦q13.10|2⟧ And the strength of his surpassing power he made plain by establishing for the nature an unchangeable coming-to-be from the contraries of what he himself suffered; for through passion giving to the nature passionlessness, and through labours relief, and through death eternal life, he restored it again; renewing by his own deprivations the habits of the nature, and through his own incarnation bestowing on the nature the grace above nature, deification.

⟦q13.11|1⟧ 44. ⟦q13.11|2⟧ God became man in truth, and gave the nature another beginning of a second coming-to-be, ending through labour in the pleasure of the life to come. ⟦q13.11|3⟧ For as Adam the forefather, having transgressed the divine commandment, brought in besides to the nature, beside the first, another beginning of coming-to-be, constituted from pleasure but ending in the death through labour, and devised, according to the counsel of the serpent, a pleasure that was not the successor of a preceding labour but rather passed over into labour, and so had all those born of him in the flesh after his likeness justly brought along with him, on account of the unjust beginning from pleasure, toward the end in death through labour: so too the Lord, becoming man, and creating for the nature another beginning of a second coming-to-be from the Holy Spirit, and accepting the most just death of Adam through labour, a death become in him, clearly, most unjust, as not having as the beginning of his own coming-to-be the most unjust pleasure of the forefather from disobedience, made the destruction of both the extremes, I mean the beginning and the end of human coming-to-be according to Adam, as [1325] not being from God primarily; and he set free from the liability for it all those mystically reborn from him.

⟦q13.12|1⟧ 45. ⟦q13.12|2⟧ The Lord, having taken away the pleasure from the law of sin, for the annulment of the birth according to the flesh of those born in him by grace through the Spirit, grants them to receive death, which formerly was for the condemnation of the nature, for the condemnation of sin; they no longer having, of Adam, the pleasure of coming-to-be from Adam, but only the pain on account of Adam operating in them, not by way of debt for sin but by economy, on account of the natural distress, death against sin. ⟦q13.12|2⟧ And this [death], whenever it does not have as the mother begetting it the pleasure of which it is naturally the avenger, manifestly becomes the father of eternal life. ⟦q13.12|3⟧ For as the life of Adam according to pleasure became the mother of death and corruption, so too the death of the Lord on account of Adam, being free of the pleasure from Adam, becomes the begetter of eternal life.

⟦q13.13|1⟧ 46. ⟦q13.13|2⟧ After the transgression the nature of men had as the beginning of its own coming-to-be the conception from seed according to pleasure, and the birth according to flux; and as its end, the death through corruption according to pain. ⟦q13.13|2⟧ But the Lord, not having this beginning of birth according to the flesh, was not liable to capture by the end either, that is, by death.

⟦q13.14|1⟧ 47. ⟦q13.14|2⟧ Sin, having at the beginning enticed Adam, persuaded him to come to the transgression of the divine commandment; according to which, having given subsistence to pleasure, and having nailed itself through pleasure in him to the very bottom of the nature, it condemned the whole nature to death, pushing the nature of the things that have come to be, through man, toward passing-away according to death. ⟦q13.14|3⟧ For this too had been contrived by the sower of sin and father of vice, the wicked devil, who had banished himself from the divine glory through pride, and through envy, both toward us and toward God, had banished Adam from paradise: to make the works of God vanish, and to dissolve the things constituted for coming-to-be.

⟦q13.15|1⟧ 48. ⟦q13.15|2+3⟧ The devil, envying God and us, having by guile persuaded man that he was envied by God, prepared him to transgress the commandment: God, so that his all-hymned power, making man divine, might not become manifest in activity; and man, manifestly, so that he might not become a partaker of the divine glory in form according to virtue. ⟦q13.15|4⟧ For the most foul one envies not only us the glory with God through virtue, but also God the all-hymned power over us for salvation.

⟦q13.16|1⟧ 49. ⟦q13.16|2⟧ As in Adam death was the condemnation of the nature, [1328] the nature having pleasure as the beginning of its own coming-to-be, so death in Christ has become the condemnation of sin, the nature in Christ again recovering a coming-to-be pure of pleasure.

⟦q13.17|1⟧ 50. ⟦q13.17|2⟧ If we, who have been deemed worthy by grace to become the house of God through the Spirit, ought to display so great an endurance of sufferings for righteousness, unto the condemnation of sin, and, being good, to bear eagerly like evildoers the outrageous death, *what is the end of those who disobey the Gospel of God?* [n: I Petr. iv, 17.] That is, what sort will be the end, that is, the judgement, of those who not only have zealously held to the end, living and active, in soul and body, by inclination and by nature, the coming-to-be of Adam according to pleasure that prevailed over the nature, but also did not receive God and Father exhorting through the incarnate Son; nor indeed the Son himself, the mediator who makes embassy on behalf of the Father, [n: II Cor. v, 20.] and who for our reconciliation to the Father, by the wish of the Father, gave himself up by volition to the death on our behalf, that he might glorify us through himself, having made us radiant with the beauty of his own Godhead, as much as he himself, on our account, accepted to be dishonoured by our sufferings.

⟦q13.18|1⟧ 51. ⟦q13.18|2⟧ Since God will be the place, uncircumscribed and without interval and infinite, of all who are saved, becoming all things to all according to the proportion of justice, or rather according to the measure of the sufferings undergone here with knowledge for righteousness, giving himself to each, as the soul to the members of the body, according to the power underlying each member, shows itself forth active, and through itself holds the members together for being, held together for life: *where will the ungodly and the sinner appear*, [n: I Petr. iv, 18.] deprived of this grace? ⟦q13.18|3⟧ For he who is unable to receive the presence of God active according to well-being, where will he appear, having suffered the falling-away from the divine life that is beyond age and time and place?

⟦q13.19|1⟧ 52. ⟦q13.19|2⟧ He who does not have God holding together his life for well-being, God who is to become a place for all the worthy, how will he be, not having God himself as place according to the abiding and establishment of well-being in God? ⟦q13.19|3⟧ And if the righteous man is saved with much difficulty, what will be, or what will he suffer, who has made no account of piety and virtue in the present life?

⟦q13.20|1⟧ 53. ⟦q13.20|2⟧ God, according to one infinitely powerful wish of goodness, will hold all together, both angels and men, good and wicked; but these all will not partake equally of God, who has pervaded all without relation, but proportionately to themselves.

⟦q13.21|1⟧ 54. ⟦q13.21|2⟧ Those who have kept throughout, with nature,

⟦q14.1|1⟧ [1329] their inclination in equal law, and have made it receptive in activity of the principles of nature according to the whole principle of ever-well-being, on account of the good affection of inclination toward the divine wish, will partake of the whole goodness, according to the divine life shining upon them, as in angels or as in men. ⟦q14.1|2⟧ But those who have made their inclination unequal in law with nature throughout, and have shown it dissipative in activity of the principles of nature according to the whole principle of well-being, on account of the antipathy of inclination toward the divine wish, will fall from the whole goodness, according to the kinship of inclination toward ill-being manifested in them; according to which there will be the distance of such men from God, since they do not have, according to the intent of inclination, the principle of well-being brought to life by the activities of good things, according to which the divine life is naturally apt to shine through.

⟦q14.2|1⟧ 55. ⟦q14.2|2⟧ The balance of each one's inclination at the judgement will be the principle of nature, testing its motion toward ill-being or well-being, according to which participation or non-participation in the divine life naturally comes about. ⟦q14.2|3⟧ For according to being and ever-being, God, being present, will hold all together; but according to ever-well-being, only the saints, angels and men, in a proper mode, leaving ever-ill-being to those who are not such, mingled in as the fruit of inclination.

⟦q14.3|1⟧ 56. ⟦q14.3|2⟧ The mystery of the divine incarnation does not bring in, together with the otherness according to nature of the things of which it is composed, the difference according to hypostasis as well: the one, so that the mystery of the Trinity may not receive an addition; the other, so that nothing may be of one kind and consubstantial with the Godhead according to nature. ⟦q14.3|3⟧ For the concourse of two natures came about toward a hypostasis, but not toward one nature; so that both the one according to hypostasis might be shown accomplished in the union out of the natures that ran together with each other, and the difference of the things that came together toward the indissoluble union might be believed according to the natural property, remaining outside all change and confusion.

⟦q14.4|1⟧ 57. ⟦q14.4|2⟧ We do not speak of a difference of hypostases in Christ, because the Trinity has remained Trinity even when the Word was incarnate, no addition of person having accrued to the holy Trinity through the incarnation; but we speak of a difference of natures, so that we may not hold the flesh consubstantial with the Word according to nature.

⟦q14.5|1⟧ 58. ⟦q14.5|2⟧ He who does not speak of a difference of natures has nowhere from which to warrant the confession that the Word became flesh without change, not knowing that there are preserved according to nature in the one [1332] hypostasis of the one Christ and God and Saviour after the union that which assumed and that which was assumed.

⟦q14.6|1⟧ 59. ⟦q14.6|2⟧ If there is a difference according to nature of flesh and Godhead in Christ after the union (for Godhead and flesh are never the same according to essence), the union of the things that came together came about in no way toward the coming-to-be of one nature, but toward one hypostasis, according to which we find in Christ no difference in any mode whatever. ⟦q14.6|3⟧ For the Word is the same as his own flesh according to the hypostasis. ⟦q14.6|4⟧ For in whatever respect Christ has difference, he cannot be one in every mode; but in whatever respect he admits no difference whatever, the one holds of him always, in every mode, piously, both as being and as being said.

⟦q14.7|1⟧ 60. ⟦q14.7|2⟧ Faith, through hope, establishes perfect love toward God; good conscience, through the keeping of the commandments, gives subsistence to love toward the neighbour. ⟦q14.7|3⟧ For good conscience has no transgressed commandment as its accuser. ⟦q14.7|4⟧ And with these the heart alone of those who aspire to true salvation is naturally apt to be entrusted.

⟦q14.8|1⟧ 61. ⟦q14.8|2⟧ Nothing is quicker than believing, and nothing easier than confessing with the mouth the grace of what has been believed. ⟦q14.8|2⟧ For the one shows the living love of the believer toward the Maker; the other, the God-loving disposition toward the neighbour. ⟦q14.8|3⟧ And love and genuine disposition, that is, faith and good conscience, are manifestly the work of the invisible movement in the heart, needing no external matter at all for coming-to-be.

⟦q14.9|1⟧ 62. ⟦q14.9|2⟧ He who is sick with total immobility regarding the good is altogether easily moved regarding evil. ⟦q14.9|3⟧ For to be unmoved in both is impossible. ⟦q14.9|4⟧ Whence Scripture is accustomed to call the soul's sloth about good things *stones*, as being insensible of virtues, and its eagerness about evil things *wood*. ⟦q14.9|5⟧ And motion according to sense, joined to the activity according to mind, makes virtue with knowledge.

⟦q14.10|1⟧ 63. ⟦q14.10|2⟧ Scripture has called, as I think, the natural law of the body a *middle wall*; and the *fence*, [n: Ephes. ii, 14.] the relation in the law of the flesh to the passions, that is, sin. ⟦q14.10|3⟧ For the relation alone to the passions of dishonour becomes the fence of the law of nature, that is, of the passible part of the nature, walling off the body from the soul, and not allowing the crossing-over of the principle of the virtues to the flesh, through the soul in the middle, according to practice. ⟦q14.10|3⟧ But reason, having come, and having wrestled down the law of nature, that is, [1333] the passible of the nature, abolished the relation in it of the passions contrary to nature.

⟦q14.11|1⟧ 64. ⟦q14.11|2⟧ When, robbing by guile through deceit the knowledge of God implanted in the nature, the wicked one appropriates it to himself, he is a thief, attempting to transfer the reverence from God to himself; leading away, namely, the soul's contemplation according to mind from the spiritual principles in the things that have come to be, and circumscribing the noetic power by the sole surface appearance of sensible things. ⟦q14.11|3⟧ But whenever, beginning from the natural movements, he drags down sophistically the practical power toward things contrary to nature, and through things supposed good persuasively nails its aspiration to the worse, he swears falsely by the name of the Lord, leading the persuaded soul to other things contrary to the undertaking. ⟦q14.11|4⟧ And he is a thief, as robbing for himself the knowledge of nature; and a perjurer, as persuading the practical part of the soul to toil in vain at things contrary to nature.

⟦q14.12|1⟧ 65. ⟦q14.12|2⟧ Or again, he is a thief who, for the deception of his hearers, practises the divine words forsooth, whose power he has not come to know through works, making the bare utterance a trade of glory, and by the word through the tongue hunting the praise of being thought righteous by the listeners. ⟦q14.12|3⟧ And, simply to say, he who has his life discordant with his word, and the disposition of his soul opposed to his knowledge, is a thief, shining not nobly from the goods of others. ⟦q14.12|4⟧ To whom the word will fittingly say: *But to the sinner God said: Why dost thou declare my ordinances, and take up my covenant through thy mouth?*

⟦q14.13|1⟧ 66. ⟦q14.13|2+3⟧ Likewise he too is a thief who, by apparent manners and character, covers over the unapparent villainy of the soul, covering over the inward disposition with a fiction of gentleness; and stealing, just as that one by the utterance of the words of knowledge [steals] the understanding of the hearers, so he too by the mode of the hypocrisy of character [steals] the sense of the beholders. ⟦q14.13|2+3⟧ To whom likewise it will be said: *Be ashamed, you who are clothed in alien garments*; and: *The Lord will uncover their form in that day.* [n: Isa. iii, 17.] For every day, in the hidden workshop of the heart, I seem to hear God saying these things to me, as being plainly condemned on both counts.

⟦q14.14|1⟧ 67. ⟦q14.14|2⟧ He is a perjurer, that is, swearing falsely by the name of the Lord, who promises God the life according to virtue, and pursues things alien to the undertaking of his own promise, and transgresses the covenant of the confession of the reverent life through idleness in the commandments. ⟦q14.14|3⟧ And, to say it briefly, he who [1336] has chosen to live according to God, and is not perfectly mortified to the present life, is a liar and a perjurer, having sworn to God, that is, having promised the blameless course in the divine contests, and not having fulfilled it, and for this reason in no way praised. ⟦q14.14|4⟧ For *everyone who swears by him shall be praised*, [n: Psal. lxii, 12.] that is, everyone who has promised God the life in God, and fulfils through the truth of the works of justice the oaths of his fair undertaking.

⟦q14.15|1⟧ 68. ⟦q14.15|2⟧ He who feigns knowledge by the utterance in words alone steals for his own glory the understanding of the hearers; and he who feigns virtue by his character steals for his own glory the sight of the beholders; and both, stealing through deceit, lead astray, the one the understanding of the soul of the hearers, the other the bodily sense of the beholders.

⟦q14.16|1⟧ 69. ⟦q14.16|2⟧ If he who fulfils his own promises has praise by all means, as swearing by God and speaking the truth, it is clear that he who becomes the transgressor of his own covenants will have blame and dishonour, as having sworn by God and lied.

⟦q14.17|1⟧ 70. ⟦q14.17|2⟧ Not every man coming into this world [n: Joan. i, 9.] is by all means enlightened by the Word; for many remain unenlightened and without part in the light according to full knowledge; but clearly every man who comes by his own inclination into the true world of the virtues [is]. ⟦q14.17|3⟧ For everyone who in truth, through the self-chosen birth, comes into this world is by all means enlightened by the Word, receiving an immovable habit of virtue and an infallible science of true knowledge.

⟦q14.18|1⟧ 71. ⟦q14.18|2⟧ Not all persons and all things that have the same enunciation will by all means be understood in one and the same mode; but each of the things said must be understood according to the power underlying, namely, the mode of holy Scripture, if we would rightly aim at the scope of what is written.

⟦q14.19|1⟧ 72. ⟦q14.19|2⟧ None of the persons, or places, or times, or other things recorded in Scripture, animate and inanimate, sensible and intelligible, understood always in the same mode, has the history or the contemplation consistent with itself throughout. ⟦q14.19|3⟧ Hence he who infallibly practises the divine knowing of Scripture must, by the differences of the things done or said, take each of those enumerated differently, and duly assign the contemplation befitting it according to the place or the time.

⟦q14.20|1⟧ 73. ⟦q14.20|2⟧ All those in the whole world must be taught to live and conduct themselves by reason alone, and to have so much care for bodies as, through much attention, to cut off [1337] the soul's relation to them, and to give the soul no image of matter at all; the sense being already quenched by reason, the sense which at the beginning thrust reason aside and received the irrationality of pleasure after the fashion of a creeping serpent, and against which death was justly decreed: so that it may cease to provide the devil entry to the soul.

⟦q14.21|1⟧ 74. ⟦q14.21|2⟧ Sense, being one in genus, is fivefold in species, through the apprehensive activity according to each species, persuading the erring soul to cherish the cognate sensible instead of God. ⟦q14.21|3⟧ Therefore he who wisely follows reason, before the forced death and the one against inclination, votes of his own will the death of the flesh, making a total separation of inclination from sense.

⟦q14.22|1⟧ 75. ⟦q14.22|2⟧ Sense, having taken the mind under its hand, teaches it polytheism, through each sense, by its slavery to the passions, worshipping the cognate sensible as divine.

⟦q14.23|1⟧ 76. ⟦q14.23|2⟧ He who sits by the letter alone of Scripture has only sense ruling the nature, according to which the relation of the soul to the flesh alone is naturally apt to shine through. ⟦q14.23|3⟧ For the letter, not understood spiritually, has only sense circumscribing its enunciation, and not allowing the power of what is written to cross over to the mind. ⟦q14.23|4⟧ And if the letter has its intimacy with sense alone, everyone who receives the letter Jewishly, according to the history alone, lives according to the flesh, dying by inclination every day the death of sin on account of living sense, unable by the spirit to put to death the deeds of the body, so as to live the blessed life in the spirit. ⟦q14.23|5⟧ *For if you live according to the flesh, you will die*, says the divine Apostle; *but if by the spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live.* [n: Rom. viii, 13.]

⟦q14.24|1⟧ 77. ⟦q14.24|2⟧ Let us not then, kindling the divine lamp, that is, the illuminating word of knowledge, through contemplation and practice, put it under the bushel, [n: Matth. v, 15.] lest we be condemned as circumscribing by the letter the incomprehensible power of wisdom; but [let us put it] on the lampstand, I mean the holy Church, which in the height of true contemplation beacons forth to all the light of the divine doctrines.

⟦q14.25|1⟧ 78. ⟦q14.25|2⟧ He who bears the assaults of involuntary temptations with an unshaken way of thinking, like the blessed Job and the noble martyrs, is a strong lamp, himself also keeping unquenchable the light of salvation, kept in the mode of the endurance of courage, as having the Lord as strength and hymn. ⟦q14.25|3⟧ And he who knows the devices of the wicked one, and is not ignorant of the entanglements of the invisible wars, he too, by the light of knowledge shone round

⟦q15.1|1⟧ [1340] another lamp has appeared, uttering, as befits the great Apostle, *For we are not ignorant of his devices.* [n: II Cor. ii, 11.]

⟦q15.2|1⟧ 79. ⟦q15.2|2⟧ The purification, for those worthy of the purity of the virtues, the Holy Spirit works through fear and piety and knowledge; the illumination of the knowledge of beings according to the principles by which they subsist he gives, through strength and counsel and intelligence, to those worthy of light; and the perfection, through the all-radiant and simple and entire wisdom, he grants to those worthy of deification, leading them up immediately to the cause of beings in every mode, as far as is possible for man, as they are made known from the divine idioms of goodness alone; according to which, knowing themselves from God and God from themselves, there being no middle thing walling them apart (for there is nothing in the middle between wisdom and God), they will have the unalterable unchangeableness, all the middle things having been wholly passed through by them, in which there once lay the danger of erring about knowledge. ⟦q15.2|3⟧ And he calls middle things the essence of the intelligible and the sensible things, through which the human mind is naturally led up to God as the cause of beings.

⟦q15.3|1⟧ 80. ⟦q15.3|2⟧ Fear, piety and knowledge work practical philosophy; strength and counsel and intelligence achieve natural contemplation in the Spirit; and divine wisdom alone grants mystical theology.

⟦q15.4|1⟧ 81. ⟦q15.4|2⟧ As without oil it is impossible for a lamp to be kept unquenched, so without a habit that nourishes the good things with suitable principles and modes and characters, and again with fitting concepts and thoughts, it is impossible for the light of the gifts of grace to be guarded unquenched. ⟦q15.4|3⟧ For every spiritual gift of grace needs a congenial habit, which ceaselessly pours on it, like oil, the noetic matter, that it may remain held fast by the habit of him who received it.

⟦q15.5|1⟧ 82. ⟦q15.5|2⟧ As without the olive it is in no way possible to find, by nature and in truth, genuine oil; and without a vessel to receive the oil it is not possible for it to be held; and the lamp's light, not nourished with oil, is wholly quenched: so without the holy Scriptures there is in truth no power of concepts befitting God; and without a habit, as a vessel receptive of concepts, a divine concept would in no way subsist; and the light of the knowledge that is in the gifts of grace, not nourished with divine concepts, is not kept unquenched for those who have it.

⟦q15.6|1⟧ 83. ⟦q15.6|2⟧ The olive on the left I take to signify the Old Testament, as caring rather for practical philosophy; and the one on the right, the New Testament, as the teacher of a new mystery and productive of the contemplative habit in each of the faithful. ⟦q15.6|3⟧ For the one provides modes of virtue, [1341] the other provides principles of knowledge to those who philosophize divine things; and the one, snatching the mind away from the mist of visible things, leads it up to what is kindred, made pure of all material imagination; the other cleanses it of material passionate attachment by the tension of courage, as with a hammer knocking out the nails of the inclination that fasten it, by relation, to the body.

⟦q15.7|1⟧ 84. ⟦q15.7|2⟧ The Old raises the body, made rational, up to the soul through the virtues as intermediaries, preventing the mind from being brought down to the body; the New raises the mind up to God, winged by the fire of love. ⟦q15.7|3⟧ And the one makes the body the same as the mind in the movement that is by position; the other renders the mind the same as God by the habit of grace, having such a likeness to God that through it God is known (he who from himself by nature is in no way at all known by anyone), as an archetype from an image.

⟦q15.8|1⟧ 85. ⟦q15.8|2⟧ The Old Testament, being a symbol of practice and virtue, prepares the body to agree with the mind in movement; the New, being productive of contemplation and knowledge, makes the mind that mystically holds fast to it bright with divine concepts and marks; and the Old furnishes the man of knowledge with the modes of the virtues, the New grants the practical man principles of true knowledge.

⟦q15.9|1⟧ 86. ⟦q15.9|2⟧ Of those God is both called and becomes Father by grace who have only the birth in the Spirit by inclination according to virtue; by which, having, as the face of the soul, the character of God who begot them appearing in the virtues throughout their life, they prepare those who behold them to glorify God through the conversion of their manners, offering their own life to them for imitation as a choice exemplar of virtue. ⟦q15.9|3⟧ For God is not naturally glorified by bare word, but by works of justice, which proclaim the divine magnificence far more than words.

⟦q15.10|1⟧ 87. ⟦q15.10|2⟧ The natural law is left, on account of sense; but it brings the modes of the virtues to reason. ⟦q15.10|2⟧ And it makes knowledge active. ⟦q15.10|3⟧ The spiritual law is right, on account of the mind; but it mixes with sense the spiritual principles in beings, and makes reasoned practice.

⟦q15.11|1⟧ 88. ⟦q15.11|2⟧ He who shows knowledge embodied by practice, and practice ensouled by knowledge, has found the exact mode of true divine working. ⟦q15.11|3⟧ But he [1344] who has either of these disjoined from the other has either made knowledge an imagination without hypostasis, or established practice as a soulless idol. ⟦q15.11|4⟧ For knowledge without practice differs nothing from imagination, not having the practice that gives it subsistence; and practice without reasoning has become the same as an idol, not having the knowledge that ensouls it.

⟦q15.12|1⟧ 89. ⟦q15.12|2⟧ The mystery of our salvation makes the life a demonstration of the word, and the word the glory of the life; and practice contemplation put into act, and contemplation practice initiated into mystery. ⟦q15.12|3⟧ And, to say it briefly, it makes virtue a manifestation of knowledge, and knowledge a power preserving virtue; and through both (of virtue I speak, and of knowledge) it shows one wisdom constituted: that we may know that the two Testaments agree with each other through all things according to grace, coming together for the completion of one mystery more than soul and body come together with each other by composition for the coming-to-be of one man.

⟦q15.13|1⟧ 90. ⟦q15.13|2⟧ As soul and body make man by composition, so practice and contemplation accomplish, by concourse, one wisdom of knowledge; and the Old and the New Testament work out one mystery. ⟦q15.13|3⟧ But to God alone by nature belongs the good, from which, by participation, all things are illumined and made good that are by nature receptive of light and goodness.

⟦q15.14|1⟧ 91. ⟦q15.14|2⟧ He who understands the visible world contemplates the intelligible; for by imagining he gives the intelligibles a type in sense, and in mind he shapes the principles he has beheld; and he transfers to sense, in many forms, the constitution of the intelligible world, and to mind, with manifold intertwining, the composition of the sensible world; and he understands in the intelligible the sensible, having transferred sense to the mind by the principles, and in the sensible the intelligible, having scientifically bound the mind fast to sense by the types.

⟦q15.15|1⟧ 92. ⟦q15.15|2⟧ The first word concerning the Monad, as the beginning [1345] of all virtue, the prophet named head; as in: *My head went down into the clefts of the mountains*; and the purposes of the spirits of wickedness, the clefts of the mountains, by which our mind was swallowed through the transgression; and lowest earth, the habit that in no way perceives divine knowledge, or has any movement whatever of the life according to virtue; and abyss, the ignorance lying upon the habit of vice, on which, as on earth, the seas of vice stand; or by earth he meant the fixed habit of vice; and everlasting bars, the passionate attachments to material things that hold the worst habit together.

⟦q15.16|1⟧ 93. ⟦q15.16|2⟧ The endurance of the saints consumes the wicked power that attacks; as persuading its own ministers to pride themselves on the toils for truth, and teaching them to be enlarged in sufferings more than those who care overmuch for the life in the flesh are in comforts, making the natural weakness of the flesh as to suffering an occasion for them of power surpassing according to the Spirit. ⟦q15.16|3⟧ For see how the natural weakness of the saints is the occasion of divine power in excess, than which the Lord showed the proud devil weaker.

⟦q15.17|1⟧ 94. ⟦q15.17|2⟧ The word of grace, crossing through many temptations to the nature of men, that is, to the Church of the nations, as Jonah crossed through many tribulations to Nineveh the great city, persuaded the reigning law of nature to rise up from its throne (that is, from its former habit, according to sense, concerning evil), and to take off its robe (I mean, to put off the arrogance of worldly glory in matters of character), and to put on sackcloth, namely mourning, and the hard and rough conduct of ill-suffering, befitting the life according to God; and to sit upon ashes. ⟦q15.17|2⟧ And ashes is the poverty of spirit, upon which everyone sits who is being taught to live piously and has the scourge of conscience over his offences tormenting him.

⟦q15.18|1⟧ 95. ⟦q15.18|2⟧ See how he says that the king is the natural law; the throne, the passionate habit according to sense; the robe, the display of vainglory; the sackcloth, the mourning of repentance; the ashes, humble-mindedness; men, those about reason; cattle, those about desire; oxen, those about anger; sheep, those who stumble about the contemplation of visible things.

⟦q15.19|1⟧ 96. ⟦q15.19|2⟧ He who through the virtue of the law forgets the passions in the flesh as left things, and through infallible knowledge is not caught over his right deeds by the disease of puffing-up conceit as by a right hand, has become a man not knowing his right hand, as not yearning for glory that is dissolved, nor his left, as not provoked by the passions of the flesh. ⟦q15.19|3⟧ The right, then, as it seems, the word called the vainglory over supposed right deeds; and the left, the licentiousness over shameful [1348] passions. ⟦q15.19|4⟧ For the principle of virtue does not know the sin of the flesh as left; and that of knowledge does not know the vice of the soul as right.

⟦q15.20|1⟧ 97. ⟦q15.20|2⟧ Knowledge of the virtues according to reason, that is, the true full knowledge in act of the cause of the virtues, naturally produces total ignorance of the excess and the deficiency that lie beside the mean of the virtues on either side, right and left. ⟦q15.20|3⟧ For if nothing at all in reason is naturally contrary to reason, clearly he who has been led up to the principle of the virtues will in no way recognize the position of the things contrary to reason. ⟦q15.20|4⟧ For it is not possible to behold both opposites at once in the same respect, and to know the one appearing together with the other at once.

⟦q15.21|1⟧ 98. ⟦q15.21|2⟧ If there is no principle of unbelief in faith, nor is light by nature the cause of darkness, nor is the devil naturally shown together with Christ, it is clear that what is contrary to reason does not coexist at all with reason. ⟦q15.21|3⟧ And if what is contrary to reason cannot be with reason at all, he who has been led up to the principle of the virtues will in no way recognize the position of the things contrary to reason, knowing virtue alone, as it is and not as it is supposed; and for this reason knowing neither the right, through excess, nor the left, through deficiency. ⟦q15.21|4⟧ For in both, what is contrary to reason is clearly beheld.

⟦q15.22|1⟧ 99. ⟦q15.22|2⟧ He calls unbelief the denial of the commandments; and faith, assent to them; and darkness, ignorance of the good; and light, its discernment. ⟦q15.22|3⟧ And Christ he has called the essence and the hypostasis of the good; and the devil, the worst habit, generative of all evils.

⟦q15.23|1⟧ 100. ⟦q15.23|2⟧ If reason is the limit and measure of beings, it is equal to irrationality, and for this reason contrary to reason, to be moved beside the limit and beside the measure, or again above the limit and above the measure. ⟦q15.23|3⟧ For both alike bring about, for those so moved, a falling away from what truly is: the one persuading them to make the movement of the course unclear and undefined, not having God as goal, through immoderateness of mind, as the end preconceived for them, as they fashion for themselves something more right than the right; the other, beside the goal, persuading them to make the movement of the course toward sense alone, through slackness of mind, thinking the preconceived end to be what is circumscribed for them by sense. ⟦q15.23|4⟧ Which things he does not know, not suffering them, who is joined to the principle of virtue alone, and has circumscribed by it the whole movement of his own power according to mind, and for this reason is able to think nothing above reason or beside reason.

⟦q15.24|1⟧ FIFTH CENTURY

⟦q15.25|1⟧ 1. ⟦q15.25|2⟧ Reason according to nature, through the virtues as intermediaries, [1349] naturally leads up to the mind him who cares for practice; and the mind leads into wisdom, through contemplation, him who aspires to knowledge. ⟦q15.25|3⟧ But passion contrary to reason persuades him who neglects the commandments to be borne down to sense, whose end is that the mind be nailed to pleasure.

⟦q15.26|1⟧ 2. ⟦q15.26|2⟧ He calls virtue the most passionless and fixed habit concerning the good, on either side of which nothing stands, since it bears the character of God, to whom nothing is contrary. ⟦q15.26|3⟧ And the cause of the virtues is God; and the knowledge of him in act is the alteration, by habit, toward the Spirit, of him who has truly come to know God.

⟦q15.27|1⟧ 3. ⟦q15.27|2⟧ If reason has defined the coming-to-be of each thing as it is natural to it, none of beings has naturally either overstepped itself or fallen below itself. ⟦q15.27|3⟧ Therefore the limit of beings is the full knowledge of the cause by aspiration; and the measure, the imitation of the cause attainable by beings in act. ⟦q15.27|4⟧ And for the aspiration of things moved to be borne above the limit and the measure makes the course profitless, since they do not arrive at God, in whom the movement of all things by aspiration comes to rest, receiving the enjoyment of God as self-subsistent end. ⟦q15.27|5⟧ And for the aspiration of things moved to be borne beside the limit and the measure makes the course profitless, since instead of God they arrive at sense, in which, by pleasure, the enjoyment of the passions, which is without hypostasis, has its standing.

⟦q15.28|1⟧ 4. ⟦q15.28|2⟧ He who sets his mind toward the cause of beings without relation will be wholly ignorant, beholding no principle in God, who in essence is beyond every principle as regards every cause; toward whom, contracted from all beings, he knows none of the principles from which he has departed, beholding inexpressibly only him toward whom he has come by grace. ⟦q15.28|3⟧ For the mind that has run up to God in ecstasy lets go both the principles of bodiless things and those of bodies. ⟦q15.28|4⟧ For none of the things after God can naturally be beheld at once together with God.

⟦q15.29|1⟧ 5. ⟦q15.29|2⟧ Arrogance is a truly accursed passion, composed by composition of two evils, pride, I mean, and vainglory; of which pride denies the cause of virtue and of nature, and vainglory renders both nature and virtue itself bastard. ⟦q15.29|3⟧ For nothing is done by the proud man according to God, and nothing proceeds from the vainglorious man according to nature.

⟦q15.30|1⟧ 6. ⟦q15.30|2⟧ It is proper to pride to deny that God is the begetter of virtue and of nature; and to vainglory, to partition nature toward lowering; of which arrogance is naturally the offspring, being a composite habit of vice, which has a willing denial of God and ignorance of the equality of honour according to nature.

⟦q16.1|1⟧ [1352] 7. ⟦q16.1|2⟧ Arrogance is the mixture of pride and vainglory; toward God having contempt, according to which it is wont blasphemously to slander providence; and toward nature having acquired alienation, according to which it handles all the things of nature contrary to nature, corrupting the comeliness of nature by the mode of misuse.

⟦q16.2|1⟧ 8. ⟦q16.2|2⟧ The spirit of burning heat signifies not only the temptations, but also the abandonment by God, which takes away from the Jews the supply of the divine gifts of grace. ⟦q16.2|3⟧ And the near-kinship according to soul through the Spirit loosens the relation of choice to the flesh, and binds it fast to God, nailed to him by longing.

⟦q16.3|1⟧ 9. ⟦q16.3|2⟧ The natural law, when it does not have sense overreaching reason, persuades all, without teaching, to embrace what is kindred and of one race, since they have nature itself as teacher of help for those in need, and [persuades] all to wish for all that which each thinks agreeable when it is done to himself by others. ⟦q16.3|3⟧ And, teaching this, the Lord says: *Whatever you wish that men should do to you, do you likewise to them.* [n: Matth. vii, 12.]

⟦q16.4|1⟧ 10. ⟦q16.4|2⟧ He says that the work of the natural law is the relation, by inclination, of all toward all, one in mind. ⟦q16.4|3⟧ For those whose nature is governed by reason naturally have one disposition. ⟦q16.4|4⟧ And of those whose disposition is the same, both the mode in character and the course of life are naturally, and manifestly, one. ⟦q16.4|5⟧ And of those whose mode of character and course of life is the same, one, clearly, and the same is the bond of relation toward one another by inclination, leading all by one inclination to the one principle of nature; in which there is in no way the division of nature which now prevails through self-love. ⟦q16.4|6⟧ But the written law, restraining by fear of penalties the disorderly impulses of the more foolish, accustoms them by teaching to look only to the distribution of the equal, according to which the strength of justice, confirmed by time, passes over into nature, making fear a disposition, gently and little by little strengthened by the inclination toward the good, and custom a habit, purified by the forgetting of former things and bringing forth together with itself mutual love.

⟦q16.5|1⟧ 11. ⟦q16.5|2⟧ The written law, he says, preventing injustice by fear, accustoms to what is just; and in time custom makes a disposition that loves justice, from which there supervenes a fixed habit for the good, bringing on forgetfulness of the wickedness that went before.

⟦q16.6|1⟧ 12. ⟦q16.6|2⟧ But the law of grace teaches those who are led by it to imitate God himself immediately, who so loved us above himself, if it is lawful to say so, and that when we were enemies through sin, that he came even [1353] into the essence that is ours without change, being above all essence; and superessentially accepted nature, and became man, and willed to be reckoned one of men, and did not refuse to make our condemnation his own; and deified us by grace as far as he himself by economy became man: that we may learn not only to hold to one another naturally and to love one another as ourselves spiritually, but also to care for one another above ourselves divinely, and to make this the proof of love toward one another, eagerly to prefer, by virtue, the voluntary death for one another. ⟦q16.6|3⟧ *For there is no other greater love than this,* he says, *that one lay down his life for his friend.* [n: Joan. xv, 13.]

⟦q16.7|1⟧ 13. ⟦q16.7|2⟧ The law of nature is, to say it in sum, natural reason taking sense under its hand for the removal of irrationality, according to which there is the division of things naturally joined. ⟦q16.7|3⟧ The written law is natural reason which, after the removal of irrationality according to sense, has taken on also a spiritual longing that holds together the mutual cohesion toward what is kindred. ⟦q16.7|4⟧ And the law of grace is reason established above nature, transforming nature without change to deification, and showing incomprehensibly to the nature of men, as in an image, the archetype that is above essence and nature, and providing the permanence of ever-well-being.

⟦q16.8|1+2⟧ 14. ⟦q16.8|1+2⟧ To hold the neighbour as oneself is to care only for his life unto being; which belongs to the natural law. ⟦q16.8|1+2⟧ To love the neighbour as oneself is to provide for the neighbour also unto well-being according to virtue; which the written law enjoins. ⟦q16.8|1+2⟧ And to love the neighbour above oneself is most proper to the law of grace.

⟦q16.9|1⟧ 15. ⟦q16.9|2⟧ He who is kept from the occasions of the body that lead to pleasure learns principles of Providence, which restrains the inflaming matter of the passions; and he who receives the scourges of the body that lead to pain is taught principles of judgement, which cleanses him by involuntary toils from previous defilements.

⟦q16.10|1⟧ 16. ⟦q16.10|2⟧ If the word of Scripture set the prophet, grieving over the booth and the gourd (I mean the flesh and the pleasure of the flesh), over against God, who holds fast to Nineveh, it is clear that, of the things precious and lovely to men, this which has appeared dear to God is far better and more precious than all beings, much more than the things that are not, and that seem to be by prejudice alone, according to a mistaken judgement, of which there is no principle at all as to existence; but only an imagination, deceiving the mind and supplying to the passion an empty shape for things that are not, but not a hypostasis.

⟦q16.11|1⟧ [1356] 17. ⟦q16.11|2⟧ The exact knowledge of the oracles of the Spirit is naturally revealed only to those worthy of the Spirit; who, having through much care for the virtues cleansed the mind of the soot of the passions, like a clean and translucent mirror, at the first approach receive the knowledge of divine things imprinted on them and falling in upon them like a face. ⟦q16.11|3⟧ But for those whose life has been spotted all over with the stains of the passions, it is hardly possible even from some likely conjectures to infer the knowledge of divine things, let alone to presume to understand and express it with exactness.

⟦q16.12|1⟧ 18. ⟦q16.12|2⟧ He who has received, as the shape of the mind, the knowledge from the virtues in the divine Spirit is said to suffer divine things, because he has taken it on not by nature as to existence, but by grace as to participation. ⟦q16.12|3⟧ But he who has not received the knowledge that is from grace, even if he says something of knowledge, does not know by experience the power of what is said. ⟦q16.12|4⟧ For bare learning does not give knowledge by habit.

⟦q16.13|1⟧ 19. ⟦q16.13|2⟧ A mind purified to the summit through the virtues is naturally, and fittingly, taught thoroughly the principles of the virtues, making the knowledge stamped from them in godlike manner its own face. ⟦q16.13|3⟧ For every mind in itself is formless and unstamped, having an acquired shape: either the knowledge that subsists from the virtues in the Spirit, or the ignorance that supervenes from the passions.

⟦q16.14|1⟧ 20. ⟦q16.14|2⟧ Everyone who has fallen from divine love has the law in the flesh reigning through pleasure, which is neither able nor willing to keep any divine commandment. ⟦q16.14|3⟧ For having set the pleasure-loving life above the life and kingdom conducted according to virtue in the Spirit of God, he has drawn upon himself ignorance instead of knowledge.

⟦q16.15|1⟧ 21. ⟦q16.15|2⟧ He who does not cross over by mind to the divine comeliness of the letter of the law that is within, in the spirit, naturally begets the pleasure-loving relation of inclination, that is, worldly passionate attachment and love of the world, from the utterance alone, in word, of the learning of the law.

⟦q16.16|1⟧ 22. ⟦q16.16|2⟧ Shame of mouth is the world-loving meditation of the thoughts in the mind, and body-loving. ⟦q16.16|3⟧ For in that in which the world is naturally born, by the relation of inclination, from the bodily fashioning that appears in the letter of the law (that is, the disposition that loves the world), there is also the pleasure-loving meditation of the thoughts in the mind. ⟦q16.16|4⟧ For toward that to which we are disposed relationally, toward that we also possess meditation in the mind.

⟦q16.17|1⟧ [1357] 23. ⟦q16.17|2⟧ Or again, shame of mouth is the movement of the mind that brings form upon the passions and fashions, for sense, the beauty that is according to pleasure. ⟦q16.17|3⟧ For without the inventive power in the mind, passion is in no way led to the fashioning of a shape. ⟦q16.17|4⟧ Or anathema is also the material and unlovely and unfigured movement of the passions; and shame of mouth, the movement of the mind that gives form to the passion for sense, and furnishes to the conceptions matter suited to the passion.

⟦q16.18|1⟧ 24. ⟦q16.18|2⟧ He who believes that God ordained according to the law the sacrifices and the feasts, the Sabbaths and the new moons, for bodily luxury and rest will altogether and in every way come under the liability of the activity of the passions, and the shame of the filth of the shameful thoughts upon them; and he will be under the world that is corrupted, and the body-loving meditation in thoughts; and under the matter and the form of the passions, able to hold nothing else precious but the things that are corrupted.

⟦q16.19|1⟧ 25. ⟦q16.19|2⟧ He who has been persuaded that it is a divine ordinance to live luxuriously in bodily fashion according to the law takes gluttony as a gift of God to live with in joy, from which he begets the modes that defile the activity of the senses by misuse.

⟦q16.20|1⟧ 26. ⟦q16.20|2⟧ When the contemplative part of the soul embraces, to live with it, the luxury according to the law in the letter as divine on account of the commandment, it pursues the use of the senses contrary to nature, allowing the use of no activity to show through according to nature. ⟦q16.20|3⟧ For it begets both the habit and the activity of the passions, and brings gluttony home as divine, for the coming-to-be of the modes that soil the senses by misuse, to the destruction of the natural principles and seeds in beings.

⟦q16.21|1⟧ 27. ⟦q16.21|2⟧ No one who attends only to the bodily worship of the law could at all admit a natural principle or thought, because the symbols are not naturally the same as nature. ⟦q16.21|3⟧ For he who remains in the symbols of the law cannot see the nature of beings according to reason, and secure the principles placed essentially in beings by the creator, because of the difference of the symbols from the nature of beings.

⟦q16.22|1⟧ 28. ⟦q16.22|2⟧ He who considers the belly God, and prides himself in shame as in glory, knows to hold with zeal, as divine, only the passions of dishonour; and for this reason he tends only temporal things, that is, matter and form, and the fivefold activity of the senses by misuse. ⟦q16.22|3⟧ For sense, entwined with matter and form by mixture, is wont to produce passion, and to kill and destroy the principles according to nature. ⟦q16.22|4⟧ For passion and nature, in the principle of being, in no way coexist with each other. ⟦q16.22|5⟧ For the principle of nature cannot [1360] at all appear together with passion, just as neither is passion born together with nature in coming-to-be.

⟦q16.23|1⟧ 29. ⟦q16.23|2⟧ He who does not believe that Scripture is spiritual does not perceive his own poverty in knowledge, but is wasted by famine. ⟦q16.23|3⟧ For famine is, truly, the failing of the goods known by experience itself, and the total lack and scarcity of the spiritual foods that hold the soul together. ⟦q16.23|4⟧ For how will anyone consider famine or loss the removal of things never at all known to him?

⟦q16.24|1⟧ 30. ⟦q16.24|2⟧ Truly the people of the faithful and of those who have come to know the truth suffers famine, and the soul of each one, having let go the spiritual contemplation in grace, and having become the typical servitude's in the letter, not nourishing the mind with the grandeurs of the concepts, but filling the sense with passionate imagination by the bodily figurations of the Scriptural symbols.

⟦q16.25|1⟧ 31. ⟦q16.25|2⟧ Everyone who does not admit the spiritual contemplation of the holy Scripture has thrust away together with it, like the Jews, both the natural and the written law, and has been ignorant of the law in grace, according to which deification is given to those led according to it. ⟦q16.25|3⟧ He, then, who takes the written law bodily does not nourish the soul with virtues; and he who does not apply himself to the principles of beings does not feast the mind lavishly with *the manifold wisdom of God*; and he who does not know the great mystery of the new grace does not exult in the hope of the deification to come. ⟦q16.25|4⟧ Therefore the lack of the contemplation according to the written law has following upon it the want of the manifold wisdom of God understood according to the natural law, which has following upon it, in every way, the ignorance of the deification to be given by grace according to the new mystery.

⟦q16.26|1⟧ 32. ⟦q16.26|2+3⟧ Every mind according to Christ, clear-sighted and strong in sight, always aspires to and seeks the face of the Lord. [n: Psal. xxv, 8.] And the face of the Lord is the true contemplation and knowledge of divine things according to virtue, seeking which he is taught the cause of his own want and lack. ⟦q16.26|4⟧ For as the face is characteristic of each one, so also spiritual knowledge characterizes the Divine reflectively. ⟦q16.26|5⟧ Which he who seeks is said to seek the face of the Lord. ⟦q16.26|6⟧ But he who is made flesh according to the letter of the law in the bloody sacrifices has a longed-for ignorance, toward only

⟦q17.1|1⟧ [1361] … taking the commandment as pleasure of the flesh, and clinging bodily, according to sense, to the matter alone of the letter.

⟦q17.2|1⟧ 33. ⟦q17.2|2⟧ He who worships bodily according to the Law begets, as matter, sin in activity, and, as form, materially fashions upon it the mind's assent to the pleasures of the senses suited to it. ⟦q17.2|3⟧ But he who receives Scripture spiritually puts to death the activity as matter, and the assent as the form of sin, together with the modes of the misuse of sense for pleasure, through the natural thoughts, on the height of contemplation.

⟦q17.3|1⟧ 34. ⟦q17.3|2⟧ The matter and the form, and the five modes of the five senses that are about the matter and the form by misuse (I mean the passionate and contrary-to-nature entanglement of the senses with sensible things, that is, with the things under time and flux), after the passing-by of the bodily worship in the Law according to the letter, and the crossing-over of ignorance, the spiritual law, or mind, hands over to be put to death by the loftier principles and thoughts of natural contemplation: that these, having come to be on the height of the law of spiritual contemplation, may abolish and put to death the most universal relation, in the symbols, of the things under time toward sense and body.

⟦q17.4|1⟧ 35. ⟦q17.4|2⟧ Without natural contemplation no one discerns the incongruity of the symbols of the Law with respect to divine things. ⟦q17.4|2⟧ For unless one, having first examined naturally the incongruity of divine and intelligible things in the symbols, shall long to come in mind, having put sense wholly outside the divine sanctuaries, to the comeliness of the intelligibles, he cannot be released once for all from the bodily variety in the types; in which, as long as he walks clinging to the letter, he fittingly does not relieve the want that comes from the famine of knowledge, having condemned himself, like the deceiving serpent, to eat the earth of Scripture, that is, its body; [n: Gen. iii, 14.] and not, according to Christ, feeding on the heaven of Scripture, that is, its spirit and soul, which is the heavenly and angelic bread: I mean the spiritual contemplation and knowledge of the Scriptures in Christ, which God gives, abundantly supplied, to those who love him, as it is written: *He gave them the bread of heaven; man did eat the bread of angels.* [n: Psal. lxxvii, 24.]

⟦q17.5|1⟧ 36. ⟦q17.5|2⟧ The reception of Scripture by sense toward the body, as being plainly begetting of passions and of the relational disposition toward temporal and flowing things, that is, of the passionate activity of the senses toward sensible things, we must abolish like the children and descendants of Saul, [1364] through natural contemplation on the mountain, that is, in the anagogy of the divine oracles, if indeed we aspire to be filled with divine grace.

⟦q17.6|1⟧ 37. ⟦q17.6|2⟧ The Law understood according to the letter alone wrongs the truth, and so does the people of the Jews, and anyone who is their imitator in understanding, circumscribing the power of the Law by the letter alone, and not admitting, for the manifestation of the mystical knowledge hidden in the letter, natural contemplation, which is the middle between types and truth: leading away from the former those who are being led, and leading them back up to the latter; but as it were denying it altogether, and putting it outside the mystagogy of divine things. ⟦q17.6|2⟧ Those, therefore, who tend the divine spectacles must kill this bodily and temporary reception of the Law, as being under time and flowing, through natural contemplation, as on a mountain, on the height of knowledge.

⟦q17.7|1⟧ 38. ⟦q17.7|2⟧ He utterly abolishes the bodily meaning of Scripture who by practice, through natural contemplation, kills the pleasure-loving and body-loving relation toward unstable and flowing matter that arose in the soul from the written Law; slaughtering, as the children and descendants of Saul, the earth-bound understanding of the Law, through the mediation of natural contemplation, as on a mountain, on the height of knowledge; and making manifest before the Lord, through confession, that is, the former reception of the Law toward the body. ⟦q17.7|3⟧ For this is what the lovers of learning can understand by *to expose to the sun before the Lord*. ⟦q17.7|4⟧ *And they exposed them to the sun*, it says, *on the mountain before the Lord*: [n: ibid. 9.] bringing into the light, through knowledge, that is, the preconception that went astray according to the letter; which is, to make manifest on the height of contemplation that the letter of the Law is dead, through knowledge in the spirit.

⟦q17.8|1⟧ 39. ⟦q17.8|2+3⟧ *The letter*, it says, *kills; but the spirit gives life.* [n: II Cor. iii, 7.] Therefore what is of a nature to kill must be killed by the life-giving spirit. ⟦q17.8|4⟧ For that the bodily element of the Law and the divine, that is, the letter and the spirit, should coexist with each other in activity at once and in the same respect is utterly impossible; since what can take life away is not of a nature even to agree with what provides life according to nature.

⟦q17.9|1⟧ 40. ⟦q17.9|2⟧ The spirit is what bestows life; the letter is what takes life away. ⟦q17.9|3⟧ Therefore the letter and the spirit cannot both act in the same respect, just as the life-giving cannot coexist with the corrupting either.

⟦q17.10|1⟧ [1365] 41. ⟦q17.10|2⟧ Mystical circumcision is the complete removal of the mind's passionate relation to the introduced coming-to-be. ⟦q17.10|3⟧ For, applying ourselves naturally to things, we know that the removal of the integrity which is from God according to nature is not perfection. ⟦q17.10|4⟧ For nature, mutilated by art and through contrivance putting off what belongs to it from God by the principle of its making, does not make perfection; lest we bring in art as stronger than God for the confirming of justice, and make the contrived deficiency of nature a filling-up of a deficiency in justice according to its making. ⟦q17.10|4⟧ But from the local position of the member that is circumcised we are taught to perform by inclination the circumcision of the passionate disposition in the soul; by which the inclination is regulated to run together rather with nature, correcting the passionate law of the acquired coming-to-be.

⟦q17.11|1⟧ 42. ⟦q17.11|2⟧ The foreskin is natural. ⟦q17.11|3⟧ And everything natural is a work of divine making, and very good, according to the voice that says: *And God saw all things that he had made, and behold, they were very good.* ⟦q17.11|4⟧ But the Law, commanding the foreskin to be removed through circumcision as unclean, brings in God correcting his own work through art, which is most impious even to conceive. ⟦q17.11|5⟧ Therefore he who applies himself naturally to the symbols of the Law knows that God does not correct nature through art, but commands the passible part of the soul that is obedient to reason to be circumcised, which is typically signified through the bodily member; which knowledge, through courage in practice, is of a nature to put away from the inclination. ⟦q17.11|6⟧ For the circumcising priest signifies knowledge, which has, like iron against passion, the courage of reason in practice. ⟦q17.11|7⟧ For the tradition of the Law is abolished when the spirit prevails over the letter.

⟦q17.12|1⟧ 43. ⟦q17.12|2⟧ The Sabbath is rest from the passions and from the motion of the mind about the nature of beings; or complete inactivity of the passions, and universal cessation of the mind's motion about things made, and complete crossing-over to the Divine; in which he who has arrived through virtue and knowledge, as far as is permitted, must not, as with sticks of wood, [n: Num. xv, 32.] take thought for any matter whatever that kindles the passions, nor indeed gather up the principles of nature at all; lest we dogmatize God, after the manner of the Greeks, as delighting in passions or measured by the limits of nature: him whom perfect silence alone has cried aloud, and complete unknowing by way of pre-eminence presents.

⟦q17.13|1⟧ [1368] 44. ⟦q17.13|2⟧ A crown of goodness is pure faith, flowered, after the manner of precious stones, with the lofty utterances of the doctrines and with spiritual words and concepts, binding round the God-loving mind as a head. ⟦q17.13|3⟧ Or rather, the crown of goodness is the very Word of God, who by the variety of the modes of providence and judgement, that is, by self-control over voluntary passions and endurance of involuntary ones, encircles the mind as a head, and by participation in the grace of deification makes the mind itself more beautiful than itself.

⟦q17.14|1⟧ 45. ⟦q17.14|2⟧ Here he says that self-control is a work of Providence, as purifying the passions of inclination; and endurance, he says, is an achievement of judgement, as arrayed against involuntary temptations, and being a symbol of practical philosophy, bringing across to virtue, as from Egypt, from sin, those held fast by it.

⟦q17.15|1⟧ 46. ⟦q17.15|2⟧ It was not because he wished the days to be honoured by men that God commanded the Sabbath and the new moons and the feasts to be honoured; for so he would have decreed, by commandment of the Law, that they worship the creation rather than the Creator, [n: Rom. i, 25.] supposing the days to be venerable by nature and therefore to be worshipped; but he indicated that he himself is honoured symbolically through the days. ⟦q17.15|3⟧ For he himself is the Sabbath, as the rest of the soul from its toils in the flesh and the cessation of labours for justice; the Pasch, as the liberator of those held fast by the bitter slavery of sin; Pentecost, as the beginning and end of beings, and the principle in which all things by nature subsist. ⟦q17.15|4⟧ Consider how the Law destroys those who understand it bodily, persuading them to worship the creation rather than the Creator, and to consider venerable by nature the things that came to be for their sake, having failed to know him for whose sake they themselves came to be.

⟦q17.16|1⟧ 47. ⟦q17.16|2⟧ The world is a limited place and a circumscribed rest; time is a circumscribed motion; whence also the motion of the life of the things in it is subject to alteration. ⟦q17.16|3⟧ But when nature, having passed through place and time in activity and in notion, that is, through the things without which it is not, namely limited rest and motion, is joined immediately to providence, it finds providence to be a principle, simple and stable by nature, and having no circumscription in any way, and therefore no motion at all.

⟦q17.17|1⟧ 48. ⟦q17.17|2⟧ Existing temporally in the world, [1369] nature has its motion subject to alteration, because of the limited rest of the world and the course of time by way of becoming-other; but when it comes to be in God, because of the natural monad of him in whom it has come to be, it will have an unmoved rest and a stable motion-in-sameness, coming to be eternally about the same and one and only; which the word knows to be immediate about the first cause, the abiding foundation of the things made by it.

⟦q17.18|1⟧ 49. ⟦q17.18|2⟧ The mystery of Pentecost is the immediate union of the things provided for with Providence; that is, the union of nature with the principle according to the conception of Providence, in which there is no reflection whatever of time and coming-to-be. ⟦q17.18|3⟧ Again, the Word is our trumpet, as sounding divine and ineffable knowledge into us; the expiation, as, having become like us, dissolving in himself the charges against us, and deifying by the gift of grace in the Spirit the nature that sinned; the feast of Tabernacles, as the practice of our unchangeableness about the good according to the God-imitating habit, and the bond that holds together our transformation into immortality.

⟦q19.13|1⟧ [1392] 98. ⟦q19.13|2⟧ Man is now moved either about irrational imaginations of passions, out of deceit, because of love of pleasure; or about the principles of the arts, out of distress, because of need; or about natural principles, out of the law of nature, because of learning: none of which drew man of necessity in the beginning, fittingly, since he had come to be above them all. ⟦q19.13|3⟧ For so it was fitting that he who was from the beginning should be: distracted in no way whatever by any of the things after him, or around him, or alongside him, and in need, for his perfection, of one thing alone, the motion without relation toward the one above him, I mean God, with the whole power of love.

⟦q19.14|1⟧ 99. ⟦q19.14|2⟧ The first man had nothing set between God and himself as a screen to knowing, and hindering the self-chosen kindredness that was to come about through love according to the motion toward God. ⟦q19.14|3⟧ For, being passionless by grace, he did not admit the deceit of the imagination of passions through pleasure; and, being in need of nothing, he was free of the necessity of distress about the arts because of need; and, being wise, he stood above the contemplation about nature because of knowledge.

⟦q19.15|1⟧ 100. ⟦q19.15|2⟧ God, who with wisdom gave subsistence to all nature, and secretly set the knowledge of himself in each of the rational essences according to its power, has given to us men too, as a munificent Master, the longing and yearning for himself according to nature, having naturally interwoven with it the power of reason; whereby we might be able with ease to know the modes of the fulfilment of the longing, and not, slipping aside, to miss what we strive to attain. ⟦q19.15|3⟧ Moved, then, by this longing, we are led on to seek concerning the truth itself, and the wisdom and governance that appear in good order in all things, eager through these to attain that for the sake of which we received the longing.

⟦q19.15|4⟧ *The end of the divine chapters of Maximus.*
