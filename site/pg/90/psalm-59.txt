⟦q1.1|1⟧ [0856] *Of the same.*

⟦q1.2|1⟧ EXPOSITION OF PSALM 59

⟦q1.3|1⟧ *Unto the end, for those who shall be changed; for an inscription on a pillar, to David; for teaching, when he burned* [0857] *Mesopotamia and Syria Soba; and Joab returned and struck the valley of salt, twelve thousand.* [n: II Reg. viii, 1; x, 7; I Paral. xviii, 1.]

⟦q1.4|1⟧ *Unto the end, for those who shall be changed* the present psalm is inscribed: both because of the transformation and change of inclination and of choice which came to pass for men at the end of the times, through the sojourn of Christ, from unbelief to faith, and from vice to virtue, and from ignorance to knowledge of God; and because of the universal natural change and renewal in grace of the whole race of men, which shall come to pass hereafter at the end of the ages through our Saviour God himself, from death and corruption to immortal life and incorruption, through the resurrection that is awaited. ⟦q1.4|2⟧ And *for an inscription on a pillar, to David*: that is, to Christ himself; both because of the destruction of vice, after the manner of a pillar, which has already come to pass by his divine incarnation, in him as author and Saviour, and after him in those who live piously according to him; and because of the utter obliteration of both death and corruption that is yet to come to pass through him.

⟦q1.4|3⟧ And *for teaching, when he burned Mesopotamia and Syria Soba*. ⟦q1.4|4+5⟧ For teaching us, who are being changed and shall be changed (plainly, with the praiseworthy and divine change), the psalm has been inscribed by the intelligible David: I mean, by Christ God. ⟦q1.4|6+7⟧ For he alone, being the true king of Israel, the Israel that sees God, as the destroyer of all vice and lack of knowledge, and the conqueror of all time and nature, through which vice is wont to be constituted in us, *burned Mesopotamia*, conversing with us through flesh: that is, the habit of vice which is flooded by the passions of the flesh that are contrary to nature. ⟦q1.4|8⟧ *And Syria Soba*: that is, the disposition enslaved to time, that is to say, deceived by this age. ⟦q1.4|9⟧ For Soba is interpreted "seven"; and this plainly shows the property of time. ⟦q1.4|10⟧ For time is hebdomadic. ⟦q1.4|11⟧ For the devil is wont to stir up nature and time along with himself against virtue and knowledge, joining the unseen wars, as Adraazar did the two Syrias against David. ⟦q1.4|12⟧ For Adraazar, rendered into the Greek tongue, is interpreted "loosing strength" [0860] or "razing power": and this is the devil, who by deceit at the beginning dissolved the spiritual strength of our nature through the transgression of the divine commandment, and enslaved man to time and to nature. ⟦q1.4|13⟧ For without the things that are under nature and time he cannot fight against men at all.

⟦q1.5|1⟧ *And Joab returned and struck the valley of salt, twelve thousand.* ⟦q1.5|2⟧ For everyone who serves as general for the intelligible king David, that is, for our Lord Jesus, against the contrary powers, strikes twelve thousand in the valley of salt. ⟦q1.5|3⟧ And the valley of salt is the flesh, which became through the disobedience a place of *passions of dishonour*: in which, as in a valley, every pious and God-loving man strikes, through reason and contemplation, nature and time, that is, the soul's irrational prepossession concerning them, in no way abiding, because of the height of virtue and knowledge, in the laws that are under nature and time. ⟦q1.5|4⟧ For the number twelve signifies nature and time; since nature is pentadic, because of the senses, and time hebdomadic, as is quite evident to all. ⟦q1.5|5⟧ And having added five to seven, thou wouldst plainly fill up the number twelve.

⟦q1.5|6⟧ And perhaps the number of the slain also signifies the triad of the powers of the soul, wickedly entwined through deceit, contrary to nature, with the tetrad of sensible things by relation, through which every sin is wont to come to be: which number he will slay outright, and well, who in the struggles on behalf of truth ministers to the intelligible David, and engages the invisible battles with knowledge, and draws up spiritually in battle array against the wicked powers. ⟦q1.5|7⟧ For every power of the soul, when it is moved contrary to nature, is wont to be entwined with sensible things. ⟦q1.5|8⟧ So when reason and anger and desire, being three in number, the generic powers of the soul, are worsted by sensible things, which are four, it makes up the number twelve. ⟦q1.5|9⟧ For four upon three, and three upon four put together with the five, fill up this number: which he destroys who goes over faithfully to the Lord, and fights nobly as a champion against every unseen and hostile spirit.

⟦q1.6|1⟧ The present psalm, then, has been inscribed *for those who shall be changed*, for us, *for teaching* about the good things that have come to us from the intelligible David; and *for an inscription on a pillar, to David*: that is, for the proclamation of his divine and saving achievements on our behalf. ⟦q1.6|2⟧ For an inscription on a pillar signifies the proclamation of achievements, according to the interpreters who are expert in such names.

⟦q1.7|1⟧ *O God, thou hast cast us off, and brought us down; thou wast angry, and hast had pity on us.*

⟦q1.8|1+2⟧ [0861] God cast us off when, not having kept the commandment at the beginning, we showed ourselves unworthy of converse with him face to face; he brought us down when, because of our vice, he estranged us from the height of the glory in paradise; he was angry when, for the requital and punishment of vice, he subjected us to the penalty of death; he had pity on us when, through his only-begotten Son, God the Word, who was incarnate and accepted the debt owed on behalf of us all, he redeemed us from death and led us back again into his own glory.

⟦q1.9|1⟧ *Thou hast shaken the earth and troubled it; heal its fractures, for it has tottered.*

⟦q1.10|1⟧ He shook our earth and troubled it, that is, the heart, giving us *the sword of the Spirit*, which distinguishes with knowledge the worse from the better, and rouses the law of the Spirit against the law of the flesh, and makes in us the praiseworthy war against the passions, and shakes the cohesion of the passions in us, and troubles the conscience over the unseemly memories that come by prepossession. ⟦q1.10|2⟧ And through the spiritual regeneration in grace he heals the fractures of our heart, which it underwent when, through the disobedience at the beginning, from the transgression in paradise, it tottered and slipped.

⟦q1.11|1⟧ *Thou hast shown thy people hard things; thou hast made us drink the wine of compunction. ⟦q1.11|2⟧ Thou hast given to them that fear thee a signal, to flee from the face of the bow; that thy beloved may be delivered.*

⟦q1.12|1⟧ God shows hard things to those who fear him through the commandments, in the practical philosophy, setting before them the modes of the mortification of the body; or, on the contrary, through his manifold providence and judgement, with love for man chastening us for our profit, us who do not of our own volition bear to come under the divine yoke of the law of the commandments. ⟦q1.12|2⟧ And he gives the wine of compunction to drink, that is, bestowing the knowledge which, in spiritual contemplation, follows on the purification that comes through practice; for all spiritual knowledge pricks the heart with compunction, bringing it to a perception of the greatness of the divine benefits toward us. ⟦q1.12|2⟧ And he gives to those who fear him *a signal, to flee from the face of the bow*, teaching those who fear him to bear about *the mortification of Jesus in the body*, [n: II Cor. iv, 10] by which the complete inactivity of sin is established. ⟦q1.12|3⟧ For the true signal is the power that through the cross mortifies the way of thinking of the flesh. ⟦q1.12|4⟧ *To flee from the face of the bow*: that is, from the deceitful surface that appears in the things that are. ⟦q1.12|5⟧ For the face of the bow (I mean, of each demonic passion) is the surface of bodies which provokes sense, wounding sense like the point of an arrow, and stirring up the soul unseemly to the passions. ⟦q1.12|6⟧ Therefore the Word says that those who fear the Lord will flee from the face [0864] of the bow, but not from the bow. ⟦q1.12|7⟧ For the bow of sin, without its face, would perhaps do nothing at all against those who fear the Lord.

⟦q1.13|1⟧ So that the beloved of God may be delivered. ⟦q1.13|2⟧ And the beloved of God are the thoughts which, by contemplation, hold together the divine knowledge; whom the modes of the virtues that are constituted in practice deliver from all error and from all the memories of sin which, by prepossession, trouble the soul.

⟦q1.14|1⟧ *Save with thy right hand, and hearken to me. ⟦q1.14|2⟧ God has spoken in his Holy One: I will exult, and divide Sikima, and measure out the valley of tents.*

⟦q1.15|1⟧ God saves with his right hand, giving his only-begotten Son (for the Son is the right hand of the Father) as a ransom for the salvation of the world. ⟦q1.15|2⟧ And he hearkens, giving to the righteous man, besides the forgiveness of sins, spiritual gifts of grace as well. ⟦q1.15|3⟧ For God hears the one who supplicates him, first giving forgiveness of sins through repentance; but he hearkens, giving, besides the forgiveness of sins, the workings that come through spiritual grace as well. ⟦q1.15|4+5⟧ And he speaks *in his Holy One*, that is, in his incarnate Word, his divine purpose, *hidden from the ages and from the generations*, according to the economy in the flesh of Christ himself, through his manner of life among us, befitting a man, as it were crying out expressly, and giving to the world, for a pattern of everlasting life, an image more resounding than any voice: the display of good works. ⟦q1.15|6⟧ And he speaks also in each holy one who keeps his commandments, showing, as it were, his words living through the works, and more resounding than any voice.

⟦q1.16|1⟧ And the righteous man exults when, perfected by the grace of the Only-begotten, he becomes able *to divide Sikima*: that is, to set before others, in proportion, from expert experience, the manly modes of the virtues in practice. ⟦q1.16|2⟧ For Sikima is interpreted "shouldering": which is the habit that holds back all the passions and is productive of all the virtues. ⟦q1.16|3⟧ For they say that the shoulder is a symbol of action.

⟦q1.17|1⟧ And he *measures out the valley of tents*, assigning to each of the things that are the principles proper to it, in natural contemplation. ⟦q1.17|2⟧ For the valley of tents is the present world, in which, for all, according to the differing inclination and disposition of each, unfixed

⟦q2.1|1⟧ [0865] and temporary after the manner of tents is the present life.

⟦q2.2|1⟧ Or again, the tents of the present valley are the modes of the virtues, which he divides with knowledge for those who are taught, giving a training suited to the habit of each, and a guarding of the precious things, he who knows how to distribute to each, through good teaching, the way that fits him for salvation.

⟦q2.3|1⟧ Or the tents of the valley are the Churches of the nations that believed, pitched through Christ our Saviour God throughout the whole inhabited world: which, I think, is also more manifest than the others.

⟦q2.4|1⟧ Or again, he measures out the valley of tents who embraces in knowledge the world of bodies as a valley of tents. ⟦q2.4|2⟧ For in many places of Scripture the body has been called a tent.

⟦q2.5|1⟧ *Galaad is mine, and Manasses is mine, and Ephraim is the strengthening of my head.*

⟦q2.6|1+2⟧ Galaad, according to one of the interpretations of this name, is "revelation"; and Manasses is interpreted "forgetfulness". ⟦q2.6|3⟧ And revelation and forgetfulness fittingly come to the righteous man: to him who receives through contemplation initiation into things ineffable, and forgetfulness of the labours of virtue, because of the gladness of spiritual contemplation that succeeds the labours of the virtues. ⟦q2.6|4⟧ And Ephraim is interpreted "enlargement": which is the unfalling hope, by faith, in the good things to come; which is the support of the head of the righteous man, that is, of faith. ⟦q2.6|5⟧ For faith is the head of every righteous man, and of every good work. ⟦q2.6|6⟧ And its sure strengthening is the unfailing hope of the good things to come, by which, beholding the things to come as present, we are enlarged in tribulations, [n: Psal. iv, 2.] abiding unfallen in temptations.

⟦q2.7|1⟧ *Judah is my king; Moab is the basin of my hope.*

⟦q2.8|1⟧ Judah is interpreted "confession". ⟦q2.8|2⟧ For confession made through prayers rules, in the saints, over every good. ⟦q2.8|3+4⟧ And Judah can be understood also of the Lord, who is the true king of the spiritual Israel, the Israel that sees God: he who, when he became man for us, was confessing to the Father and saying, *I confess to thee, holy Father*. [n: Matth. xi, 25.] And Moab is interpreted "bowel of a father", and is understood as our body, through which, in the practical philosophy, after the manner of a bath (which is what the basin plainly signifies), the purification of sins is wont to come about. ⟦q2.8|5⟧ For the basin of hope is purification: that is, [0868] the readiness, through a life according to purification, for the inheritance of the good things laid up for us in hope.

⟦q2.9|1⟧ And Moab can again be understood also of the incarnation of the Lord. ⟦q2.9|2⟧ For the Son of man became for our sake, and was called, the true basin of our hope: that is, the one who washes and purifies our stains and defilements that come from sin.

⟦q2.10|1⟧ *Upon Idumea I will stretch out my shoe.*

⟦q2.11|1⟧ Idumea is interpreted "blood-like clay"; and it can be taken also of the flesh of the Lord, through which, as through a shoe, he set foot among men, since creation could not otherwise contain the Maker, who is by nature infinite and uncontainable.

⟦q2.12|1⟧ And it can again be understood also of the flesh of each of us, upon which we stretch out the shoe: that is, the ascesis and self-mastery that guard the soul from the wicked thistles and thorns of the devil; for this is what the shoe must be understood to be. ⟦q2.12|2⟧ For without ascesis and exact self-mastery it is impossible for the ascetic of piety to wrestle down the passions of the flesh.

⟦q2.13|1⟧ And again, the shoe of the soul can be understood also as the sense that is mortified through reason and contemplation; by which the soul, setting foot on sensible things, passes unharmed through this age, gathering up their principles through the medium of sense, as through a shoe (sense forming images of the differences of visible things by form and shape), toward the understanding and full knowledge of the one, sole and sovereign Word.

⟦q2.14|1⟧ *To me the allophyles have been subjected.*

⟦q2.15|1⟧ For when through flesh the Lord dwelt in our regions, there were subjected to *the might of his strength* not only the spirits of wickedness, brought to nought, but also all the nations, which, having received the proclamation through the calling of the Gospel, bowed down. ⟦q2.15|1⟧ And the allophyles are subjected also to every righteous man when, having put upon the earth of his flesh, as a shoe, the self-mastery that mortifies the law of sin, he shall tame not only the unclean passions that lurk in it, but shall also bring to nought the wicked demons who work them.

⟦q2.16|1⟧ *Who will bring me into the fortified city?*

⟦q2.17|1⟧ The fortified city is the wisdom gathered together from many divine and mystical objects of contemplation, enclosed, that is to say, as another of the translators has it, fenced about, by the strongholds of the virtues.

⟦q2.18|1⟧ Or again, the fortified city is the state in which he who is truly God-fearing and God-loving becomes impregnable and unapproachable to [0869] his enemies, because of the encompassing wall of the holy Powers that guards him.

⟦q2.19|1⟧ Or again, the fortified city is the soul walled by reason and life, and full of all the graces of the virtues.

⟦q2.20|1⟧ Or again, the fortified city is the habit of true and unstumbling knowledge, which is enclosed by all the moral and natural and theological objects of contemplation; into which every pious man is brought, by no other, but by God alone, who draws all to himself through the unspeakable greatness of his goodness.

⟦q2.21|1⟧ *Or who will guide me as far as Idumea?*

⟦q2.22|1⟧ Idumea, as our account showed before, is the constitution of all the creatures that are under sense, about which natural contemplation in the Spirit is wont to be constituted; according to which, *from the beauty and greatness of creatures, by analogy, the Author of coming-to-be is beheld* [n: Sap. xiii, 5.] by him who piously gathers up the knowledge of the things that are.

⟦q2.23|1⟧ Or perhaps it is the practical philosophy of virtue through the flesh, by which our body becomes a temple of the Holy Spirit, built up together with the beauties of the commandments; to which the same Lord again guides, bestowing on those who love him the science of the things that are seen as well, and teaching the mortification of the members of this earthly and perishable flesh.

⟦q2.24|1+2⟧ Or, to put it in brief, the fortified city is the contemplation, with knowledge, of intelligible things; and Idumea the science of sensible things: into which the sole Wisdom of the God and Father, the only-begotten Son, [n: I Cor. i, 24.] both brings and guides. ⟦q2.24|3⟧ Wherefore the Prophet, continuing, says:

⟦q2.25|1⟧ *Is it not thou, O God, who hast cast us off?*

⟦q2.26|1⟧ That is: Thou, O God, who at the beginning didst cast us off because of sin, thou thyself, having had pity through the bowels of thy mercy, bringest us into the fortified city, and guidest us as far as Idumea, according to the explanation given before.

⟦q2.27|1⟧ *And thou wilt not go forth, O God, with our powers.*

⟦q2.28|1⟧ For God will not go forth with him who is confident in himself, and hopes in the bow, and ascribes his own salvation to the sword. ⟦q2.28|2⟧ But with those who in God himself butt their enemies with the horn, and in his name set at nought those who rise up against them, [n: Psal. xliii, 6, 7.] God will fittingly go forth to the war against the invisible enemies.

⟦q2.29|1⟧ *Give us help out of tribulation, and vain is the salvation of man.*

⟦q2.30|1⟧ In the manner of a prayer the Prophet, in the person of our common nature, begs God and Saviour that help be given us out of the tribulation: plainly, the tribulation which was poured out over nature because of sin and pressed it down as far as [0872] the gates of Hades. ⟦q2.30|2⟧ For tribulation, to speak as I see it, is the tyranny and dominion of death and corruption; and help is the sure hope of the resurrection through grace, looking to which the pious bear the dejection of death without grief.

⟦q2.31|1⟧ Or again, tribulation is the labour on behalf of virtue; and help is the passionlessness that succeeds it by the grace of God: compared with which every salvation of men is vanity and utter non-existence. ⟦q2.31|2⟧ For this is what it is right to understand the vanity to be.

⟦q2.32|1⟧ *In God we shall work power, and he will set at nought those who afflict us.*

⟦q2.33|1⟧ They work power in God who do not ascribe to themselves the achievements of the virtues, nor to their own wisdom the comprehension of the divine mysteries: in whom God himself, alone in the highest degree, and entering wholly in, works every virtue and bestows knowledge, setting at nought all the wicked demons who oppose both virtue and knowledge, and who attempt, through vice and lack of knowledge, to afflict those who fear him. ⟦q2.33|2⟧ For he is Saviour of all, because he is also Creator of all. ⟦q2.33|3⟧ To him be the glory unto the ages. ⟦q2.33|4⟧ Amen.
