Maximus Margunius

died 1602

Cretan-born Greek humanist and bishop of Cythera, a scholar of the Greek diaspora in Venice, who published his Greek hymns in the anacreontic manner, with Conrad Rittershausen's Latin, at Augsburg in 1601. Migne prints three of them, after Daniel, among the works of Maximus the Confessor.

1419Midway between earth and sky
Set as a living creature, alas.

Shining by participation
In the image of the great God.

But the serpent, the all-wicked,
Creeping up in vehement envy,
With ruinous counsels
And ill-omened sayings
Whispering dreadfully to me,
Deceived me, alas,
And out of the fair delight
That was there he thrust me,
And from the Father's orchards,
Whose tiller, ah, the Father had set me,
Banishing me utterly.

And it was a piteous spectacle
To the august choir of the minds.

But since my wound
Would bear no physician,
The Moulder, skilled in every art,
Seeing me with warm compassion,
Strangely came down to me,
In human form, purely,

1420Fire in me, which is blended in.

But do thou grant me
Thy hand, saviour of the people,
Cutting out root and all
The wiles of bitter Beliar;
Thou who of old didst stay a stream
Turned back most swiftly
In a backward-rushing course,
Achieving a strange work [the Latin has Cladem exterorum, the destruction of the foreigners].

Thou didst dry up the sea's
Full-flowing flood,
Bringing across on foot
Thy people Israel,
And thou didst speak, and all things
By thy word were brought into being;
And thou didst check the chariot of flame
Of the swift-running giant,
The greatest light-bringer.

Thou didst turn, in the sevenfold
Furnace of the three children,
The flame into deep dew.

Thou hadst mercy on the prodigal;
Thou didst loose the harlot's knot
Of evil-binding loves,
As she shed hot tears.

And thou didst stay, of her that flowed with blood,
By a touch of thy hem,
The ever-flowing defilement.

Thou didst raise from the dead
Also the child of Jairus,
And didst set breathing once more.

And thou didst cleanse lepers,
And to the blind didst give sight.

And thou didst thrust out a spirit
From the Sidonian girl.

Another took up his bed,
Having gathered the slackness of his limbs,
And the bent woman thou straightenest,
And thou drivest out demons.

And of Peter's mother-in-law
By a touch of the hand thou didst still
The fiery heat.

And thou didst ward off more besides [the Latin has egisti, thou didst],
As many as escape number.

What wonder, if thou cool
The embers of my flame,
From an ever-flowing bowl
Letting thy dew drip
Upon my coals?

Which mayest thou bring me, Saviour,
Through all my life,
That I may hymn endlessly
Thy goodness,
And the Father's, and thy breath's.

1421And for my mind's ill counsel
And slavish constraint;
And for the mischievous toil
That I, being vain, took on,
From childhood shutting
Myself in dread fetters
Of a life of evil ways,
Of wanderings and of loves.

I thought myself the best
Builder of fair houses
And of high-roofed towers.

But for the foundations I was laying,
It was not firm rock
That set a support within,
But sand, which most easily
The winds shake,
And the streams from the springs
Drag off with a roar,
And the rain, falling on it,
Shows soaked through.

And thence the houses fall,
And in ruins the roof
Of the dwellings is piteously seen.

And what then were these
That I, evil-minded, built
To my soul's destruction?

Stony was the heart's
Will, in sins
Ever resting at ease.

It, a strange, whitening
Colour wearing outwardly,
To the unknowing a festering
Way of thinking put on,
That hid within
An unseemly face,
Hypocrisy thou wouldst call it,
Abhorrent to all.

And I kept house-slaves [the Latin has Damnosos … hospites, ruinous guests],
Who cared for glory
Falsely so called, and for puffing-up,
And which of old hurled the Typhon
Swiftly from the sky,
The darkened Morning-star,
By God's just judgement.

And to them there were sons
Numberless, and daughters,
Who lent an obedient
Ear to the behests of evil.

Then the rain coming
And the winds blowing
And the streams rushing in
Shook it from the foundations,
An ill-omened jumble,
And the fall was strange to me,
And for my soul a slipping;

1422She gathered so great
A harm to dwell within her,
And dread anger from above,
And the enmity of mortals.

But now I have resolved to lay
The rock down as foundation,
That the house may be kept safe,
Having those stones
Which are precious,
With gold and with silver
Spotlessly adorned,
Riches to the godly,
And the best of blessedness.

For what than august temperance
Has been established better?

Without which to behold
God is not given to mortals.

Again, than humility,
High-flying, what has been
Better to those who know?

Which true height
Provides to those who possess it.

And than a pure conscience [the Latin has Consciente agno, the Lamb being witness],
Under the eyes of God
Who looks into the heart,
What will ever be better,
Which, like to the angels,
Presents a mortal [the Latin has Angelicum … Cibum, angelic food]?

And a divine winging
The mortal mind undergoes,
If it is pure from above,
With far-shining breaths,
So as to behold the might,
The greatest, and the root
Self-sprung, and the breath
Life-bearing in its blowing,
And of glorious mysteries
To tread the depths,
And how much, under providence,
Of good comes down to the world,
And how much in times
That are for ever there will be.

Who could name a thing more blessed?

Such, O Saviour, do I,
The best of buildings,
Thou giving the strength,
Upon the firm rock,
The chief corner-
Stone, which thou thyself art,
Hasten to raise.

And mayest thou thyself supply me
Thy grace as fellow-worker,
To give these an end
Such as may be best,
And saving for me.

Neither violent rain
Nor springing streams
Nor furious winds
Shall then be able to shake my house
So much as a little,
Built upon the foundation,
Thee, Saviour, unshaken.

And mayest thou give me a hand,
And be a fellow-labourer
With me, and accomplish
The whole, that I may hymn thee,

1423Giver of the best things
And their accomplisher,
Which save mortals.

For apart from thee, O mighty one,
Neither work nor counsel,
Thought, nor utterance,
Nor anything of all things,
As many as are of the better lot,
Shall at all attain its end.

Thou didst give me flesh
And, breathing, a spirit too.

Thou also, raising me fallen,
Didst lead me up skyward,
Lifting me with thy flesh;
Thou raisest me up, dead,
By thy gifts of grace
And boundless tender compassion,
With which thou goest before me, Saviour,
And deemest me worthy of thy flesh's
Unspeakable communion.

Thou wilt also save me at the last,
If thou find not fruitless
Altogether my journey,
If I bear not a mass
Of lead, weighing heavy
And thrusting me into Gehenna
And unquenchable fire,

Μέμνησό μου, ἀνάρχου
Ἄῤῥητε βλαστὲ ῥίζης,
Ξένον τ' ἄημα ἀμφοῖν
Ἶσον γόνῳ καὶ ἀρχῇ,
Μορφὴ τρισήλιός μοι·
Ὅταν κρίνῃς μ' ἀλοιτὴν
Σῶσον πυρός με λάτριν
Τὸν σὸν, ζάλης τε πάσης
Ἄνελκε, Σῶτερ, αἰνῆς·
Δεσμόν τε λῦσον αἶψα
Ἑσμοῦ λυγρῶν κακῶν μου.

Γράψον δὲ σῇ με βίβλῳ
Ῥόον φυγόντα τόνδε
Ἄτρωτον ἠδὲ σῶον.

Ψάλλειν τε καὶ γεραίρειν
Ἐν ἀπλέτοις χρόνοισι
Νεῦσον τεὸν κράτος με.

1424III. Hymn of supplication to the holy Trinity.

Remember me, of the root without beginning
Ineffable shoot,
And strange breath, to both
Equal, to offspring and to source,
My three-sunned form;
When thou judgest me, a sinner,
Save me, thy servant, from the fire,
Thine own, and from every dread
Tempest draw me up, Saviour;
And loose at once the bond
Of the swarm of my grievous evils.

And write me in thy book,
Escaped from this stream
Unwounded and safe.

And that I sing and honour,
In times without measure,
Thy might, grant by thy nod.

On this text

Three short hymns in Greek verse, printed in Migne's volume of Maximus the Confessor (PG 91.1417–1424) from Daniel's Thesaurus hymnologicus (III, 97 ff.): a hymn of thanksgiving and supplication to God, a hymn of supplication to Christ in the form of a confession, and a hymn of supplication to the Trinity. Authorship. They are not the Confessor's. They are by Maximus Margunius, bishop of Cythera (died 1602), and stand in his Hymni Anacreontici (Augsburg, 1601) as his first, sixth and ninth hymns; the acrostic of the third, Maximus wrote these, is his own signature, and his name is the same. The heading «Hymns of Saint Maximus the Confessor» is Migne's, and we keep it as printed. They are written in short seven-syllable lines of the kind called anacreontic, with a Latin verse rendering facing them line for line; that Latin is not Conrad Rittershausen's, which the 1601 edition prints, and we do not know whose it is. On the text. The Greek is our own reading of Migne's plates, every line read against the page image; where a breathing or an accent cannot be told in our scan the standard form is written, and each doubtful reading is checked and recorded. Where the Latin departs from the sense of the Greek the English says so, in a bracketed mark, and its ordinary freedoms are left unmarked. English. The English has one line for each printed Greek line, with no metre and no rhyme; the words move between lines only within a sentence. Whether another English translation exists has not been researched, so this is offered as a new translation and not as the first.
