Maximus the Confessor

Maximus Confessor

c. 580 – 662

Monk, theologian and spiritual writer of the seventh century, the most searching Greek theologian of his age. He wrote on prayer, love and the ascetic life, on the difficulties in Gregory of Nazianzus and Dionysius, and on Christ's two natures and two wills against the Monothelite policy of the emperors and the patriarchs of Constantinople. Arrested and tried at Constantinople in 655 and again in 662, he was condemned, mutilated, and died in exile on the Black Sea coast. Migne prints his letters as Combefis edited them, in Greek with Combefis's Latin facing.

For having once heard me recount, cursorily and briefly, as far as was possible, the things contemplated, beautifully and mystically, and, as far as could be, in the manner of a teacher, by another, a great elder and truly wise in divine things, concerning the holy church and the holy synaxis performed in it, thou didst demand of me, 660urging at once, that I make for thee a written account of these things: having wished to have the writing as a remedy for forgetfulness and a help for memory; saying that memory naturally has time as its tamer, which is able imperceptibly, through forgetfulness, to plunder and wholly to efface the impressions and the images of the good things laid up in it; and that for this reason it needs by all means the renewing mode by which the power of the word, ever flourishing, is of a nature to keep memory impassible and undiminished. And I, for my part, hesitated at the beginning (for the truth shall be told), declining the subject of the discourse; not from not wishing to grant you, beloved, in every way and according to my power, what you desire; but because I had neither partaken of the grace that leads the worthy to this, nor had any experience of the power and practice of speaking, having been reared in unlettered plainness, and being wholly uninitiated in the technical discourses which have their charm in delivery alone, in which the many chiefly delight, bounding pleasure by hearing, even if they often have nothing of worth in depth; and, to say it more properly and more truly, because I feared lest I should outrage, by the poverty of our discourse, that blessed man's lofty utterance and intellection concerning divine things. Yet nevertheless afterward, yielding to the force of love, which is stronger than all things, I accepted the command willingly, choosing rather to be laughed at by the fault-finders for presumption and want of education because of my ready obedience, than to be thought, through delay, unwilling to share your eagerness in every good thing; casting the care of how to speak upon God, the only wonder-worker, who teaches man knowledge, and makes plain the tongue of stammerers, Psal. xciii, 10. and devises a way for those without a way; and who raises the poor from the earth and lifts up the needy from the dunghill: Psal. cxii, 7. from the carnal way of thinking, I mean, and the foul-smelling mire of the passions; the poor in spirit, or the one who is poor in vice and lacking the habit that accords with it; or, the contrary, the one still held by the law of the flesh and by the passions, and for this reason poor and lacking in the grace that accords with virtue and knowledge.

But since by the all-holy and truly God-revealing Dionysius the Areopagite, in his treatise On the Ecclesiastical Hierarchy, the symbols of the 661rite of the holy synaxis too have been contemplated in a manner worthy of his greatness of mind, be it known that our discourse does not now go through the same things, nor proceed by the same ways as he. For it is rash and presumptuous and near to madness, for one who can neither contain nor understand him, to attempt to set hand to his things, and to bring forward as his own the mysteries made manifest in an inspired way to him alone through the Spirit; but only such things as were left by him, by the will of God, in love for man, as graspable by others too, for the exposition and exercise of their own habit concerning divine things according to their aspiration; and through which, in proportion to them, the all-radiant beam of the things performed, being contemplated, becomes known, and holds to itself those seized by longing; so that those after him may not be altogether idle through the whole day of the time of the present life, having no word that hires them for that divine vine-dressing, the word that pays out, for the spiritual labour of the spiritual vineyard, the spiritual denarius of the divine and most royal image, which was plundered in the beginning by the evil one through deceit in the transgression of the commandment. Nor do I promise to tell in order all the things mystically contemplated by the blessed elder; nor the things told, as they were both understood by him and told. For he, besides being a philosopher and a teacher of all learning, having made himself free, through abundance of virtue and long and most scientific practice and diligence concerning divine things, from the bonds of matter and from the imaginations attached to it, had, as was fitting, his mind illumined by the divine rays, and for this reason able at once to see what the many do not see; and his word the most exact interpreter of what had been understood; and, like a mirror hindered by no stain of passions, able purely both to bear and to speak what others cannot even understand; so that his hearers could see the whole mind riding upon the word, and all the things understood shining forth purely in the whole mind, and could receive them ferried across to them through the mediation of the word: but I tell such things as I carry in memory, and as I am able to understand dimly and to speak more dimly; yet piously, by the grace of God who illumines what has been darkened. Since to seek equal things from those not equal in virtue and knowledge seems to me not far from those who try to show the moon 664shining equally with the sun, and who force things not in every way the same to be able to agree with one another in all respects: which is impracticable and impossible.

Let God lead the things said and understood, he who alone is mind of those that understand and of the things understood, and word of those that speak and of the things spoken, and life of those that live and of the things given life; and who is and becomes all things to all for the sake of the things that are and become, but for his own sake is in no way, in no manner, at all either being or becoming any of the things which are something among the things that are and become, since he is not at all ranked by nature with any of the things that are; and for this reason he rather admits not-being, because of his being beyond being, as more properly said of him. For it is necessary, if indeed it is truly necessary for us to know the difference of God and creatures, that the positing of the One beyond being be the removal of the things that are, and the positing of the things that are be the removal of the One beyond being; and that both names be contemplated properly concerning the same, and that neither can be so properly: being, I mean, and not-being. Both properly, since the one posits the being of God as cause of the things that are, and the other, by the pre-eminence of the cause, removes from him all the being of the things that are; and again neither properly, since neither presents the positing of the very what-it-is of what is sought according to essence and nature. For to him with whom nothing at all is naturally yoked by way of cause, whether being or not-being, to him nothing of the things that are and are said, nor of the things that are not and are not said, is fittingly near. For he has an existence simple and unknown and inaccessible to all, and altogether uninterpretable, and beyond all affirmation and negation. And so much for these things; let us come to the proposed subject of the discourse.

For as God, having made all things by his infinite power and brought them into being, holds together and gathers and circumscribes them, and binds them providentially to one another and to himself, both the intelligible and the sensible; and, encompassing all things about himself as cause and beginning and end, makes the things that stand apart from one another by nature to converge with one another by the one power of relation to himself as beginning; by which all things are led to an 665identity of movement and existence, incorruptible and unconfused, none of the things that are being at variance with or divided from any other primarily by a difference of nature or of movement; all things having grown together with all, without commixture, according to the one indissoluble relation and guardianship of the sole beginning and cause; a relation which annuls and covers over all the specific relations contemplated in all according to the nature of each of the things that are, not by corrupting them and destroying them and making them not to be, but by conquering and outshining them, as a wholeness does its parts; or as the cause of the wholeness itself, appearing, by which both the wholeness itself and the parts of the wholeness are of a nature to appear and to be, as having their whole cause shining above them; and, as the sun outshining both the nature and the power of the stars, so having their cause covering their existence as things caused. For as the parts are of a nature to be from the wholeness, so too the things caused are of a nature to be, properly, from the cause, and to be known, and to have their own property at rest, when, embraced by their reference to the cause, they are qualified throughout according to the one power, as has been said, of relation to it. For God, who is all in all, he who in infinite measures is above all, will be seen alone by those who are pure in understanding, when the mind, contemplatively gathering up the principles of the things that are, comes to its end in God himself, as the cause and beginning and end of the production and coming-to-be of all things, and the ground, without interval, of the circumference of all.

In the same way the holy church of God too will be shown working the same things as God concerning us, as an image to its archetype.

On how and in what manner the holy church of God is an image of the world, which subsists of visible and invisible essences.

At the second approach of contemplation, he used to say that the holy church of God is a type and image of the whole world, which subsists of visible and invisible essences, as admitting the same union and distinction as it. For as, in its building, it is one house, it admits a difference according to the placing of its shape by a property of quality, being divided into the place allotted to priests and ministers alone, which we call the sanctuary, and the place open for access to all the faithful peoples, which we call the nave.

How and in what manner the holy church of God symbolically images man, and is itself imaged by him as a man.

And again, by another mode of contemplation, he used to say that the holy church of God is a man: having the sanctuary as soul, and the divine altar as mind, and the nave as body; as being an image and likeness of man, who came to be according to the image and likeness of God; and through the nave, as through a body, putting forward moral philosophy; through the sanctuary, as through a soul, expounding natural contemplation spiritually; and, as through a mind, through the divine altar, entering upon mystical theology. How and in what manner holy Scripture too is called a man.

And just as, by the contemplation according to anagogy, he said that the church is a spiritual man, and man a mystical church, so too he said that the whole of holy Scripture, taken together, is a man: having the Old Testament as body, and the New as soul and spirit and mind. CHAPTER 7

How the world is called a man, and in what manner man too is called a world.

And again, following this image in apt imitation, he suggested that the whole world too, constituted from visible and invisible things, 685is a man; and again, that man, constituted from soul and body, is a world. For he said that the intelligible things hold the ratio of soul, as the soul too holds that of the intelligibles; and that the sensible things hold the figure of body, as the body too that of the sensibles. And that the intelligibles are soul of the sensibles, and the sensibles body of the intelligibles. And that, as soul is in body, the intelligible world is in the sensible world; and the sensible in the intelligible, as body knit together with soul; and that one world is from both, as also one man is from soul and body, neither of these, which have grown together with each other by union, denying and dismissing the other, because of the law of him who bound them together; according to which the principle of the unifying power has been sown in them, not allowing their identity according to hypostasis upon the union to be ignored because of the natural otherness; nor the property that circumscribes each of these to itself to be shown more powerful toward severance and partition than the friendly kindredness placed in them mystically by union; according to which the universal and single mode of the invisible and unknown presence in all things of the cause that holds beings together, being present in all variously, constitutes all things, both in themselves and in one another, uncommixed and undivided; and shows them to be, by the unifying relation, rather of one another than of themselves; until it please him who bound them to loose them, for the sake of a greater and more mystical economy, at the time of the hoped-for universal consummation; at which the world of the things that appear, as a man, will die, and will rise again new from having grown old, at the resurrection expected presently; when this man of ours too, as a part with the whole and a small one with the great, will rise together with the world, having obtained the power of being no longer able to be corrupted; when the body will become like the soul, and the sensibles like the intelligibles, in comeliness and glory, one divine power shining forth upon all by a manifest and active presence, proportionately to each, and through itself preserving the bond of the union unloosed unto the infinite ages.

CHAPTER 10

Of what things the divine readings are a symbol.

The divine readings of the all-holy books, the teacher said, show forth the divine and blessed wishes and counsels of the all-holy God; through which each of us, proportionately, according to the power that is in him, receives the precepts of what is to be done; and we learn the laws of the divine and blessed contests, contending lawfully according to which we are counted worthy of the victory-worthy crowns of the kingdom of Christ.

CHAPTER 12

What the salutations of peace signify.

Through the acclamations of peace that are made from within the sanctuary at the command of the high priest at each reading, the wise man defined, are shown the divine acceptances conveyed through the holy angels; through which God sets an end to the contests of those who lawfully contend for the truth against the opposing powers, 692dissolving the invisible grapplings, and giving peace in the annulling of the body of sin, Rom. vi, 6. and giving the saints in return, for their labours on behalf of virtue, the grace of passionlessness; so that, released from warring, they may refit the powers of the soul for spiritual husbandry, that is, the working of the virtues; through which they broke up the ranks of the wicked spirits, the God and Word being their general, and scattering the bitter and hard-to-escape devices of the devil.

CHAPTER 13

Of what, specifically in each one, the reading of the holy Gospel is a symbol, and the mystical things after it.

Hence, immediately after these, the sacred ordinance of the holy church has legislated that the divine reading of the holy Gospel take place: which specifically introduces to the zealous the suffering of hardship for the sake of the word; after which the Word, as high priest of the contemplation of knowledge, coming to them from heaven, contracts for them the way of thinking of the flesh, as a kind of sensible world; thrusting away the thoughts that still bend down toward earth, and having led them thence, through the closing of the doors and the entrance of the holy mysteries, to the beholding of the intelligibles, both principles and things, their senses now shut, and having come to be outside flesh and world, he teaches them the unutterable things; gathered now through the kiss both to one another and to him, and bringing to him in return for his great benefaction toward them, gratefully, only the thankful confession on behalf of their own salvation, which the divine Creed intimates in riddle. Then, having set them among the number of the angels through the thrice-holy hymn, and having granted them the same science as theirs of the sanctifying theology, he brings them to God and Father, adopted as sons in the Spirit through the prayer through which they have been counted worthy to call God Father. And thence again, as having now passed scientifically through all the principles in beings, he leads them unknowably, through the One is Holy and what follows, to the unknown Monad, united by grace, and by participation likened to it in the identity undivided as far as possible.

CHAPTER 14

Of what the divine reading of the holy Gospel is a symbol, and what it signifies generally.

And generally, it intimates the consummation of this world.

Μετὰ γὰρ τὴν θείαν τοῦ ἁγίου Εὐαγγελίου ἀνάγνωσιν, ὅ τε ἀρχιερεὺς κάτεισι τοῦ θρόνου· καὶ ἡ τῶν κατηχουμένων, καὶ ἡ τῶν λοιπῶν 693τῶν ἀναξίων τῆς θείας τῶν δειχύησομένων μυστηρίων θεωρίας, ἀπόλυσίς τε καὶ ἐκβολὴ διὰ τῶν λειτουργῶν γίγνεται· σημαίνουσα καὶ προτυποῦσα δι᾿ ἑαυτῆς τὴν ἀλήθειαν, ἧς εἰκὼν ὑπάρχει καὶ τύπος, καὶ οἷον ἐν τούτοις βοῶσα, ὅτι μετὰ τὸ κηρυχθῆναι τὸ Εὐαγγέλιον, καθὼς γέγραπται, τῆς βασιλείας ἐν ὅλῃ τῇ οἰκουμένῃ, εἰς μαρτύριον πᾶσι τοῖς ἔθνεσι, τότε ἤξει τὸ τέλος· παραγινομένου κατὰ τὴν δευτέραν αὐτοῦ παρουσίαν, ἐξ οὐρανῶν δηλαδὴ, μετὰ δόξης πολλῆς τοῦ μεγάλου Θεοῦ καὶ Σωτῆρος ἡμῶν Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ· Αὐτὸς γὰρ ὁ Κύριος ἐν φωνῇ ἀρχαγγέλου καὶ ἐν σάλπιγγι Θεοῦ καταβήσεται ἀπ᾿ οὐρανοῦ, φησὶν ὁ θεῖος Ἀπόστολος· καὶ ποιοῦντος ἐκδίκησιν ἐν τοῖς ὑπεναντίοις, καὶ ἀφορίζοντος διὰ τῶν ἁγίων ἀγγέλων τοὺς πιστοὺς ἀπὸ τῶν ἀπίστων, καὶ ἀπὸ τῶν δικαίων τοὺς ἀδίκους, καὶ τῶν ἁγίων τοὺς ἐναγεῖς· καὶ ἁπλῶς, ἵνα συνελὼν εἴπω, τῶν Πνεύματι Θεοῦ στοιχησάντων, τοὺς ὀπίσω σαρκὸς πορευθέντας· καὶ ἐπ᾿ αἰῶσι ἀπείροις τε καὶ ἀτελευτήτοις, ὡς ἡ τῶν θείων λογίων ἀλήθειά φησι, κατ᾿ ἀξίαν τῶν βεβιωμένων ἑκάστῳ δικαίαν ἀποδιδόντος τὴν ἀμοιβήν.

ΚΕΦΑΛ. ΙΕʹ. Τίνος σύμβολόν ἐστι ἡ κλεῖσις τῶν θυρῶν τῆς ἁγίας ἐκκλησίας, ἡ μετὰ τὸ ἅγιον Εὐαγγέλιον γιγνομένη. Ἡ δὲ μετὰ τὴν ἱερὰν ἀνάγνωσιν τοῦ ἁγίου Εὐαγγελίου, καὶ τὴν ἐκβολὴν τῶν κατηχουμένων γινομένη κλεῖσις τῶν θυρῶν τῆς ἁγίας τοῦ Θεοῦ ἐκκλησίας, τήν τε τῶν ὑλικῶν δηλοῖ πάροδον, καὶ τὴν γενησομένην μετὰ τὸν φοβερὸν ἐκεῖνον ἀφορισμὸν καὶ τὴν φοβερωτέραν ψῆφον, εἰς τὸν νοητὸν κόσμον, ἤτοι τὸν νυμφῶνα τοῦ Χριστοῦ, τῶν ἀξίων εἴσοδον· καὶ τὴν ἐν ταῖς αἰσθήσεσι τῆς κατὰ τὴν ἀπάτην ἐνεργείας τελείαν ἀποβολήν.

Οὐ γὰρ μὴ πίω, φησὶ πρὸς τοὺς ἑαυτοῦ μαθητὰς ὁ Θεὸς καὶ Λόγος, ἀπ᾿ ἄρτι ἐκ τοῦ γεννήματος τῆς ἀμπέλου, ἕως τῆς ἡμέρας ἐκείνης, ὅταν αὐτὸ πίνω μεθ᾿ ὑμῶν καινὸν ἐν τῇ βασιλείᾳ τοῦ Πατρὸς ἐμοῦ τῶν οὐρανῶν c. ΚΕΦΑΛ. ΙΖʹ. Τίνος ἐστὶ σύμβολον ὁ θεῖος ἀσπασμός.

For after the divine reading of the holy Gospel the high priest comes down from the throne; and there takes place, through the ministers, the dismissal and expulsion of the catechumens and of the rest 693who are unworthy of the divine sight of the mysteries about to be shown; signifying and prefiguring through itself the truth of which it is image and type, and as it were crying out in these things that after the Gospel of the kingdom has been preached, as it is written, in the whole inhabited world, for a testimony to all the nations, then shall the end come; Matth. xxiv, 14. when our great God and Saviour Jesus Christ comes at his second coming, from heaven, namely, with much glory Tit. ii, 13. — For the Lord himself, with the voice of an archangel and with the trumpet of God, shall descend from heaven, says the divine Apostle I Thess. iv, 15. — and takes vengeance on the adversaries, and through the holy angels marks off the faithful from the unfaithful, and the unjust from the just, and the defiled from the holy; and simply, to put it briefly, those who have gone after the flesh from those who have walked by the Spirit of God; and for infinite and endless ages, as the truth of the divine oracles says, renders to each the just recompense according to the worth of what he has lived.

CHAPTER 15

Of what the closing of the doors of the holy church, which takes place after the holy Gospel, is a symbol.

The closing of the doors of the holy church of God, which takes place after the sacred reading of the holy Gospel and the expulsion of the catechumens, shows both the passing away of material things, and the entrance of the worthy, which will take place after that fearful marking-off and the more fearful sentence, into the intelligible world, that is, the bridal chamber of Christ; and the perfect casting off of the activity of deception in the senses.

CHAPTER 16

What the entrance of the holy mysteries signifies.

The entrance of the holy and venerable mysteries is the beginning and prelude (as that great elder said) of the new teaching that will take place in heaven about the economy of God toward us, and the revelation of the mystery of our salvation which is in the inner shrines of the divine hiddenness. For I will not drink, says God and Word to his own disciples, henceforth of the fruit of the vine, until that day when I drink it new with you in the kingdom of my Father. Matth. xxvi, 29.

Ὁ δὲ πᾶσι προσφωνούμενος πνευματικὸς Θεῖος ἀσπασμὸς καὶ πνευματικὸς c. Λόγου γὰρ σύμβολον τὸ στόμα, καθ᾿ ὃν μάλιστα πᾶσιν ἅπαντες οἱ λόγου μετειληφότες, ὡς λογικοὶ, καὶ τῷ πρώτῳ καὶ μόνῳ Λόγῳ, καὶ παντὸς αἰτίῳ λόγου συμφύονται.

CHAPTER 17

Of what the divine kiss is a symbol.

The spiritual kiss proclaimed to all prefigures and sketches beforehand the concord and oneness of inclination and identity in reason that there will be of all with one another, in faith and love, at the time 696of the revelation of the ineffable goods to come; through which the worthy receive kinship with the Word and God. For the mouth is a symbol of the word, according to which above all all who have partaken of reason, as rational, grow together with all, and with the first and only Word, the cause of every word.

CHAPTER 18

What the divine Creed signifies.

The confession of the divine Creed made by all signifies beforehand the mystical thanksgiving that will take place in the age to come for the paradoxical principles and modes of the all-wise providence of God toward us by which we have been saved; through which the worthy show themselves grateful for the divine benefaction, having nothing else whatever besides this to bring in return for the infinite divine goods toward them.

CHAPTER 19

What the doxology of the Trisagion signifies.

The threefold acclamation of the sanctification of the divine hymnody made by all the faithful people intimates the union and equality of honour with the bodiless and noetic powers that will appear in the future; in which the nature of men will be taught, in concord with the powers above, through the identity of the unchangeable ever-moving around God, to hymn and to sanctify with three sanctifications the one Godhead of three hypostases.

CHAPTER 20

Of what the holy prayer «Our Father, who art in heaven» is a symbol.

The all-holy and venerable invocation of the great and blessed God and Father is a symbol of the enhypostatic and enhyparctic adoption that will be given by the gift and grace of the Holy Spirit; according to which, all human property being overcome and covered over by the visitation of grace, all the saints will be called and will be sons of God, as many as, through the virtues, from here and now have already brightened themselves splendidly and gloriously with the divine beauty of goodness.

Ἡ δὲ κατὰ τὸ τέλος τῆς μυστικῆς ἱερουργίας παρὰ παντὸς τοῦ λαοῦ γινομένη τοῦ, Εἷς Ἅγιος, καὶ τῶν ἑξῆς, ὁμολογία, τὴν ὑπὲρ λόγον καὶ νοῦν πρὸς τὸ ἓν τῆς θείας ἁπλότητος κρύφιον γενησομένην τῶν μυστικῶς τε καὶ σοφῶς κατὰ Θεὸν τετελεσμένων, συναγωγήν τε καὶ ἕνωσιν οηλοῖ, ἐν τί 697ἀφθάρτῳ τῶν νοητῶν αἰῶνι· καθ᾿ ὃν τῆς ἀφανοῦς καὶ ὑπεραῤῥήτουδόξης τὸ φῶς ἐνοπτεύοντες, τῆς μακαρίας, μετὰ τῶν ἄνω δυνάμεων, καὶ αὐτοὶ δεκτικοὶ γίνονται καθαρότητος· μεθ᾿ ἣν, ὡς τέλος πάντων, ἡ τοῦ μυστηρίου μετάδοσις γίνεται· μεταποιοῦσα πρὸς ἑαυτὴν, καὶ ὁμοίους τῷ κατ᾿ αἰτίαν ἀγαθῷ κατὰ χάριν καὶ μέθεξιν ἀποφαίνουσα τοὺς ἀξίως μεταλαμβάνοντας, ἐν μηδενὶ αὐτοῦ λειπομένους, κατὰ τὸ ἐφικτὸν ἀνθρώποις καὶ ἐνδεχόμενον. Ὥστε καὶ αὐτοὺς δύνασθαι εἶναί τε καὶ καλεῖσθαι θέσει κατὰ τὴν χάριν θεοὺς, διὰ τὸν αὐτοὺς ὅλως ὕλης c. A. πληρώσαντα ὅλον Θεὸν, καὶ μηδὲν αὐτῶν τῆς αὐτοῦ παρουσίας κενὸν καταλείψαντα.

ΚΕΦΑΛ. ΚΒ. Πῶς, καὶ τίνι τρόπῳ καὶ ἡ τῆς ψυχῆς καθ᾿ ψυχὴ ἡ τῆς κ. cod. Marg. ἑαυτὴν νοουμένης ἐπὶ τοῦ καθ᾿ ἕκαστον ἰδικῶς, ἐκθεωτικὴ καὶ τελειοποιὸς διὰ τῶν εἰρημένων θεωρεῖται κατάστασις. CHAPTER 21

What the end of the mystical sacred rite signifies, of the hymns proclaimed: that is, «One is holy, one is Lord», and what follows.

The confession of One is Holy and what follows, made by all the people at the end of the mystical sacred rite, shows the gathering and union, beyond reason and mind, that will take place toward the one hidden of the divine simplicity, of those who have been perfected mystically and wisely according to God, in the 697incorruptible age of the intelligibles; in which, gazing upon the light of the invisible and more-than-ineffable glory, they themselves too become receptive, with the powers above, of the blessed purity; after which, as the end of all, the communication of the mystery takes place, transforming into itself, and showing those who worthily partake to be like the good by cause, by grace and participation, lacking it in nothing, as far as is attainable and possible for men. So that they too can both be and be called gods by adoption according to grace, because of the whole God who has wholly filled them and left nothing of them empty of his presence.

That the first entrance of the holy synaxis is a symbol of the virtues of the soul.

Behold, then, thou who hast become a true lover of the blessed wisdom of Christ, with the eyes of the mind, at the first entrance of the holy synaxis, from the outward error and confusion of material things, according to what is written, Women coming from a spectacle, come hither Isa. xxvii, 11. — I mean the wandering in form and shape over the outward look of sensible things; for it is not true to call it contemplation, as do the unwise among those called wise by the Greeks (for let them never be called wise by us, who were not able, or not willing, to know God through his works) — the surface of sensible things, I say, by which the unceasing war of sensible things with one another has been constituted, working in all their corruption through one another, all corrupting one another and being corrupted in one another; and having this alone fixed, to be unstable and to be corrupted, and never to be able to concur with one another in a permanence without strife and without faction: behold the soul coming, and fleeing headlong, and as into a church and an inviolable palace of peace, into the natural contemplation in the spirit, which is without strife and free of all confusion, with the Word, and under the great Word and …

700… our true God and high priest, entering; and, as through symbols, through the divine readings that take place, being taught the principles of beings, and the wonderful and great mystery of the divine providence made plain in the law and the prophets; and receiving in each, for its good discipleship in these things, from God, through the holy powers who converse with it noetically in the understanding, the peace-bestowing intimations, together with the strengthening and preserving charm of the divine and fiery aspiration toward God, through the pleasure of the divine songs mystically and intelligibly sung to it.

Then again passing over from these, and being gathered toward the one and only summit, which comprehends these principles in a unified way: I mean the holy Gospel, in which all the principles both of providence and of beings pre-subsist uniformly according to one power of comprehension. After which it is again possible and lawful for the God-loving to see by divine sense, with fearless eyes of the mind, the Word and God coming to it from heaven, as the descent of the high priest from the priestly throne signifies; and him perfectly separating from it, after the manner of catechumens, the thoughts that still imagine sense and what is partible according to it. And hence, the soul having again come to be outside the sensible things, as the closing of the doors of the holy church of God suggests be understood, him leading it to the immaterial and simple and unalterable and God-formed science of the intelligibles, free from every form and shape, which is signified through the entrance of the ineffable mysteries; according to which, having gathered its own powers on its own side, it brings itself to the Word, having united through the noetic kiss the ineffable principles and modes of salvation that concern it, him thoroughly teaching it to confess them with thanksgiving through the Creed.

Accordingly the blessed elder thought it needful, and did not cease exhorting every Christian, to give his leisure to the holy Church of God, and never to absent himself from the holy synaxis performed in it: both because of the holy angels who remain in it, and who 704record each time those who enter and present them to God, and make supplications on their behalf; and because of the grace of the Holy Spirit, invisibly present always, but in a peculiar way especially at the time of the holy synaxis, which transforms and refashions each of those found there, and remoulds him, as something true, toward what is more divine, in proportion to himself, and leads him toward what is signified through the mysteries performed, even if he himself does not perceive it, if he is still among the infants in Christ, and is unable to see into the depth of the things taking place, and the grace signified through each of the divine symbols of salvation performed, working in him and proceeding in sequence and order from the nearest things to the end of all.

At the first entrance: the casting off of unbelief, the increase of faith, the diminution of vice, the advance of virtue, the abolition of ignorance, the addition of knowledge. Through the hearing of the divine oracles: the firm and immovable habits and dispositions of these things that have been named, I mean of faith and virtue and knowledge. Through the divine songs that follow these: the soul's voluntary assent to the virtues, and the noetic pleasure and delight that arises in it over them. Through the sacred reading of the holy Gospel: the consummation of the earthly way of thinking, as of a sensible world. Through the closing of the doors after these: the passage and transfer of the soul, by disposition, from this corruptible world to the intelligible world; through which, having shut the senses like doors, it renders them pure of the idols of sin. Through the entrance of the holy mysteries: the more perfect and more mystical and new teaching and knowledge concerning God's economy toward us. Through the divine kiss: the identity of concord and oneness of inclination and love of all toward all, and of each toward himself first, and toward God. Through the confession of the Creed: the fitting thanksgiving for the paradoxical modes of our salvation. Through the Trisagion: the union and equality of honour with the holy angels, and the harmonious intensity of the unceasing sanctifying doxology of God. Through the prayer, through which we are deemed worthy to call God Father: the truest adoption in the grace of the Holy Spirit. Through the One is Holy and what follows: the grace that makes one with God himself, and the intimacy toward him. Through the holy partaking of the undefiled and life-giving mysteries: the communion and identity with him by participation, possible through likeness; through which man is deemed worthy to become god from man.

For of the gifts of the Holy Spirit which here, in the present life, we believe we have partaken through the grace in faith, of these, in the age to come, in truth, enhypostatically, in the very reality, according to the unfailing hope of our faith and the firm and inviolable promise of him who 705promised, having kept the commandments according to our power, we believe we shall receive, passing from the grace in faith to the grace in sight; our God and Saviour Jesus Christ, that is, transforming us toward himself by the removal of the marks of corruption in us, and graciously granting us the archetypal mysteries shown forth to us through the sensible symbols here.

But for ease of remembering, if it seems good, let us run over the force of what has been said in summary, and so recapitulate it. The holy Church, then, is, as has been said, a type and image of God; because the unconfused union which he works, according to his infinite power and wisdom, among the different essences of beings, holding them together with himself at the summit as creator, [?] and by one faith and grace and calling joining the faithful to one another in a unified way; the practical and virtuous by one identity of inclination; and the contemplative and knowers, in addition to these, also by an unbroken and undivided concord.

And of the world, both the intelligible and the sensible, it is a type; as having the sanctuary as symbol of the intelligible world, and the nave of the sensible.

Again, it is an image of man, as imitating the soul through the sanctuary, and putting forward the body through the nave. And of the soul itself, understood by itself, it is a type and image, as bearing through the sanctuary the glory of the contemplative part, and having through the nave the orderliness of the practical.

Of the holy synaxis performed in it, the first entrance generally signifies the first coming of our God; specifically, the conversion, through him and with him, of those who are being brought in from unbelief to faith, and from vice to virtue, and from ignorance to knowledge.

The readings that take place after it generally make known the divine wills and purposes, according to which all must be educated and conduct their lives; specifically, the teaching and progress according to the faith of those who have believed; and of the practical, the firm disposition according to virtue, by which, walking in line with the divine law of the commandments, they stand manfully and unshaken against the wiles of the devil, and escape the opposing activities; and of the knowers, the habit according to contemplation, by which, gathering, so far as they can, the spiritual principles of the sensible things and of the 708providence over them, they are borne unerringly toward the truth.

The divine melodies of the songs: the divine pleasure and delight that arises in the souls of all; by which, mystically strengthened, they forget the past toils of virtue, and grow young again toward the vigorous aspiration for the divine and pure goods that remain.

The holy Gospel generally is a symbol of the consummation of this age; specifically, of those who have believed it signifies the complete abolition of the ancient error; of the practical, the mortification and consummation of the law and the way of thinking according to the flesh; of the knowers, the gathering and referral of the many and different principles to the most comprehensive principle, the more discursive and more varied natural contemplation having been completed and brought to its limit for them.

The descent of the high priest from the throne, and the expulsion of the catechumens, generally signifies the second coming from heaven of our great God and Saviour Jesus Christ, and the separation of the sinners from the saints, and the just requital according to the worth of each; specifically, of those who have believed, the perfect assurance in faith which God and the Word makes, coming invisibly, through which every thought still limping in any way concerning the faith, in the manner of a catechumen, is driven away from them; of the practical, perfect passionlessness, through which every passionate and unenlightened thought departs from the soul; of the knowers, the comprehensive science of the things known, through which all the images of material things are chased out of the soul.

The closing of the doors, and the entrance of the holy mysteries, and the divine kiss, and the proclamation of the Creed, generally signify the passing-by of the sensible things, and the manifestation of the intelligible things, and the new teaching of the divine mystery concerning us, and the identity of concord and oneness of inclination and love that will come to be of all toward all, toward themselves and toward God, and the thanksgiving for the modes by which we have been saved; specifically, of the faithful, the progress from simple faith to the teaching and initiation in doctrines, and to harmony of voice and piety; for the closing of the doors signifies the first, and the entrance of the holy things the second, and the kiss the third, and the proclamation of the Creed the fourth; of the practical, the transfer from practice to contemplation of those who have shut the senses and come to be outside flesh and world through the casting off of the

709activities according to them, and the ascent from the mode of the commandments to their principle; and the kindred intimacy and union of the commandments themselves, according to their own principles, with the powers of the soul, and the habit apt for theological thanksgiving; of the knowers, the passage from natural contemplation to the simple apprehension of the intelligibles, according to which they no longer pursue the divine and ineffable principle through sense or through anything that appears; and the union of its powers with the soul, and the simplicity which, according to the mind, comprehends in a unified way the principle of providence.

The unceasing sanctifying doxology of the Trisagion by the holy angels generally signifies the equality of conduct and way of life and harmony of the divine doxology, of the heavenly and earthly powers, which will come to be at once and in the same in the age to come, when the body of men has been made immortal through the resurrection, and no longer weighs down the soul with corruption, nor is weighed down, but through the change into incorruption has received both power and aptitude for the reception of the presence of God; specifically, of the faithful, the theological emulation of angels according to faith; of the practical, the angel-like brilliance in life, so far as is attainable to men, and the intensity of theological hymnody; of the knowers, the angel-like intellections and hymnings and ever-movings concerning Godhead, so far as is possible to men.

The blessed invocation of the great God and Father, and the proclamation of the One is Holy and what follows, and the partaking of the holy and life-giving mysteries, signify the adoption, union and intimacy, and divine likeness and deification, which will be, through the goodness of our God, upon all and over all the worthy; through which God himself will be all in all those who are saved, likewise; as archetypal beauty, according to cause, shining in those who, through virtue and knowledge, by grace, likewise shine together with him.

He used to call faithful and virtuous and knowers those who are being introduced, and those who are advancing, and the perfect, that is, slaves and hirelings and sons: the three orders of those who are saved.

Who then is so slow toward virtue and so hard to move as not to aspire to Godhead, when its acquisition is so cheap and easily procured and easy? And a safe and inviolable guard of these, and an easy road to salvation, without which, I think, none of the goods will in truth be preserved unharmed for him who has it, is the doing of one's own business, that is, the minding of one's own affairs; through which, learning to look at and examine our own affairs alone, we are freed from having harm in vain from others. For if we should learn to see and examine ourselves alone, we shall never set upon the affairs of others, however they may chance to be; knowing that the one only judge, wise and just, is God, who judges wisely and justly all that comes to pass, according to the principle by which it has come to be, but not according to the mode in which it has been manifested; which perhaps men too could judge, looking dimly at what appears, about which the truth does not altogether lie, nor the principle of the things that come to pass.

And to God, too, all that is offered genuinely from the soul according to power is dear, even if it appears small by comparison with great things; who did not even reject the widow who brought the two mites; Marc. xii, 41 seqq.; Luc. xxi, 2 seqq. whatever this widow was, and the two mites: whether a soul widowed of vice, and having cast off the old law as a husband, but not yet worthy of the utmost conjunction with the Word and God, yet bringing to him by way of pledge, 717like mites, the reason and life proportioned to her so far; or faith and a good conscience; or the habit and activity concerning good things; or the contemplation and practice fitting to these; or the proportionate knowledge and virtue; or those a little above these, I mean the principles in the natural and in the written law, which the soul, having acquired them, brings, in ecstasy from them and release from them as from her whole livelihood and life, wishing to be conjoined to the Word and God alone; and accepts to be widowed of the violent modes and ordinances and customs according to nature and law, as of husbands; or whether the word hints at something else more spiritual than these, and to be contemplated only by those pure in understanding, through the letter fulfilled according to history. For all things, compared with the beholding principle of theology, the things among men that seem to be great in virtue, turn out to be small. Save that, even if small, and of cheap material and not much prized, yet, equally with the gold coins of the more precious among materials, which the wealthier offer, they bear the royal stamp; and perhaps they have over and above [also] the intent of the woman who offers from her whole disposition.

Imitating this widow, I too have offered to God and to you, beloved, these small and cheap notions and words, brought forth from a cheap and poor understanding and tongue, like mites, concerning the things you commanded; beseeching your blessed and holy soul: first, no longer to seek a written record of anything said by me, for two reasons: one, that I have not yet acquired the fear of God that is pure and abiding, Psal. xviii, 10. nor a solid habit of virtue and a steady and unshaken fixity of true righteousness, the things that most of all bear witness to the firmness of words; the other, that, still tossed about like a wild sea by much surge of passions, and far from the divine harbour of passionlessness, and having the end of my life uncertain, I do not wish to have, in addition to my works, the word in writing too as my accuser. And then, accepting it, if it is fitting, for the sake of my obedience, commend me through prayers to Christ, the great and only God and Saviour of our souls; to whom be the glory and the might, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, unto the ages. Amen.

DRAFT (Wilson to read). Maximus the Confessor's interpretation of the church building and of the liturgy (written c. 628–630): the Mystagogia, as Migne prints it (PG 91.657–717), with a Latin version facing. Migne's notice before it (col. 653) reprints, from the Augsburg edition of the Mystagogia, the letters exchanged over it by the editor David Hoeschel and Maximus Margunius, bishop of Cythera. After a proem to a friend who had asked for it, twenty-four short chapters read the church as an image of God and of the world, of the human being and of the soul, and then follow the liturgy of the synaxis, the entrance, the readings, the kiss, the creed, the Sanctus and the communion, as the soul's passage from the visible to the intelligible.
