John Scottus Eriugena

Joannes Scotus Erigena

c. 800 – c. 877

Irish scholar and master at the court of Charles the Bald, and the one Latin writer of his century who read Greek theology in Greek. He translated the Pseudo-Dionysius, Maximus the Confessor and Gregory of Nyssa into Latin, and out of them built his own system in the Periphyseon. His treatise on predestination was condemned in his lifetime and the Periphyseon long after it. His commentary on the Gospel of John survives only in fragments, from a single manuscript at Laon.

In propria ergo venit, hoc est, in humana natura incarnatus est. Et sui eum non receperunt. Hoc dictum est de perfidis Judaeis et omnibus impiis, qui noluerunt Dei Verbum recipere, hoc est, neque in eum credere, neque eum intelligere voluerunt. Et ne quis putaret, omnes homines eum non recepisse, nam ab initio mundi nullum tempus erat, in quo receptores divini Verbi non essent, propterea addidit:

Quotquot autem receperunt eum, hoc est, quicumque crediderunt in eum, dedit eis potestatem filios Dei fieri. Non dixit: dedit eis potestatem salvari, 297Baut in pristinum statum et dignitatem humanae naturae dedit eis potestatem redeundi, sed, quod ineffabile est, et omni naturae per se ipsam impossibile, dedit eis per sublimitatem suae gratiae filios Dei fieri. His qui credunt in nomine ejus, hoc est, his, qui credunt, notitiam ejus Floss, var. lect. Cod. ei. et intelligentiam per fidem in hac vita, et per speciem in altera se recepturos.

Qui non ex sanguinibus, hoc est, qui non ex seminibus; neque ex voluntate carnis, hoc est, neque ex sexu femineo; caro quippe femineum sexum saepe significat; neque ex voluntate viri, hoc est, neque ex semine virili. Potest etiam sic intelligi neque ex voluntate carnis, ut expositio sit, quod sequitur, neque ex voluntate viri. Non enim desunt, qui irrationabilem 297Cmotum, quo homines concipiuntur in carne, soli carni attribuunt, quasi nihil ad animam pertineat, dum caro sine anima nihil in talibus praevaleat. Ideoque sequitur, neque ex voluntate viri, hoc est, neque ex voluntate totius hominis. Saepe sapientes viri vocabulo hominem solent appellare. Sed ex Deo nati sunt, per gratiam videlicet baptismatis, in quo incipiunt credentes in Christum ex Deo nasci. Sed ne quis impossibile existimaret, hominem mortalem, carnalem, fragilem, corruptibilem, in tantam gloriam exaltari, ut filius Dei fieret, veluti evangelista respirans, occultisque infidelium cogitationibus respondens, fortissimum posuit argumentum:

Et Verbum caro factum est. Ac si dixerit: Non 298Amireris, carnem, id est mortalem hominem, in filium Dei posse transire per gratiam, cum majoris miraculi sit Verbum caro factum. Nam si, quod superius est, ad inferius descendit, quid mirum, si, quod inferius est, in id, quod superius, superioris gratia agente ascendat, praesertim cum ad hoc Verbum caro factum sit, ut homo filius Dei fieret? Descendit enim Verbum in hominem, ut per ipsum ascenderet homo in Deum. Sicut enim dicimus: Verbum caro factum est, ita possumus dicere: et caro Verbum facta est. Et Verbum caro factum est, haec evangelica sententia proloquiorum more recurrit. Et habitavit in nobis, hoc est, conversatum est inter nos homines; Verbum habitavit in nobis, hoc est, naturam nostram possedit. Et vidimus gloriam ejus; nos videlicet, quos elegit 298Bde mundo, vidimus gloriam ejus in manifestissimis miraculis, in transfiguratione in monte spirituali, in claritate resurrectionis; et non aliam gloriam, sed gloriam unigeniti a Patre. Plenum gratiae, subauditur: plenum vidimus gratiae secundum humanitatem, et veritatis secundum deitatem.

Joannes testatur de ipso, Verbo videlicet, et clamat, vel sicut in Graeco legitur: et clamavit dicens: Hic erat quem dixi, vel, sicut in Graeco habetur: quem dicebam, quod multo significantius est. Nam si praeteritum perfectum, quod est dixi, poneret, peractum jam praedicationis ejus de Christo opus significaret; praeteritum vero imperfectum, quod est dicebam, et inchoationem praedicationis Christi ab Joanne significat, et adhuc in ipsa praedicatione perseverantiam. 298CHic erat; hic pronomen est demonstrativum praesentis personae; ac per hoc datur intelligi, adfuisse Christum in illo loco, in quo Joannes talem de eo demonstrationem aperte declaravit. Nec hoc mirum; saepe enim ipse Dominus, adhuc fere omnibus ignotus, priusquam baptizaretur, et praedicare inchoaret, ad Joannem solitus erat venire. Ideoque ait Joannes: Hic erat quem dixi. In hoc loco, sicut frequenter et in superioribus, verbum quod est erat, non significationem temporis, sed substantiae declarat, ita ut intelligamus hic erat, ac si aperte diceret hic est, hic subsistit, quem dicebam vobis. Ad quos dicebat? Ad discipulos profecto suos, quibus et absentem Christum praedicabat, et praesentem demonstrabat. 299AQuod in sequentibus manifestatur, ubi ait discipulis suis: Ecce Agnus Dei qui post me venturus est, vel, sicut in aliis codicibus scribitur: qui post me venit. Nam quod in Graeco scriptum est ἐρχόμενος , et praeteriti temporis participium est et futuri. Ante me factus est. Ac si aperte diceret: Qui post me ordine temporum in conceptione et nativitate venit in mundum, ante me factus est, mihi praelatus ordine dignitatis. Ego enim purus et simplex homo sum, ille plus quam homo, ex humanitate et divinitate compositus; ille Dominus, ego servus; ille rex, ego praecursor. Ideoque sequitur: Quia prior me erat, non solum aeternitate divinitatis, verum etiam humanitatis dignitate. Sed si quis intentus graecum sermonem inspexerit, alio modo intelliget quod ait: 299Bqui post me venturus est, hoc est, qui post me venturus foret, vel qui post me venit in mundum, ante me factus est, hoc, est coram me apparuit; et quemadmodum prophetavi et cognovi eum spiritualibus oculis, dum essem in utero, ita et nunc carnalibus oculis coram me factum, id est venientem conspicor. Quod enim in Graeco scriptum est ἔμπροσθέν μου , proprie interpretatur: coram me, hoc est, ante oculos meos. Et quid in eo perspicio, sive spiritualibus sive corporalibus oculis? Non aliud, nisi quia prior me erat, seu, ut significantius in Graeco, quia primus mei erat. Ad hoc itaque in praesentia mea factus est, ut cognoscerem illum primum esse, hoc est, in omnibus me praecedere. Praecedit enim me in plenitudine gratiae. Quidquid gratia possideo, non 299Caliunde, nisi ex plenitudine gratiae ipsius accipio. Ideo consequenter adjunxit:

Et de plenitudine ejus nos omnes accepimus. Quam plenitudinem dicit? Non aliam, nisi eam, de qua superius dixit: Et vidimus gloriam ejus, gloriam quasi unigeniti a Patre, plenum gratiae et veritatis. Plenitudo quippe gratiae secundum humanitatem, et plenitudo veritatis juxta divinitatem in Christo habitat. Unde ait Apostolus: in quo plenitudo divinitatis corporaliter, hoc est, veraciter habitat. Sed quomodo in Christo plenitudo gratiae secundum humanitatem habitat Floss, var. lect. Cod. possidet.? Non aliter, nisi quod ipse sit primum et maximum divinae gratiae exemplum, in eo quod homo Christus, nullis praecedentibus meritis, in unitatem substantiae, vel, ut usitatius dicam, personae 299Dassumptus est. Plenitudo autem veritatis in ipso naturaliter est, quia ipse est veritas, ipso testante: « Ego sum via, veritas et vita » . De plenitudine igitur ejus secundum humanitatem et divinitatem nos omnes accepimus. Nos omnes dicit, qui de Israelitico populo eum recepimus, et qui de ceteris totius mundi nationibus in eum crediderunt, gratiam accepimus, qua in eum credimus, et veritatem, qua ipsum intelligimus. Et hoc est quod sequitur: Gratiam pro gratia, ut subaudiatur: de plenitudine ejus accepimus gratiam pro gratia; gratiam videlicet contemplationis veritatis pro gratia fidei inhumanationis 300Aejus confessionis, hoc est, gratiam speciei pro gratia fidei, gratiam deificationis in futuro, pro gratia actionis et scientiae in praesenti. Et ne quis eorum, qui se gloriantur in lege, existimaret, se gratiam et veritatem ex lege accepisse, continuo subjecit:

Quia lex per Moysen data est. Ac si dixisset: Propterea dico ex plenitudine Christi nos omnes accepisse gratiam et veritatem, et non ex lege, quia lex tantummodo per Moysen data est, quae nullam gratiam accipientibus eam secundum litteram impertitur, quoniam nihil aliud est, nisi umbra quaedam et symbolum Novi Testamenti. Gratia et veritas per Jesum Christum facta est. Potest et sic intelligi: quia lex, dum consideratur in Moysi, id est, in littera nuda, 300Bsolummodo lex est; dum vero in Christo, gratia et veritas est. Spiritus enim legis gratia et veritas est in Christo Jesu. Lex itaque, considerata in Moysi, non est gratia et veritas, in Christo autem gratia et veritas est. Beatus Augustinus ita Vetus Testamentum discernit a Novo; ait enim: Lex data est, ut gratia quaereretur, gratia autem data est, ut lex impleretur. Tria itaque proponit: legem, gratiam, veritatem; tres ierarchias insinuans, unam quidem in Veteri Testamento, in obscurissimis aenigmatibus traditam; secundam, quam et mediam dicimus, in Novo Testamento, in quo abundantia gratiae, et eorum, quae mystice in lege et dicta et facta sunt, apertissima declaratio est; tertiam, caelestem dico, jam in hac vita inchoantem, et in altera vita perficiendam, 300Cin qua purae veritatis contemplatio in his, qui deificantur, absque ulla caligine donabitur. Prima itaque ierarchia legis nomine, secunda gratiae, tertia veritatis appellatione insinuatur. Deum nemo vidit unquam. Ad cumulum laudis plenitudinis Christi additur quod ait: Deum nemo vidit unquam, et quasi hac occasione divinae invisibilitatis omni beatitudine humana privaretur natura--ipsius namque contemplatio vera est beatitudo--si non succurrerit divina bonitas per incarnationem unigeniti Filii Dei, qui in carne, hoc est, in toto homine, quem accepit, non solum se ipsum aperuit, sed omnino antea incognitum 300DDeum Patrem hominibus manifestavit, sicut ait ipse: « Manifestavi nomen tuum hominibus, quos dedisti mihi » , et iterum Philippo quaerenti et dicenti, « Ostende nobis Patrem, et sufficit nobis » , respondetur: « Philippe, qui me videt, et patrem videt meum » ; et iterum: « Nemo vidit Filium nisi Pater, neque Patrem nisi Filius, et cui voluerit Filius revelare » . Nec solum hoc de hominibus, verum etiam de angelis intelligendum. Nam et angeli Deum suum, qui omnem intellectum exsuperat, in sua natura cognoscere non potuerunt, quia invisibilis et incognitus est, Verbo vero incarnato dominum 301Asuum intellexerunt, Dei videlicet Filium, et in ipso et per ipsum totam remotam ab omnibus Trinitatem. Universaliter itaque et rationali et intellectuali creaturae profuit Dei Verbi inhumanatio, rationali videlicet ad libertatem naturae ex morte et servitio diabolico, et ex ignorantia veritatis, intellectuali vero ad cognoscendam suam causam, quam prius ignorabat. Hinc Apostolus: « In quo restaurata sunt omnia, quae in caelo et in terra » . Sed non immerito quaeritur, cur Floss, var. lect. Cod. cur cum. in Veteri Testamento et in Novo frequenter legatur de Deo, hominibus apparuisse, sive visibiliter carnalibus oculis, sive invisibiliter propheticis visionibus, dum Evangelium ait: « Deum nemo vidit unquam » . Sed si de solo Patre diceretur, quod eum nemo vidit, facillime solveretur 301Bquaestio, ut persona Filii et Spiritus sancti saepe intelligantur visibiliter apparuisse, solum vero Patrem remotum ab omni visione. Quoniam vero non de una persona, sed de sola Trinitate, quae Deus unus est, hoc intelligitur dictum, « Deum nemo vidit unquam » , essentiam videlicet et substantiam unius Trinitatis, quoniam superat omnem intellectum rationalis et intellectualis creaturae, non immerito investigandum est, quod apparuit, dum dicitur Deus sive visibiliter sive invisibiliter apparuisse. Beatus Augustinus incunctanter aperit, Filium apparuisse in Veteri Testamento, non tamen in ea substantia, qua Patri aequalis est, sed in aliqua subjecta creatura, sive visibili sive invisibili. Similiter quando apparuisse Spiritus legitur, verbi gratia 301Cin specie columbae, non per se ipsum in ea substantia, qua Patri et Filio coessentialis est, sed in subjecta creatura est existimandum. Id ipsum de Patre intellige. Visiones etiam prophetarum, quibus prophetae Deum vidisse perhibentur, de subjecta quadam spirituali creatura factae sunt; substantiam vero divinam per se ipsam nullo modo eis apparuisse Dionysius incunctanter astruit, ne invisibilis et incomprehensibilis aliquo modo videri aut comprehendi posse existimetur. Quaeritur etiam, utrum hoc [dicatur de] hominibus solummodo adhuc in hac vita degentibus, non autem de virtutibus caelestibus, quae semper in divina contemplatione consistunt. Ad hoc dicendum, quia nullus humanae seu angelicae naturae particeps ipsum Deum per se ipsum in sua 301Dpropria natura potest contemplari. Quod enim in latino codice scriptum, « Deum nemo vidit » , in graeco « Deum nullus vidit » ; οὐδείς quippe et nemo et nullus interpretatur. Quid ergo sanctae animae hominum et sancti intellectus angelorum vident, dum Deum vident, si ipsum Deum non vident, quem videre perhibentur? Verbi gratia, ut pauca exempla de multis ponamus, Esaias vidit Dominum sedentem super solium excelsum. Dominus dicit in Evangelio: « Angeli eorum semper vident faciem Patris mei, qui in caelis est » . Item: « Qui diligit me, diligetur a Patre meo, et ego diligam eum, et manifestabo me 302Aipsum ei » . In epistola sua Joannes: « Scimus quia filii Dei sumus, sed nondum apparuit quid erimus. Scimus autem quia, cum ipse apparuerit, videbimus eum, sicuti est » . Paulus item: « Videmus nunc per speculum in aenigmate, tunc autem faciem ad faciem » , ceteraque id genus. Quid, inquam, vident homines et angeli, vel visuri sunt, dum apertissime et sanctus Ambrosius et Dionysius Areopagita absque ulla cunctatione inculcant, Deum, summam dico Trinitatem, nulli per se ipsam unquam apparuisse, nunquam apparere, nunquam apparituram? Apparebit itaque in theophaniis suis, hoc est divinis apparitionibus, in quibus juxta altitudinem puritatis et virtutis uniuscujusque Deus apparebit. Theophaniae autem sunt omnes creaturae 302Bvisibiles et invisibiles, per quas Deus, et in quibus saepe apparuit, et apparet, et appariturus est. Item virtute purgatissimarum animarum et intellectuum theophaniae sunt, et in eis quaerentibus et diligentibus se Deus manifestat, in quibus veluti quibusdam nubibus rapiuntur sancti obviam Christo, sicut ait Apostolus: « Rapiemur in nubibus obviam Christo » , nubes appellans altitudines clarissimas divinae theoriae, in qua semper cum Christo erunt. Hinc est, quod Dionysius ait: « Et si quis eum, Deum videlicet, vidisse dixerit, non eum vidit, sed aliquid ab eo factum. Ipse enim omnino invisibilis est, qui melius nesciendo scitur, et cujus ignorantia vera est sapientia » . Unigenitus Filius, qui est in sinu Patris; vel ut in Graeco scribitur: qui est 302Cin sinum Patris, vel in sinibus Patris. In quibusdam codicibus Graecorum singulariter sinus Patris dicitur, in quibusdam pluraliter, quasi sinus multos Pater habeat. Ipse enarravit. Unigenitus itaque Filius Deum narravit, hoc est, in se ipso manifestavit, non in quantum divinitas ejus, quae omnino invisibilis est, sed in quantum humanitas, quam, ut se ipsum et Patrem suum, necnon et Spiritum sanctum suum hominibus manifestaret et angelis, accepit. Nam humana anima, dum per se ipsam invisibilis sit, per motus suos corporeos, non quid sit, sed quia sit, manifestat. Et quid est, quod ait: « Qui est in sinu Patris » vel « in sinibus Patris » ? Utrum aliud est sinus Patris, aliud unigenitus Filius Patris? Non aliud, sed unigenitus Filius 302Dipse est sinus Patris. Sinus autem Patris dicitur Filius, quia Patrem insinuavit mundo. Sinus etiam Patris unigenitus Filius est, quia in secretis paternae naturae semper est, sicut ipse ait: « Ego in Patre, et Pater in me est » . Quemadmodum igitur domus Patris unigenitus Filius est, sicut ipse ait: « In domo Patris mei mansiones multae sunt » ; ac si diceret: In me, in quo Pater veluti in sua propria domo habitat, multae mansiones sunt; in ea enim unusquisque suam receptionem pro suis meritis et ipsius gratia possidebunt: ita et sinus Patris est Filius; in ipso enim recipit Pater, et colligit, quos vult 303Arecipere et colligere. Et hoc est testimonium Joannis. Ac si diceret: Hoc totum, quod diximus, ab eo loco « Joannes testimonium perhibet » usque ad id quod est, « ille enarravit » , testimonium Joannis est praecursoris de eo, cujus praecursor est. Deinde veluti ex alio primordio narrationis incipit, dicens: Quando miserunt Judaei ab Jerosolimis cet. Et est ordo verborum: Et confessus est, et non negavit, quando miserunt Judaei ab Jerosolimis sacerdotes et levitas, et reliqua. 303BQuid autem non negavit, et quid confessus est?

Quia non sum ego Christus. Putabant enim Judaei Joannem Baptistam Christum fuisse, quia in prophetis praedictum, quod in mundum venturus esset. Putabant quoque eum Heliam Thesbiten, propter nimiam sui abstinentiam, et castitatem, et solitariam vitam, et asperrimam delictorum reprehensionem, et durissimum vindictae futurae terrorem. Et praeter hoc putabant, eum, Joannem dico, unum aliquem ex prophetis iterum surrexisse, videntes maximam prophetiae gratiam in eo profecisse. « Non sum ego » , inquit, « Christus, quia Christi praecursor sum et vox clamantis in deserto » .

Helias es tu? Et dixit: Non sum. Haec negatio duobus modis intelligitur. Non sum, dixit, Helias, 303Csed in spiritu et virtute Heli veni. Item non sum Helias; ille enim solummodo propheta, ego vero non solum propheta, sed et praecursor. Non quod omnes prophetae praecursores Christi non sint, sed quod nullus prophetarum simul cum Christo uno eodemque anno natus est, qui fieret praecursor parvo spatio interposito. Propheta es tu? Respondit: Non. Item dupliciter intelligitur: aut, quia plus quam propheta est, sicut de eo Christus praedicat, ideo negat se prophetam esse, aut quod opinioni eorum respondit. Putabant enim, Joannem unum ex mortuis prophetis praeteriti temporis fuisse. Ait ergo: Non sum unus ex ipsis prophetis, de quorum numero existimatis me surrexisse, et praesentialiter vobis praedicare.

Dixerunt ergo ei: Quis es? Si Christus, seu Helias, 303Dseu aliquis prophetarum non es, dic, quis es, ne absque responso atque ulla cognitione de te ad eos, qui nos miserunt, redeamus, nullumque responsum eis reddere valeamus. Quid dicis de te ipso? Audivimus enim, te de Christo praedicasse, de te autem ipso nil manifestum asseris. Quid ergo dicis de te ipso?

Ego sum vox clamantis in deserto. Si quaeritis, inquit, quid de me ipso profero, cognoscite, quia ego sum vox. Non dixit, « quia ego sum homo » , 304Aseu « ego sum Joannes filius Zachariae » , sed dixit: « Ego sum vox » . Non enim in se humanam substantiam consideravit, nec humanam generationem, siquidem ultra haec omnia exaltatus est praecursor Verbi. Deseruit omnia, quae intra mundum continentur, ascendit in altum, factus est vox Verbi, ita ut nullam in se substantiam fateretur, praeter id quod extra omnem creaturam ex abundantia gratiae accepit, esse videlicet vox Verbi. Hoc autem nomen non a se ipso, sed longe ante ab Esaia propheta, imo a Spiritu sancto per Esaiam sibi est impositum. Itaque ego sum vox, non mea vox, sed clamantis vox; vox enim relative dicitur. Clamantis igitur Verbi, hoc est, per carnem praedicantis vox est Joannes. Et ubi clamat Verbum, cujus vox est 304BJoannes? In deserto. Omne enim, quod intra semetipsum prius animus et cogitat et ordinat invisibiliter, per vocem in sensus audientium sensibiliter profert. Animus itaque, id 304Cest, intellectus omnium, Dei Filius est. Knowing therefore that the Jordan bore the figure of baptism, and that Elias and Eliseus crossed it with dry feet, they did not doubt that the figure of baptism had gone before in Elias and Eliseus, or that those very men had risen 306Aand baptized. Penance cannot come to be except after the transgression of justice; human nature therefore, which transgressed the divine laws in paradise, under the person of John preaches and does penance, and manifestly declares the substantial justice of the Word of God, which goes before it through divinity and is to come after it through the dispensation of the flesh, so that it might recall it, human nature I mean, living according to the divine laws which 306Cby pride it had spurned, to the pristine state of eternal justice. Whose I am not worthy, and the rest. The shoe of the Word is his flesh, which he had assumed from the virgin, which is intimated through the figure of the shoe. For as a shoe is made from the hide of dead animals, so the flesh of Christ was made mortal for our sake, that by his death our death might be altogether destroyed. For the flesh of Christ was not made mortal by the desert of its own sin, as our flesh is, but it was passible by condescension to our nature after sin. For he did not refuse to undergo death for our sake, but the cause of death, sin I mean, he did not receive. And therefore death could not hold him, because he was not a debtor of death. Death could not hold as captive him whom it had not found guilty. 306DFrom the first man before sin, therefore, he received to be without sin; from the same after sin he received to be able to die; by which two he restored our whole nature in himself. But if thou seekest the difference of these three laws, of nature I mean, and the written, and of grace, know it briefly. The law of nature is that which commands all men to love one another equally, so that, just as one and the same nature is the same in all, so also there may be a common love of all by all. The written law is that which forbids the law of nature to be violated, and discerns between virtues and vices, and bears the figure of the law of grace. And I say that the written law itself is a help of the law of nature, so that, since the law of nature by itself alone was not able to correct human 309Bnature, it might by the force of the written law restrain what is to be forbidden, and teach what is to be fulfilled. For so far has the law of nature been almost abolished in men, that it neither knew its founder, nor could hold any difference between virtues and vices. The law of grace is that which teaches men not only to love one another, and discerns virtues and vices, but also, above these things, what is possible to divine grace alone, to die for men not only good but also evil, if it is necessary. Which law Christ fulfilled in himself, when he suffered not only for all men, but also for all the impious. Where John was baptizing. For it is one thing to take the arrow out of the wound, another afterwards to heal the wound. This, therefore, is the whole that the grace of baptism confers on us. For it frees us from the guilt of sin, 314Aand exalts us into the grace of divine sonship, so that, the guilt having been remitted, the wounds of our offences may begin to be healed. FRAGMENT II. CHAP. III, 1--IV, 28.

There was a man of the Pharisees, Nicodemus by name, a ruler of the Jews. Pharisee is interpreted divided; Pharisees, divided ones St Jerome: Pherez(?)i, separating, or scattered, or bearing fruit. For that name is derived from the root (Hebrew), to separate, that is, to divide.. For their sect was divided from the rest of the Jews, that is, from the Scribes and Sadducees. For the Pharisees alone believed in the resurrection of the dead, the rest contending against it. Nicodemus 314Bis interpreted victory of the people, or victor people, bearing the figure of all who from the Jewish people believed in Christ. And everyone who believes in Christ conquers the world. A ruler of the Jews, that is, one of the rulers of the Jews, learned in the letter of the law, a master of Israel.

This man came to Jesus by night. According to Augustine, Nicodemus is set in the figure of the catechumens. For catechumens, that is, those instructed in the faith, yet not yet baptized, nor imbued with the sacrament of the body and blood of the Lord, having faith alone, come to Christ; still ignorant of the power of baptism, which they do not yet receive, they approach as in a kind of night of ignorance of the sacraments. But according to Gregory the Theologian, Nicodemus is taken as a kind of 314Cnight disciple, conforming those who believe most perfectly in Christ, so that nothing of the integrity of the catholic faith is hidden from them, yet they lack the light of perfect works; fearing the assault and envy of their carnal thoughts and actions, as of the faithless Jews, they enjoy converse with Christ by faith alone, not having the confidence of good works. Therefore the Evangelist says: « This man came to him by night » . And said to him: Rabbi, we know that thou art come a master from God; for no man can do these signs which thou dost, unless God be with him. From these words, with which Nicodemus is seen to have addressed Christ, it is given to understand that he himself, Nicodemus I mean, was one of those who had believed a little before, on the day of the Pasch, of whom it was said: « Now when he was 314Dat Jerusalem at the Pasch, upon the festival day, many believed in his name, seeing his signs which he did » . For to this he looks back when he says: « No man can do these signs which thou dost, unless God be with him »

Jesus answered, and said to him: Amen, amen I say to thee, unless a man be born anew [Vulg. renatus, "born again"], he cannot see the kingdom 315Aof God. It is asked: why did Jesus answer Nicodemus about the second nativity, which is according to the spirit, when he himself seems to have asked nothing about it? But it must be answered: since the Lord saw that he had believed, as the rest also believed who saw his signs, and had supposed that faith alone sufficed them, without the power and sacraments of spiritual generation, he at once began to teach him about the nativity which is according to the spirit. As if he openly said to him: It does not suffice thee only to have believed in me, unless thou receive the sacraments of baptism and understand the power of spiritual generation. It is to be noted that in the codices of the Greeks ἄνωθεν is read, where in the Latin codices denuo, "anew," is found, so that the sense is: unless a man be born ἄνωθεν , that is, from above, 315Bso that we say "from above" for "anew"; which is both more easily understood, and more fitting to the two nativities, namely the earthly and the heavenly. For the Apostle says: « The first man of the earth, earthly; the second man from heaven, heavenly » . For there are two nativities, as Augustine says; of which one is from the earth, the other from heaven, that is, from above; one from parents, according to the flesh, the other from Christ and the Church, according to the spirit; one in the likeness of beasts of burden, through sin, unto death, the other in the likeness of the heavenly powers, through grace, unto life; one against nature through offence, the other according to nature through the remedy of offences. But Gregory the Theologian establishes four nativities, which our Lord Jesus Christ also deigned to undergo for the salvation 315Cof human nature. Of which the first is that nativity in which the whole human race was born together from nothing; of which it is written: « And God made man to his image and likeness » . The second, which, following upon the offence of man, is from both sexes, after the likeness of the other animals, of which the same Scripture says: « Male and female he made them » ; through which the whole human race is multiplied without limit by earthly succession. The third, which is according to the spirit, of which the Lord now says: « Unless a man be born anew » ; in which nativity human nature begins to return to its former seat, from which it fell. The fourth will be in the resurrection of all, when our whole nature will be born together, 315Ddeath having been slain, into life eternal. The first therefore is natural, the second on account of sin, the third through the grace of the Redeemer, the fourth according to nature and grace together. For the power of resurrection is naturally in human nature, since eternal death is altogether contrary to it. But what nature contributes to the resurrection, and what grace, is another place for discussion. He cannot see the kingdom of God. Of the kingdom of God the understanding is manifold. The kingdom of God is the church of the faithful, of which it is written: « The kingdom of God is among you » . The kingdom of God is the universe of corporeal and spiritual creation. Yet he said truly that no one can be born twice 316Baccording to the flesh, just as no one can be born twice according to the spirit. Unless a man be born of water and the spirit, that is, of the visible sacrament and the invisible understanding. It is asked for what reason the Lord established that the visible sacrament of baptism be done in water, since spiritual teaching alone through faith seems to suffice for the nativity of man from the spirit. But this also must be said: since man is constituted of a visible body and an invisible soul, a visible sacrament was necessary for the purification of the visible body, just as the invisible teaching of faith is necessary for the sanctification of the invisible soul. And no man hath ascended into heaven, but he who descended from heaven, the Son of man, who is in heaven. Hath ascended: it is ambiguous of what tense the verb is, whether past or present. But in the Greek it is not ambiguous; it is of past time. In this sentence it is asked: what is the descent of the Son of man, and what the ascent; and what heaven is it into which he ascended, and from which he descended, and in which he always remains, while he ascends and descends? But lest we make delays by telling the senses of many on this, it is enough to say what seems more likely. In Holy Scripture the heavenly Father, who is the principle of all things, is frequently named by the name of heaven. 319COften it is read in the Gospel that the Lord rose into heaven, that is, into the Father. For it is not to be believed that he raised his eyes to this starry, corporeal, circumscribed, mortal heaven. Into the Father therefore he raised his eyes, so that those who beheld him might believe without doubt that he had always been in the Father, toward whom he was gazing. What therefore he says, no man hath ascended into heaven, but he who descended from heaven, the son of man, who is in heaven, is beautifully expounded in other words: no man hath ascended into the Father, but he who descended from the Father, the son of man, who is in the Father. But of what kind his descent and ascent are, he himself expounded openly elsewhere, saying: « I came forth from the Father and am come into the world, and again I leave the world and go to the Father » . His going forth from the Father, therefore, is humanation, and his return to the Father 319Dis the deification of the man whom he received, and his assumption into the height of divinity. He alone descended, because he alone was incarnate; but, if he alone ascends, what hope is there for those for whom he descended? Great indeed and inexplicable, since all whom he saved ascend in him, now through faith in hope, but at the end through sight in reality, as John says in his Epistle: « We know that we are sons of God; it hath not yet appeared what we shall be; but when he shall appear, we shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is » . He alone therefore descends, and he alone ascends, because he with all his members is one God, the only Son of God. For in him all who believe in him are one; one Christ therefore, the body with the members, ascends into the Father.

320AAnd as Moses lifted up the serpent in the desert, so must the Son of man be lifted up, that everyone believing in him may not perish, but may have life eternal. The truth itself expounds its own figure; the body itself lays claim to its own shadow. The divine history relates that the people of God was afflicted by serpents in the desert, and that there was a great slaughter from their frequent bites; but Moses, at the Lord's command, had made a brazen serpent, which he had hung on a high tree, and had commanded the people that whoever had been bitten by the dragons should look up at the brazen snake, and be healed, and be freed from the serpent's wound. All this is the shadow of what was to come, that is, the figure of Christ who was to die for the salvation of all whom the serpents of their offences slay with venomous bites. Moses therefore is Christ himself; the brazen 320Bserpent, the death of Christ: the wood on which the serpent was hung, the cross of Christ, on which he underwent death for the salvation of all who believe in him. Beautifully also is the death of Christ figured by the serpent, by the form in which the cause is put for the effect. For the serpent was the cause of the death of Christ. And through this the serpent also figuratively held forth its effect, that is, death, that everyone who believes in him may not perish, but may have life eternal. Great is the distance between the letter and the spirit, between the figure and the truth, between the shadow and the body. The letter is the deed which holy history relates; the spirit is the death of Christ, which the Gospel hands down; the serpent is figured hung on the wood; to those who looked upon it, it afforded temporal life; the death of Christ affords eternal life to those who believe in him. 320CBut what have these things to do with the second nativity from the spirit, it is not undeservedly asked? No one ascends in Christ to the Father but he who is born of the spirit, that he may be made conformable to the image of the Son of God, that is, that Christ may be formed in him, and he be one with Christ. He it is of whom I bore testimony, saying: « Of his fullness we all 325Bhave received. » I am therefore a man; from myself I have received nothing, who have nothing to receive from myself. Whatever I have received, I have received from him. If I have received the grace of a forerunner, I have received it from him. You suppose that I am beyond human nature. Because you have heard that I was born not without a miracle of divine power, namely of parents of advanced age, and was born as it were beyond the custom of human nature, on this account you think that I am beyond man. Not so; I am only a man. If you had known what I am, you would not exalt me beyond what I am; and if you knew what he is, in no way would you compare me to him. For I am man, he God; whatever I have received, I have received from him. The most wise forerunner of Christ separates nature and grace in himself, attributing to himself that he is man, that is, that he is 325Cmortal, that he is a sinner, that from the first earthly man he was made earthly, and the other things which he considered in himself and in other men concerning the infirmity of human nature and sin and the penalty of sin, setting apart from himself the grace which he had received not from merits, but from the abundance of the fullness of him whose forerunner he was. Two things are considered in man: namely the given and the gift. The given is referred to nature, the gift is referred to grace. And although none of these is except from God, as the Apostle says: « O man, what hast thou that thou hast not received » ? yet Scripture is accustomed to ascribe the given of nature as it were to man himself, but the gift to none but God. For to this end God gave nature, that he might adorn it through grace. And this is what he says: A man cannot, that is, human 325Dnature, receive anything of grace, unless it be given him from heaven. John has put given in this place for gift, which is very often found in Holy Scripture. For given can be taken for gift, and gift for given. For the givens of nature and the gifts of grace are so joined that they seem to become inseparable. For there is no rational nature which is wholly without the gift of grace. Unless it be given from heaven. From what heaven? From the Father, namely. Hear James the apostle saying: « Every best given and every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights. » With which words what John says wholly agrees: 326AA man cannot receive anything, unless it be given him from heaven, that is, from the Father, who is the principle of all good things; and, if from the Father, surely first through the Son, of whose fullness we all have received.

You yourselves bear me witness. This alone in your words agrees with me, you who not only do not believe in him who sent me, but even think his baptism void, that you bring testimony for me. That I said: I am not the Christ, but that I am sent before him. You heard these words from me when I was speaking of him beyond the Jordan; you heard them surely, since you say: « He that was with thee beyond the Jordan, to whom thou gavest testimony » ; and if you heard, why do you accuse to me him of whom I bore such testimony, as if 326Bto my injury he had baptized many? I said: I am not the Christ, but a simple man; I am not anointed by the Father with the fullness of the spirit, but he is anointed; and of his fullness I have received. But that I am sent before him, I was sent into the world first, by being born before him. He, before all ages, God with God the Father; I, a man sent in the flesh at the end of the age, before he was born in the flesh.

He that hath the bride is the bridegroom. The forerunner, seeing the thoughts of his disciples, who were putting forth such things about Christ and his baptism, and were hearing their master's answer concerning himself and his baptism, saying: « A man cannot receive anything » , humbling himself, and truly pronouncing concerning himself that he is an earthly man, and they wished 326Cto ask: If thou art only a man, and hast not greater power and grace from thyself, besides what thou hast received from him who now baptizes in Judaea, and thou sayest that he is sent before him, tell us, we beseech thee, what thou art besides a man, or how great, and what grace thou hast received from heaven; he answered: He that hath the bride is the bridegroom. He who came for the sake of the Church came that he might have a bride, whom he now begins to call from the Jewish people, baptizing many of the Jews. He is the bridegroom. But I, as you suppose, am neither the bridegroom, nor have the bride, but I count myself in the number of the bride. But that you may know what I am, and what grace I have from that bridegroom who has the bride, hear: But the friend of the bridegroom. I am not the bridegroom, but I have great grace of the bridegroom, 326DI am his friend. Who standeth and heareth him, rejoiceth with joy. I am the friend of the bridegroom, and I stand in his grace; I abide in his friendship; I do not fall; he guards me, lest I fall; he grants me not to desert his grace, but always to stand and to hear him, to hear his teaching, not only outwardly with the ear of the body, but also inwardly with the ear of the heart, and more inwardly than outwardly. His voice is his testimony, by which he declares himself the Son of God and the bridegroom of the Church; and because I hear his word, I rejoice because of the bridegroom's voice. Of himself therefore John utters what he says: But the friend of the bridegroom, who standeth and heareth with, rejoiceth with joy 327Abecause of the bridegroom's voice, that his disciples might openly know what he himself was, and what Christ was, in whom they had not yet believed, nor received his baptism. This my joy therefore is fulfilled. As if he said: In this my joy is fulfilled, in this, that I have been made the friend of the bridegroom, and stand in his grace, and hear the voice of the bridegroom, now calling the bride in Judaea out of the Jews, and beginning by himself both to preach and to baptize; which you do not now perceive, who do not believe in him. And do not wonder that he baptizes, and all come to him, that they may believe and be baptized, and that he has more disciples than I:

He must increase, but I must decrease. He must increase in your minds, whom 327Byou still suppose to be only a man and of no power; but he must increase, that you may know him to be not only true man, but also true God, and equal to his Father. He must therefore increase in your thoughts, but I decrease. You think [me] greater than I am, but him less than he is. Your opinion of me will be diminished in you, your opinion of him will be increased in you. Which if you knew, you could have been increased from visible signs. I shall be beheaded, he will be exalted on the cross, that through this you may know that he must increase above all things in the minds of those who believe perfectly in him, but I decrease, that no one may think of me beyond what I am and have received from him. The very same is signified by this, that he was born with the light beginning to increase, 327Cbut the darkness to decrease; but I was born with the light beginning to be diminished, but the darkness beginning to increase. It can also be understood otherwise. John bears the figure of the law, but Christ his own figure, that is, of the truth. The truth therefore appearing in the world, the law began to decrease, that is, carnal works. Knowing these things, the forerunner and prophet and more than a prophet says: He must increase, but I must decrease. He, because he is the truth, will increase in the world, that is, will be most clearly known by those who believe and who will believe in him. But the law, whose figure I bear, will decrease, as the truth increases.

He that cometh from above is above all. Again the servant returns to the comparison of himself and his Lord. He that cometh from above, that is, he who came from the Father into 327Dthe world, that he might save the world, is above all, not only surpasses me, his servant and forerunner, but also all men, and not only men, but also all the heavenly powers, and all things which are, and which are not. Nor is this according to divinity, but according to humanity; for he is man and more than man. His divinity surpasses nothing, because it is all things. He that is of the earth is earth, and speaketh of the earth. [Vulg. de terra est, "is of the earth"] Of himself he says this, as if he said: I, who am of the earth, that is, of earthly parents, born of him to whom it was said, « Earth thou art, and into earth thou shalt go » [Vulg. pulvis es et in pulverem reverteris, "dust thou art, and unto dust thou shalt return"], that is, of the earthly man himself, from whom 328Aall the earthly drew sin and the penalty of sin, that is, death, from thence am I. And therefore I am earth, because I was born of the earth, nor do I judge myself to be anything else. If I have anything above these things, I receive it from the fullness of him before whom I was sent. And speaketh of the earth, that is, I speak of the frailty of my nature, knowing it to be nothing, if it did not receive from above. And do not wonder that John spoke of himself as of a certain third person, nor without reason, since he shows in himself the person of every earthly man. He that cometh from heaven is above all. The words are the same, except that before it was cometh from above, but now cometh from heaven. And what is this repetition, and what does it mean? Or perhaps « cometh from above » is one thing, « cometh from heaven » another? Christ cometh from above, Christ 328Bcometh from heaven, and therefore is above all men, and above all things. Such a distinction, therefore, can be understood between « cometh from above » and « cometh from heaven » , that in what he said, « cometh from above » , we understand the height of human nature before the offence of the first man, from which height we do not doubt that Christ came as man. For if he came from man after sin, perhaps he would not be without sin; but since he was without sin, he is rightly to be believed to have received man before sin. For our Lord Jesus Christ, the founder and saviour of human nature, received both from the first man before sin, and as it were from a second man after sin. What did he receive from man before sin? To be without sin, because human nature, before it sinned, 328Cwas wholly without sin. What did he receive from man after sin? To die for sin, without any guilt of sin. What he said first, therefore, « he that cometh from above » , is rightly understood as he who came from the sublimity of human nature, made after the image of God, before it sinned. For no one came from there, except he himself alone. For as many as have come into the world have come from sinning man; he alone, without sin, proceeded from man before sin. But what he said in what follows, he that cometh from heaven is above all, there we understand the Father signified by the name of heaven, by whom Christ was sent into the world, as he himself says: « I came forth from the Father, and am come into the world » . In two ways, therefore, the only-begotten Son of God is to be believed to be above all things: first, because he is exalted above all humanity, 328Dwho came from it before it sinned; second, because according to the height of divinity, in which he is equal to the Father, he surpasses every creature.

And what he hath seen and heard, that he testifieth. What is it that the Son of God saw? Nothing other than that he knew himself to be in the Father, and to be his equality. And heard. What did he hear? He heard from the Father: « This day have I begotten thee » . He saw himself, therefore, according to his divinity in his Father, and heard from his Father that he had begotten him equal to himself. That he testifieth. What does he testify? That he is the Son of God. And where does he testify it? Go back to what is above, where he himself says of himself: « For God sent not his Son 329Ainto the world to judge the world, but that the world may be saved by him. He that believeth in him is not judged. But he that believeth not is already judged, because he believeth not in the name of the only-begotten Son of God » . This, therefore, is what the Son of God saw, and testifies of himself. And no man receiveth his testimony. If no man receives his testimony, why did he come, and what usefulness did he confer on the world? John the forerunner, full of the grace of divine prophecy, foresaw those who would believe in Christ, receiving his testimony, and those who would not believe in him, rejecting his testimony. Of those who do not believe nor will believe, prepared by themselves [for] destruction, no one received the testimony of the Son of God. But of those who believe and will believe, there is no one who does not receive his testimony, 329Band through this, who is not saved. And therefore there follows:

He that receiveth his testimony hath sealed that God is truthful, that is, he has made it firm in his heart, and as with an unchangeable seal of perfect faith has concluded, that God is truthful. Which is more easily understood if the Greek speech is read: That God is true, so that the sense is: He who receives the testimony of the Son of God concerning himself, that he is the Son of God, and that his Father sent him for the salvation of the world, firmly believes that God is true, who bore testimony of himself that he is true God, the Son of the true God.

For he whom God hath sent speaketh the words of God. This he says of the Son of God only, who, since he was sent by God the Father, speaks the words of God. Were not the prophets also sent by him? Were not the apostles also sent by him, 329Cand did they not speak the words of God? Why then was it said of Christ alone: He whom God hath sent speaketh the words of God? For he seems to have said: He whom God sent speaks the words of that God who sent him; which the prophets did, and likewise the apostles. What great thing then is given to Christ, which was not given to others? Attend to the words: He whom God hath sent, the Father namely, speaketh the words of God; since he too who was sent is God, therefore he speaks the words of God, so that the sense is: He whom God sent is surely God, and through this his words are the words of God; therefore the words of the Son concerning himself, bearing testimony that he is God, are the words of God. The words of the prophets and apostles are the words of God, because they spoke the words of God. For the Holy Spirit spoke in them, and yet those words are not referred to 329Dthem, so as to be their words, but the words of the true God. The words of Christ are referred to himself, and are deservedly called the words of God, because he is God by nature, and wholly God, and wholly wisdom, and in him is the fullness of the gifts of God. Therefore there follows: For God giveth not the spirit by measure. As if he had said: God gives his spirit by measure to individual men, as the Apostle says: « To one is given through the Spirit the word of wisdom, to another the word of knowledge » , and the rest of the division of spiritual gifts. By which words it is shown that the Holy Spirit is given by measure to the members of the Church. Which the Apostle says consequently, saying: « But one and the same Spirit worketh, 330Adividing to each severally his own, as he will » . But to Christ, who is the head of the Church, God gives the spirit not by measure, but just as he begot his Son whole from his whole self, so to his incarnate Son he gave his whole spirit, not by participation, not by subdivisions, but generally and universally.

The Father loveth the Son and hath given all things into his hand. He renders the cause why God gives the spirit by measure to other men, but to his Son not by measure, but gives the spirit from the whole. Why? For no other reason but because the Father loveth the Son, therefore he gave him his whole spirit, and gave all things into his hand. What all things? Perhaps all things that were made in him? For in the beginning God made heaven and earth; and the psalm, 330B« In wisdom thou hast made all things » . All those things therefore which were made in the Son, the Father gave into his hand. More deeply, hath given all things into his hand, that is, all things which the Father has substantially he gave to the Son, by begetting him from himself equal to himself in all things.

He that believeth in the Son hath life everlasting. Why? Because the Son himself is life everlasting, as he himself says: « I am the truth and the life » . Rightly therefore he that believeth in the Son hath life everlasting. As if he had said: He who believes in the Son has the Son himself, and through this has life everlasting, because the Son himself is life everlasting. But he that believeth not in the Son shall not see life everlasting. By the same kind of locution: but he who believes not the Son shall not see the Son, who is life. But the wrath of God abideth upon him. He does not say: the wrath of God will come 330Cupon him, but he says: abideth upon him. What is that wrath of God? Nothing else but that curse by which God cursed the sin of the first man, who transgressed his commandment, saying: « Cursed is the earth in thy works » . The effect of that wrath is death, and corruption, and altogether ignorance of the truth, all which abide upon him. Of that wrath the Apostle says: « You were once children of wrath, even as the rest » . The wrath of God can be understood as original sin, since that original sin provoked the wrath of God against human nature; which original sin remains in those who will not believe in him who died for them.

When therefore Jesus knew that the Pharisees had heard that Jesus maketh and baptizeth more disciples than John, although Jesus himself baptized not, but his 330Ddisciples. [Vulg. quamquam Jesus non baptizaret, "though Jesus did not baptize"] Christ baptized and did not baptize. He baptized, that is, he cleansed in the spirit those whom not he himself, but his disciples dipped with water in the body.

He left Judaea, and went again into Galilee. After the Lord, to whom all hearts are known, knew that the Pharisees had heard that more disciples had come to him than to John, and that he had baptized more than John, he left Judaea. For the Pharisees, moved by envy against Christ and his disciples, seeing the baptism of John grow a little cheap, since the disciples of John were mostly from the Pharisees, as then also those of Christ, 331Abut the baptism of Christ growing, and the number of his disciples, and moreover also knowing that through the doctrine of Christ the law was being made void, and the ceremonies of the Old Testament, which they had not been able to know in the doctrine of John, disturbed therefore by such zeal, were thinking to persecute Christ and his disciples and all who believed in him. On this occasion Jesus left Judaea, that is, the unbelieving Pharisees, who were thinking to persecute him. But this he did not as one fearful or powerless; for he could have remained in Judaea unharmed by the persecution of the carnal and impious Pharisees; but that he might leave an example to those who would believe in him, giving them by his example the power of fleeing from the face of those who persecute them, and of deserting them for a time, lest before the time pleasing to God and foreordained they should be 331Bseized by them; and through this he would have remained longer in Judaea, if he had known that those whom he left would believe. But according to the laws of theoria Jesus left Judaea after he had taken up from the Judaic people the beginning of the Church in those who believed in him; he left the carnal and faithless Jews, who follow the letter of the law and are unwilling to approach the spirit of that letter, which is Christ. The Lord therefore tarried in Judaea, that he might save whom he would; he left Judaea, deserting those whom, by the desert of their pride, he neglected to save. He deserted the letter of the law, which leads no one to the perfect; for the law led no one to the perfect; and from them he again [went away] into Galilee, that is, into the spiritual understanding of that letter, which is symbolically figured through Galilee. Nor is it idle that he says: 331CHe went again into Galilee. For from Galilee he came into Judaea, as was written above: « After these things came Jesus and his disciples into the land of Judaea » . Galilee bears the figure of the heavenly life and of divine goodness, from which the Lord, being born in the flesh, came into into Judaea, that is, into the sacraments of that law which was first given to the Jews, as the Apostle says: « But when the fullness of times came, God sent his Son, made of a woman, made under the law » . But since he would not remain under the law, nor those who were to believe in him, he left the law, that they might not be under the law, but under the spirit. And this is what the Apostle says, « that he might redeem those who were under the law » , that is, recall them from the servitude of the carnal law into the liberty of the spirit. And this is: he went into Galilee.

331DBut it behoved to pass through Samaria. Between Judaea and Galilee Samaria is set, that is, between the law of the letter, which is signified by Judaea, and the eternal divine laws revolving about God, of which Galilee bears the figure, the natural law is set in the middle. Since therefore Christ first took the foundations of his Church from the law of the letter, 332Ait behoved him to pass through the nations set under the law of nature, that from them likewise he might call stones chosen through faith into the structure which he had begun. For Samaria was possessed by foreigners, that is, by those who used, or abused, both the legal letter, which [was] imposed on carnal Israel alone, and the natural law, so that with the universal Church, gathered and built both from the Jews and from the Gentiles, the builder himself might return into the Galilee of eternal beatitude. But that Galilee signifies eternal life can be conjectured not only from its interpretation, but also from the divine deeds which were accomplished in it. For Galilee is interpreted transmigration accomplished or revolution Saint Jerome: Galilee, that may be rolled, or transmigration accomplished, or wheel. J. Scotus rightly derives the word from (Hebrew), to roll; the interpretation transmigration looks to (Hebrew), Gilgal, Josua V, 9.; but the transmigration of human nature was accomplished in its Saviour, first from the carnal law into the natural laws, 332Band from these again beyond nature into the eternal laws. In Galilee also, according to the faith of the things done, the transformation of Christ took place, by which is signified the transmutation of human nature into the pristine glory which it deserted by sinning. In Galilee he went up into the mountain, on which he delivered the eightfold doctrine to his disciples, through which those who believe in Christ will pass over to the beatitude of the future [life], which is intimated by the number eight.

But Jesus came into a city of Samaria, which is called Sichar, near the estate which Jacob gave to his son Joseph. Samaria, as we said before, is the type of the Gentile people set under the natural law; the city of Samaria, which was called Sichar, [the type] of the gathering of the faithful of that people receiving the faith of Christ. For Sichar 332Cis interpreted enclosure or branch Thus saint Jerome: Sichar, enclosure or branch.. The people of the Gentiles was enclosed. Scripture hath enclosed all things under sin. He is the branch cut out of the wild olive and grafted into the olive whose branches were broken. It is to be noted that Sichar is put corruptly for Sychem. Sychem is interpreted numbers Saint Jerome: It is corruptly for Sichem, which is rendered "shoulders"; usage has obtained that Sichar should be read. J. Scotus seems to have read numeri, numbers, for humeri, shoulders; but the interpretation numbers is not accurate enough; for Sichem is derived from (Hebrew).: an investigation awaits the chosen numbered from among the Gentiles. And since natural reason itself rises from nowhere else but from the cause of all good things, from God namely, it is beautifully called Jacob's fountain, that is, infinite reason, proceeding from the height of the Father. Jacob's fountain also not unfittingly signifies this lowest part of the universe, that is, all visible things confined within the bounds of this sensible world. Briefly, therefore, it is to be gathered: Jacob's fountain is the symbol either of sensible nature or of intelligible reason.

332DJesus therefore, wearied with the journey, sat thus upon the fountain. The weariness of Jesus is his incarnation. For he received our nature, wearied, by the desert of original sin, with the labours and hardships of this world. His journey, the descent of his divinity to take up the likeness of our nature. Without labour he created us through divinity, with labour he created us through 333Ahumanity. Remaining eternally and immutably in himself and in his Father, he moved himself as by a certain journey of temporal dispensation through the flesh. He sat upon the fountain. The sitting of Christ, the possession of his incommutable divinity in our nature. But our nature is twofold: for it consists of a visible body and an invisible soul. Which two the twofold interpretation of the fountain itself indicates. For it was said before that the fountain figured both the rational soul and the visible creature. But since the humanity of Christ surpasses not only every visible but also every intellectual creature, for he is called man and more than man, he is fittingly related to have sat upon the fountain, although wearied from the journey. For the infirmity of his humanity is stronger than every creature, deservedly, 333Bbecause assumed into God. It was about the sixth hour. The sixth hour prefigures the sixth age of the world. Whose first age is reckoned from the expulsion of the first man from paradise up to the altar which Noe, going out of the ark after the flood, had built. The second, thence up to the altar on which Abraham [was] commanded by God to sacrifice Isaac. The third, thence to the altar of king David on the threshing-floor of Ornan the Jebusite. Then the fourth, up to the altar of Zorobabel in the rebuilt temple. Then the fifth, up to the baptism of John, or, as seems to many not unreasonably, up to the true altar, that is, up to the cross of Christ, which all the aforesaid altars typified. From here the sixth age extends to the end of the world; it is now in course. For the seventh age is perfected in the other life, in souls released from the body; 333Cwhich age begins with the martyrdom of Abel, and will be ended at the end of the world by the resurrection of all. After which the eighth will begin to appear, which will be circumscribed by no end. The perfect brightness of heavenly grace, which, Christ being inhumanated, shone most fully upon the world, can also be understood as typically figured in the sixth hour. For the number six is perfect.

A woman came out of Samaria to draw water. Samaria, as we said before, suggests the figure of the Gentiles. The woman gone out of Samaria is the Church, gathered from those very Gentiles, which, having received the faith of truth, desires to draw its fountain, that is, Christ. Likewise the woman gone out of the city indicates human nature, which naturally seeks the fountain of reason, whence it may be able to satisfy its thirst, that is, the appetite for true knowledge implanted in it; 333Dwhich before the incarnation of its maker, who is the fountain of life, it had not been able to fulfil. Yet it drank laboriously from the natural fountain of reason implanted in it, tracking the nature of things by physical motion, and the creator and cause of that nature. Jesus saith to her: Give me to drink. For his disciples were gone into the city, to buy meats. Jesus, sitting upon the fountain, asks of the primitive Church, which he had chosen from the Gentiles, the drink of faith, by which he is believed in. He asks of nature the drink of reason, by which its maker and redeemer is searched out. The disciples going away into the city to buy meats are the apostles sent into the world to buy 334Aspiritual foods, that is, faith and action and knowledge, with which the spiritual masters of the Church are satisfied. For first they ask faith of those to whom they preach, then actions befitting that faith, lastly the knowledge of truth, for the sake of which both faith is preached and action and knowledge are perfected.

Then the Samaritan woman saith to him: How dost thou, being a Jew, ask to drink of me, while I am a Samaritan woman? For the Jews do not deal with the Samaritans. That Samaritan woman, who bore the type of the Church or of nature, wonders at the Lord, whom she did not yet know, but had known only to be a Jew, why he should have asked to drink from her, since she was a Samaritan woman. For, as she says, the Jews do not deal with the Samaritans. 334BWhere according to the letter it is asked what it is that the woman says, for the Jews do not deal with the Samaritans, when the Samaritans both lived under the law of Moses and all its precepts and symbols, and dwelt together [with them], and through this, if they lived under one law, it must be thought that they also shared in foods. In what then did they not deal together? To this it must be said: The Jews and Samaritans, living under one law, adored one God, namely the Father, but contended about the place of prayer. For the Jews thought he was to be adored in no other place but in the temple which was at Jerusalem. But the Samaritans thought that God was to be worshipped on the mountain of Samaria, on which Jacob dwelt. In this therefore they did not deal together, disagreeing about the place of prayer. For if the Judaic law forbade the Jews to deal 334Cwith the Samaritans, perhaps Christ would not ask the figured drink from the woman, nor allow his disciples to buy foods from them, since it must be believed without doubt that he asked not the drink of bodily water, but the drink of spiritual faith.

Jesus answered and said to her: If thou knewest the gift of God, and who he is that saith to thee: Give me to drink, thou wouldst have asked him, and he would have given thee water. [Vulg. tu forsitan petisses ab eo, et dedisset tibi aquam vivam, "thou perhaps wouldst have asked of him, and he would have given thee living water"] As if he had said: If thou, woman, knewest me, and believedst in me perfectly, and if thou knewest the gift of God, that is, the Holy Spirit, thou wouldst have asked of me to drink, and I would give thee, by the merit of thy faith, living water, namely the Holy Spirit.

The woman saith to him: Lord, neither hast thou a drawing-vessel, and the well is deep. How then hast thou living water? The woman, still carnal and not yet believing, 334Ddid not know the words of him who spoke with her; for she had thought that the Lord spoke of sensible water, when he was treating of intelligible water. The deep well, as we said before, signifies either the depth of human nature or the lowliness of the sensible and bodily creature, which is therefore called deep, because nothing in the universe of created things is lower than bodily nature. But the drawing-vessel, the study of wisdom, by which the wave of reason is brought forth from the inmost recesses of nature, while physical investigation treats of human substance or the sensible creature. Saint Augustine asserts that the deep well signifies the delight of bodily 335Athings, from which and in which, like flowing water, that delight itself rises; but the drawing-vessel, the cupidity of the carnal soul, which always desires to be satisfied with the delight of temporal and bodily things. There follows:

Art thou greater than our father Jacob, who gave us the well, and himself drank of it, and his sons, and his cattle? The Samaritan woman was a foreigner; yet she calls Jacob her father. And not undeservedly, since she had lived under the law of Moses, and possessed the estate which Jacob had given to his son Joseph. What she says therefore, Art thou greater than our father Jacob? seems to mean this, as if she said: Art thou of greater power than our father Jacob, who had dug this well, and had a drawing-vessel, with which he supplied water both to himself and to his sons and 335Bto his cattle? But thou hast neither a drawing-vessel, nor any instrument for drawing water, and thou promisest to give me to drink; not yet understanding the words of him who spoke with her.

Jesus answered and said to her: Every one that drinketh of this water, which thou wishest to draw, woman, and thinkest that I ask of thee, shall thirst again, for it quenches a temporal thirst temporally. But whosoever shall drink of the water which I will give him, shall not thirst for ever. As if he said: To whomsoever, believing in me, I shall have given the gift of the Holy Spirit proceeding from the Father through me, [he] shall not thirst for ever. For he will say with the Psalmist: « I shall be satisfied when thy glory shall appear » .

But the water which I will give him shall become in him a fountain of water springing up into life everlasting. The spiritual water, that is, 335Cthe gift of the Holy Spirit, springs up into life everlasting. Bodily water flows downward, spiritual water springs upward, and carries up with it those who drink it in into eternal glory and beatitude.

The woman saith to him: Lord, give me this water, that I may not thirst, nor come hither to draw. Nature begins to know its maker; for there is in the rational creature an appetite for beatitude and for true knowledge, which was prefigured through the Samaritan woman. The Church begins to know more deeply the faith which before it began simply to draw, while it begins to enter into theological reasons.

Jesus saith to her: Go, call thy husband, and come hither. As if he said: Thou askest of me water springing up into life 335Deverlasting, namely the Holy Spirit, by whose gift I give life everlasting. Thou canst not drink such water unless thou call thy husband. Go therefore, if thou wilt drink, call thy husband, and come hither with him; that is: believe in me, that both thou and thy husband may drink in the Holy Spirit. In this place a transition of theoria from genus to species is understood. For it was said before that the Samaritan woman is the symbol both of the general Church gathered from the Gentiles and of general human nature. But now the same woman holds forth the figure both of each one set in the unity of the Church and of each soul 336Aparticipating in human nature. The woman therefore is the rational soul, whose husband is understood [as] the animus, which is named in many ways; for sometimes intellect, sometimes mind, sometimes animus, often also spirit. Of this husband the Apostle says: « The head of the woman is the husband, the head of the husband is Christ, the head of Christ is God » ; as if he openly said: The head of the rational soul is her husband, her intellect; but the head of the intellect itself is Christ. For this is the natural order of the human creature, that the soul be subjected under the governance of the mind, but the mind under Christ; and thus the whole man is joined through Christ to God and the Father. For the twofold sex also, namely male and female, which is seen outwardly in the body alone, holds forth the interior dispositions of the animus and the soul. 336BFor the division of the rational soul is threefold: into animus, and reason, and interior sense. The animus always revolves about God, and therefore is deservedly called the husband and ruler of the other parts of the soul, since between it and its Creator no other creature is interposed. But reason is occupied about the causes and knowledge of created things, and whatever the animus perceives from supernal contemplation it hands on to reason, and reason commends it to memory. The third part of the soul is the interior sense, which is subjected to reason as to a superior part, and through this is subjected through reason to the mind. But under that interior sense, by natural order, the exterior sense is placed, through which the whole soul quickens and rules the fivefold sense of the body, and vivifies the whole body. Since 336Ctherefore the rational soul can perceive nothing of the supernal gifts except through her husband, that is, through the animus, which holds the rule of the whole nature, the woman, namely the soul, is deservedly bidden to call her husband, her intellect, with whom and through whom she can drink the spiritual gifts, without whom she [can] in no way be a partaker of supernal grace. Therefore he says, call thy husband and come hither; do not at all presume to come to me without thy husband. Also, the intellect being absent, no one knows how to ascend to the height of theology, nor to participate in the spiritual gifts.

The woman answered and said to him: I have no husband. Jesus saith to her: Thou hast said well, that thou hast no husband. [Vulg. quia non habeo virum, "I have no husband", the woman's own words] Thou hast said well, he says; I praise what thou hast said, thou who hast said truly. For I know that thou hast no husband, nor do I know it for this reason, 336Dbecause thou hast said that thou hast none, but through myself, for whom there is leisure to know all things.

For thou hast had five husbands, and he whom thou now hast is not thy husband; this thou hast said truly. Concerning the five husbands of the mystical woman, that is, of the rational soul living carnally, the opinions of the authors vary. Some take them of the five books of the Mosaic law; whose opinion is not approved for this reason, that the Mosaic law was given to the Jews only, but this woman signifies generally every human soul. Augustine understands the five senses of the body [as] the five husbands, 337Aunder whom there is no doubt that every soul lives before it comes to the perfect age in which it can use reason. For sight flourishes in infants, likewise hearing, smell, taste, touch. Beyond these the soul, still carnal and infantile, can participate in nothing. But when it comes to adult age, it is joined as to a certain sixth husband, that is, to reason. Yet often the carnal and foolish soul, having neglected the rational motion, is subjected, as if to a natural husband, to error, by whom and through whom it is deceived, and, having abandoned the natural order, desires those things which are against its own nature. And therefore it is said of him, and he whom thou now hast is not thy husband; thou hast deserted thy husband and wouldst not obey him, thou hast followed an adulterer, and through this hast been made an adulteress. But if thou wilt drink of the water 337Bwhich I will give thee, go, call thy husband, to whom thou oughtest to be made subject and to be joined in spiritual wedlock. Desert the adulterer, lest thou be an adulteress; desert the error by which thou hast been seduced; thou hast left thy husband, and hast violated the natural beauty of thy chastity. Thus far Augustine. Maximus asserts that the five husbands signify five laws given to the human soul. Of which the first was given to man in paradise before sin, concerning the unlawful and forbidden tree. The second after his prevarication and expulsion from paradise, concerning the multiplication of the human offspring. The third to Noe before the flood, concerning the building of the ark. The fourth after the flood, concerning the divisions of the nations. The fifth to Abraham, concerning circumcision and the sacrifice of his son. To these five laws, as to some five husbands, the human 337Csoul was subject from the beginning of the world up to the law of Moses. But the law of the letter, which was given through Moses, was not given to free or to justify the soul, but to convict it, and to press down the weight of the sacraments, which had conferred no salvation on it, unless it should flee for refuge to the grace of the New Testament. For the law was given that grace might be sought, but grace given that the law might be fulfilled. Since therefore the law of the letter conferred nothing on human nature for salvation, for the law led no one to the perfect, but availed for a heap of offences, as the Apostle says: « The strength of sin is the law » , and again: « But the law entered in, that sin might abound » , the Lord says beautifully to the Samaritan woman: And he whom thou now hast is not thy husband; let go the letter, it is not thy husband; go to thy husband, to 337Dthe spirit of the letter; call the law of grace, which is thy husband, from whom thou shalt conceive a spiritual offspring of virtues, with whom I will give thee the gifts of the Holy Spirit, by which thou shalt be able to believe in me. The sixth husband therefore, namely the carnal law, being repudiated, the rational soul is bidden to call her husband, that is, the New Testament, the law of grace, under which alone she could be saved.

The woman saith to him: Lord, I see that thou art a prophet. When the woman knew, from those things which the Lord [said] to her concerning the number of her husbands, and concerning her adultery with another's husband, that he was a prophet, forthwith 338Ashe proposed a question, as if she said: Since I see thee and know without doubt that thou art a prophet, solve for me the question about which the Jews and Samaritans contend, namely about the place of adoring God, on account of which the Jews themselves do not deal with us, that is, dissent from us.

Our fathers adored on this mountain. That is, Jacob, and his sons, and all the Samaritans who lived under the Mosaic law; and you, Jews, say that in Jerusalem is the place where one must adore. In this therefore we dissent, in this with one accord we do not deal together. On this mountain of Samaria we adore God, where Jacob [adored], you in the temple of Jerusalem. Since therefore I see thee a prophet, I do not doubt that thou knowest all things; and through this make me certain concerning this contention, that is, 338Byours and ours, where we ought to adore God, in this place or at Jerusalem.

Jesus saith to her: Woman, believe me, that the hour cometh when neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem shall you adore the Father. You adore what you know not, we adore what we know. By these words he crushes the boasting of the woman, the Samaritan, and of all the Samaritans. For the Samaritans thought that they were of no lesser religion than the Jews. He speaks therefore in the person of the Jews, not of the impious who did not receive him, but of the patriarchs and prophets, and of those who had believed in him a little before in Judaea. Therefore he says: You adore the Father whom you know not, but we adore the same Father whom we know. For no one can adore the Father unless he has first adored 338Cthe Son. He who believes in the Son, he knows the Son, and through this knows the Father also. For he says: « Philip, he who seeth me seeth my Father also » . Thou therefore, woman, with thy whole [nation], knowest not the Father whom thou adorest, because thou hast not yet believed in his Son, through whom one comes to the knowledge of the Father. And this is what follows: Because salvation is from the Jews. As if he openly said: Therefore we know what we adore, joining himself to the believing Jews, because salvation is from the Jews, because Christ, who is the salvation of the whole world, is from the Jews, not only by origin of flesh, but also by propagation of faith; for from them arose the faith and the primitive Church.

But the hour cometh and now is. He calls his presence in the flesh the hour. Thou, he says, woman, consultest me about the place 338Dof adoring; therefore I say to thee, the hour cometh, I am present, and now I am in the flesh. When the true adorers shall adore the Father in spirit and truth. Before I came, no one could adore the Father in spirit and truth, except the patriarchs and prophets, to whom my presence in the flesh was revealed through the Spirit before I came into the world. Not therefore, O woman, on this mountain, nor in Jerusalem, shall the true adorers adore me and my Father, but inwardly, in the inmost temple of their heart and of their understanding. It will first be purged through faith, illumined through 339Aknowledge, perfected through deification. On the mountain of theology they will adore in spirit who shall have been illumined by the Holy Spirit. For the Father also seeketh such, adoring him, who adore him in their spirit and in the truth of their knowledge. What he said before he concluded by reason, saying:

God [is] spirit, and those that adore him must adore in spirit and truth. As if he said: If God were corporeal or a body, perhaps he would seek bodily places for adoring him. Now, since he is spirit, he seeks those who adore him in their spirit and in their intellect through true knowledge.

The woman saith to him: I know that the Messias cometh, who is called Christ. Since the Lord had said before, « the hour cometh and now is » , the woman thought that he had spoken of the Messias, that is, 339Bof the Christ who was to come, whom she did not yet know as present, as though of another. For she had heard from the prophets that Christ was to come. Therefore she says: I know that the Messias cometh. Cometh, she said, not « will come » , that she might compare her words with the words of the prophet with whom she was speaking; or in some way, illumined by the spirit, she understood Christ coming as present; or because it is a usage of speech that of one who is on the way it is said he cometh, when he has not yet come. Messias is a Hebrew name, which in Greek is interpreted Christ, that is, anointed. When he is come, he will announce to us all things. She did not yet understand Christ perfectly as present; yet she believed, taught by the prophets, the coming of Christ and his doctrine to come. He will announce to us all things, namely all things which pertain to the worship of him and 339Cof his Father.

Jesus saith to her: I am, who speak to thee. First the Lord opened the intellect of the woman, that she might believe, then he manifested himself to her. For she believed the prophets, who foretold the coming of Christ; and therefore she merited to speak with him whom she had believed to be to come, although she did not yet know him present. By the merit of which faith she came to the knowledge of Christ speaking of himself and saying: I am the Christ himself; whom thou knowest to be to come, now know present.

And forthwith his disciples came, and they wondered that he was speaking with a woman. Why did they wonder, when Christ very often spoke with women? They did not therefore wonder that their Lord spoke 339Dwith a woman, but this alone they wondered at, that with a Samaritan, that is, a foreign woman, not knowing the mystery of the Church that was to be from the Gentiles. Yet no one was saying: What seekest thou, or what speakest thou with her? For the disciples did not dare to question their Lord, fearing lest they be rebuked by him if they questioned him incautiously, being not yet able to know the mystery of the Church to come.

The woman therefore left her water-pot. After the Church, or human nature, knew the presence 340Aof the divine substance in the flesh, she left her water-pot, she left her carnal usage, she left the water and study of carnal knowledge. . . . . . . . . . . . . .. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .

Where it must first be asked what difference there is between the mysteries of each law, namely of the letter and of grace, and the symbols. Mysteries, then, are properly those which are handed down according to the allegory both of deed and of word, that is, they were both done according to things done and said, because they are narrated. For example, the Mosaic tabernacle was both constructed according to the thing done, and said and narrated in the text of Holy Scripture. In the New Testament also the mysteries of baptism, and also of the Lord's body and blood, and also of the holy chrism, are performed according to things done, and are handed down and said in letters. And this form of sacraments is reasonably called by the holy Fathers the allegory of deed and word. The other form is that which has properly received the name of symbol, and is called the allegory of word, but not of deed, since it is constituted only in the words of spiritual doctrine, but not in sensible deeds. Mysteries, then, are those which in either Testament were both done according to history, and narrated according to the letter; but symbols are those which, only not done, but as it were done, 345Bare said by doctrine alone. For example, in the old law it is written: « Thou shalt not boil a kid in the milk of its mother ». Again in the Psalms: « The mountains skipped like rams, and the hills like the lambs of the sheep ». For in these places of Holy Scripture and in many like them the allegory of word alone, and not of deed and word, is understood. Eriugena's commentary on the Gospel of John, as Migne reprints it from Heinrich Joseph Floss's edition of 1853, made from the single manuscript that preserves it (Laon, Bibliothèque municipale 81, ninth century). Only three fragments survive: chapter 1, verses 11–29; chapter 3, verse 1 to chapter 4, verse 28; and chapter 6, verses 5–14. Where the manuscript breaks off, Floss prints rows of dots, and they are kept here dot for dot. The commentary reads each verse twice, first for the history and then for the theoria, the spiritual understanding above the letter, drawing throughout on the Greek theologians Eriugena had translated. The apparatus at the foot of the Latin is Floss's, not Migne's: his Variae lectiones give the manuscript's reading where he printed something else, and are marked “Floss, var. lect.” His Notae, marked “Floss, notae”, are a nineteenth-century Catholic editor's comments. Several of them warn the reader against Eriugena's teaching on original sin and the Eucharist, and they are translated as he wrote them.
