John Scottus Eriugena

Joannes Scotus Erigena

c. 800 – c. 877

Irish scholar and master at the court of Charles the Bald, and the one Latin writer of his century who read Greek theology in Greek. He translated the Pseudo-Dionysius, Maximus the Confessor and Gregory of Nyssa into Latin, and out of them built his own system in the Periphyseon. His treatise on predestination was condemned in his lifetime and the Periphyseon long after it. His commentary on the Gospel of John survives only in fragments, from a single manuscript at Laon.

De egressu et regressu animae ad Deum

On the Going-Forth and Return of the Soul to God

1023A. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . They possess flowing and not standing. It has deservedly been called the play of God by the teacher, as of one leading us across through these things into those which truly are, and are in no way moved.

Another theoria on the same.

But if we ourselves too, according to the sequence of our nature that prevails at present, at one time born after the likeness of the other animals on the earth, then made boys, then, like the flower of the oximori, dead in youth into the horror of oncoming old age, and translated to another life, have been called the play of God by this God-bearing teacher. Nor is it outside the sequence. For the present life, compared with the future principal form of the divine and true life, 1023Bis play, and so is all, if anything else concerning this has been established more efficaciously. For he himself shows it more openly in the epitaph on Caesarius his brother, saying thus: Such is our life, brethren, of us who live for a time, as is play upon the earth. Not existing, we are made, and, having been made, we are dissolved, we are an unstable dream, the flight 1024Aof a passing bird, yet further than that. For the disposition of present and visible things, compared with the very truth, properly speaking, of the πρωτοτύπων of divine things, that is, of their principal exemplars, will not be reckoned to be at all by those who have been made worthy to take the way, as far as is possible, to the form of the divine beauty, just as neither is a plaything, compared with any true and existing thing, judged universally to be.

Another theoria on the same.

But perhaps also the changeable character of material things, which we commend, things that carry over and are carried over otherwise into another privation, and that have no foundation save the first reason, through 1024Bwhich they are borne, wisely and providently they both bear, and, while they are thought by us to be held, they flee away rather than are held, and suffer our desire to be held in them; but rather of those unable both to hold and to be held, us ourselves, as being repulsed. Cruces for this work — where the plate is defective or the reading uncertain →

A fragment on the soul's going out from God and its return to him, in the Latin Eriugena made from the Greek of Maximus the Confessor. It begins and ends in the middle of the argument, and the gaps are marked with Floss's rows of dots. The apparatus at the foot of the Latin is Floss's, not Migne's: his Variae lectiones give the manuscript's reading where he printed something else, and are marked “Floss, var. lect.”
