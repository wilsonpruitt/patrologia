Thales Pherecydi.

  • 1147A

Audio te primum Ionum, de divinis rebus, apud Graecos palam disserere parare. Verum justiore fortasse sententia, inter amicos ea quae scripseris, leges, quam passim quibuscumque permittes, nullo illis emolumento futura. Equidem si tibi gratum cognovero, volo conscius eorum quae scribis esse. Ac siquidem jubes, ad te mature proficiscar. Neque enim adeo amentes ac stolidi sumus, ego et Solon Atheniensis, ut cum navigio Cretam petierimus visendi gratia, Aegyptumque itidem penetraverimus, ut istic sacerdotibus et astronomis congrederemur, ad te non pari studio navigaturi simus. Veniet enim et Solon ipse, si annuas. Neque tu loci illius amore detentus, raro in Ioniam transis, neque peregrinorum 1147Bhominum desiderio tangeris. Verum uni tantum, ut spero, scribendi negotio incumbis. At nos, qui nihil scribimus, Graeciam, Asiamque, peragramus. Vale.

Thales to Pherecydes.

  • 1147A

I hear that you are preparing to be the first of the Ionians to discourse openly among the Greeks concerning divine matters. But it would perhaps be a juster judgment to read among friends what you have written than to hand it about indiscriminately to any and all, since to them it will bring no profit. For my part, if I learn that it would be agreeable to you, I wish to be privy to the things you are writing. And if indeed you bid me, I will set out to you in good time. For we are not so senseless and dull, I and Solon of Athens, that, when we sailed to Crete for the sake of seeing it, and likewise made our way into Egypt to meet there with the priests and astronomers, we should not sail to you with equal zeal. For Solon himself will come too, if you consent. And you, held fast by love of that place of yours, cross but rarely into Ionia, nor are you touched by any longing for foreign 1147Bmen. But you apply yourself, as I hope, to the one business only of writing. Yet we, who write nothing, travel through Greece and Asia. Farewell.

Thales Soloni.

Athenis si excedas, Mileti, ut equidem reor, commodissime habitare poteris. Est enim vestra colonia, patierisque grave nihil. Quod si et apud Milesios exsecraris tyrannidem, aeque enim ubique tyrannos infestos habes, at cum amicis nobiscum vivere erit jucundissimum. Scripsit ad te Bias, ut Prienam proficiscaris: eam tu urbem si libentius inhabitaveris, et ipsi eo ad te advolabimus.

Thales to Solon.

If you depart from Athens, you will be able, as I for my part think, to dwell most conveniently at Miletus. For it is your colony, and you will suffer nothing grievous there. But if among the Milesians too you abhor tyranny — for everywhere alike you find tyrants hostile — yet to live with us among friends will be most pleasant. Bias has written to you that you set out for Priene: if you would more gladly dwell in that city, we too will fly there to you.

Pisistratus Soloni.

Neque solus Graecorum tyrannidem arripui, neque 1147Cmihi rem alienam usurpavi, quippe genus a Cecrope duco. Id enim mihi meo jure vindico, quod Athenienses olim cum in jurejurando firmassent Codro, illiusque genti praebituros, postmodum abstulerant. Caeterum in deos, vel in homines nihil alias pecco. Leges, quas ipse Atheniensibus dedisti, servari, atque secundum eas vivere jubeo: et quidem melius servantur, quam possent, si multitudinis imperio res ageretur. Permitto enim nemini injuriam fieri: ac tyrannus ego praeter dignitatem et honorem nihil a plebe differo, solis eis stipendiis contentus, quae iis quoque, qui ante me regnarunt, debebantur. Denique Atheniensium singuli decimas frugum suarum separant, non in usus nostros consumendas, verum sacrificiis publicis commodisque communibus: et si 1147Dquando bellum contra nos ingruerit, in sumptus deputandas. Tibi equidem nihil succenseo, quod mentem meam consiliumque detexeris: quippe qui id potius reipublicae studio civitatis, quam mei odio detexeris: ac praeterea quod ignorares cujusmodi 1148Aego rex futurus essem. Id enim si didicisses, aequo animo et conatus meos forte tolerasses, et te fugae haudquaquam commisisses. Domum igitur bona fide redi, mihi et injurato credens, nihil esse periculi a Pisistrato Soloni. Nosti enim neminem, etiam ex inimicorum numero, mali quippiam a me perpessum. Denique si placuerit nostra amicitia frui, inter primos eris: nihil enim in te fraudis, perfidiaeve conspicio. Quod si alioqui habitare Athenis nolueris, pro arbitrio tuo id facies: tantum ne nostri causa, patria excederis. Vale.

Pisistratus to Solon.

I am neither the only one of the Greeks to have seized the tyranny, nor 1147Chave I usurped a thing that was another's, seeing that I trace my descent from Cecrops. For I claim for myself by my own right what the Athenians once, when they had confirmed it by oath to Codrus and his line that they would render it, afterward took away. For the rest, I sin in nothing else against the gods or against men. The laws that you yourself gave the Athenians I command to be kept, and to live according to them; and indeed they are better kept than they could be if affairs were conducted by the rule of the multitude. For I permit no one to be wronged; and I, though a tyrant, differ from the common people in nothing but rank and honor, content with only those revenues that were owed also to those who reigned before me. In short, the individual Athenians set apart the tithes of their crops, not to be consumed for our uses, but for the public sacrifices and the common advantages; and if 1147Dat any time a war should press upon us, to be assigned to the expenses. With you, for my part, I am in no way angry, that you laid bare my mind and design — seeing that you laid it bare rather out of zeal for the commonwealth of the city than out of hatred of me; and besides, because you did not know what sort of 1148Aking I would prove to be. For had you learned this, you would perhaps have borne my endeavors too with an even mind, and would by no means have committed yourself to flight. Come home, then, in good faith, believing me even unsworn, that there is no danger to Solon from Pisistratus. For you know that no one, even from among my enemies, has suffered any evil at my hands. In short, if it should please you to enjoy our friendship, you will be among the first; for I perceive in you nothing of fraud or perfidy. But if for other reasons you are unwilling to dwell at Athens, you will do so at your own discretion; only do not depart your fatherland on our account. Farewell.

Solon Periandro.

Scribis tibi plurimos insidiari; at tu, siquidem omnes e medio tuleris, ne sic quidem proficies. Insidiabitur enim tibi quispiam ex his, quos minime 1148Bsuspectos habes, partim sibi quidem metuens, partim te reprehendens, quod omnia formides, nihilque non metuas: partim item civitati gratificari cupiens. Esset ergo optimum abstinere tyrannide, ut causas omnis metus evaderes. Quod si tyrannidi omnino insistendum censes, compares tibi, necesse est, externas vires et peregrina auxilia, urbanis viribus majora, ut jam tibi nullus infestus sit, et tu neminem extorrem agas. Vale.

Solon to Periander.

You write that very many lie in wait for you; yet you, even if you should take them all out of the way, will not even so gain your end. For someone will lie in wait for you from among those whom you least hold suspect — partly indeed fearing for himself, partly reproaching you because you dread everything and fear nothing not, 1148Bpartly likewise desiring to gratify the city. It would therefore be best to abstain from the tyranny, that you might escape all causes of fear. But if you judge that you must cling to the tyranny at all costs, you must needs procure for yourself external forces and foreign auxiliaries, greater than the forces of the city, so that now none may be hostile to you, and you may drive no one into exile. Farewell.

Solon Epimenidi.

Neque leges meae profecto Atheniensibus profuturae multum erant: neque tu eis antiquatis, civitati fuisti emolumento. Deus enim, ac latores legum non soli juvare civitates possunt, sed qui multitudinem agunt, in quamcumque sententiam volunt. His enim, 1148Csi recte rem administrent, Deus ac leges utiles sunt: sin autem male, nihil prosunt. Neque sane meae leges ac jura, quae scripsi, quidquam profuerunt: sed qui illas transgressi sunt, magna reipublicae invexere detrimenta, qui Pisistrato, ne tyrannidem invaderet, non obstiterunt. Neque vero futura praedicenti mihi fides habebatur. Ille fidelior existimabatur Atheniensibus blandiens, quam ego vera proloquens. Positis igitur armis pro curia, his quidem qui Pisistratum gestire tyrannidem non animadvertunt, sapientiorem: eis vero qui libertatem reipublicae asserere metuerent, fortiorem me esse dixi; at illi Solonis interpretabantur insaniam. Tandem ita patriam contestatus, excessi: O Patria, hic quidem Solon ille tibi opem ferre, et facto et dicto, promptus 1148Det paratus est, caeterum hisce et insanire videor. Itaque, te deserta, proficiscor, solus ex omnibus Pisistrati inimicus: isti vero et favere et obsequi parati sunt. Nosti enim hominem amice, quanto ille astu, quo ingenio, qua arte tyrannidem invaserit. 1149ANam coepit blande plebem illicere, deinde sibi etiam vulnera inflixit: ac progressus in publicum, eaque se ab hostibus accepisse vociferans, orabat quadringentos robustissimos juvenes sibi custodes darent. At illi, me quidem reluctante et reclamante, acquieverunt. Erant autem hi armati validis fustibus; his itaque stipatus, statum reipublicae evertit. Dumque illi spe inani pauperes suos, ne mercede servirent liberare student, omnes una sub jugum missi sunt, unique serviunt Pisistrato.

Solon to Epimenides.

Neither, in truth, were my laws going to profit the Athenians much; nor, when you had made them ancient, were you of any advantage to the city. For God, and the givers of laws, cannot by themselves aid cities, but those who lead the multitude, into whatever opinion they wish. For to these, 1148Cif they administer the matter rightly, God and the laws are useful; but if ill, they profit nothing. Nor indeed did my laws and ordinances, which I wrote, avail anything; but those who transgressed them brought great harm upon the commonwealth — they who did not withstand Pisistratus, that he should not seize the tyranny. Nor was any faith placed in me foretelling what would come. He was reckoned more trustworthy, flattering the Athenians, than I speaking the truth. So, having laid down my arms before the senate-house, I said that I was wiser than those who did not perceive that Pisistratus was straining after the tyranny, and braver than those who feared to assert the freedom of the commonwealth; but they construed it as Solon's madness. At last, having thus called my fatherland to witness, I departed: O Fatherland, here indeed is that Solon, ready and prepared 1148Dto bring you aid both by deed and by word; but to these men I even seem to be mad. And so, forsaking you, I set out, alone of all men the enemy of Pisistratus; but they are ready both to favor and to obey him. For you know the man, my friend — with what cunning, with what wit, with what art he seized the tyranny. 1149AFor he began winningly to entice the common people, then even inflicted wounds upon himself; and coming forth in public, and crying aloud that he had received these from enemies, he begged that they give him as guards four hundred of the most robust young men. And they, though I indeed struggled and cried out against it, consented. Now these were armed with stout cudgels; and so, hemmed about by these, he overthrew the state of the commonwealth. And while those men strive with vain hope to free their poor, that they might not serve for hire, all together were sent under the yoke, and serve Pisistratus alone.

Solon Pisistrato.

Credo equidem nihil me abs te passurum mali. Nam ante tyrannidem tibi amicus eram, ne nunc quidem magis infensus, quam alius quivis Atheniensium, qui tyrannidem non ament. Sive autem 1149Bunius imperio regi, sive publice rem administrari illis utilius sit, ex sua quisque sententia statuat. Certe tyrannorum te omnium esse praestantissimum fateor. Athenas autem redire mihi utile non esse cognosco, ne quis me juste reprehendat, qui Atheniensibus dudum rempublicam ex aequo administrandam tradideram, praesensque oblatam mihi tyrannidem sponte declinaveram, modo rediens factum tuum subita poenitentia probare videar.

Solon to Pisistratus.

I believe indeed that I shall suffer no evil from you. For before the tyranny I was your friend, and not even now am I more hostile than any other of the Athenians who do not love the tyranny. Whether, moreover, 1149Bit be more useful to them to be ruled by the command of one, or for the affair to be administered publicly, let each determine according to his own judgment. Certainly I confess you to be the most excellent of all tyrants. But I recognize that it is not useful for me to return to Athens, lest anyone justly reproach me — I who long ago had handed over to the Athenians the commonwealth to be administered on equal terms, and, being present, had of my own accord declined the tyranny offered to me — should now, returning, seem by a sudden repentance to approve your deed.

Solon Craeso.

Amplector mirifice tuam in nos benevolentiam: et per deos immortales, nisi apud me jampridem statuissem ibi sedem habere ubi respublica sit libera, mallem apud te, in tuo regno vitam, quam Athenis agere, violenter tyrannidem exercente Pisistrato. 1149CVerum ibi suavius ex instituto nostro vivimus, ubi sunt omnibus aequa et communia jura. Veniam tamen ad te, ut tuo vel hospitio tantisper fruar.

Solon to Croesus.

I marvelously embrace your goodwill toward us; and by the immortal gods, had I not long since resolved with myself to have my seat where the commonwealth is free, I would rather pass my life with you, in your kingdom, than at Athens, while Pisistratus violently exercises the tyranny. 1149CBut there we live more sweetly, according to our principle, where there are laws equal and common to all. Yet I will come to you, that I may for a while enjoy at least your hospitality.

Chilo Periandro.

Jubes uti militia vacem, extorrisque agam: quasi tu in tuto futurus sis. Atqui ego monarchae nec sua tuta esse puto: felicemque eum tyrannum censeo, cui in sua domo absque sanguine contigerit mori.

Chilon to Periander.

You bid me be free of military service, and live in exile — as though you yourself were going to be in safety. And yet I think that not even his own affairs are safe for a monarch; and I judge that tyrant happy to whom it has fallen to die in his own house without bloodshed.

Pittacus Craeso.

Jubes me in Lydiam venire, spectatum opes tuas. Ego etsi illas minime aspexi, nihil ambigo, Alyattis filium, regum omnium esse opulentissimum, auroque refertissimum. Neque vero amplius quidquam habituri sumus, si Sardeis ad te accesserimus. Auro enim ipsi non indigemus, contenti modico vitae stipendio, 1149Dquantum satis sit mihi, et amicis. Veniam tamen, ut tibi perhumano et hospitali viro familiaris efficiar. Vale.

Pittacus to Croesus.

You bid me come into Lydia to behold your wealth. Though I have by no means beheld it, I in no way doubt that the son of Alyattes is the most opulent of all kings, and most crammed with gold. Nor indeed shall we have anything more, if we come to you at Sardis. For gold we ourselves do not lack, being content with a modest provision of life, 1149Das much as suffices for me and my friends. Yet I will come, that I may become the intimate of a man so very kindly and hospitable. Farewell.

Cleobulus Soloni.

Amici quidem tibi sunt permulti, singulisque domus est: verum ego Soloni commodissimam fore ad inhabitandum, Lindum censeo, civitatem liberam. Estque insula maritima, ubi si morari volueris, nihil a Pisistrato mali formidabis, atque ad te amici undique confluent. Vale.

Cleobulus to Solon.

You have indeed very many friends, and each of them has a house; but I judge that Lindus will be most convenient for Solon to dwell in, a free city. And it is a maritime island, where, if you should wish to tarry, you will dread no evil from Pisistratus, and your friends will flock to you from every side. Farewell.

Periander Sapientibus.

Gratias immortales ago Pythio Apollini, quod in unum coactos epistolae meae repererunt, ipsaeque Corinthum, ut confido, perducent. Exspecto itaque 1150Avos. Certe ipsi videbitis, quam civiliter vos excipiam. Sicut igitur anno praeterito Sardeis, in Lydiam venistis: ita nunc oro, ne pigeat ad me quoque proficisci, Corinthi tyrannum; videbunt enim vos non sine gratulatione Corinthii, Periandri domum adeuntes.

Periander to the Sages.

I render immortal thanks to Pythian Apollo, that my letters have found you gathered into one, and will themselves, as I trust, lead you through to Corinth. And so I await 1150Ayou. Certainly you yourselves will see how courteously I receive you. As, therefore, last year at Sardis, you came into Lydia, so now I pray that it may not irk you to set out to me also, the tyrant of Corinth; for the Corinthians will see you, not without rejoicing, coming to the house of Periander.

Periander Proclo.

Nobis quidem non ex sententia fuit uxoris scelus: tu autem sponte filio ingrato si quid egeris, peccas. Aut igitur immanitatem in filium compesce, aut ego illi opem feram: nam et ipse satis diu poenas dedit.

Periander to Procles.

To us indeed the crime committed against my wife was not according to our wish; but you, if of your own accord you do anything against my son who is guiltless, do wrong. Either, therefore, restrain your savagery against my son, or I will bring him aid; for he too has paid the penalty long enough.

Thrasybulus Periandro.

Praeconi quidem tuo nihil dissimulavi, sed ipsum in segetem inducens, eminentiores spicas bacillo feriens, decutiebam, subsequente illo: tibique, si 1150Binterrogares, referret quidquid vel viderit vel audierit a me. Tu ergo sic facito, siquidem tyrannidem tuto tenere cupis, atque in ea constabiliri. Civitatis principes tolle, sive illi amici, sive inimici videantur: quippe tyranno amici quoque saepe suspecti sunt.

Thrasybulus to Periander.

To your herald indeed I dissembled nothing, but leading him into the standing corn, and striking the more eminent ears with my staff, I beat them down, he following after; and to you, if 1150Byou should ask, he would report whatever he saw or heard from me. Do you therefore act thus, if indeed you desire to hold the tyranny in safety and to be established firmly in it. Take away the leading men of the city, whether they seem friends or enemies; for to a tyrant even friends are often suspect.

Anarcharsis Craeso.

Ego, Lydorum rex, in Graeciam adveni, Graecorum mores et studia et instituta percepturus. Auro autem nihil egeo, satisque mihi est, ut ad Scythas redeam melior atque doctior. Veniam tamen ad te Sardeis, plurimi faciens tibi familiarem et amicum fieri.

Anacharsis to Croesus.

I, O king of the Lydians, have come into Greece, to learn the manners and pursuits and institutions of the Greeks. But I have no need of gold, and it is enough for me that I may return to the Scythians better and more learned. Yet I will come to you at Sardis, making very much of becoming intimate and friend to you.

Epimenides Soloni.

Confide, o amice. Si enim vel servire assuetis, vel non bene institutis Atheniensibus Pisistratus immineret, 1150Cimperium profecto diuturnum habuisset. Caeterum viros haud sane malos, aut ignavos servire compulit: sed qui monitorum Solonis memores, prae pudore ingemiscant, servire tyrannidi diutius haudquaquam ferent. Verum etsi Pisistratus civitatem occuparit, non tamen ad illius liberos imperium spero deducendum. Est enim perdifficile hominibus liberis, praeclarisque institutis legibus, sub servitute durare. Tu autem, noli, quaeso, vagus agi, sed in Cretam venire ad nos matura, ubi tyrannus nullus cuiquam molestus est. Si vero illius amici forte, ut fit, in itinere inciderint, metuo ne quid sinistri tibi eveniat.

Epimenides to Solon.

Take heart, O friend. For if Pisistratus were bearing down upon Athenians accustomed to servitude, or ill-instructed, 1150Che would assuredly have held a lasting rule. But he has compelled to servitude men in no way base or cowardly; and they, mindful of Solon's admonitions, groaning for shame, will by no means bear to serve the tyranny any longer. For even though Pisistratus has seized the city, yet I do not expect the rule to be carried down to his children. For it is exceedingly difficult for free men, and for those instructed by excellent laws, to endure under servitude. But you, do not, I pray, let yourself be driven as a wanderer, but make haste to come to us into Crete, where no tyrant is troublesome to anyone. But if perchance his friends should fall in with you on the road, as happens, I fear lest something ill befall you.

Pherecydes Thaleti.

Bene moriaris, cum tibi fatalis dies supervenerit. 1150DMorbus me invaserat, cum tuas accepi litteras, pediculis opperiebar, et febri quatiebar totus. Mandavi itaque quibusdam ex familiaribus, ut cum me sepelierint, ad te perferant quae scripsi. Tu autem, siquidem ea probaveris cum sapientibus reliquis, ita legenda demum trades: sin autem improbaveris, nolito edere. Mihi certe necdum satis placebant, est ibi quidem non certa rerum fides. Neque enim id recepi, neque quid sit verum me scire professus sum. Forte quaedam de Theologia reservavi, caetera intelligere oportet. Omnia quippe judico potius, quam aperio. Morbo autem singulis diebus invalescente, neque medicorum quempiam, neque amicorum penitus admitto. Caeterum assistentibus pro foribus, 1151Aet interrogantibus quo in statu sim, digito per ostii claustra dimisso, quam pestilenti malo tenear, ostendi: admonuique ut postridie conveniant ad solemnes Pherecydis inferias. Vale.

Pherecydes to Thales.

May you die well, when your fated day shall come upon you. 1150DA sickness had assailed me when I received your letter; I was beset by lice, and was shaken all over by fever. And so I have charged certain of my household that, when they have buried me, they carry to you what I have written. But you, if indeed you approve it together with the rest of the sages, then at last hand it over to be read; but if you disapprove it, do not publish it. To me certainly it was not yet quite pleasing; there is in it indeed no sure warranty of things. For I neither undertook this, nor professed that I knew what is true. Perhaps I have reserved certain things concerning Theology; the rest one must understand. For all things I rather judge than lay open. But as the sickness grows stronger day by day, I admit to me neither any of the physicians nor of my friends at all. But to those standing before my doors, 1151Aand asking in what state I am, I showed, by letting a finger through the door's bar, with what pestilent evil I am held; and I bade them assemble on the morrow for the solemn funeral rites of Pherecydes. Farewell.

Anaximenes Pythagorae.

Thales, ab aetatis flore, ad senectutem, per studia virtutis humanitatisque pervectus, inclyta morte defungitur. Is cum, ut consueverat, una cum ancillula, sidera inspecturus, atrio domus matutinus exisset, immemor ut sese loci situs haberet, dum aethera securus explorat, in subjectam foveam cecidit. Milesii siderum observatoris hunc finem tradunt. Caeterum nos, qui litterarum amore tenemur, tanti viri jugiter meminimus, atque in illius maxime doctrina acquiescimus, initiumque sermonis nostri Thaletem 1151Bsemper instituimus.

Anaximenes to Pythagoras.

Thales, having been carried through, from the flower of his age to old age, by the pursuits of virtue and humane learning, has met a renowned death. For when, as was his custom, he had gone out in the morning, together with his little maidservant, into the courtyard of his house to inspect the stars, forgetful of how the lay of the place stood, while he securely explores the heaven, he fell into a pit lying below. Such an end do the Milesians relate of this observer of the stars. But we, who are held by love of letters, remember so great a man continually, and rest most of all upon his teaching, and always make Thales 1151Bthe beginning of our discourse.

Anaximenes Pythagorae.

Consultius nobis egisti, ut quietus viveres, qui ex Samo Crotonem commigrasti. Nam Aeacidae cum aliis infesti sunt, tum Milesiis tyranni dominantur. Medorum item rex nobis acriter imminet, nisi velimus esse tributarii. Quamquam videntur Iones pro libertate omnium cum Medis dimicare. Ita enim illi nos undique urgent, ut nulla nobis spes salutis sit. Quonam igitur animo possit Anaximenes coeli secreta rimari, cui jugis aut mortis, aut servitutis incumbit metus? Atqui tu Crotoniatis atque Italis caeteris gratus atque in pretio es, quem accedunt et ex Sicilia studiosi quique.

Anaximenes to Pythagoras.

You acted more prudently for us, in that you might live in quiet — you who migrated from Samos to Crotona. For the Aeacidae are hostile to others, and tyrants lord it over the Milesians. Likewise the king of the Medes bears down sharply upon us, unless we are willing to be tributaries. Although the Ionians seem to be fighting with the Medes for the freedom of all. For so do they press us on every side that there is no hope of safety for us. With what mind, then, could Anaximenes search out the secrets of heaven, upon whom lies the perpetual fear either of death or of servitude? But you are pleasing to the Crotoniates and to the rest of the Italians, and held in esteem, whom the studious also from Sicily approach.

Archytas Dionysio.

1151CMisimus omnes Platonis necessarii Lamiscum, et Photidam, virum illum abs te recepturi jure antiquae nostrae amicitiae. Recte igitur feceris, si memineris quanto a nobis studio efflagitasti Platonis ad te adventum, ut nos illum venire hortaremur, facturum te spondens omnia illumque libere accedere et abire permissurum. Memor esto igitur, quanti illius adventum feceris, quodque eo tempore plus caeteris amaveris. Quod si qua orta simultas est, humanius te agere convenit, illumque nobis restituere illaesum. Haec enim si facis, justitiam coles, ac nobis gratificaberis.

Archytas to Dionysius.

1151CWe, all the intimates of Plato, have sent Lamiscus and Photidas, to receive that man from you by the right of our ancient friendship. Rightly, therefore, will you have acted, if you remember with how great zeal you demanded of us the coming of Plato to you, that we should urge him to come, promising that you would do all things, and would permit him freely to come and to depart. Be mindful, therefore, how much you made of his coming, and that at that time you loved him more than the rest. But if any quarrel has arisen, it befits you to act more humanely, and to restore him to us unharmed. For if you do these things, you will cultivate justice, and will gratify us.

Arcesilaus Thaumasiae.

Testamenta mea Diogeni tradidi ad te perferenda. Quia enim saepius aegroto, et corpore non recte 1151Dvaleo, testamentum conficere placuit: ut si quid contingat inopinatum, sine tua injuria proficiscar ex vita, qui unus ex omnibus maximo me affectu prosecutus es. Fidelissimus autem omnium, qui hic sunt, cum mihi semper fueris, diligenter et servare stude, tum propter aetatem, tum propter necessitudinem. Cura igitur memor, quantum tuae fidei committam, ut quod apud te totum deponetur, quantum in te est, et negotia mea honeste disposita sint.

Arcesilaus to Thaumasias.

I have handed my testament to Diogenes to be carried to you. For since I am rather often sick, and am not rightly 1151Dwell in body, it has pleased me to make a testament: so that, if anything unforeseen should happen, I may depart from life without wrong to you — you who, alone of all men, have attended me with the greatest affection. And since you have always been the most faithful of all who are here, be diligent both to keep it carefully, both for your age's sake and for our close bond's sake. Take care, therefore, mindful of how much I commit to your trust, that what is deposited with you wholly may, as far as in you lies, and that my affairs be honorably arranged.

Rex Antigonus Zenoni philosopho.

Ego fortuna me quidem et gloria vitam tuam anteire existimo: caeterum disciplinis studiisque liberalibus, et perfecta felicitate, quam tu possides, longe abs te praecelli sentio. Quocirca te orare statui, 1152Auti ad me proficiscaris, id mihi persuadens, te preces meas minime irritas fieri passurum. Tu igitur modis omnibus enitere, ut tuo contubernio fruamur: certo sciens non mei tantum, sed omnium simul Macedonum eruditorem fore. Nam qui Macedoniae regem erudit, atque in virtutem imbuit, eum et subditos quosque instruere ad fortitudinem et probitatem certum est. Nam cujusmodi fuerit dux, tales ut plurimum subditos fieri necesse est. Vale.

King Antigonus to Zeno the philosopher.

I for my part reckon that I go before your life in fortune and glory; but in the liberal disciplines and studies, and in the perfect felicity which you possess, I feel myself far outdone by you. Wherefore I have resolved to entreat you, 1152Athat you set out to me, persuading myself of this, that you will by no means suffer my prayers to be made vain. Do you, therefore, strive by all means, that we may enjoy your company; knowing for certain that you will be the educator not of me only, but of all the Macedonians together. For he who educates the king of Macedonia, and imbues him with virtue, it is certain that he instructs also each of his subjects unto fortitude and probity. For of whatever sort the leader has been, of such sort must the subjects for the most part become. Farewell.

Zeno Antigono regi.

Amplector tuum tam vehemens discendi studium, quod veram, utilem atque necessariam, non popularem et quae ad pervertendos mores tendit, eruditionem apprehendere instituis. Qui enim philosophiae amore ac studio tenetur, declinatque celebrem illam 1152Bet vulgarem voluptatem, quae quorumdam adolescentulorum animos effeminat: eum non natura modo nobilitatem tueri, sed verum quidem virtutis quoque institutum, manifestum est. Porro liberali ac nobili ingenio si adjiciatur modica exercitatio, neque desit praeceptoris copia, mature ad perfectam virtutis evadit frugem. Equidem nisi senectus mihi obsisteret, sum enim octogenarius, corpusque praeterea invalidum, ipse ad te venirem, ut jubes. Quia vero id datur, quosdam ex conturbernalibus meis ad te mitto, qui animi bonis me minime inferiores sunt, et corporis etiam commodis exsuperant. His si tu studiose congrediare, nihil quod ad perfectam pertinet beatitudinem, desiderabis.

Zeno to King Antigonus.

I embrace your so vehement zeal for learning, in that you resolve to grasp the true, useful, and necessary erudition — not the popular, and that which tends to the perverting of morals. For he who is held by the love and study of philosophy, and turns aside from that famous 1152Band vulgar pleasure which effeminates the minds of certain young men — it is manifest that he not only guards his nobility by nature, but has also the true institution of virtue. Moreover, if to a liberal and noble disposition there be added moderate exercise, and the abundance of a teacher be not wanting, it comes early to the perfect fruit of virtue. For my part, were old age not against me — for I am an octogenarian, and my body besides is weak — I would myself come to you, as you bid. But because this is not granted, I send to you certain of my companions, who are by no means inferior to me in the goods of the mind, and even surpass me in the advantages of the body. If you consort with these zealously, you will lack nothing that pertains to perfect blessedness.

Pythagoras Anaximeni.

1152CAt tu, virorum optime, si nihil Pythagora, et genere et gloria praestantior esses, profecto ad nos jam ex Mileto migrasses: nunc vero te continuit paterna et avita gloria, quae et me continuisset, si essem Anaximeni similis. Caeterum vos si augurio urbes deseretis, eis quidem ornatus adimetur, Medique illis infestius instabunt. Neque vero semper convenit astrorum ac coeli vestigare rationes. Praestat interdum, ad patriam curas cogitationesque convertere. Quamquam ego non semper meis vaco fabulis, verum et bellis interdum quibus inter se invicem Itali dissident.

Pythagoras to Anaximenes.

1152CBut you, best of men, if you were in no way more excellent than Pythagoras, both in birth and in glory, would assuredly have migrated to us already from Miletus; but as it is, your fatherland's and forefathers' glory has held you back — which would have held me back too, if I were like Anaximenes. But you, if by augury you shall desert your cities, their adornment indeed will be taken from them, and the Medes will press upon them more hostilely. Nor indeed does it always befit one to trace out the reckonings of the stars and of heaven. It is better at times to turn to the cares and thoughts of one's fatherland. And yet I do not always have leisure for my own tales, but also at times for the wars in which the Italians are at variance among themselves.

Archytas Platoni.

Facis tu quidem recte, quod nobis re convaluisse ex aegritudine significaris: de Damisco item certiores 1152Dfeceris. De Commentariis autem curavimus venimusque ad Lucanos, ibique convenimus Occelli nepotes. Quae igitur de regno, legibus, justitia, omniumque generatione, et ipsi habemus, et ex his quaedam misimus. Reliqua modo reperiri non possunt: cum inventa fuerint, ad te deferentur.

Archytas to Plato.

You do indeed rightly, in that you have signified to us that you have recovered your strength from your ailment; and likewise have made us more certain concerning Damiscus. 1152DConcerning the Commentaries, moreover, we have taken care, and have come to the Lucanians, and there we met the descendants of Occellus. Whatever things, then, concerning kingship, laws, justice, and the generation of all things, we ourselves have, and of these we have sent certain of them. The rest cannot just now be found; when they have been found, they will be conveyed to you.

Plato Archytae.

Quae abs te nobis allata sunt Commentaria, dici non potest, quam libenter accepimus; quique illa scripsit, in primis admirati sumus. Ostendit enim profecto vir ille, dignum se majoribus illis suis, antiquissimis atque optimis viris. Feruntur autem isti viri, Myraei fuisse: hi autem ex illis fuere Trojanis, qui cum Laomedonte migrarunt, viri boni, ut de 1153Aillis tradita significant. Quae apud me sunt Commentaria, de quibus scripsisti, nondum satis elucubrata sunt, neque umquam satis erunt; ea tamen misi. De custodia vero ambo consentimus: nihil itaque adhortatione opus est. Vale.

Plato to Archytas.

The Commentaries that were brought to us from you — it cannot be told how gladly we received them; and him who wrote them we admired above all. For that man has assuredly shown himself worthy of those great ancestors of his, most ancient and best of men. Now these men are said to have been Myraeans; and these were of those Trojans who migrated with Laomedon, good men, as the things handed down concerning 1153Athem signify. The Commentaries that are with me, concerning which you wrote, are not yet sufficiently wrought out, nor ever will be sufficiently; yet I have sent them. But concerning safekeeping we both agree; and so there is no need of exhortation. Farewell.

Rex Darius, Hystaspis filius, Heraclitum Ephesium, sapientem virum, salutat.

Librum de Natura scripsisti obscurum, difficilemque in plerisque, qui si ad verbum exponatur, vim quamdam speculationis continere videtur mundi totius, et quae in eo fiunt omnium, quae quidem sunt in divino motu constituta. In quibus plurimi haeserunt adeo, ut et qui complura legerunt, ambigant, cum recta abs te narratio conscripta videatur. Rex igitur Darius, Hystaspis filius, auditor esse tuus cupit, 1153Bparticepsque Graecae eruditionis fieri. Venias itaque quam primum ad conspectum meum, ac regiam 1154Adomum. Graeci enim, ut plurimum sapientibus viris observandis minus dediti, aspernantur ea quae ab his recte fuerint elucubrata: tametsi ejusmodi sint, ut eruditione et gravitate non careant. Apud me autem aderit tibi omnis honor, primatusque omnis, quotidieque sollicita observatio, et grata collocutio, vitaque tuis moribus probabilis. Vale.

King Darius, son of Hystaspes, greets Heraclitus of Ephesus, a wise man.

You have written a book On Nature, obscure and difficult in most parts, which, if it be expounded to the letter, seems to contain a certain force of speculation about the whole world, and about all the things that come to pass in it — things indeed that are established in the divine motion. In these very many have stuck fast, so that even those who have read much are in doubt, though the narration written by you seems correct. King Darius, therefore, son of Hystaspes, desires to be your hearer, 1153Band to become a partaker of Greek erudition. Come, therefore, as soon as possible into my sight, and to the royal 1154Ahouse. For the Greeks, being for the most part little given to regarding wise men, spurn the things that have been rightly wrought out by them — even though they be of such a kind as not to lack erudition and gravity. But with me there will be for you every honor, and every precedence, and daily anxious regard, and welcome converse, and a life approvable by your manners. Farewell.

Heraclitus Ephesius regi Dario, Hystaspis filio.

Quotquot mortales in terris vivunt, a veritate ac justitia abstinent, avaritiae autem et inani gloriae inexplebiliter intendunt, perditae dementiae causa. Ego autem, omnis immemor nequitiae, fastidiumque devitans omnis invidiae domesticae, quae splendorem comitari solet, numquam in Persarum solum advenirem, 1154Bpaucis contentus, quae sunt secundum meam sententiam.

Heraclitus of Ephesus to King Darius, son of Hystaspes.

However many mortals live upon the earth abstain from truth and justice, but strain insatiably after avarice and empty glory, for the sake of ruinous madness. But I, forgetful of all wickedness, and shunning the loathing of all domestic envy, which is wont to attend splendor, would never come into the soil of the Persians, 1154Bcontent with the few things that are according to my judgment.
