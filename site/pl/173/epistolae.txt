## I. *To Richenza, empress of the Romans.--A letter of consolation on the death of the emperor Lothar and of Henry, duke of Bavaria, her son-in-law.* [n: (Anno 1139.)]

[1137B]

To the lady RICHENZA, empress of the Romans, protected by God, PETER THE DEACON wishes, beyond the bounds of the present life, the reckoning-stone of the eternal kingdom.

Many times, unconquered empress, have I taken up the impulse to console your eminence; many times have I checked it. Very many things invited me to do it at once — chiefly the fidelity owed to our lord the emperor Lothar, now dead, and then the love your imperial clemency has shown toward us who are unworthy of it — so that, even if I could not altogether lull your tears to sleep, I might at least in the meantime have wiped them away. But as for holding back, I knew that your grief, while it raged fresh, was not [1137C] to be met too hastily, lest those very consolations should rather chafe, aggravate, and inflame it: for often we see that in bodily sicknesses too an untimely medicine is most destructive. And so I was waiting until the grief should itself lay down its strength, so that, softened by delay, it might permit itself in some measure to be touched and handled and to bear remedies. Yet before I close with it, I will stand by it, and will bring back into memory a number of things by which it may be stirred, and will cut open and bring forward what already seem as it were skinned over — that is, by the best and most solemn manner of consoling. Let those, then, weep and groan the longer whose most delicate minds long good fortune has unnerved and luxury unmanned, who have fixed their hope wholly in this world; [1137D] who either are altogether ignorant of what those everlasting goods may be, or, if they know, lulled by worldly delights, neglect them. But as for those who have passed almost all of them sound through various trials, whose conversation, according to the Apostle, is in the heavens, who weigh both the vanities of the world and the joys of heaven with a prudent and discerning heart, they also endure any burden whatever, however heavy, with unmoved constancy. Why should I go on recounting? Number how many calamities, [1138B] how many misfortunes you have been tossed by from infancy itself, seeing that you were, so to speak, all but born into them. Our lord the emperor Lothar of happy memory, who had chosen you for his wife, had advanced you to the imperial summit, and had committed to you alone all the affairs of the Roman empire — after the many straits you endured together with him, after the Roman world, if I may so speak, had been traversed with him, when day by day you were hastening your return with him to the imperial dwelling — alas, ah the grief! you bore him out to burial amid the most grievous sorrow of many companions. I pass over so many perils, so many terrors, which without interval assailed you and which you bore along with so great a Caesar. And now, just now, when you have not yet fully composed your earlier mournings, while you still mourn, a mourning has been added to you; and when you thought you had, as a consolation [1138C] for so many hardships, the one thing that was left, namely your living son-in-law HENRY, duke of Bavaria, suddenly, when you were not expecting it, his untimely death was reported to you. Plainly it is the gravest of all the things that have befallen you since the passing of our lord the emperor Lothar, this fresh wound; and it did not break the sum, but has divided, I confess, the breast and the very inward parts. Nor did this blood flow from a body untouched, but was struck through the scars themselves; but just as raw recruits, though lightly wounded, still cry out, as veterans, however run through, suffer themselves to be treated patiently and without a groan. For the faithful, most merciful lady, are not to be mourned, says the Apostle, as are the rest who have no hope: *For if [1138D] we believe that Jesus died and rose again, so also will God bring with him those who have slept through Jesus.* Therefore, most merciful lady, we exhort you to receive consolation; for what else is this death but the leaving behind of the body, the laying down of a heavy burden? You were born, O empress; you are going to die. Where will you go that you may not die? What can you do that you may not die? That the Lord your God might console you, he deigned of his own will to die. [1139A] When you see Christ dead for you, do you scorn to die? Therefore you are going to die, willing or unwilling. Whither will you get away? Whither will you flee that you may not die? Be it today, be it tomorrow, it is to come; it is not doubtful; it is to be believed. What then are you doing, you fear, you flee! It is to come. Therefore, my most devoted lady, put manfully away from you lamentations and wailings and womanish uproars; for the harder this grief of yours is, the greater the courage you must summon, and the more fiercely you must engage, as with an enemy known and already often beaten. Neither do prosperous things lift up the brave and wise man, nor do adverse ones cast him down; he bears those things easily who always expects them; things are heavy to him which are sudden. He who has not puffed himself up in glad circumstances, nor shrunk when they changed, keeps against either state an unconquered soul [1139B] of a firmness already tested. For in good fortune itself he has learned by trial what he could do against ill fortune. To be affected with unbounded grief when you have lost one of your dearest is indeed a foolish indulgence; but to be affected with none is an inhuman hardness. For not to feel grief is not a man's part, and not to bear it is not the part of virtue. The best tempering lies between the two: both to feel the longing and to restrain it — especially since it does not befit your excellence to grieve beyond measure. It is the greatest consolation to consider that this has happened to you which all before you have suffered and all will suffer. Rejoice, then, that you had a Roman emperor for a husband; consider that it is most delightful that you had him, and human that you lost him; that you had such a man, set down among your highest goods. You must not consider how much longer [1139C] you might have had him, but how long you did have him. In him death did not choose to follow your satisfaction, but its own law. Only let the soul stand ready for battle; death will seize one man at one time and another at another; it will pass no one by, it will certainly except no one. Whoever is brought forth to life is destined for death; but let the soul of the wise man never fear what is necessary, and always expect what is uncertain.



## II. *To the same.--On the same subject.* [n: (Anno eod.)]

To his most excellent and most devoted lady Richenza, protected by God, the great and peace-making Augusta and empress of the Romans, PETER, the servant of your empire, wishes, beyond the power of the present kingdom, eternal happiness.

Although a severed sinew gives pain, and a sorrowful spirit [1139D] grinds the bones, yet our lord the emperor Lothar is not to be mourned as are those also who, as the Apostle says, have no hope. He has gone forth to his own, in whom is the highest good, in whom is all goodness, all justice, all mercy, and all holiness. He has gone forth to him who does not cease to cry out to those who keep his commandments: Come to me, all you who labor and are burdened, and I will make you to rest. Elijah in his haste asked that his soul might be taken from him.

*And after much more:*

Is there, then, cause to grieve at a departure? By no means. For what is more absurd than that you should bewail as something particular to yourself what you know has been laid down for all? This is to exalt the mind above its condition, not to accept the common law, to refuse fellowship, to be puffed up with the mind of the flesh, [1140A] and to be ignorant of the measure of that same flesh. What is more absurd than not to know who you are, and to aim at what you are not? Or what is more imprudent than to be unable to bear, when it has happened, the thing you knew was going to happen? Nature herself calls us back, and by a certain comparison with herself draws us out from mournings of this sort. For what mourning is so heavy, or what grief so sharp, that in it the mind is not now and then relaxed? Nature has this property, that although men are set among sad circumstances, yet, if only they are men, they draw the mind away from mourning for a little while. There are even said to have been certain peoples who mourned the births of men and celebrated their deaths — and not imprudently; for they thought that those who had come into this age of life were to be mourned over, while those who had emerged from the storms and waves of this world they judged were to be attended with a joy that was not [1140B] unjust. Death is equal for all men, making no distinction for the rich. This so great a Caesar had daily before his eyes — a man who, as we saw and learned by experience while we were engaged in his service, displayed beneath the imperial cloak the raw service of another warfare. For first, in the twilight, he would hear the Mass for the dead; then the Mass for the army; last the Mass of the day; and at length, distributing food and drink lavishly to widows, orphans, and the other poor, he would relieve the suits and oppressions brought against churches, and at the last would sweat over the care of the empire. For as long as he stayed at Cassino, every night he went about the cloister and all the workshops of the monastery just as an abbot or any dean would, wishing to know how each man was living under the mastership of blessed Benedict. And when [1140C] morning came, he would walk with unshod feet through all the churches of the monastery for the sake of prayer. And although he did this, he was never cut off from the company of archbishops, bishops, abbots, clerics, or monks, so that there was fulfilled in him what we read: And his conversation is with the wise and the religious. And who would not marvel at his gravity, when, in order to settle the dissensions of the brothers which had arisen over the election of an abbot, he came to the chapter and, from the first hour of the day until evening, indulging in neither food nor drink, would not go out from there? And with what affection, with what love he loved the church of Cassino, he showed when he said that he would sooner allow all the privileges and grants of the churches to be torn up [1140D] than allow anything of the dignities and privileges of the Church of Cassino to be diminished; and that he would sooner allow the election of all the bishops, archbishops, and abbots in the whole Roman world to be taken away than that of the abbot of Cassino alone. For he used to say: If an abbot has not been elected by the consent of the monks, he is not an abbot; and whoever takes away the election from the monks tears up all the rights of the monastery. And while he did all these things, he was nevertheless a staff to the blind, food to the hungry, the hope of the wretched, the solace of mourners; and he so stood out in each single virtue as though he did not possess the rest. He honored priests as lords, clerics as fathers, the poor as sons, widows as mothers; frequent in admonitions, sleepless in vigils, he offered his tears to God, not to men. Tears roll over my face, and though my mind is set hard [1141A] I cannot conceal the grief I suffer. For after [sic: *Posquam*] he had made our littleness a disciple of Bertulf the chancellor; after he had rewarded me with the office of logothete, of recorder and of auditor; after he had granted me a seat at his feet; after he had made me his private secretary; after he had instructed me sufficiently in the things that pertain to eternal life — in your presence and with all the courtiers of the empire standing by, taking my hand and commending me to abbot Wibald, he said to him: If there are any traces of my esteem, any of my love, in your heart, show them upon this man; and for love of me hold him in the place of a most dear and only son. When the august emperor and Roman Caesar, named also father of his country, had said these things, while he was striving, [1141B] together with your excellency, to reconcile discordant clerics in peace, he rendered body and soul to heaven. Take comfort, therefore, most valiant lady, and according to that precept of Solomon: Receive consolation in place of sadness. But perhaps you say: How can I receive consolation, when I have lost him whom I loved? Are not these things common to us with the world itself and its elements, that we cannot hold in perpetuity what was created for a time? She has not lost her husband who displays chastity; she is not widowed of her marriage who has not changed her husband's name. Recall what David did: for when he had lost his son, he rose up, ate, drank, and said: I am going to him, but he will not return again to me. Considering these things, therefore, most excellent lady, so temper yourselves against the sadness that presses upon you, and so strive, [1141C] that you may be able to behold God, as is fitting, with a happy gaze. The congregation of Cassino also sends you the service of its greeting and consolation and prayer; and commending to you the boy Mino, son of the lord Ptolemy, who is with you, they ask that you hold him in the place of a son.



## III. *To the emperor Conrad II.--He congratulates him on his election as emperor, and exhorts him to rule the Roman empire vigorously and in a Christian manner, and to protect the monastery of Cassino.* [n: (Anno eod.)]

To CONRAD, most excellent emperor and most vigorous enlarger of the Roman empire, PETER THE DEACON wishes that he may be unceasingly protected by the shield of the Lord, and may triumph perpetually by his conquering right hand over enemies both spiritual and bodily.

Blessing and brightness and wisdom and thanksgiving [1141D] be to our almighty God for ever, who through you and in you has raised up for us the miracles of old, and by your recent election renews for the Roman empire its ancient joys. For neither the death of Hannibal, nor the extermination of the Goths, nor the slaughter of Lucius Catiline, nor the death of Attila and Totila so delights us as to have heard of your magnificent election, and to proclaim unceasingly the divine power in your successes. For through you the glory and honor of the eternal city is stretched out even to the sun's rising, and is enlarged, and, unfaithfulness being driven off, receives its sovereignty over the nations that rebel against it. Through you the dignity of the Roman empire is repaired, is lifted up, and is barely raised again to the ancient state of the dominion of the consuls — of Pompey, Scipio, Julius, Octavian, and [1142A] Trajan. Let all Christendom, then, utterly rejoice in your election; let east and west be glad; let the south echo back with the north; let the eternal city, mistress after God of all earthly things, celebrate festal calendars and solemn days, and pay her vows in the praises of our Lord Jesus Christ, bring forth songs, and with harmonious voices of gladness renew the ancient harmony, saying: *Let us sing to the Lord, for he is gloriously glorified.* For truly God is honored in you, who has chosen such a man as may, being one, pursue a thousand and put to flight ten thousand. Behold, through the four bounds of the Roman world your deeds, your labors, your notable struggles are reported; almost every tongue speaks of the glory of your empire. Indeed you have been made, as the [1142B] Apostle says, a spectacle to the world and to angels and to men; you have been made a champion of the Lord, the glory of the Roman empire, its increase, its ornament, and its marvelous diadem, the defense of the apostolic see, the solace of the sorrowful, the refreshment of the poor. At last the city of Rome has, by God's mercy, received back her own son, and rejoices that she keeps an emperor from the ancient stock of the Caesars. Yet for you, whom the divine majesty has chosen for so great an empire, it is fitting and becoming that, the more you have been raised by him above other mortals, so much the more you should be subject to him in all humility. For those whom he has given to rule in time, if you ascribe all your acts to his mercy and his power, he will grant to triumph perpetually. Guard therefore, unconquered emperor, before all things and above all things [1142C] the good of peace; let no spite, no envy creep into your breast. And treat justly and mercifully the Roman empire subjected to you, that above all the Roman church, through which you have received the seat of the Roman empire, may perceive — and perceiving may rejoice — that they have deserved so great and so good an Augustus, and have come under the rule of one who fears God. Let no man's [sic: *opos*] ever so please you that justice does not always outweigh it in your breast, so that justice should lose the place of equity. For what does it profit to have taken up the helm of the Roman empire, if you do not strive to excel all mortals in mercy, in justice, and in short in every goodness? For uprightness of mind ought to be a pattern and an example of virtue not only to you, but also to those who come after. And by doing so [1142D] you will always have the grace of [sic: *omnipontentis*] God as your fellow-worker; by whose supports you will both subdue all enemies, and will rejoice in peace both present and to come. Know, moreover, that the Church of Cassino daily entreats the almighty Lord for the standing of your empire, that he may bring the works of his mercy begun through you to a worthy end, and that he may protect and defend you from all evils, and for so great a labor may grant you hereafter the citizenship of eternal life. And we ask the loftiness of your nobility, most exalted conqueror, that you protect the Church of Cassino, the mother of all monasteries; for it is known to have been once defended by the protections of your predecessors and enriched by their wealth. You [1143A] therefore, as you are shown to be their equal in the nobility of your wisdom, in power, in prudence, in all the things that are of this world, and — what is more distinguished — in upright character, so also strive to be their equal in the exaltation of the monastery of Cassino. Make blessed Benedict your debtor on earth, that whatever you bestow on him you may deserve to receive a hundredfold in heaven. For to this end has heavenly Providence, which governs all things and orders all things from the beginning of the world, chosen a man of such vigor and prudence in the Roman commonwealth — especially at this time, when, our sins requiring it, [1144A] soundness has failed, and truths have been diminished among the sons of men — that you should exalt the Churches. And may God our Lord, who freely lavishes the gifts of his goodness on those who ask, protect and bless you with the right hand of his strength; and, when the enemies of both substances have been separated off, may he open to you the doors of the heavenly life as you return to him with the palm of the contest, and may he grant you to rejoice perpetually with himself in heavenly goods.
