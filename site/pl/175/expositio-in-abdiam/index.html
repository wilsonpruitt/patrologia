Hugh of Saint Victor

Hugo de S. Victore

c. 1096 – 11 February 1141

Theologian and head of the school of the abbey of Saint-Victor in Paris, and the founding master of the Victorine tradition of learning and contemplation. His work ranges across the liberal arts (the Didascalicon), sacramental theology (De sacramentis christianae fidei), and mystical writing. The present piece is a short exposition of the Magnificat, the canticle of the Blessed Virgin Mary.

PREFACE.

371CObadiah, fourth in the order of the prophets, simple in speech and manifold in sense; sparing in words, but copious in judgments. According to that saying of Solomon: A wise man is made known by few words (Eccle. XX), he directs his prophecy literally against Idumaea; allegorically against the world, tropologically against the flesh he sharpens his pen; bearing the type of the Savior, he subtly introduces his coming, through whom the world is destroyed, through whom the flesh is worn away, through whom liberty 372Cis restored. Hence that word: If the Son shall make you free, you shall be free indeed (Joan. VIII). This is the Obadiah who under king Ahab fed a hundred prophets hidden in caves; and while he ministered bodily nourishment, he received spiritual nourishment from God. Now the aforesaid man prophesied at the same time as the other prophets also, namely Amos, Joel and Hosea. And he lies buried in Samaria with Elisha and John the Baptist.

Propterea ad sanandum hoc vitium deprecari nos oportet misericordiam Domini, ut ipse solita pietate animae taedio suo languenti internae refectionis pabulum admoveat: ut quod ipsa absens nescit appetere, gustus praesentis admonita incipiat amare. Datur ergo huic petitioni spiritus fortitudinis, ut fatiscentem animam erigat: quatenus illa, pristini vigoris virtute recepta ab affectu sui taedii ad desiderium interni saporis convalescat. Creat ergo spiritus in corde famem justitiae, ut dum hic per desiderium pietatis fortiter accenditur, illic 404Bpro praemio plenam beatitudinis consequatur satietatem.

Quinta petitio est contra avaritiam, qua dicitur: Dimitte nobis debita nostra, sicut et nos dimittimus debitoribus nostris. Justum enim est ut in reddendo debito non debeat esse anxius, qui in exigendo noluerit esse avarus. Atque ideo cum a nobis per Dei gratiam vitium avaritiae tollitur, qualiter a nostro debito absolvi debeamus, exposita salutis conditione, donatur. Huic ergo petitioni datur spiritus consilii, qui doceat nos in hoc saeculo libenter peccanti in nos misericordiam impendere, quatenus in futuro cum pro peccatis nostris rationem reddituri sumus, mereamur misericordiam invenire.

Sexta petitio contra gulam, qua dicitur: Et ne 404Cnos inducas in tentationem, id est, ne induci permittas in tentationem. Haec est tentatio quae nos illecebra carnis saepe per naturalem appetitum ad excessum trahere nititur, et latenter voluptatem subjicit, dum manifeste nobis de necessitate blanditur. In quam profecto tentationem tunc nequaquam inducimur, si sic studemus secundum mensuram necessitatis naturae subsidium impendere ut tamen semper meminerimus appetitum ab illecebra voluptatis coercere. Quod ut implere valeamus datur nobis petentibus spiritus intelligentiae, ut interna refectio verbi Dei appetitum exteriorem cohibeat, et mens spirituali cibo roboretur, ut eam non valeat corporalis egestas frangere, nec carnis voluptas superare. Propterea namque et ipse Dominus tentatori suo 404Ddum esurienti sibi fraudulentiam de exterioris panis suggestione faceret, respondit dicens: Non in solo pane vivit homo, sed in omni verbo quod procedit de ore Dei (Matth. IV); ut aperte demonstraret quod cum mens illo interius pane reficitur, non magno opere curat si foris ad tempus famem carnis patiatur. Datur igitur contra gulam spiritus intelligentiae: sed ille ad cor veniens emundat illud atque purificat.

Septima petitio est contra luxuriam, qua dicitur: Libera nos a malo. Nam licet, ut alibi diximus, multiplex est malum, ut malum corporis et malum animae; malum quod est culpa, malum quod est poena; malum hujus saeculi, et malum futuri; tamen 405Apraecipuum malum potest quodammodo censeri luxuria, quae hominem illecebris captum in 406Aservitutem redigit. Unde per donum sapientiae liberari petimus dicentes: Libera nos a malo. Amen.

The eyes therefore are doves upon the brooks of waters; because both by the truth of judgment he freely enlightens, and by the kindness of mercy he freely adorns with gifts. First against ignorance, secondly against concupiscence, thirdly against misery. Whence the Apostle: I know, he says, that there dwelleth not in me (that is, in my flesh) that which is good (Rom. VII). Likewise the same: They who are in the flesh cannot please God (Rom. VIII). But it is to be known that when the divine discourse inveighs against the flesh, it is not nature but fault that is arraigned; not the condition of nature, but the defect of justice; not the joining of the elements, but the motion of vices. For as in man there are two 383Cnatures, namely spirit and flesh, so there are two motions by which each of them is moved: one toward the affection of justice, the other departing from the affection of grace. Thence are born virtue and vice: so that if it be a motion of the spirit, it is virtue; but a motion of the flesh is nothing else than a motion of the mind not obeying the spirit. That one, being spiritual, makes men spiritual. This one, being carnal, makes men carnal. That one is called spirit; this is designated by the name of flesh. Further, since occasion has offered itself concerning the origin of virtue and of vice, it is well to take it up a little more deeply, and to discuss more subtly all the general motions of the mind. The general motion of the mind, then, is divided fivefold. For sometimes the heart of man is moved only by the devil, sometimes by man alone, sometimes by God alone, sometimes by man and the devil, sometimes by man and God. The first is 383Dthrough the suggestion of fault; the second through a simple consideration of nature; the third through a momentary inspiration of grace; the fourth through unlawful delight; the fifth through the affections of the virtues and the contemplation of the Bridegroom. The first and the second are harmless to man; the third glorious; the fourth penal; the fifth rewarding. The fourth is vicious and to be condemned. The fifth is virtuous and to be crowned. The third is to be received eagerly. The second is indifferent along with the first. The fourth is referred to the flesh, the fifth to the spirit; the third to God; the first and the second to neither. But the general motion of the flesh is sevenfold. For sometimes it is against God, sometimes against the neighbor, sometimes against itself, often against the world, 384Amany times against business. Sometimes upon the neighbor, sometimes upon itself. Against God, pride; against the neighbor, envy; against itself, anger; against the world, avarice; against business, sloth. Upon the neighbor, lust; upon itself, gastrimargia, or gluttony. These seven vices are that household which the Lord left in the midst of his people, that in it he might train Israel; whence also from the allotment of their names their property may be designated. And these are the names. The first is called the Pherezite; the second the Hethite; the third the Hevite; the fourth the Amorrhite; the fifth the Gergesite; the sixth the Jebusite; the seventh the Chanaanite. The first is interpreted surpassing; the second, wasting away or enduring a voyage; the third, gathering stones; the fourth, embittered or embittering; the fifth, driving out the 384Bsettler; the sixth, trodden down or treading down; the seventh, changed or changing. By the first is designated pride; by the second envy; by the third anger; by the fourth sloth; by the fifth avarice; by the sixth lust; by the seventh is designated gluttony. But we, since we purpose to pass on to what follows, leave the fitting of each one to the easy exercise of the reader. It is to be known, however, that that motion is called carnal specially which serves the flesh alone and is administered through the flesh, which here is designated by Esau. He prepares snares for his brother Jacob; because he assails virtue in three ways: namely through the incontinence of lust, through the immoderation of gluttony, and through the wantonness of the senses. By the first the flower of chastity is stormed; by the second the honor of sobriety; by the third 384Cthe comeliness of decency. ALLEGORICAL. Behold, Obadiah in the present passage describes the land historically and divides it spiritually. We read of many divisions in the divine utterance: the divisions, namely, of the Red 397DSea, whence comes that word: Who divided the Red Sea into divisions (Psal. CXXXV). And in the Apostle: Now there are divisions of graces, but the same Spirit. And there are divisions of ministries, but the same Lord. And there are divisions of operations, but the same God, who worketh all things in all (I Cor. XII). But we read that there have also been four divisions of the land. The first was made by Josue; the second by Ezechiel; the third here in Obadiah. And the fourth by the Saviour. The first is the sacrament of the second, the second of the third, the third of the fourth. Whence the first, by the same number, so stands historically as the second does spiritually according to that law; the third to the fourth. Consider, reader, the form of the order and the splendor of the proportion. For as the first 398Ais to the second, so the third stands to the fourth. And as the first to the third, so the second to the fourth, so the second to the third. As to the judgment of proportions, I commit the reader to arithmetic for his instruction. But our purpose is to search out the hiding-places of allegorical obscurity, and to bring its darkness out into the light of explanation. The first division of the land, then, is made when the storming of the enemies has gone before, or their subjugation; the second without hostile surrender or civil upheaval; the third with a voluntary subjugation of the enemies; the fourth without condition or sinew of subjection, but with the glory and joy of mutual love. The first division is made with punishment and misery; the second with punishment without misery; the third without punishment and misery, though the shame of it is retained; the fourth without punishment and misery, every weakness being removed. The battle is to be unwilling 398Bto consent to sin; the misery is that the unwilling man be harassed by an unlawful incitement; the shame of the misery is to be titillated by it; the removal of the weakness is the shutting out of temptation. There are four liberties: liberty of choice, liberty of exercise, liberty of counsel, liberty of joy. The first division was made with liberty of choice, but not with liberty of exercise. For the will, moved by its own choice, stormed whom it could, but did not subjugate all whom it wished. Whence it had liberty in the faculty and motion of judgment, but did not have it in the business of exercise; for if it had truly had it, it would have subjected all men to itself at a nod. The second had the first liberty and the second, but lacked the third; for moving itself by its own will, it uses the wished-for faculty of exercise; but liberty of counsel is wanting to it, because it did not know how to reach the bounds of knowledge and of order, 398Cof the outcome, of the beginning. The third had all these three but lacked the fourth; because it obtained both will in motion, and faculty in act, and brightness in understanding, but did not have security in affection. But the fourth possesses all things; for it possesses both free will in choosing, and swift faculty in acting, and sincere brightness in intention, and secure perpetuity in enjoyment. The first liberty, then, is common to all; the second is general to the converted, but special to the imperfect; the third is general to the perfect, and special to those who soldier for God in this world; the fourth is singular to those who contemplate the Bridegroom and rejoice with him. The first belongs properly to servants; the second belongs properly 398Dto hirelings; the third properly to friends; the fourth properly to sons. The first division is made through the grace of compunction; the second through the discipline and purpose of conversion; the third through the ecstasy of mind and the eminence of contemplation; the fourth through the taking up of glory and honor, and of immortality, straightway unto the vision of God. The first comes to pass when we withdraw from the darkness and servitude of sin; the second, when we follow the fleeing Bridegroom to the mountain of myrrh and to the hills of Lebanon; the third, when we go up through the desert like an army set in array; the fourth, when we enter the courts of the heavenly city with hymns. There are four things which have been given to the elect for their profit and salvation and happiness: namely exercises, joys, merits, rewards — the exercises of religion, the joys of contemplation, the merits 399Aof perfection, the rewards of the most blessed vision; the exercises, I say, of discipline, the joys of spiritual union and of the heavenly bond, and the merits of justice, the rewards of glory. The first division, then, pertains to the exercises, the second to the joys, the third to the merits, the fourth to the rewards. III). Hence again it is written: But the sons of Judah could not destroy the Jebusite, the inhabitant of Jerusalem (Judic. I). Likewise in the same place: The sons of Manasses could not overthrow these cities, but the Chanaanite began to dwell in his own land. But after the children of Israel grew strong, they subjected the Chanaanites and made them their tributaries, and did not slay them (Ibid.).

399BOf the second division it is thus read in Ezechiel: This is the land which you shall cast by lot to the tribes of Israel, and these are their portions, saith the Lord (Ezech. XLVIII). Toward the northern quarter, three gates of three tribes. On the southern side, as many of another three; on the eastern side, as many of another three; on the western side, as many of another three; from each quarter thou shalt measure five hundred cubits and four thousand. What are these four quarters, if not the four principal kinds of compunction, through which the holy mind enters and walks toward the temple of holy contemplation? And these are: fear of punishment, grief at the present exile, hope of the heavenly reward, love of the spiritual marriage. Fear is the western quarter; grief, the northern; hope, the eastern; love, 399Cthe southern. And what are the three gates set at each of them, if not the occasions of the aforesaid compunction, dedicated to the Trinity, consecrated to faith, hope, charity. For he who rightly fears punishments flees vices by faith, presses toward merits by hope, runs to rewards by charity. But he to whom the misery of the present exile is a grief presses by faith toward obedience, hastens by hope toward justice, and flies on the wings of charity to glory. Furthermore, he who thirsts for the unfading reward flees by faith the shipwreck of vices, enters by hope the racecourse of the spiritual contest, and, at the urging of love's zeal, hastens to the reward. And he who longs for the bond of the one Heavenly One removes from himself by faith the stain of all iniquity, follows everywhere and always by hope the rule of innocence, burns with the ardent desire of charity for the heavenly marriage, and with vehement 399Dzeal hastens to the spiritual nuptials. In all these, the vision of the Trinity is sought, the eternity of glory, the possession of happiness. But that from the gate up to the temple he reckons five hundred cubits and four thousand — this is because the hundred and the thousand both signify perfection. The hundred multiplied five times signifies the honorable and perfect continence of the senses. The thousand multiplied four times, according to the faith and rule of the Gospel, signifies purity of conscience, perfection of life, innocence of manners. By the four thousand, then, is designated integrity of life; by the five hundred cubits, soundness of reputation. From the gate, therefore, up to the temple these things are reckoned; because from the entrance of compunction up to the joy of holy contemplation there is need 400Aof honorableness of the senses, integrity of life, purity of conscience. There is need, I say, of continence of the senses, justice of acts, innocence of affections. Thus one goes from the gate to the temple; because through the guarding of and care for the aforesaid one arrives at the bond of the spiritual marriage and at the sweetness of the Bridegroom. Whence there is added next: And the name of the city from that day, The Lord is there (Ibid.). From that day, that brightness, that bond, that union, the city's name is, The Lord is there; because he guards the going out of his bride to action, and her entrance to contemplation. He guards the gates of profession, watches over the streets of religion, venerates the temple of sanctification, fortifies the citadel of perfection, protects the people of spiritual exercises, and guards the choir of heavenly manners. 400BIn her too the Lord is the bread of life, the wine of heavenly justice, the garment of justice, the law of clemency, the light of spiritual understanding, the water of spiritual doctrine. What more. From the time when the city's name is, The Lord is there (Rom. I), in her God becomes all things to all. These things we have said briefly of the first division and the second.

It remains that we say something of the third and the fourth. And as the first pertains to the exercises, the second to the joys, so the third looks to the merits, the fourth to the rewards. And as those two are distinguished by the number ten because of the perfection of action, so these are divided by the number seven because of spiritual perfection and the joy of heavenly contemplation. Therefore Ab dias says:

400CAnd they that are toward the south shall inherit the mount of Esau, etc. Under this sevenfold number there are set against the seven principal vices seven divisions of the land, seven petitions of the Lord's Prayer, seven gifts of the Holy Spirit, seven virtues contrary to those vices, and last of all seven beatitudes. In the first place are set the seven vices, against which are set the antidotes of all the foregoing. The first of the vices is pride, the second envy, the third anger, the fourth accidie or sadness, the fifth avarice, the sixth gluttony, the seventh lust. Against these, in the second place, are established the seven petitions which are contained in the Lord's Prayer. The first, in which it is said to God: Hallowed be thy name; the second, in which it is said: Thy kingdom come; the third, in which it is said: Thy will be done, as 400Din heaven and on earth; the fourth, in which it is said: Give us this day our daily bread; the fifth, in which it is said: Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors; the sixth, in which it is said: And lead us not into temptation, the seventh, in which it is said: But deliver us from evil. Afterward, in the third place, there follow the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit. The first, the spirit of the fear of the Lord; the second, the spirit of piety; the third, the spirit of knowledge; the fourth, the spirit of fortitude; the fifth, the spirit of counsel; the sixth, the spirit of understanding; the seventh, the spirit of wisdom. Then in the fourth place there follow four virtues. The first, poverty of spirit, that is, humility; the second, meekness, or kindness; the third, compunction, or grief; 401Athe fourth, hunger for justice, or good desire; the fifth, mercy; the sixth, cleanness of heart; the seventh, peace. But in the very last place are arranged the seven beatitudes. The first, the kingdom of heaven; the second, the possession of the land of the living; the third, consolation; the fourth, the fullness of justice; the fifth, mercy; the sixth, the vision of God; the seventh, the sonship of God.

Pride is the love of one's own excellence. Envy is hatred of another's happiness. Anger is unjust rage, or the unlawful madness of a mind stirred up. Accidie, or sadness, is the disordered bitterness of the soul, or a perverse weariness of mind joined with sorrow. Avarice, which is the immoderate appetite for having, is signified by the mount of Esau. By the Philistines, which is interpreted falling by the cup, envy: for it falls as though by a foreign 401Bdraught, because it envies another's happiness. Distinguish these thus in the first place, that thou mayest understand the vices themselves as certain sicknesses of the soul, or wounds of the inner man; but the man himself as one sick, God as the physician, the gifts of the Holy Spirit as the antidote, the virtues as health, the beatitudes as the joy of happiness. There are, then, seven capital or principal vices, and from these all evils arise. These are the springs of the dark abyss, from which the rivers of Babylon go forth and, drawn out into every land, pour abroad the droppings of iniquity. Of which rivers the Prophet, in the person of the faithful people, sang, saying: Upon the rivers of Babylon, there we sat and wept, when we remembered thee, O Sion (Psal. CXXXVI). Of these seven vices — layers waste, corrupting the whole integrity of nature, 401Cand at the same time bringing forth the seeds of all evils — let us speak so far as we think sufficient for the present task of explanation. There are seven, then: of these, three strip the man; the fourth scourges him when stripped; the fifth casts out the scourged; the sixth seduces the cast out; the seventh subjects the seduced to servitude. For pride takes God away from a man. Envy takes his neighbor from him. Anger takes himself from him. Sadness scourges him when stripped. Avarice casts out the scourged. Gluttony seduces the cast out. Lust subjects the seduced to servitude. Now, returning, let us explain each in order. For pride is the love of one's own excellence, when the mind loves the good which it has singly — that is, apart from him from whom it received the good. 401DO pestilent pride, what art thou doing? Why dost thou persuade the streamlet to divide itself from the spring? Why dost thou persuade the ray to take itself away from the sun? Why? Save that the one, when it ceases to be poured into, may dry up; and the other, when it turns away from the one that illumines it, may become dark? And that each, while it ceases to receive what it does not yet have, may straightway lose also that which it has? And so it comes about that it cannot profitably have even what it has, while it does not love that good in him from whom it has it. For as every good is truly from God, so no good can profitably be had outside God; nay rather, by this very thing is what is had lost, that it is not loved with him and in him from whom it is had. For whoever knows only this, to love the good in himself which he has, must needs, when he has looked upon in another a good which he does not have, 402Abe tormented by his own imperfection all the more bitterly, the less he loves him in whom every good consists. And therefore envy always follows pride: because he who does not fix his love there where every good is, the more perversely he is exalted over what is his own, the more grievously is he tormented at another's good. Most justly, then, has a punishment of its own been assigned to elation, namely the envy which it begets out of itself: which, because it would not love the whole and common good, now rightly wastes away with spite at another's good. Assuredly the success of another's happiness would not burn it, if it possessed by love him in whom every good is. Now, therefore, as much as it lifts itself up by elation against the Creator, so much does it fall by spite beneath its neighbor; and as much as it is deceitfully raised up there, so much is it truly cast down here. But neither can a corruption once begun come to a stand hisistere 402B. For as soon as envy has been born of pride, the wretched mind itself brings forth anger out of itself. For it is angry with itself over its own imperfection for this reason, that it does not rejoice through charity at another's good. And therefore even that which it has begins to displease it, since it recognizes in another that which it cannot have. Therefore what it could have had only in God through charity, because it tried also through elation to have outside God, it loses its neighbor through envy, and itself through anger. Since therefore, all things being lost, there is nothing whence the unhappy conscience may rejoice, it is dashed against itself through sadness; and she who would not devoutly rejoice at another's good is justly tormented by her own evil. After pride, then, and envy, and anger, which strip the man, 402Cthere straightway follows sadness, which scourges him when stripped bare. Then avarice succeeds it, which casts out the scourged, because, the inward joy being lost, it compels him to seek consolation outside. Afterward gluttony comes on, which seduces the cast out; because this vice, tempting first of all as it were from close at hand a soul that gapes after outward things, entices it through natural appetite itself into excess. Last of all lust comes upon him, which violently subjects the seduced to servitude; because after the flesh has been puffed up by surfeiting, the soul, grown soft and nervelessly dissolved, cannot conquer the burning of lust that comes upon it. For the mind, shamefully subdued, serves a most savage domination; and unless the Saviour's mercy, entreated, comes to its aid, there will now be no source from which the lost liberty may be restored 402Dto the captive in his servitude.

And so there follow seven petitions against the seven vices, by which he is entreated to come to our aid who both taught us to pray and promised that he would give to those who pray the good that would serve to heal our wounds and to loose the yoke of our captivity. But before we come to the explanation of these, we wish first to show by yet another likeness how great a corruption the aforesaid vices beget in us, so that the more dangerous the sickness is shown to be, the more necessary the medicine may be proved. Through pride, then, the heart is puffed up; through envy it withers; through anger it bursts; through sadness it is ground down and, as it were, reduced to dust; through avarice it is scattered; through gluttony it is tainted and, as it were, made sodden; through lust it is 403Atrampled underfoot and reduced to mud, so that the wretch may now say: I am stuck fast in the mire of the deep, and there is no sure standing. I am come into the depth of the sea, and a tempest hath overwhelmed me (Psal. LXVIII). And when the soul has been stuck fast in this mire of the deep, and wrapped about in the mud of defilement and uncleanness, and can by no means be plucked out, let it cry to him and beg his help of whom the Psalmist speaks, saying: With expectation I have waited for the Lord, and he was attentive to me, and he heard my prayers, and he brought me out of the pit of misery and out of the mire of dregs (Psal. XXXIX). For this reason, then, he himself taught us to pray, that we might understand our whole good to be from God. The first petition, therefore, is against pride, in which we say to God: Hallowed be thy name. For here we ask that he grant us to fear and venerate his name, 403Bso that we may be subject to him through humility, because through pride we have shown ourselves rebellious and stubborn. To this petition is given the spirit of the fear of the Lord, that he, coming to the heart, may create in it the virtue of humility, which may heal the disease of pride, so that a humble man may be able to attain to the kingdom of heaven, which the proud angel lost through elation.

The second petition is against envy, in which it is said: Thy kingdom come. For the kingdom of God is the salvation of men: because God is then said to reign in men when men themselves are made subject to him, both now by cleaving to him through faith, and afterward by inhering in him through sight. He therefore who asks that the kingdom of God may come assuredly seeks the salvation of all; and in that he petitions for the common salvation of all, 403Che shows that he reproves the vice of spite. To this petition is given the spirit of piety, that he, coming to the heart, may kindle it to kindness, so that a man may himself attain to that same possession of the eternal inheritance to which he desires others to attain.

The third petition is against anger, in which it is said: Thy will be done, as in heaven and on earth. By this he declares that whatever the will of God dispenses, whether in himself or in others, according to the judgment of his own good pleasure, is pleasing to him. To this petition, then, is given the spirit of knowledge, that he, coming to the heart, may instruct it and wholesomely prick it, so that a man may know that the evil which he suffers comes from his own fault, but that whatever good he has proceeds from the mercy of God; and that by this he may learn, whether in the evils which he endures or in the goods which he does not have, 403Dnot to grow angry against the Creator, but in all things to show patience. Most excellently, then, is the anger and indignation of the soul mitigated by compunction of heart, which is born of humility, the spirit of knowledge working inwardly; because, on the contrary, anger kills the foolish man, when, driven and blinded by the vice of impatience, he does not recognize either that he has deserved the evil which he suffers, or that he has received through grace the good which he has. And this virtue — that is, compunction or grief — the reward of consolation follows, so that he who here of his own accord afflicts himself before God with lamentations may there deserve to find true joy and gladness.

The fourth petition is against sadness, or accidie, in which it is said: Give us 404Athis day our daily bread. For sadness is a weariness of mind joined with sorrow: when the mind, in a manner wasted away and embittered by its own vice, does not desire inward goods, and, all vigor being dead, grows glad at no desire of spiritual refreshment. Therefore, to heal this vice, we must entreat the mercy of the Lord, that he with his wonted kindness may set the food of inward refreshment before the soul languishing in its weariness; so that what she, being absent from it, knows not how to desire, she may, admonished by the taste of it when present, begin to love. To this petition, then, is given the spirit of fortitude, to raise up the fainting soul, so that she, having received back the strength of her former vigor, may grow strong out of the disposition of her weariness into the desire of the inward savor. The Spirit, therefore, creates in the heart a hunger for justice, so that while here it is strongly kindled by the desire of piety, there 404Bit may obtain for its reward the full satiety of blessedness.

The fifth petition is against avarice, in which it is said: Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors. For it is just that he should not have to be anxious in paying his debt who has been unwilling to be grasping in exacting it. And therefore, when the vice of avarice is taken from us by God's grace, it is granted us — the condition of salvation being set forth — how we ought to be absolved from our own debt. To this petition, then, is given the spirit of counsel, which may teach us in this world willingly to bestow mercy upon him who sins against us, so that in the world to come, when we are to render an account for our sins, we may deserve to find mercy.

The sixth petition is against gluttony, in which it is said: And lead 404Cus not into temptation — that is, do not permit us to be led into temptation. This is the temptation which by the allurement of the flesh often strives to draw us through natural appetite into excess, and secretly slips pleasure beneath us while openly it flatters us with talk of necessity. Into which temptation we are assuredly in no way led, if we so strive to furnish nature its support according to the measure of necessity that we nevertheless always remember to restrain the appetite from the allurement of pleasure. And that we may be able to fulfill this, the spirit of understanding is given to us when we ask, that the inward refreshment of the word of God may check the outward appetite, and the mind be strengthened by spiritual food, so that neither bodily want may be able to break it, nor the pleasure of the flesh to overcome it. For this reason too the Lord himself, 404Dwhen his tempter was practising deceit upon him in his hunger by the suggestion of outward bread, answered, saying: Not in bread alone doth man live, but in every word that proceedeth from the mouth of God (Matth. IV); that he might plainly show that when the mind is refreshed inwardly by that bread, it does not greatly care if outwardly it suffers for a time the hunger of the flesh. There is given, therefore, against gluttony the spirit of understanding: and he, coming to the heart, cleanses and purifies it.

The seventh petition is against lust, in which it is said: Deliver us from evil. For although, as we have said elsewhere, evil is manifold — as the evil of the body and the evil of the soul; the evil which is fault, the evil which is punishment; the evil of this world, and the evil of the world to come — nevertheless 405Alust can in a manner be reckoned the chief evil, which reduces to 406Aservitude the man it has caught with its allurements. Whence through the gift of wisdom we ask to be delivered, saying: Deliver us from evil. Amen.
