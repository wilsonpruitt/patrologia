William of Saint-Thierry

Guillelmus abbas

c. 1085 – 1148

Benedictine abbot of Saint-Thierry near Reims, who later laid down his abbacy to become a Cistercian monk at Signy; a close friend of Bernard of Clairvaux and the author of the first book of his life. He wrote his own exposition of the Song of Songs; the present work is an anthology he wove together from Bernard's sermons on the same book — hence 'ex Bernardo contexta,' compiled out of Bernard.

262 ADMONITIO.

407Sequens expositio in priora duo capita Cantici canticorum ex codice Dunensi a nobis vulgata est in superiori editione, et Bernardi in Cantica Sermonibus subjuncta tanquam genuinus ejus fetus, quem Guillelmus Sancti Theoderici abbas designaverit in lib. I de ejus Vita cap. 12, his verbis: « Itaque tunc disseruit mihi de Cantico canticorum, quantum tempus illud infirmitatis meae mihi permisit, moraliter tantum, intermissis mysteriis Scripturae illius, quia sic volebam, et sic petieram ab eo: singulisque diebus quaecunque super hoc audiebam, ne mihi effugerent, scripto alligabam, in quantum mihi Deus donabat et memoria juvabat. » Quibus verbis ipsammet expositionem indicari tunc nobis visum est. Verum re attentius expensa, nihil aliud esse existimamus, quam breve compendium priorum 51 Bernardi sermonum in Cantica, a quodam homine studioso et pio concinnatum. Quam vero designat Guillelmus, ipsa esse videtur quae sub ejus nomine edita est in tomo IV Bibliothecae Cisterciensis: ubi attendenda sunt Praefationis haec verba: « Non autem profundiora illa mysteria, quae in eo continentur, attentamus de Christo et Ecclesia; sed cohibentes nos intra nos . . . sensum tantummodo moralem aliquem perstringimus. » Quae verba recte praemissis ex Vita Bernardi conveniunt, cum ait Guillelmus se a Bernardo expositionem in Cantica petiisse « moraliter tantum, intermissis mysteriis Scripturae illius. » Haec ratio nos movit, ut sequentem expositionem inter Collectanea ex Bernardi operibus facta referremus, et quidem post superiores duos Guillelmi libros, quibus subjicitur sub nomine sancti Bernardi in codice Dunensi: cui Guillelmo si quis etiam sequentem commentationem tribuere velit, non repugnabimus; maxime, cum stylus ejus satis ad Guillelmi stylum accedat.

407C263 1. Tres sunt status amoris Dei in anima christiana. Primus, sensualis vel animalis; secundus, rationalis; tertius, spiritualis vel intellectualis. De quibus Dominus in Evangelio: Diliges Dominum Deum tuum ex toto corde tuo, et ex tota anima tua, et ex tota virtute tua (Matth. XXII, 37). Primus significatur in corde, id est in particula infirmae carnis, propter pium circa humanitatem Salvatoris affectum: secundus, in anima, quo jam primus ille amor animatur et vivificatur; cum jam mysteria fidei et sacramentorum virtutem humili pietate scrutatur: tertius, in omnibus viribus; quia in hoc gradu, quantumcunque profeceris, dicere poteris, 408CEt dixi, Nunc coepi (Psal. LXXVI, 12). Primus adeo delectatur in contemplatione et dulcedine humanitatis Christi, ut merito eis dicat Christus: Expedit vobis ut ego vadam (Joan. XVI, 7). Secundus cor jam habet ardens, sed oculi ejus adhuc tenentur, cum ei loquitur Christus, sed in via, et aperit ei Scripturas (Luc. XXIV, 15-32). Tertius jam tota fiducia dicit, Et si novimus Christum secundum carnem, sed nunc jam non novimus (II Cor. V, 16): quod nequaquam primus, et vix potest dicere secundus. Quod enim apostoli in corporali Domini praesentia passi sunt, hoc isti patiuntur in quadam pia mentis suae phantasia de humanitatis Christi dulcissima memoria, 409Aet conversationis et operum ejus, et passionis et resurrectionis et ascensionis ejus, et bonorum nobis per haec collatorum, eum amoris affectum induentes, quo Petrus tenere et sicut homo hominem Deum amans, suggerebat ei passionis suae seriem praedicenti: Absit a te, Domine, propitius esto tibi, non fiet istud (Matth. XVI, 22). Tertius autem gradus totus extra hominem, supra hominem; totus in Deo, Deum propter Deum, justum diligit propter justum, et bonum propter bonum; non quia bonum nobis, sed quia bonum in se: sic affectus a Deo et bono et justo, ut malit non esse, quam bonus et justus in Deo non esse. Haec est fortis ut mors dilectio (Cant. VIII, 6): haec est virtus induens Apostolos ex alto (Luc. XXIV, 49). Unus enim spiritus 409Beffecta cum Deo sancta anima, non magis potest pati ut vel ad modicum declinet a justitia, quam ipsa justitia pati potest ut desinat esse justitia.

262 NOTICE.

407The following exposition on the first two chapters of the Song of Songs, from the Dunes manuscript, was published by us in the earlier edition, and appended to Bernard's Sermons on the Song of Songs as a genuine offspring of his, which William, abbot of Saint-Thierry, is said to have designated in Book I of his Life, chapter 12, in these words: « And so at that time he discoursed to me on the Song of Songs, so far as that time of my infirmity permitted me, in a moral sense only, the mysteries of that Scripture being set aside, because so I wished, and so I had asked of him: and each day, whatever I heard upon this, lest it escape me, I bound down in writing, so far as God granted me and memory helped. » By which words it then seemed to us that this very exposition was being indicated. But, the matter having been more carefully weighed, we judge it to be nothing other than a brief compendium of the first 51 sermons of Bernard on the Song of Songs, put together by some studious and pious man. But the one which William designates seems to be that which was published under his name in Volume IV of the Cistercian Library: where these words of the Preface are to be noted: « But we do not attempt those deeper mysteries which are contained in it, concerning Christ and the Church; but restraining ourselves within ourselves . . . we touch only upon some moral sense. » Which words rightly agree with the passage set forth above from the Life of Bernard, when William says that he had asked of Bernard an exposition on the Song of Songs « in a moral sense only, the mysteries of that Scripture being set aside. » This reasoning moved us to place the following exposition among the Collectanea made from Bernard's works, and indeed after the two earlier books of William, to which it is appended under the name of Saint Bernard in the Dunes manuscript: and if anyone should wish to attribute the following commentary also to this William, we shall not resist; especially since its style comes close enough to William's style.

407C263 1. There are three states of the love of God in the Christian soul. The first, sensual or animal; the second, rational; the third, spiritual or intellectual. Of which the Lord in the Gospel: Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with thy whole heart, and with thy whole soul, and with thy whole strength (Matth. XXII, 37). The first is signified in the heart, that is, in the little portion of weak flesh, on account of the devout affection toward the humanity of the Savior: the second, in the soul, by which that first love is now animated and quickened, when it now searches out with humble devotion the mysteries of faith and the power of the sacraments: the third, in all thy strength; because in this degree, however far you have advanced, you will be able to say, 408CAnd I said, Now have I begun (Psal. LXXVI, 12). The first so delights in the contemplation and sweetness of the humanity of Christ, that Christ rightly says to them: It is expedient for you that I go away (Joan. XVI, 7). The second now has a burning heart, but his eyes are still held, when Christ speaks to him, but on the way, and opens the Scriptures to him (Luc. XXIV, 15-32). The third now says with all confidence, And if we have known Christ according to the flesh, yet now we know him so no longer (II Cor. V, 16): which the first can by no means say, and the second scarcely can. For what the apostles experienced in the bodily presence of the Lord, this these men experience in a certain devout imagining of their mind, out of the most sweet memory of the humanity of Christ, 409Aand of his manner of life and his works, and of his passion and resurrection and ascension, and of the good things conferred upon us through these — putting on that affection of love with which Peter, loving tenderly and, as a man, loving the man who was God, urged upon him as he foretold the course of his passion: Far be it from thee, Lord; be merciful to thyself; this shall not be (Matth. XVI, 22). But the third degree is wholly beyond man, above man; wholly in God, loving God for God's sake, the just for the sake of the just, and the good for the sake of the good; not because it is good for us, but because it is good in itself: so affected by God and by good and by justice, that it would rather not be at all than be good and just apart from God. This is love strong as death (Cant. VIII, 6): this is the power that clothes the Apostles from on high (Luc. XXIV, 49). For the holy soul, made 409Bone spirit with God, can no more endure to turn aside even a little from justice, than justice itself can endure to cease to be justice.

2.

Inter hunc vero per summum et inferiorem illum statum positus status rationalis, inferiorem quidem regit et illuminat, superiorem vero aliquando attentans manu pii desiderii, mox ad se refugit, et sicut David percutit cor suum, quia in Christum Domini manum mittere praesumpsit. Si enim bestia tetigerit montem, lapidatur (Hebr. XII, 20); et cum fit vox super firmamentum, quod imminet capiti animalium, stant, nec procedunt, et submittunt alas conatuum suorum. Nihil enim ibi agit rationalis disciplina, ubi totum constat ex amoris et intellectus et affectus experientia. Status enim iste quasi quoddam 409Cfirmamentum est coeli, in quo habitat Deus, nec ratione inferiore fulcitur: sed tamen ipse nonnunquam sui generis sibi creat rationes, quibus de abscondito faciei Dei ad suos illos quodammodo socios inferiores regrediens, lumine illos supernae claritatis restringit. Sed sicut dicitur in Apocalypsi, Factum est silentium in coelo quasi dimidia hora (Apoc. VIII, 1); sic et illos momentanea quadam consolatione laetificans, dicere videtur eis, Currite, ut comprehendatis (I Cor. IX, 24). Sed nonnisi dies diei eructat verbum hoc. Sunt autem rationes istae non ratiocinantium perplexionibus 264 involutae, sed de mera et simplici veritate merae et simplices, laetae de oleo laetitiae Dei, melleae de melle coeli; inventorem 409Dsuum non scientia inflantes, sed charitate aedificantes; pauperibus spiritu perfaciles, alta sapientibus incomprehensibiles; tam efficaces, ut videatur dictum de eis, Spiritus omnia scrutatur etiam profunda Dei (I Cor. II, 10); tam solidae in se et nullius extrinseci egentes, ut dictum de eis intelligatur, Judicia Domini vera, justificata in semetipsa (Psal. XVIII, 10). Sed et omnes tres isti amores, vel amoris status, saepe sibi concurrunt et cooperantur, et mutua quadam et amica liberalitate, de quibus singuli abundant, invicem dant et accipiunt: quia et supremus in deliciis inferioris saepe delectatur et jucundatur, et inferior nonnunquam superioris gaudio perfunditur et afficitur, medio alternatim inter utrumque discurrente, et utrique congaudente. 410A

2.

But the rational state, set between this highest one and that lower one, rules and illumines the lower; yet the higher, sometimes reaching toward it with the hand of devout desire, at once flees back to itself, and, like David, strikes its own heart, because it presumed to lay a hand upon the Lord's Christ. For if a beast touch the mountain, it shall be stoned (Hebr. XII, 20); and when a voice comes over the firmament that hangs above the head of the living creatures, they stand, and do not go forward, and lower the wings of their endeavors. For rational discipline does nothing there, where the whole consists of the experience of love and understanding and affection. For this state is, as it were, a kind of 409Cfirmament of heaven, in which God dwells, and is not propped up by lower reason: yet it nevertheless sometimes creates for itself reasons of its own kind, by which, returning in a manner from the hiding-place of God's face to those lower companions of its own, it restrains them with the light of the supernal brightness. But as it is said in the Apocalypse, There was silence in heaven for about half an hour (Apoc. VIII, 1); so too, gladdening them with a certain momentary consolation, it seems to say to them, Run, that you may obtain (I Cor. IX, 24). But only day unto day uttereth this word. But these reasons are not wrapped up in the perplexities of those who reason, 264 but, arising from pure and simple truth, are pure and simple, joyful from the oil of God's gladness, honeyed from the honey of heaven; not puffing up 409Dtheir finder with knowledge, but building him up with charity; very easy to the poor in spirit, incomprehensible to those who are wise in lofty things; so effective, that what was said seems said of them, The Spirit searcheth all things, yea, the deep things of God (I Cor. II, 10); so solid in themselves and needing nothing external, that what was said may be understood of them, The judgments of the Lord are true, justified in themselves (Psal. XVIII, 10). But all these three loves, or states of love, often run together and cooperate with one another, and, with a certain mutual and friendly generosity, give to and receive from one another the things in which each abounds: for the highest often takes delight and pleasure in the delights of the lower, and the lower is sometimes flooded and moved by the joy of the higher, the middle one running alternately between the two, and rejoicing with each. 410A

3.

Sed libet adhuc contemplari subtilius divitias, quibus utrique abundant. Primus est contra tentationes; secundus, contra haereses; tertius cantat, In pace in idipsum dormiam et requiescam (Psal. IV, 9). In primo, remissio peccatorum et emundatio vitiorum; in secundo, exercitium virtutum; in tertio, virtutum perfectio, et summi boni adhaesio vel fruitio. In primo, usus sacramentorum; in secundo, mysteriorum; in tertio, res sacramentorum vel mysteriorum. Primus in Maria peccatrice humilis, in Petro fervens, in Joanne pius et dulcis. Secundus dormit a saecularibus, vigilat in spiritualibus, languet cum deficit in salutare Dei anima ejus, videns et non pervidens, et ad pervidendum amore et desiderio languens. Tertius audit verba ineffabilia, silentium, 410Bpacem scilicet illam Dei, quae exsuperat omnem sensum (Philipp. IV, 7). Primus oves suas, id est infirmos sensualitatis affectus, minans ad interiora deserti, id est ad interiores contemplationis recessus, et considerans rubum qui ardebat et non comburebatur, id est mysteria incarnationis Domini, volensque propius accedere per intellectum, audit a Domino: Ne appropies huc, sed solve calceamenta de pedibus tuis; locus enim in quo stas, terra sancta est (Exod. III, 1-5). Secundus ascendit in montem, auditque a Domino, Tu vero sta hic mecum, et docebo te leges ac mandata quae proponas filiis Israel (Deut. V, 31): videtque exemplar tabernaculi aeterni quod fecit Deus et non homo, auditque a Domino, Vide ut omnia facias secundum 410Cexemplar, quod tibi ostensum est in monte (Exod. XXV, 40). Tertius in monte sepelitur a Domino, id est absconditur in abscondito faciei ejus, et non novit homo, id est humana sapientia, sepulcrum ejus usque in hunc diem (Deut. XXXIV, 6). Primus audit ab hominibus, et dicitur ei quotidie, Ubi est Deus tuus? secundus recordatur, et effundit in se animam suam; tertius transit in locum tabernaculi admirabilis usque ad domum Dei (Psal. XLI, 4, 5). Primus servit fidei, secundus spei, tertius charitati: et sicut fides Deo nos generat, spes nutrit, charitas consummat; sic primus status in nobis humanum affectum Deo dedicat; secundus exuit veterem hominem cum actibus suis, et induit novum hominem 410Dqui secundum Deum creatus est in justitia et sanctitate veritatis (Ephes. IV, 22, 24); tertius consummat, Deoque conformat et mentis puritate, et vitae sanctitate. Sive enim prophetiae, sive linguae, sive fides, sive spes caeteraque omnia charismata cessabunt: charitas autem nunquam excidit (I Cor. XIII, 8). Interdum autem superior ille status 265 quasi vicem rependens paedagogis et tutoribus et actoribus suis, quemdam eis divinum affectum vel plus quam humanum nonnunquam infundit, ut miro et incognito modo saepe mirentur se rapi supra se; et eo eos deducit et enutrit, ut oratio illa Domini compleatur in eis: Pater, volo ut sicut ego et tu unum sumus, ita ipsi in nobis unum sint (Joan. XVII, 11-21).

3.

But it is pleasing to contemplate yet more subtly the riches in which both abound. The first is against temptations; the second, against heresies; the third sings, In peace in the selfsame will I sleep and take my rest (Psal. IV, 9). In the first, the remission of sins and the cleansing of vices; in the second, the exercise of virtues; in the third, the perfection of virtues, and the cleaving to or enjoyment of the highest good. In the first, the use of the sacraments; in the second, of the mysteries; in the third, the reality of the sacraments or mysteries. The first is humble in Mary the sinner, fervent in Peter, devout and sweet in John. The second sleeps from worldly things, watches in spiritual things, faints when his soul fails for the salvation of God, seeing and not seeing through, and fainting with love and desire to see through. The third hears unspeakable words, silence, 410Bnamely that peace of God which surpasseth all understanding (Philipp. IV, 7). The first, driving his sheep, that is, the weak affections of sensuality, to the inner parts of the desert, that is, to the inner recesses of contemplation, and beholding the bush that burned and was not consumed, that is, the mysteries of the Lord's incarnation, and wishing to draw nearer through understanding, hears from the Lord: Come not nigh hither, but loose the shoes from off thy feet; for the place whereon thou standest is holy ground (Exod. III, 1-5). The second ascends the mountain, and hears from the Lord, But stand thou here with me, and I will teach thee the laws and the commandments which thou shalt set before the children of Israel (Deut. V, 31): and he sees the pattern of the eternal tabernacle which God made and not man, and hears from the Lord, See that thou make all things according to the 410Cpattern which was shown thee in the mount (Exod. XXV, 40). The third is buried by the Lord on the mountain, that is, is hidden in the hiding-place of his face, and no man, that is, no human wisdom, has known his sepulchre unto this day (Deut. XXXIV, 6). The first hears from men, and it is said to him daily, Where is thy God? the second remembers, and pours out his soul within himself; the third passes over into the place of the wonderful tabernacle, even to the house of God (Psal. XLI, 4, 5). The first serves faith, the second hope, the third charity: and as faith begets us to God, hope nourishes, charity consummates; so the first state dedicates to God the human affection in us; the second puts off the old man with his deeds, and puts on the new man 410Dwho according to God is created in justice and holiness of truth (Ephes. IV, 22, 24); the third consummates, and conforms to God both by purity of mind and by holiness of life. For whether prophecies, or tongues, or faith, or hope and all the other gifts shall cease: but charity never faileth (I Cor. XIII, 8). But now and then that higher state, 265 as though repaying its tutors and guardians and agents, sometimes pours into them a certain divine affection, or more than human, so that in a wondrous and unknown manner they often marvel that they are caught up above themselves; and it leads and nurtures them to this, that that prayer of the Lord may be fulfilled in them: Father, I will that, as I and thou are one, so they also may be one in us (Joan. XVII, 11-21).

4.

411ASecundum hos tres status in Cantico canticorum modo adolescentulae, modo sodales, modo sponsus et sponsa loquuntur suo unusquisque modo. Sponsa igitur de sponso: Osculetur me, inquit, osculo oris sui (Cant. I, 1). Sed sunt linguae Angelorum, sunt et linguae hominum. Lingua igitur hominum, id est consueto hominibus modo; scilicet quasi quibusdam parabolis, Spiritus sanctus sua hominibus manifestat secreta, quorum infirmitas aliter intelligere ea non poterit. Unde et Dominus in parabolis loquebatur. Quinimo et ipse Deus ideo homo pro omnibus factus est, ut qui Deum cogitare non noverant, per eum quem noverant, scilicet hominem, ad cogitandum et intelligendum Deum paulatim edocti ab ipso, rationabilem mentis intelligentiam 411Berigerent. Propter hoc non solum in parabolis loquebatur hominibus, sed tota ejus conversatio in terris quasi quaedam insignis erat eis parabola, ut per visibilia ejus invisibilia ejus intellecta conspicerentur. In omni enim carnali affectione nihil dulcius, nihil solet esse desiderabilius conjunctione sponsae ad sponsum; in spirituali vero, creati spiritus ad increatum. Ibi enim de duabus una caro; hic autem de duobus unus spiritus efficitur. Unde ducta similitudine de carnalibus ad spiritualia, de humanis ad divina, describitur hic sponsus et sponsa, Christus et Ecclesia vel quaelibet sancta anima, quae aliquatenus jam experta duos primos amoris gradus, jam anhelat ad tertium, et totam se super 411Cse effundere gestit in sponsi amorem et dulcedinem, et jam amplexum amoris ejus et osculum notitiae ejus desiderans: Osculetur me, inquit, osculo oris sui. Osculum signum est pacis. Et si peccata nostra, ut Scriptura dicit, separant inter nos et Deum, cum pro his satisfacimus ut reconciliemur, quasi pacis requirimus osculum.

4.

411AAccording to these three states, in the Song of Songs now the maidens, now the companions, now the bridegroom and the bride speak, each in its own manner. The bride, then, of the bridegroom: Let him kiss me, she says, with the kiss of his mouth (Cant. I, 1). But there are tongues of Angels, and there are also tongues of men. By the tongue of men, then, that is, in the manner customary to men, namely as it were by certain parables, the Holy Spirit manifests his secrets to men, whose weakness could not otherwise understand them. Whence the Lord too spoke in parables. Nay rather, God himself was for this reason made man for all, that those who had not known how to conceive God might, through him whom they knew, namely man, being little by little taught by him to conceive and understand God, raise up the rational understanding of the mind. 411BFor this reason he not only spoke to men in parables, but his whole manner of life on earth was, as it were, a kind of notable parable to them, so that through his visible things his invisible things might be beheld when understood. For in every carnal affection nothing is wont to be sweeter, nothing more desirable, than the joining of a bride to a bridegroom; but in the spiritual, of the created spirit to the uncreated. For there, of two there is made one flesh; but here, of two there is made one spirit. Whence, a likeness being drawn from carnal things to spiritual, from human to divine, here are described the bridegroom and the bride, Christ and the Church or any holy soul, which, having now to some extent experienced the first two degrees of love, now pants for the third, and longs to pour her whole self above 411Cherself into the love and sweetness of the bridegroom, and now desiring the embrace of his love and the kiss of his acquaintance: Let him kiss me, she says, with the kiss of his mouth. The kiss is a sign of peace. And if our sins, as Scripture says, separate between us and God, when we make satisfaction for them that we may be reconciled, we seek, as it were, the kiss of peace.

5.

Nam secundum tres status amoris, tria sunt etiam oscula amantis. Primum, propitiatorium vel reconciliatorium; secundum, promeritorium; tertium, contemplatorium. Primum ad pedes, secundum ad manum, tertium ad os. Primum, cum sordens in pulvere poenitentiae osculando duos pedes Domini, cum peccatrice indulgentiam percipit peccatorum. Duo autem pedes Domini sunt 411Dmisericordia et judicium, quibus totum mundum perambulat, et ambulat super undas maris. His circuit jugiter et perambulat spirituales mentes, exsultans ut gigas ad currendam viam (Psal. XVIII, 6); si tamen tales sint de quibus merito dicat: Et habitabo in illis, et deambulabo in eis (II Cor. VI, 16). Hos pedes osculatur qui coaptando se eis, misericordiam et judicium cantat Domino. Deinde surgitur ad manum, ut secundum ei osculum offeratur, cum jam fidelis anima in novitate vitae ambulans, de humilitate poenitentis ad manum surgit largientis, gratias jam agens pro perceptione affectuum et effectuum bonorum operum, quae per manum designantur. Nec enim in claritate bonorum suorum suam manum osculatur, quae, sicut dicit Job, est 412Amaxima iniquitas, et negatio contra Deum altissimum (Job XXXI, 27, 28): sed osculatur manum largientis, cujus gratiae totum quod est ascribit cum dulci gratiarum actione, quae signatur per osculum. Tertium est osculum contemplationis, seu contemplatorium, quo sponsa jam duobus primis exhilarata, et fiducia collecta, desiderat insigniri, et sponso jam per notitiam et amorem conjungi, et secretorum ejus aliquatenus esse conscia. Hoc osculum oris sui est, et longe dissimile a superioribus osculis. Osculetur, inquit, me osculo oris sui. Videndum est quale sit istud osculum, quale os illud suum: qualia labia illud imprimentis, qualia suscipientis.

5.

For according to the three states of love, there are also three kisses of the lover. The first, propitiatory or reconciliatory; the second, meritorious; the third, contemplative. The first to the feet, the second to the hand, the third to the mouth. The first, when, filthy in the dust of penance, by kissing the two feet of the Lord, one receives, with the woman who was a sinner, the pardon of sins. But the two feet of the Lord are 411Dmercy and judgment, with which he walks through the whole world, and walks upon the waves of the sea. With these he continually goes about and walks through spiritual minds, rejoicing as a giant to run the way (Psal. XVIII, 6); if only they be such of whom he may rightly say: And I will dwell in them, and walk in them (II Cor. VI, 16). He kisses these feet who, fitting himself to them, sings mercy and judgment to the Lord. Then one rises to the hand, that a second kiss may be offered to him, when the faithful soul, now walking in newness of life, rises from the humility of the penitent to the hand of the giver, now giving thanks for the reception of the affections and effects of good works, which are signified by the hand. For he does not, in the splendor of his own good deeds, kiss his own hand, which, as Job says, is 412Athe greatest iniquity, and a denial against the most high God (Job XXXI, 27, 28): but he kisses the hand of the giver, to whose grace he ascribes the whole of what he is, with a sweet giving of thanks, which is signified by the kiss. The third is the kiss of contemplation, or the contemplative, by which the bride, now gladdened by the first two, and having gathered confidence, desires to be marked out, and now to be joined to the bridegroom through acquaintance and love, and to be to some extent privy to his secrets. This is the kiss of his mouth, and far unlike the kisses above. Let him kiss me, she says, with the kiss of his mouth. We must see what kind of kiss that is, what kind that mouth of his: what kind the lips of him who imprints it, what kind of him who receives it.

6.

In Deo non est aliud esse, et sapere: nec habet Pater sapere a Filio, ne habere videatur et esse, 412Bquod absurdum esset. Habet igitur Pater a seipso et esse, et sapere; 266 et si est Filio esse a Patre, est et sapere. Habet igitur Filius a Patre et esse et sapere, sicque plenae sunt subsistentiae, quas Graeci dicunt hypostases; nec fulcitur alter ab altero ut subsistat, vel sit quod est per alterutrum; sed sunt, sicut diximus, vel secundum Graecos, plenae per se subsistentes hypostases, vel secundum Latinos personae vel per se sonantes, licet una sit et perfecta Trinitas Deus. Nullum autem conturbent, nulli caliginem obducant nomina substantiae, vel subsistentiae, vel personae, quae sunt nomina nostrae paupertatis, divinitatis essentiam non exprimentia, sed ex defectu suo incomprehensibilitati ejus testimonium 412Cperhibentia. Nomen enim subsistentiarum exprimere conatur proprietatem personarum, nomen vero substantiae simplicitatem et unitatem summae illius essentiae. Et est quaedam conversio a Patre ad Filium, et a Filio ad Patrem: sed prima est Patris ad Filium, quia Filius a Patre est, non Pater a Filio; prima autem non tempore, sed quadam quasi relatione, sicut Pater ad Filium. Conversio autem ista est in osculo et amplexu. Osculum est mutua de se cognitio, amplexus est mutua dilectio. Unde dicit Filius in Evangelio: Nemo novit Patrem nisi Filius, et nemo novit Filium nisi Pater. Osculum igitur Patris et Filii et amplexus, est Spiritus sanctus ab utroque procedens, amor Patris ad Filium, et amor Filii ad Patrem, de quo etiam subditur: 412DEt cui voluerit Filius revelare (Matth. XI, 27). Voluntas quippe ista, ipse est Spiritus sanctus, voluntas Patris et Filii, qui ipsam Patris et Filii nobis revelat unitatem, unus cum Patre et Filio Deus existens. Proprium quippe est Patris revelare Filium sicut in Evangelio: Non, inquit Filius, caro et sanguis tibi revelavit, sed Pater meus qui in coelis est (Matth. XVI, 17). Debitum Filii glorificare Patrem, ut ibi: Glorifico, inquit, Patrem meum qui est in coelis (Joan. VIII, 49). Officium Spiritus sancti revelare utrumque, sicut dicit Apostolus: Quod oculus non vidit, nec auris audivit, nec in cor hominis ascendit, quae praeparavit Deus diligentibus se; nobis autem revelavit per Spiritum sanctum (I Cor. II, 9, 10). Cui igitur Filius per Spiritum revelat, non dicendus 413Aest osculari ore suo Deus, sed osculo oris sui. Non enim os tangimus, sed osculo tangimur. Osculo tangimur, cum amore et cognitio nobis infunditur. Non os tangimus, id est mutuum illum intellectum: non os tangimus, quia, ut dictum est, Nemo novit Filium nisi Pater, et nemo novit Patrem nisi Filius; sed osculo tangimur, cui voluerit Filius revelare. Non os tangimus, quia nec oculus vidit, nec auris audivit. Osculo tangimur quia nobis revelavit Deus per Spiritum sanctum: cui enim revelat, nonnisi per Spiritum sanctum. Ipse est itaque osculum: ipsum tangit nos, cum se gemina nobis gratia infundit, id est cognitione et amore veritatis. Et haec sunt non ipsa labia Dei, sed vestigium labiorum; imo, si dici fas est, labii: quod enim in nobis duplum 413Best, in Deo est simplum, cui idem est nosse quod amare. Hoc autem vestigium imprimitur in osculo sancto duobus labiis sponsae, voluntati scilicet et rationi; amor voluntati, notitia rationi. Quae tamen duo, notitia scilicet et amor, quia, ut diximus, in Deo unum sunt, pulchra oris singularitate exprimuntur, ubi dicitur: Osculetur me osculo oris sui. Vel osculetur me Filius eo osculo quo osculatur os suum, id est Patrem, qui ideo dicitur os ejus, quia Verbum Patris est Filius. Et in Sapientia: Ego, inquit, ex ore Altissimi prodii (Eccli. XXIV, 5). Vel osculetur me Deus Pater osculo oris sui, id est, Filii vel Verbi sui (Verbum quippe ex ore procedit, et est figurata locutio): id est, infundat mihi eam locutionem, 413Cqua novit Pater Filium, et Filius Patrem. Vel ipse Pater osculetur me osculo oris sui; id est, mittat in cor meum Spiritum Filii sui, ut diligamus Filium, sicut ipse diligit. Sed quare, Osculetur; et non, Osculare? Sponsa enim et sponsus carnalis mutua quadam se solent praevenire reverentia. Unde et ista quasi Rebecca ad occursum novi sponsi quasi quodam verecundiae pallio faciem operta (Gen. XXIV, 65), non imperativo modo, sed desiderativo vel optativo modo: Utinam, inquit, tandem aliquando osculetur me osculo oris sui.

6.

In God, to be and to be wise are not different things: nor does the Father have his being-wise from the Son, lest he seem to have also his being 412Bfrom him, which would be absurd. The Father, therefore, has from himself both being and being-wise; 266 and if it belongs to the Son to have being from the Father, it belongs to him also to have being-wise. The Son, therefore, has from the Father both being and being-wise, and thus they are full subsistences, which the Greeks call hypostases; nor is the one propped up by the other in order to subsist, or to be what it is through the other; but they are, as we have said, either according to the Greeks, full hypostases subsisting through themselves, or according to the Latins, persons or things that sound of themselves, although God is one and a perfect Trinity. But let the names of substance, or subsistence, or person disturb no one, cast a mist over no one — names which are names of our poverty, not expressing the essence of the divinity, but by their own deficiency bearing witness 412Cto its incomprehensibility. For the name of subsistences tries to express the property of the persons, but the name of substance the simplicity and unity of that highest essence. And there is a certain turning from the Father to the Son, and from the Son to the Father: but the first is of the Father to the Son, because the Son is from the Father, not the Father from the Son; yet first not in time, but by a certain relation, as it were, as the Father is to the Son. But this turning is in a kiss and an embrace. The kiss is the mutual knowledge of each other, the embrace is the mutual love. Whence the Son says in the Gospel: No one knoweth the Father but the Son, and no one knoweth the Son but the Father. The kiss, therefore, of the Father and the Son, and the embrace, is the Holy Spirit proceeding from both, the love of the Father toward the Son, and the love of the Son toward the Father, of whom it is also added: 412DAnd to whomsoever the Son shall will to reveal him (Matth. XI, 27). For that will is the Holy Spirit himself, the will of the Father and the Son, who reveals to us the very unity of the Father and the Son, being one God with the Father and the Son. For it is proper to the Father to reveal the Son, as in the Gospel: It is not, says the Son, flesh and blood that hath revealed it to thee, but my Father who is in heaven (Matth. XVI, 17). It is the Son's due to glorify the Father, as there: I glorify, he says, my Father who is in heaven (Joan. VIII, 49). It is the office of the Holy Spirit to reveal both, as the Apostle says: That which eye hath not seen, nor ear heard, neither hath it entered into the heart of man, the things which God hath prepared for them that love him; but to us he hath revealed them by his Holy Spirit (I Cor. II, 9, 10). To whom, therefore, the Son reveals through the Spirit, God is not to be said 413Ato kiss with his mouth, but with the kiss of his mouth. For we do not touch the mouth, but we are touched by the kiss. We are touched by the kiss, when love and knowledge are poured into us. We do not touch the mouth, that is, that mutual understanding: we do not touch the mouth, because, as it was said, No one knoweth the Son but the Father, and no one knoweth the Father but the Son; but we are touched by the kiss, to whomsoever the Son shall will to reveal him. We do not touch the mouth, because neither eye hath seen, nor ear heard. We are touched by the kiss, because God hath revealed it to us by the Holy Spirit: for to whom he reveals, he reveals only through the Holy Spirit. He, then, is the kiss: he himself touches us, when he pours himself into us with a twofold grace, that is, with the knowledge and love of truth. And these are not the very lips of God, but the imprint of the lips; nay, if it is lawful to say so, of the lip: for what in us is twofold 413Bis in God single, for whom to know is the same as to love. But this imprint is stamped, in the holy kiss, upon the two lips of the bride, namely upon the will and the reason; love upon the will, knowledge upon the reason. Which two, however, namely knowledge and love, because, as we have said, in God they are one, are expressed by the beautiful singular of 'mouth,' where it is said: Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth. Or, let the Son kiss me with that kiss with which he kisses his mouth, that is, the Father, who is for this reason called his mouth, because the Son is the Word of the Father. And in Wisdom: I, he says, came forth from the mouth of the Most High (Eccli. XXIV, 5). Or, let God the Father kiss me with the kiss of his mouth, that is, of his Son or his Word (for the Word proceeds from the mouth, and it is a figurative expression): that is, let him pour into me that speech 413Cby which the Father knows the Son, and the Son the Father. Or, let the Father himself kiss me with the kiss of his mouth; that is, let him send into my heart the Spirit of his Son, that we may love the Son, as he himself loves. But why, Let him kiss; and not, Kiss thou? For a bride and bridegroom in the flesh are wont to anticipate each other with a certain mutual reverence. Whence she too, like Rebecca at the meeting of the new bridegroom, her face veiled as with a certain cloak of modesty (Gen. XXIV, 65), speaks not in the imperative mood, but in the desiderative or optative mood: Would that, she says, at length at last he would kiss me with the kiss of his mouth.

Est et aliud osculum, quod multifariam, multisque modis olim loquens Patribus in Prophetis, Ecclesiae 267 suae promiserat, et novissime diebus 413Distis locutus est nobis in Filio (Hebr. I, 1, 2). Quod enim dicitur, locutus est nobis in Filio, ipsum est osculum. Conjunctio enim Verbi et audientis, divinitatis et humanitatis, quasi quoddam osculum est charitatis. Quod praevidens Isaias in spiritu, Egredietur, inquit, virga de radice Jesse, et flos de radice ejus ascendet; et requiescet super eum spiritus Domini, spiritus sapientiae et intellectus, spiritus consilii et fortitudinis, spiritus scientiae et pietatis, et replebit eum spiritus timoris Domini (Isai. XI, 1-3). Haec sunt septem dona, vel septem charismata sancti Spiritus, quae specialiter in Domino Jesu enituerunt, sicut specialiter super eum requievit Spiritus sanctus. In timore enim humilitas designatur; in pietate, misericordia; in scientia, 414Aterrenorum notitia; in fortitudine, patientia: in consilio, utilitas; in intellectu, divinitatis conjunctio; in sapientia, potestatis moderatio. Per quinque superiora coaptavit se homini Christus in assumpta hominis substantia: per duo ultima unita semper fuit divinitati Christi persona. Nam sicut medicus bonus ad aegrum se humiliter inclinavit, misericorditer vulnera tractavit, noto sibi figmento prospexit, patienter insanum sustinuit, utilia cuique morbo apposuit, unum cum Patre indivisibiliter manens, singulariter in ipsa hominis veritate Deum intellexit, quia nemo novit Patrem nisi Filius; per illam quae ipse est, sapientiam Patris, a fine usque ad finem fortiter attingens, moderate ac suaviter omnia disposuit. Haec igitur conjunctio divinae 414Bet humanae naturae quoddam est osculum, sponsi et sponsae: quod desiderans Ecclesia, Osculetur me, inquit, osculo oris sui. Sunt et alia tria oscula, quibus sponsa sponso conjungitur; scilicet osculum naturae, osculum doctrinae, osculum gratiae: de quibus jam, quia de osculis multa dicta sunt, legentium meditationi relinquitur.

There is also another kiss, which, at sundry times and in divers manners speaking of old to the Fathers in the Prophets, he had promised to his Church, 267 and in these last 413Ddays hath spoken to us in his Son (Hebr. I, 1, 2). For what is said, he hath spoken to us in his Son, is itself the kiss. For the joining of the Word and the hearer, of divinity and humanity, is, as it were, a kind of kiss of charity. Foreseeing which in the spirit, Isaiah says, There shall come forth a rod out of the root of Jesse, and a flower shall rise up out of his root; and the spirit of the Lord shall rest upon him, the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and fortitude, the spirit of knowledge and godliness, and the spirit of the fear of the Lord shall fill him (Isai. XI, 1-3). These are the seven gifts, or seven graces of the Holy Spirit, which shone forth especially in the Lord Jesus, as the Holy Spirit especially rested upon him. For in fear humility is signified; in godliness, mercy; in knowledge, 414Athe knowledge of earthly things; in fortitude, patience; in counsel, usefulness; in understanding, the joining of the divinity; in wisdom, the moderation of power. Through the five foregoing Christ fitted himself to man in the assumed substance of man: through the last two the person of Christ was ever united to the divinity. For as a good physician he humbly bent down to the sick man, mercifully handled the wounds, provided for the frame he had made and knew, patiently bore with the madman, applied useful remedies to each disease, remaining indivisibly one with the Father, singularly, in the very truth of man, understood God, because no one knoweth the Father but the Son; through that wisdom of the Father which he himself is, reaching mightily from end to end, he disposed all things moderately and sweetly. This joining, therefore, of the divine 414Band human nature is a kind of kiss, of the bridegroom and the bride: desiring which, the Church says, Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth. There are also three other kisses, by which the bride is joined to the bridegroom; namely, the kiss of nature, the kiss of doctrine, the kiss of grace: concerning which now, since many things have been said about kisses, it is left to the meditation of the readers.

Quia meliora sunt ubera tua vino (Cantic. I, 1). Scriptum est: Desiderium pauperum exaudivit Dominus; praeparationem cordis eorum audivit auris tua (Psal. X, 17). Quia semper ergo desiderium solet praecedere fiduciam, respondet sponsus, et fiduciam dat sponsae desideranti, causam etiam fiduciae subjiciens, Quia, inquit, meliora sunt ubera 414Ctua vino. Videor mihi trepidantem Esther hic videre, quasi pavidam accedentem ad solium Regis, et vix audentem attollere oculos ad claritatem vultus ejus; Regem vero virgam clementiae protendentem ad eam ac dicentem: Quid vis, Esther? aut quae est petitio tua (Esther V, 1-3). Sic et sponsus ad sponsam: Quid, inquit, trepidas rogare quod desideras? Multa jam a me accepisti, quae fiduciam tibi facere debeant majora audendi. Ex perceptione praeteritorum munerum, firma potest esse exspectatio futurorum. Quae enim desideras contemplationem, jam in activa vita ex munere meo accepisti perfectionem. Quia meliora sunt ubera tua vino. Uva semel expressa, totam semel effundit; sic et saecularis sapientia, vel legis sensus 414Dcarnalis: ubera vero sponsae quanto plus premuntur, tanto plus exuberant. Ubera tua ex gratia mea meliora sunt vino, id est, efficaciora amore et sapientia saeculi. Inebriant ista, inebriant illa: sed ubera tua multo uberius in bonum, quam illa in malum. Simplex enim et lactea christiana et apostolica doctrina, omnia dialecticorum solvit argumenta. Sed et musto Spiritus sancti quo impleti sunt Apostoli et inebriati, sic impleti sunt beati pauperes spiritu, filii gratiae, filii novi Testamenti, ut amplecterentur amorem Dei usque ad contemptum sui, ut arbitrarentur omnia, tanquam stercora, ut Christum lucrifacerent. Ergo ubera sponsae meliora sunt vino. Haec autem ubera sponsae duo sunt: unum compassionis, alterum congratulationis. Ex 415Aubere compassionis sugitur lac consolationis; ex ubere congratulationis sugitur lac exhortationis. Haec ubera ut meliora sint vino, fragrant unguentis optimis; quia efficaciam in illis operatur unguentum optimum, id est, supereminentis charitatis. Uncta igitur sunt sponsae ubera, id est, tactu sponsi per unctionem lenificata: fragrantia boni exempli longe quasi bono odore dilatata, vel usque ad sponsi odorem per sacrificium piae intentionis et sancti desiderii promota.

For thy breasts are better than wine (Cantic. I, 1). It is written: The Lord hath heard the desire of the poor; thine ear hath heard the preparation of their heart (Psal. X, 17). Because, therefore, desire is always wont to go before confidence, the bridegroom answers, and gives confidence to the desiring bride, adding also the cause of her confidence, For, he says, thy breasts are better 414Cthan wine. I seem to myself to see here the trembling Esther, approaching as if fearful to the throne of the King, and scarcely daring to lift up her eyes to the brightness of his countenance; and the King stretching out to her the rod of clemency and saying: What wilt thou, Esther? or what is thy petition (Esther V, 1-3). So too the bridegroom to the bride: Why, he says, dost thou tremble to ask what thou desirest? Thou hast already received many things from me, which ought to give thee confidence to dare greater things. From the receiving of past gifts, the expectation of future ones can be firm. For as to the contemplation which thou desirest, thou hast already received in the active life, by my gift, perfection. For thy breasts are better than wine. The grape, once pressed, pours out its whole at once; so too worldly wisdom, or the carnal 414Dsense of the law: but the breasts of the bride, the more they are pressed, the more they overflow. Thy breasts, from my grace, are better than wine, that is, more effective than the love and wisdom of the world. These make drunk, and those make drunk: but thy breasts far more abundantly unto good, than those unto evil. For the simple and milky Christian and apostolic doctrine dissolves all the arguments of the dialecticians. But by the new wine of the Holy Spirit, with which the Apostles were filled and made drunk, the blessed poor in spirit, the sons of grace, the sons of the New Testament, were so filled, that they embraced the love of God even to the contempt of themselves, that they counted all things as dung, that they might gain Christ. Therefore the breasts of the bride are better than wine. But these breasts of the bride are two: one of compassion, the other of congratulation. From 415Athe breast of compassion is sucked the milk of consolation; from the breast of congratulation is sucked the milk of exhortation. That these breasts may be better than wine, they are fragrant with the best ointments; because the best ointment, that is, of surpassing charity, works efficacy in them. The breasts of the bride, therefore, are anointed, that is, softened by the touch of the bridegroom through the anointing: made fragrant with good example, spread abroad afar as with a good odor, or advanced even unto the odor of the bridegroom through the sacrifice of devout intention and holy desire.

Fragrantia unguentis optimis (Cantic. I, 2). Tria sunt unguenta: compunctionis, ex recordatione peccatorum; devotionis, 268 ex recordatione beneficiorum; pietatis, ex recordatione miserorum. Primo unguntur pedes Domini, secundo caput, 415Btertio totum corpus. De primo inquit: Maria unxit pedes Jesu, et extersit capillis suis, et domus impleta est ex odore unguenti (Luc. VII, 37, 38; Joan. XII, 3). De secundo: Attulit mulier alabastrum nardi spicati pretiosi, et effudit super caput recumbentis (Marc. XIV, 3). De tertio: Maria Magdalene, Maria Jacobi et Salome abeuntes, emerunt aromata, ut venientes ungerent Jesum (Marc. XVI, 1). Nota ordinem profectus ad superiora ab inferioribus, sicut supra de statu et osculo amoris. Denique Maria Magdalene quae peccatrix erat, unxit pedes Jesu. Nec mediocre sane hoc unguentum est, de quo scriptum est: Et domus impleta est ex odore unguenti. Nec mirum, cum etiam in coelestibus sentiatur 415Chujuscemodi unguenti fragrantia, Veritate attestante, quia magnum gaudium est inter angelos Dei, super uno peccatore poenitentiam agente (Luc. XV, 10). Verum quantumlibet unguentum hoc pretiosum videatur, tamen comparatum ei quod appellatur devotionis, et conficitur ex divinorum memoria beneficiorum, quo et ungitur caput Domini, vile et nullius pretii aestimatur. Denique de illo dicitur, Cor contritum et humiliatum, Deus, non despicies (Psal. L, 19): de isto vero, Sacrificium laudis honorificabit me (Psal. XLIX, 23). Caput autem ex eo ungis, cum de suis donis Deo gratias agis. Siquidem caput Christi Deus. Deitas ergo in Christo tangitur, quoties ad laudem ipsius, ejus in nobis beneficia memoramus: sicut non tam deitas quam humanitas necesse 415Dest cogitetur, cum non ejus dona, sed nostra peccata recordamur. Nam in carnis assumptione duos illos pedes ad hoc accepisse cognoscitur, id est misericordiam et judicium, ut peccator qui ad caput, id est ad deitatem, accessum non habebat, ad pedes, hoc est ad humanitatem, accederet. Nisi enim pes ille, quem diximus misericordiam, ad hominem assumptum pertineret, Paulus Apostolus de eo non diceret: Unde debuit per omnia fratribus assimilari, ut misericors fieret (Hebr. II, 17). Et nisi judicium aeque ad hominem pertineret, ipse homo Deus de seipso non dixisset: Et potestatem dedit ei judicium facere, quia Filius hominis est (Joan. V, 27). Itaque ad hos pedes viri, juxta prophetam, dolentis et scientis infirmitatem (Isa. Non enim habemus Pontificem qui non sciat compati infirmitatibus nostris (Hebr. IV, 15, 16). Peccatrix ergo ad pedes, justa ad ungendum caput accedit.

Fragrant with the best ointments (Cantic. I, 2). There are three ointments: of compunction, from the remembrance of sins; of devotion, 268 from the remembrance of benefits; of piety, from the remembrance of the wretched. With the first are anointed the feet of the Lord, with the second the head, 415Bwith the third the whole body. Of the first he says: Mary anointed the feet of Jesus, and wiped them with her hair, and the house was filled with the odor of the ointment (Luc. VII, 37, 38; Joan. XII, 3). Of the second: A woman brought an alabaster box of precious spikenard, and poured it upon the head of him as he reclined (Marc. XIV, 3). Of the third: Mary Magdalene, and Mary the mother of James, and Salome, going away, bought spices, that coming they might anoint Jesus (Marc. XVI, 1). Note the order of advance to the higher things from the lower, as above concerning the state and the kiss of love. In short, Mary Magdalene, who was a sinner, anointed the feet of Jesus. And this ointment is surely no mean one, of which it is written: And the house was filled with the odor of the ointment. Nor is it a wonder, since even in the heavenly places the fragrance 415Cof such an ointment is perceived, the Truth bearing witness, that there is great joy among the angels of God over one sinner doing penance (Luc. XV, 10). But however precious this ointment may seem, yet compared to that which is called of devotion, and is compounded from the memory of divine benefits, with which the head of the Lord is also anointed, it is esteemed cheap and of no value. In short, of the former it is said, A contrite and humbled heart, O God, thou wilt not despise (Psal. L, 19): but of the latter, The sacrifice of praise shall glorify me (Psal. XLIX, 23). But thou dost anoint the head therewith, when thou givest thanks to God for his gifts. Since indeed the head of Christ is God. Therefore the Godhead in Christ is touched, as often as, to his praise, we call to mind his benefits in us: just as not so much the Godhead as the humanity must 415Dbe thought upon, when we remember not his gifts, but our sins. For in the assumption of flesh he is known to have received those two feet for this end, that is, mercy and judgment, that the sinner who had no access to the head, that is, to the Godhead, might come to the feet, that is, to the humanity. For unless that foot, which we called mercy, belonged to the assumed man, the Apostle Paul would not say of it: Wherefore it behoved him in all things to be made like unto his brethren, that he might become merciful (Hebr. II, 17). And unless judgment equally belonged to the man, the man who is God would not have said of himself: And he hath given him power to do judgment, because he is the Son of man (Joan. V, 27). And so to these feet of the man who, according to the prophet, is sorrowful and acquainted with infirmity (Isa. LIII, 3), the sinner, 416Ano longer doubting to come, speaks confidently: But now with confidence we come to the throne of his grace. For we have not a High Priest who cannot have compassion on our infirmities (Hebr. IV, 15, 16). The sinful woman, therefore, comes to the feet, the just woman to anoint the head.

Tanto autem capitis unguentum illo alio, quod pedibus apponitur, pretiosius aestimandum est, quanto species quibus hoc conficitur, his quibus conficitur illud constat esse pretiosiores. Has quippe facile et absque labore (nam in nostra regione) reperimus: peccatores siquidem omnes sumus. Porro illas difficile ac de longinquo valde, utpote de paradiso Dei asportatas suscipimus: omne enim datum optimum, et omne donum perfectum desursum est, 416Bdescendens a Patre luminum (Jacob. I, 17). Quid denique hujuscemodi unguento excellentius, de cujus effusione etiam Apostoli murmurasse videntur, dicentes: Utquid perditio haec? potuit enim venumdari multo, et dari pauperibus. Sed et nunc quoque cum forte videtur quispiam vacare Deo, tantaeque esse devotionis et gratiae, ut merito credatur ungere caput Christi, persistens utique jugiter in sancta quiete, et gratiarum actione, et divinae contemplationis delectatione; non desunt qui hoc perditionem dicant, et justo, ut sibi vident, murmure conquerantur, quod is qui pluribus prodesse poterat, quiescat sibi: non quod sanctitati invideant, sed quod provideant charitati. Caeterum ipsa charitas Deus 416Chujuscemodi animae plerumque parcit, quam videt spiritualibus studiis delectari; maxime si talem eam noverit, quae adhuc pusillanimitate vel imbecillitate animi mulier sit, et necdum in virum perfectum profecit. Quod utique melius ipse discernit qui intuetur cor, quam homines, qui in facie tantum vident, et secundum faciem tantum judicant: cum videlicet minime attendunt non esse ejusdem facilitatis devote quiescere, et fructuose occupari; humiliter subesse, et utiliter praeesse; regi sine querela, et regere sine culpa; obedire sponte, et imperare discrete; bonum denique inter 269 bonos, et bonum esse inter malos; imo etiam esse pacatum inter filios pacis, et his qui oderunt pacem exhiberi pacificum. 10.

But the ointment of the head is to be esteemed more precious than that other one which is applied to the feet, by so much as the spices from which the former is compounded are agreed to be more precious than those from which the latter is compounded. For the latter we find easily and without labor (for they are in our own region): since indeed we are all sinners. But the former we obtain with difficulty and from very far off, as being carried away from the paradise of God: for every best gift, and every perfect gift is from above, 416Bcoming down from the Father of lights (Jacob. I, 17). In short, what is more excellent than an ointment of this kind, at the pouring out of which even the Apostles seem to have murmured, saying: To what purpose is this waste? for it might have been sold for much, and given to the poor. But even now too, when perchance someone is seen to be at leisure for God, and to be of such devotion and grace, that he is rightly believed to anoint the head of Christ, persisting continually in holy quiet, and in giving of thanks, and in the delight of divine contemplation; there are not lacking those who call this waste, and complain with a murmur that seems to them just, that one who could have been of profit to many rests to himself: not that they envy holiness, but that they take thought for charity. But charity itself, which is God, 416Cfor the most part spares such a soul, which it sees delighting in spiritual pursuits; especially if it has known it to be such as is still, by faintheartedness or weakness of mind, a woman, and has not yet advanced into the perfect man. Which he who beholds the heart surely discerns better than men, who see only in the face, and judge only according to the face: since, namely, they by no means consider that it is not of the same ease to rest devoutly and to be fruitfully occupied; humbly to be a subject, and usefully to preside; to be ruled without complaint, and to rule without fault; to obey willingly, and to command discreetly; in short, to be good among 269 the good, and to be good among the evil; nay, even to be peaceable among the sons of peace, and to show oneself peaceful to those who hate peace. Knowing, therefore, who they are who truly are not 416Dfit to be entangled in ecclesiastical cares and burdens, on behalf of such a delicate soul, which because of its tenderness he still feels to be less sufficient for the handling of affairs, against those who feel otherwise, and for this reason accuse its quiet as unfruitful, with good zeal indeed, but not according to knowledge, he answers with the very same affection: Why are you troublesome to this woman? (Matth. XXVI, 8-10.) For although, as must be confessed, that to which you attempt to draw her is better, yet the work which she hath wrought upon me is good. Suffer her meanwhile to work the good that she can, while she cannot yet do better: for I know that she is still a woman. But when, by the change of the right hand of the Most High, from a woman she shall have been made a man (which, when it shall be, if indeed it ever shall be, cannot be hidden from me), because at my calling she shall be advanced, 417Aand at my keeping she shall be held: and then even the iniquity of a man will be better than a woman doing good (Eccli. XLII, 14). But I hope for that better thing too: and this good I meanwhile by no means despise, nor do I reckon the pouring out of this ointment a waste, in which both the devotion of the woman is signified, and my burial is foreshown. To this is added, that it spreads its fragrance so widely on every side, that wheresoever this Gospel shall be preached in the whole world, that also which she hath done shall be told in memory of her.

11.

Let us now come to the third ointment. Now in the mutual comparison of the two set forth above, the later one, without any ambiguity, is recognized to be better than the former, and far more excellent. But it seems a wonder, 417Bwhether any third one can be found which ought by right to be preferred to both, according to which the bride glories that her breasts smell sweet with the best ointments. Otherwise they are not the best, if they do not surpass even the better ones: just as neither are those truly called better which do not surpass the good. Now that second ointment, which anoints the head, has been found to be of such excellence, that scarcely any riches whatever seem able — I do not say to be preferred to it, but even to be compared to it. Perhaps, if we search in the Gospel, we shall find that a figure of this third ointment also has gone before. Mary, it says, Magdalene, and Mary the mother of James, and Salome, bought spices, that coming they might anoint Jesus (Marc. XVI, 1). Dost thou see, at the very front of the chapter set forth, 417Chow highly even this material ointment is to be esteemed, for the buying of whose spices one or two were not able to suffice? One brought the first ointment, one also the second; but to buy this one, three came together alike, that they might, namely, buy together what each by herself could not, and so coming might anoint Jesus. Not the feet only or the head only, but that coming, it says, they might anoint Jesus; that is, the whole body. But note that Christ, as it were, did not wish any waste of this ointment to be made, since, the body not being found, they carried it back whole, and were bidden to present to the living what they had prepared for the dead. Which they also did, when, the joy of the resurrection being at once announced, they took care to soothe the sorrowful hearts of the disciples, who without doubt were members of Christ, and living members. 417DWhich members surely, unless Christ loved them more than that crucified body, he would not have delivered it up to be crucified for them. It is clear, then, concerning those two, that this last ointment excels them by as much as this body of his, to which it is applied — that is, the Church, which he willed to be anointed all over from them, and for whose redemption he also willed to be delivered up — is agreed to be more lovable to Christ. The breasts of the bride, therefore, are fragrant with ointments not only good and better, but even best; and her two breasts of compassion and congratulation, from piety and consideration of the wretched, abound with the milk of consolation and exhortation. Healed, therefore, by the first ointment, purified by the second, sanctified by the third, she has breasts fragrant with the best ointments. 418A

Or otherwise, that these too may be words of the bride, as if she plainly said: It is indeed presumption that I ask for the kiss of contemplation, but the consciousness of good action gives confidence; for my breasts; mine, because given to me by thee; thine, because filled by thee, and serving thee; are better than wine. Here we see three things in the breasts of the bride: touch, smell, and taste: touch, in the softness of the anointing; 418Bsmell, in the fragrance of the ointment; taste, in the wine. To the three interpretations, the things that follow are to be joined in two ways: Thy name is oil poured out. If they are the words of the bridegroom, it is to be understood that here the bridegroom, having consoled the desire of the bride, after grace was shown, after confidence was given, gave her the kiss. The bride, having received the kiss, and the oil with the kiss, that is, through interior inspiration having to some extent received the knowledge of the bridegroom, and with the knowledge of love, of gladness, cried out saying: Thy name is oil poured out, therefore have the maidens loved thee (Cantic. I, 2). Whether, says the Apostle, 418Cwe be beside ourselves, it is to God; or whether we be sober, it is for you (II Cor. V, 13). Thy name is oil poured out, these are the words of one beside herself unto God: therefore have the maidens loved thee, these are the words of sobriety toward the neighbor. For after she was beside herself unto God, out of the abundance of the oil received from God, she was made sober toward the maidens. For the charity of the bridegroom pressed her. For at the name of the bridegroom, that is, the knowledge of the bridegroom, from him of whose fullness we have all received, so great a pouring out of oil, that is, of charity, was made, that the bride, being full, cried out for herself, Thy name is oil poured out: and pouring back from her own fullness upon the maidens, said, therefore have the maidens loved thee. For the pouring out denotes abundance. This is the pouring out in faith or in hope, but only in the fullness of charity. Or according to the 418Dtwo interpretations above: I said, says the bride, that thy breasts are better than wine, that is, filled by thee, serving thee; but by the grace of God I am what I am: for thy name is oil poured out. But the grace of God in me was not void, therefore have the maidens loved thee. According to the anagogy, the oil and the name, that is, the knowledge and love of God, before it was poured out, and abounded unto all the nations, was infused, not poured out, because God was known only in Judea: but when the pouring out was made from the infusion, the maidens, the Churches of the Gentiles, were made not maidens, but brides, because they loved. For she who through faith is at first a maiden, or a handmaid, begins to be a bride when she begins to love. Because, therefore, thy name is oil poured out, therefore have the maidens loved thee. 419A

14.

Secundum moralem sensum in effusione olei, cum fit nobis per gratiam infusio, cavendum est, ne inordinate fiat effusio. Quiddam enim nobis infunditur quod nostrum est, quiddam quod proximi. Nostrum est sine quo non salvamur, caetera sunt proximi. Quod nostrum est si effundimus, stulti sumus et vacui: quod alienum est si retinemus, fraudulenti. Quod nostrum est, fides est, spes et charitas. Fides facit servos, spes mercenarios, charitas filios. Fides facit ancillam vel pedisequam, spes concubinam, charitas sponsam. Fides dicit, Sic est: spes, Habebis: charitas, Da gloriam Deo. Fides est malorum vel bonorum scientia; spes, cavendi vel adipiscendi fiducia; et charitas, utriusque gratia, cum securus de utroque gratias agit. Haec igitur 419Bcum nostra sint, non sunt effundenda, id est, pro aliis perdenda: sed agendum ut operentur in nobis quod suum est, id est, ut sanemur, ut servemur, ut salvemur. Sanemur per laborem corporis, servemur per lectionem et meditationem, salvemur per orationem. Labori insidiatur desidia; lectioni, inquietudo vel otiositas; orationi, nimia securitas. Quod igitur nostrum est, peccamus si effundimus, ut dictum est: id est, si laborem negligimus propter desidiam, lectionem propter inquietudinem vel otiositatem, orationem propter nimiam securitatem. Nostrum vero non est, sed aliorum, quidquid habemus sine quo salvamur: ut sunt sermo sapientiae, sermo scientiae gratia, curationum, et caetera quae 419Chaberi possunt praeter charitatem, in qua sola salvamur. Haec si retinemus in tempore, ut dictum est, fraudulenti 271 sumus Cum enim res exigit ut effundantur, cavendum est ne aut tepide, aut trepide, aut invidiose retineantur. Tepide, de auditoribus diffidentes; trepide, si irrationabiliter timentes; invidiose, fratrem non amantes. In primo fidei, in secundo spei, in tertio charitatis defectus deprehenditur. Cum vero effunduntur, cavendum est ne incaute, ne temere, ne ambitiose effundantur. Incaute, ne sanctum det canibus; temere, ut fidei regulas non excedat qui loquitur; ambitiose, ne quaerat quae sua sunt, sed quae Jesu Christi. Cavendum etiam ne intempestive, ne praesumptuose, ne infructuose: intempestive, ante maturitatem animae et 419Dcorporis; praesumptuose, sine officio; infructuose, sine gratia. In his omnibus, si bona sit effusio, ubi facta est olei hujus infusio, tres suas oleum operatur virtutes. Oleum enim pascit, lucet et ungit: charitas etiam pascit se, lucet proximo, ungit Deum.

14.

According to the moral sense, in the pouring out of the oil, when the infusion is made to us through grace, care must be taken lest the pouring out be made in a disordered way. For something is infused into us that is our own, something that is the neighbor's. That is ours without which we are not saved; the rest are the neighbor's. If we pour out what is our own, we are foolish and empty: if we retain what is another's, we are fraudulent. What is our own is faith, hope, and charity. Faith makes servants, hope hirelings, charity sons. Faith makes a handmaid or an attendant, hope a concubine, charity a bride. Faith says, So it is: hope, Thou shalt have: charity, Give glory to God. Faith is the knowledge of evils or goods; hope, the confidence of avoiding or attaining; and charity, the grace of both, when, secure concerning both, one gives thanks. These, therefore, 419Bsince they are our own, are not to be poured out, that is, lost for the sake of others: but it must be brought about that they work in us what is their own, that is, that we be healed, that we be kept, that we be saved. Let us be healed through the labor of the body, kept through reading and meditation, saved through prayer. Sloth lies in wait for labor; restlessness or idleness for reading; excessive security for prayer. What, therefore, is our own, we sin if we pour it out, as has been said: that is, if we neglect labor because of sloth, reading because of restlessness or idleness, prayer because of excessive security. But not ours, but others', is whatever we have without which we are saved: such as the word of wisdom, the word of knowledge, the grace of healings, and the rest that 419Ccan be had apart from charity, in which alone we are saved. If we retain these in time, as has been said, we are fraudulent. 271 For when the matter requires that they be poured out, care must be taken lest they be retained either lukewarmly, or timidly, or enviously. Lukewarmly, distrusting the hearers; timidly, if fearing unreasonably; enviously, not loving the brother. In the first a defect of faith, in the second of hope, in the third of charity is detected. But when they are poured out, care must be taken lest they be poured out incautiously, or rashly, or ambitiously. Incautiously, lest one give the holy thing to dogs; rashly, that he who speaks may not exceed the rules of faith; ambitiously, lest he seek the things that are his own, but the things that are of Jesus Christ. Care must also be taken lest they be poured out unseasonably, presumptuously, unfruitfully: unseasonably, before the maturity of soul and 419Dbody; presumptuously, without office; unfruitfully, without grace. In all these, if the pouring out be good, where the infusion of this oil has been made, the oil works its three powers. For oil feeds, shines, and anoints: charity too feeds itself, shines for the neighbor, anoints God.

Draw me after thee, we will run in the odor of thy ointments. The bride, condescending to the maidens from that height in which she had been beside herself unto God, after she poured back upon the maidens that pouring out of oil infused into her, while she labored among them; remembering the kiss and the oil and the bridegroom, and sighing toward him from whom the care of the neighbor drew her against her will, Draw me after thee, she says. For when she is beside herself, it is unto God; when she is sober, it is unto the neighbor. When she is beside herself, it is shown to her how much she is loved by God: when she is sober, she shows 420Ain the care of the neighbor how much she loves God. The charity of truth draws her upward, the truth of charity draws her below. But although this necessity here detains her, yet that sweetness cannot depart from her mind. Therefore draw me, she says, O bridegroom. For there are four things that draw us away from God: the flesh by allurements, the devil by wiles, the world by cares, the neighbor by affection. Drawn from these, or from some of these, against her will away from God, the bride longs and prays to be drawn to God. For one drawing is voluntary, another compulsory: as into prison, compulsory; voluntary, as for the weary or the blind. The bride wishes to be drawn, who prays to be drawn; and that she wishes to be drawn, this is because of blindness, or because of weakness, or because of both. But she was suffering the weariness of the world or of the devil, 420Bbut also the keenness of her mind was disturbed in the care of the neighbor. Against all these she desires to be drawn from them to God, by God, by the power of God. For this drawing is in power. Sed non est praetereundum, quod grex Domini tribus modis pascitur a pastoribus; exemplo, 425Averbo, oratione. Exemplo, contra concupiscentiam carnis, verbo, contra concupiscentiam oculorum; oratione, contra ambitionem saeculi. Si ignoras te, o pulchra inter mulieres. Quaerebat sponsa sponsum in meridie, id est in manifesta luce veritatis, scilicet ne ei aliud occurreret pro alio: quaerebat ubi cubaret in meridie, id est in qua affectione 275 meridianae perfectionis, ut videret quae quando eligenda esset affectio, ubi sponsus cum ea cubaret, et ipsa cum sponso. Respondit itaque sponsus: O pulchra inter mulieres, scilicet inter Mariam et Martham, quia nolo ut alteram de istis eligas, sed utramque teneas, contemplativam scilicet vitam et activam; o pulchra inter mulieres, si vis nosse me, noli ignorare te. Hoc est responsum Delphici Apollinis: 425B« Scito teipsum. » Duo sunt quae nos faciunt ignorare nosipsos, vel nimia de nobis temeritas, vel nimia humiliationis timiditas. Secundum haec duo respondit sponsus sponsae, vel de temeritate revocans eam ad humilitatem, per experientiam sui, vel de timiditate ad confidentiam, ex perceptione praeteritorum munerum. Duae enim sunt ignorantiae sui, vel quando plus, vel quando minus de se quis aestimat: et duo sunt quae hoc efficiunt, praesumptio et pusillanimitas; duae vero horum effectuum causae, superbia, et minor sui experientia. Experientia vero sui facit humilitatem; humilitas, sui cognitionem. Reprimens itaque sponsus temerariam: Praepropere, inquit, et impatienter desideras meridiem angelicae beatitudinis; quia primo tibi 425Cagendum est multum, et laborandum in matutino virtutis. Si igitur ignoras te, egredere a me, vel a bono conscientiae tuae, et non stes mecum, sed abi post vestigia gregum et pasce haedos tuos, id est pravos motus carnis, vel perditos auditores, qui statuentur a sinistris, juxta tabernacula pastorum; id est, secuta magistrorum reproborum doctrinas, non in Ecclesia, sed in conventiculorum suorum prava docentium tabernaculis. Quod autem dicit, Egredere, permittit, non praecipit, quod etiam nec ex toto permittere videtur, sed revocat ad laborem.

After the flocks. For he who departs from the perfection of truth into the byway of error, does not even go with the wandering flocks, but after the flocks; because he is found worse than all these. But it must not be passed over, that the flock of the Lord is fed by its shepherds in three ways: by example, 425Aby word, by prayer. By example, against the concupiscence of the flesh; by word, against the concupiscence of the eyes; by prayer, against the ambition of the world. If thou know not thyself, O fairest among women. The bride was seeking the Bridegroom at noon, that is in the manifest light of truth — namely, lest one thing should meet her instead of another; she was seeking where he would lie down at noon, that is in which affection 275 of noonday perfection, that she might see which affection was to be chosen and when, where the Bridegroom would lie down with her, and she with the Bridegroom. And so the Bridegroom answered: O fairest among women — namely, between Mary and Martha, because I do not wish that thou choose one of these, but that thou hold both, namely the contemplative life and the active; O fairest among women, if thou wouldst know me, be not ignorant of thyself. This is the answer of the Delphic Apollo: 425B« Know thyself. » There are two things that make us ignorant of ourselves: either too much rashness about ourselves, or too much timidity of humiliation. According to these two the Bridegroom answered the bride: either recalling her from rashness to humility, through experience of herself, or from timidity to confidence, from the perception of past gifts. For there are two kinds of ignorance of oneself: either when one esteems more, or when one esteems less of oneself; and there are two things that produce this, presumption and pusillanimity; and two causes of these effects, pride and too little experience of oneself. But experience of oneself makes humility; humility, knowledge of oneself. And so the Bridegroom, restraining the rash one: Too soon, he says, and impatiently dost thou desire the noon of angelic beatitude; because first thou must do much, and labor in the 425Cmorning of virtue. If therefore thou know not thyself, go forth from me, or from the good of thy conscience, and stand not with me, but go after the steps of the flocks and feed thy kids, that is the depraved motions of the flesh, or the lost hearers, who shall be set on the left, beside the tents of the shepherds; that is, having followed the doctrines of reprobate masters, not in the Church, but in the tents of their conventicles teaching depraved things. But what he says, Go forth, he permits, he does not command — which also he does not even seem to permit wholly, but recalls her to labor.

Contra hos tres currus, tres sunt currus Dei. Currus continentiae duas habet rotas, orationem et exercitationem corporis: equus trahens devotio, cui insidet miles Dei, contemptus sui in sella puritatis; frenum habens disciplinam; stimulum, stimulum timoris Domini. Currus sapientiae duas habet rotas, prudentiam et simplicitatem; equum, fortitudinem; equitem, amorem Dei; sellam, vigilantiam ne cadat; frenum, modestiam, ut regat; stimulum, zelum justitiae. Cum ergo, amica, currus habeas ut Pharao, et equitatui meo in curribus Pharaonis assimilaverim te, satagendum nunc est de victoria, non de praemio victoriae: nimis enim est, et praeproperum quod quaeris. I have likened thee, my love, to my company of horsemen in the chariots of Pharaoh (Cant. I, 8). O my love, though I restrain thee as rash, though I recall thee to labor, yet not on that account less my love. Already thou wouldst sit with me in my kingdom; I should be indignant at the presumption, but I pardon the love. O my love, thou must not now reign, but fight; because I have likened thee to my company of horsemen in the chariots of Pharaoh. 425DThou art set in the midst of the chariots of Pharaoh: not only must thou go forth, but thou hast to lead forth my people out of the land of bondage. Or, that I may raise up the pusillanimous one: Thou shalt not perish, O my love, because in the chariots of Pharaoh I have likened thee to my company of horsemen, that is, I have made thee like to the company of horsemen of the Angels: so that as they have charity, and chastity, and humility, and order, so also thou, set in the midst of the chariots of Pharaoh. For as long as we are here in readiness for battle, and our wrestling is not against flesh and blood, but against the 426Aspiritual wickednesses in the heavenly places, both armies, both the chariot of God and the chariot of Pharaoh, are joined as in war: for as the chariot of Pharaoh, that is of the devil, so also the chariot of God is manifold with ten thousand. It is pleasing to describe more subtly these very chariots both of God and of the devil, and to show more evidently who serve as soldiers of God or of the devil. The chariots of the devil are three, with which he traverses the whole world: luxury, pride, malice. The chariot of luxury has two wheels, gluttony and softness, which the impulse of the flesh draws like an untamed horse; upon which sits love of self in the saddle of pleasure, having want for its bridle, and for its spur, the spur of lust. The chariot of pride has two wheels, presumption of self and rashness: the horse that draws is the appetite of vainglory, upon which sits love 426Bof the world, in the saddle of adulation, having avarice for its bridle, and for its spur, the spur of spite. The chariot of malice has two wheels, feigning and dissembling: the horse that draws is malevolence, upon which sits the relish of evil, in the saddle of fraud, having tergiversation for its bridle, and for its spur, the spur of wrath. Against these three chariots, there are three chariots of God. The chariot of continence has two wheels, prayer and bodily exercise: the horse that draws is devotion, upon which sits the soldier of God, contempt of self, in the saddle of purity; having discipline for its bridle, and for its spur, the spur of the fear of the Lord. The chariot of innocence has two wheels, the humility of him who is unwilling to be over others, and the meekness of him who is willing to be under: these the horse draws, that is love of peace, having contempt of the world for its rider, 276 in the saddle 426Cof benevolence; for its bridle, zeal of truth; for its spur, the spur of the love of God and of neighbor. The chariot of wisdom has two wheels, prudence and simplicity; for its horse, fortitude; for its rider, the love of God; for its saddle, vigilance lest it fall; for its bridle, moderation, that it may govern; for its spur, zeal of justice. Since therefore, my love, thou hast chariots as Pharaoh, and I have likened thee to my company of horsemen in the chariots of Pharaoh, thou must now be busy about victory, not about the reward of victory: for what thou seekest is too much, and too soon. Thou shalt not now see my face, but thou shalt see my back parts. For though thou dost not now receive the noon which thou askest, yet many things meanwhile thou dost receive from me, in which thou art deservedly praised. 426D

For already by my grace thy cheeks are beautiful as the turtledove's; that is, great are both the outer and the inner gifts with which I have enriched thee. For what thou hast received within, the honesty and maturity of thy manner of life shows outwardly, which are the two cheeks of the turtledove, that is of the soul, mourning in the absence of the Bridegroom, and receiving no consolation, and dwelling alone. As in the pallor or ruddiness of the cheeks the quality of the outer man is discerned, so in the honesty and maturity of the manner of life the sincerity of a well-composed mind is shown. And not only this, but also thy neck is as jewels. Purity of soul is commended in thee, as I have said, before men, by the honesty and maturity of thy manner of life; but before God, by the directing of thy intention. For thy intention, which like a neck 427Ajoins the whole body of thy work to the head, Christ, is as a jewel, when it is made manifest to all that it serves God and the neighbor. For this is what the jewel signifies, the ornament of the neck, which outwardly has gold, but inwardly a gem — namely that the intention be affected with the love of God, and the love of neighbor. When the Bridegroom declares this to men, he adorns the bride's neck with a jewel; nay, he makes it to be as jewels. Muraenulas aureas faciemus tibi, inquit, vermiculatas argento (Cantic. I, 6-10). Muraenulae sunt ornatus aurium, quae a sponso sponsae dantur, cum bonus datur auditus ejus praedicationi. Vermiculatas argento, id est, distinctas eloquio: quia alii datur sermo sapientiae, quod significat aurum; alii sermo scientiae (I Cor. XII, 8), quod significat argentum. Promittit itaque sponsus, quod interius et exterius eam glorificabit. Muraenulas, inquit, faciemus, ego et Pater et Spiritus; me sapientiam, Patre efficaciam, Spiritu sancto praebente benignitatem. Faciemus 427Cautem de nihilo, id est ex nullo praecedente merito; hoc enim significat nomen facturae. Unde et ibi dicitur: Faciamus hominem (Gen. I, 26). Vel faciemus, ego et Angeli et doctores mei: ego inspirando, Angeli suggerendo, homines docendo. Deus enim videt intentionem, et angelus affectionem, homo factum. Linguam ergo Angelorum intelligit, qui suggestiones illorum intelligit. 28.

There follows: Behold, thou art fair, my love; behold, thou art fair, thy eyes are the eyes of doves. The Bridegroom, seeing the devotion of the bride, and that she gave him a place of abiding between her breasts: Behold, he says — that is, in this thy state, in this thy confession and knowledge of thyself, since whence thou art fair, thence I am not fair and have neither beauty nor comeliness — thou, he says, art fair. And again, Behold, thou art fair: that is, fair in things inward, and fair in things outward; fair in intention, and fair in action. My love. Two things make friendship: mutual consciousness and the same will. That it may be able to see clearly, the eye has these necessary things: a strong and pure ray, which proceeds from the pupil; a pure and clear air, which does not impede its passage; a body into which it may pour itself, a reason to which it may report, a memory which the reason may consult. If any of these be lacking, the sight will not be perfect. And this is the dovelike eye.

Again the Bridegroom withdraws from the bride, and hides himself in the secret of his heaven — he who was already thought to be held in the bed of conscience. The shadow between the deity of Christ and us is his body, under which the bride sits, while modestly and humbly she contemplates the mystery of his Incarnation: 431Cand his fruit is sweet to her throat, because it does not pass into her belly, that is into satiety, as is that word of Job, Nor dost thou suffer me to swallow down my spittle (Job VII, 19). For the garden contains the historical sense; the cellar, the moral; the house, the mystical. The garden contains the histories of the New and Old Testament, of the present and the future age; the cellar, the moral instruction of Christ; the house, the glory of the eternal reward. In the garden, the creation of all things that are; in the cellar, the reconciliation of all men who are saved; in the house, the confirmation and glorification of all the reconciled. 32.

Introduxit me rex in cellam vinariam (Cant. II, 4). Fructus enim horti, ut dictum est, defertur in cellarium: amoenitas in horto remanet, utilitas alibi quaerenda est; si eam invenire vis, ingredere cellarium. Tria habet merita, quasi tres moralitatis cellas: habitum ad praelatos, quae est disciplina; habitum ad pares, quae est justitia vel amicitia; habitum ad subditos, quae est charitas. Quod ut brevius 432Cet lucidius dicamus, est ut sciat quis subesse, coesse, praeesse. Prima cella plena est odoribus; secunda, saporibus; tertia, gratia. Prima enim magis proficit exemplis; secunda, moralibus vel mysticis studiis; tertia, charismatibus Spiritus sancti. Haec est enim cella vinaria. In hac bibunt amici, inebriantur charissimi. Nullus enim coram Deo altior est ordine praelatorum, qui pleni sapientia Dei, et ipsius in subditis vicem gerentes, a fine possibilitatis attingunt fortiter ad finem impossibilitatis, et inter haec duo disponunt omnia suaviter. Cella haec juxta domum regis est. Saepe ergo exinde domum regis ingreditur, et secretum cubiculi, tam per se quam per subditos Domino sponso serviens de commissis sibi bonis et horti, et cellarii. 32.

The king brought me into the wine cellar (Cant. II, 4). For the fruit of the garden, as was said, is carried down into the cellar: the pleasantness remains in the garden, the usefulness is to be sought elsewhere; if thou wilt find it, enter the cellar. It has three merits, as it were three cells of morality: the disposition toward prelates, which is discipline; the disposition toward equals, which is justice or friendship; the disposition toward subordinates, which is charity. And that we may say it more briefly 432Cand more clearly, it is that one may know how to be under, to be alongside, to be over. The first cell is full of odors; the second, of savors; the third, of grace. For the first profits more by examples; the second, by moral or mystical studies; the third, by the charisms of the Holy Spirit. For this is the wine cellar. In it the friends drink, the most beloved are inebriated. For none before God is higher in rank than that of prelates, who, full of the wisdom of God, and bearing his stead among their subordinates, from the limit of possibility reach mightily to the limit of impossibility, and between these two dispose all things sweetly. These, for their brethren, according to the Apostle, wish to be anathema from Christ (Rom. IX, 3), but from Christ the beatitude, not from Christ the justice. For to us Christ is each, 432Dboth beatitude and justice; but so much the more gloriously do they acquire beatitude, as for beatitude's sake they are unwilling to forsake justice. For these words of the Apostle are of affection, not of effect. For this inebriation, by which the most beloved are inebriated when for the love of God and of the brethren one casts away his inmost things, this is the wine cellar, into which the bride merited to be brought, when she learned to be over others in charity. This cell is beside the house of the king. Often therefore thence she enters the house of the king, and the secret of the bedchamber, serving the Lord the Bridegroom, both by herself and through her subordinates, from the good things committed to her both of the garden and of the cellar. For whether prelate or subordinate, whoever has put on this affection of charity has free access hither. Whence the bride, above, when she desired to be drawn after the Bridegroom, and yet herself to draw the maidens 433Aafter her, saying, We will run after thee to the odor of thy ointments: at once, as she herself declares, the king brought her into these cellars, that thence she might receive wherewith to refresh them, lest they fail in the course; and in order, from the garden to the cellar, from the cellar to the king's bedchamber, he might bring them in; now indeed through the affection of contemplation and of love, but afterward through the effect of beatitude. Moreover the house has one reward of the Lord, save that through this it is divided into three rewards, that we may know, what, how great, in what manner; that is, what God is, body or spirit; how great, created or uncreated; in what manner he is to be found.

Introduxit me ergo, ait sponsa, in cellam vinariam, id est in plenitudinem charitatis, in qua ne 433Binebriata musto Spiritus sancti perperam aliquid agerem, cum ad subditos redirem, ordinavit in me charitatem (Cant. Nos enim primum, quia nobis conceditur principatus, ut primum amemus nos, deinde proximos sicut nos. Sed et in nobis debemus saepe actum charitatis corporis praeponere actui charitatis animae; et actum charitatis animae, actui charitatis Dei, id est contemplationi. Haec tamen omnia cum exigit 281 necessitas, quae non habet legem. Est enim lex charitatis, et est necessitas charitatis. Lex charitatis praecipit actum charitatis Dei praeponere actui animae, et actum animae actui corporis. Necessitas charitatis saepe omnia his contraria. Inde est quod multi dicunt, pauci intelligunt: Necessitas non habet legem. Procedit ordo 433Det regula charitatis, quae praecipit suum corpus et alterius minus diligere quam suam et alterius animam; suam vero animam et alterius plus diligere quam suum corpus vel alterius: suam animam non plus quam alterius, sed alterius sicut suam. Haec quantum ad affectum charitatis. Caeterum actus ipsius charitatis saepe aliter ordinatur. Praeponitur enim saepe, ut dictum est, in actu corpus animae, anima Deo. Quod si fratri tuo, ejusdem meriti et utilitatis tecum in eadem necessitate posito, prius subvenias, abundas charitate; si tibi, non peccas. Nam in eo quod dicitur sicut te (Matth. XXII, 39), primatus tibi conceditur: si autem disparis meriti fueris, meliori est subveniendum. Et cum omnes aequaliter diligere debeamus, non tamen sine causa familiares orationes 434Afacimus: non quod omnibus prodesse nolimus, sed quia illis maxime prodesse credimus orationes nostras qui nobis se committunt, vel quia magis corda nostra excitantur. Itaque hoc ordine Sponsus in me ordinavit charitatem.

33.

He brought me therefore, says the bride, into the wine cellar, that is into the fullness of charity, in which, lest, 433Binebriated with the new wine of the Holy Spirit, I should do anything amiss when I returned to my subordinates, he set charity in order in me (Cant. II, 4). Charity has two things, affection and act. Of the affection: Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with thy whole heart, and with thy whole mind, and with all thy strength (Matth. XXII, 37); in which all kinds of affection are comprehended. Affection therefore is owed to God principally, secondarily to the neighbor, whom in God we ought to love as ourselves. For ourselves first, because to us is granted the primacy, that first we love ourselves, then our neighbors as ourselves. But to the charity of God there is owed a certain purgatory act, that is one purging us to the vision of God, such as are vigils, fasts, meditations, readings, compunctions: and an act is owed also to the neighbor, such as 433Cthe works of mercy, and the rest that the necessity of the active and social life demands. But the affection of God we ought in all things to prefer to the affection of the neighbor, yet not always the act of God to the act of the neighbor. But even in ourselves we ought often to prefer the act of the charity of the body to the act of the charity of the soul; and the act of the charity of the soul to the act of the charity of God, that is to contemplation. Yet all these things when necessity demands, which has no law. For there is a law of charity, and there is a necessity of charity. The law of charity commands the act of the charity of God to be preferred to the act of the soul, and the act of the soul to the act of the body. The necessity of charity often [commands] all things contrary to these. Hence it is that many say, few understand: Necessity has no law. There proceeds the order 433Dand rule of charity, which commands one to love his own body and another's less than his own and another's soul; but to love his own soul and another's more than his own body or another's: his own soul not more than another's, but another's as his own. This as far as regards the affection of charity. But the act of charity itself is often ordered otherwise. For often, as was said, in the act the body is preferred to the soul, the soul to God. But if thou first succor thy brother, set with thee in the same necessity, of the same merit and usefulness, thou dost abound in charity; if thyself, thou dost not sin. For in that it is said as thyself (Matth. XXII, 39), the primacy is granted to thee: but if thou be of unequal merit, the better is to be succored. And though we ought to love all equally, yet not without cause do we make private prayers 434Afor particular ones: not that we are unwilling to profit all, but because we believe our prayers to profit especially those who commit themselves to us, or because our hearts are the more stirred up. And so in this order the Bridegroom set charity in order in me.

34.

Et quandoquidem Christi charitas urget nos ad vos, Fulcite me floribus affectionum vestrarum, stipate me malis operum, quia amore absentis langueo. Est amor pius, est amor fervens, est languens. Pius ad impetrandam veniam peccatorum, fervens in exhibitione operum, languens, cum deficit anima in salutari Dei, id est, intuitu contemplationis. Cum haec dicens sponsa langueret, rursum sensit sponsum praesentem, et laevam ejus sub capite suo, dexteram vero ejus se amplexantem. Ideoque respirans, 434BEn, inquit, iterum sponsus; Laeva ejus sub capite meo, et dextera ejus amplexabitur me. Ponit ergo sponsa caput ut requiescat, non super laevam, sed in laeva sponsi, cum afficitur mysteriis humanitatis Christi: dextera vero sponsi eam amplectitur, dum illuminatis oculis ejus, totam eam possidet amor Divinitatis, et intellectus. In hoc tam pio amplexu sponsa in id ipsum obdormivit et requievit. Sponsus etiam exsultans in amplexu charitatis ejus, filias Jerusalem adjurat, id est quamdam eis reverentiam erga sponsam inspirat. Adjurat autem eas per capreas cervosque camporum, cum exhibet eis agilitatem et alacritatem bonorum operum. Adjuro, in quit, vos, filiae Jerusalem, per capreas, cervosque camporum, ut non suscitetis occasionibus necessitatum 434Cvestrarum, neque evigilare faciatis sponsam impulsu malorum vestrorum, quoadusque ipsa velit; id est, contemplatione gratiae meae satiata, ex impetu concepti amoris, ipsa per se ad vos redibit. Sponsa de medio consolationis et contemplationis somno audiens verba sponsi, id est, videns affectum gratiae ejus, quia adolescentulae nec eam excitarent, nec evigilare eam facerent; sed sicut capreae cervique camporum in alacritate bonorum operum se exercerent, ipsamque in contemplationis suae quiete inquietari nefas ducerent: exsurgens in laudem sponsi, Vox, inquit, dilecti mei (Cantic. II, 5-8). Haec est vox dilecti mei, haec est voluntas ejus, hoc est opus ejus, haec est virtus ejus in illarum conversione, haec 434Dest dulcedo ejus in mea dilectione. Ecce quomodo ordinata charitate omnia ordinata sunt et peccata, et in pace factus est locus ejus, et in Sion habitatio ejus (Psal. LXXV, 3), cum sponsa in amplexu sponsi quiescit, cum sponsus eam fovet et protegit, cum filiae non suscitant eam, nec evigilare faciunt; sed potius bonis suis studiis laetam faciunt et jucundam.

34.

And since the charity of Christ urges us toward you, Stay me up with the flowers of your affections, compass me about with the apples of works, because I languish with love of the absent one. There is a love that is devout, there is a love that is fervent, there is one that languishes. Devout, to obtain pardon of sins; fervent, in the performance of works; languishing, when the soul faints in the salvation of God, that is, in the gaze of contemplation. While the bride, saying these things, was languishing, again she felt the Bridegroom present, and his left hand under her head, but his right hand embracing her. And therefore, taking breath, 434BLo, she says, again the Bridegroom; His left hand is under my head, and his right hand shall embrace me. The bride therefore lays her head that she may rest, not upon the left, but in the left of the Bridegroom, when she is affected by the mysteries of the humanity of Christ: but the right hand of the Bridegroom embraces her, when, her eyes being enlightened, the love and understanding of the Divinity wholly possesses her. In this so devout embrace the bride slept and rested in the selfsame. The Bridegroom also, exulting in the embrace of her charity, adjures the daughters of Jerusalem, that is, inspires in them a certain reverence toward the bride. And he adjures them by the roes and the harts of the fields, when he shows them the agility and alacrity of good works. I adjure you, he says, you, daughters of Jerusalem, by the roes and the harts of the fields, that you stir not up, by the occasions of your necessities, 434Cnor make to awake the bride, by the impulse of your evils, till she herself please; that is, satiated with the contemplation of my grace, from the impulse of the love conceived, she herself of her own accord will return to you. The bride, out of the midst of the sleep of consolation and contemplation hearing the words of the Bridegroom, that is, seeing the affection of his grace — because the maidens neither roused her nor made her to awake, but like roes and harts of the fields exercised themselves in the alacrity of good works, and counted it wrong to disquiet her in the quiet of her contemplation — rising up in praise of the Bridegroom, The voice, she says, of my beloved (Cantic. II, 5-8). This is the voice of my beloved, this is his will, this is his work, this is his power in their conversion, this 434Dis his sweetness in my love. Behold how, charity being set in order, all things are set in order, even sins, and his place is made in peace, and his dwelling in Sion (Psal. LXXV, 3), when the bride rests in the embrace of the Bridegroom, when the Bridegroom cherishes and protects her, when the daughters do not stir her up, nor make her to awake; but rather by their good pursuits make her glad and joyful.

35.

Hoc significabat mulier illa, quae clamabat ad Elisaeum mortuo viro suo, servo ejus, sponsam Deum Patrem invocantem per mortem Filii sui viri ejus: Servus, inquit, tuus, vir meus, mortuus est; et ecce venit creditor, ut tollat sibi duos filios meos ad serviendum sibi. Sic et sponsa duos filios, alterum verbo, alterum exemplo genuerat, quos tulerat de manu 435Adiaboli. Cumque charitate ordinata sibi et Deo vacaret, filiosque negligeret, actumque Dei actui eorum praeponeret; facta est fames verbi Dei: parvuli petierunt panem, nec erat qui porrigeret eis. Coepit creditor diabolus redire, et jam non sicut perditor, sed sicut creditor illos repetere ad serviendum sibi. Mater vero quia non volebat labore bonorum studiorum et actuum agere, putans se acturam sola oratione, coepit ad Deum clamare. Et quid dixit illi responsum divinum? Quid habes, inquit, in domo tua, id est, in conscientia tua? At illa, Vivit, inquit, Dominus, non habeo ego ancilla tua aliquid in domo mea, nisi parum olei in vase, id est, devotionis in mente, quo ungar. Nota delicias. Non eget pasci, sed desiderat ungi. At ille, Vade, inquit, et pete mutuo vasa vacua non pauca, id est auditorum corda, et mitte 436Ade oleo devotionis quo abundas per omnia vasa illa, et cum impleta fuerint, revertere ad me. Non enim, ut dicis, parum habes olei, quae in tempore famis non solum pascendam te, sed ungendam praesumis. Audiens haec sponsa, charitatem reordinavit; ut missa fuerat, oleum Spiritus sancti mentibus auditorum infudit: Deus autem vacua vasa implevit. Quod cum factum esset, Vade, inquit, vende oleum, et libera te a creditore; id est, pro effusione devotionis tuae, aeternae vitae exspecta praemia. Tu autem et filii tui vivite de reliquo (IV Reg. IV, 1-7); quia quanta a te aliis fiet olei hujus effusio, tanta a me tibi fiet infusio. Sed vivite, inquit; non, Ungimini: vos instate ut vivatis, tu videlicet et filii tui; ego tempore suo de unctione deliciarum vestrarum providebo.

35.

This that woman signified who cried out to Elisha, her husband, his servant, being dead — the bride invoking God the Father through the death of the Son, her husband: Thy servant, she says, my husband, is dead; and behold, the creditor is come to take my two sons to serve him. So also the bride had borne two sons, the one by word, the other by example, whom she had taken from the hand 435Aof the devil. And when, charity being set in order, she was free for herself and for God, and neglected the sons, and preferred the act of God to their act, there arose a famine of the word of God: the little ones asked for bread, and there was none to reach it to them. The creditor, the devil, began to return, and now not as a destroyer, but as a creditor, to demand them back to serve him. But the mother, because she was unwilling to act by the labor of good pursuits and acts, thinking she would accomplish it by prayer alone, began to cry out to God. And what did the divine answer say to her? What hast thou, he says, in thy house, that is, in thy conscience? And she, The Lord liveth, she says, I thy handmaid have not anything in my house, but a little oil in a vessel, that is, of devotion in the mind, wherewith I may be anointed. Note the delights. She needs not to be fed, but desires to be anointed. And he, Go, he says, and borrow vessels not a few, empty ones, that is, the hearts of hearers, and pour 436Aof the oil of devotion, in which thou aboundest, through all those vessels, and when they shall be full, return to me. For thou hast not, as thou sayest, little oil, who in the time of famine dost presume not only to be fed but to be anointed. Hearing these things, the bride set charity in order again; as she had been sent, she poured the oil of the Holy Spirit into the minds of the hearers: but God filled the empty vessels. And when this was done, Go, he says, sell the oil, and free thyself from the creditor; that is, for the pouring out of thy devotion, await the rewards of eternal life. But thou and thy sons, live of the rest (IV Reg. IV, 1-7); because as great as shall be the pouring out of this oil made by thee to others, so great shall be the infusion made by me to thee. But live, he says; not, Be anointed: do you press on that you may live, namely thou and thy sons; I in its own time will provide for the anointing of your delights.
