Innocent I

Innocentius I

d. 417

Bishop of Rome, and the first pope from whom a long series of decretal letters survives: answers to bishops in Gaul, Spain, Italy, Illyricum and the East on discipline, orders, marriage, the canon and the date of Easter. Pierre Coustant edited them in 1721 as the first volume of his Epistolae Romanorum Pontificum, and Migne reprints that edition with Coustant’s notes. His letters to the African councils on the Pelagian controversy stand among them, and three of them are also numbered as Augustine’s Epistles 181–183.

MONITUM.

623CIn vetusta collectione illa Lucensi, de qua non semel egi, in epitome plurimorum conciliorum et decretalium quarumdam epistolarum occurrit excerptum de Epistola Innocentii Papae ex concilio Nicaeno, quam epistolam Innocentii I papae esse nemo prudens dubitaverit, cum omnem alium Innocentium pontificem superet codicis hujus aetas annorum saltem nongentorum. Quaenam vero sit epistola haec Innocentii, quo ex codice descripta, an singularem epistolam de Nicaenis canonibus Innocentius dederit, vel illos data occasione in variis epistolis suis adduxerit et inculcaverit, quos dein epitomis hujus auctor in unam canonum seriem continuaverit, plane ignoro. Quamquam vero nihil novi his exhibetur, juvat tamen qualecumque est hoc dare, cum vetustissima haec sit canonum Nicaenorum Latina editio. Quae enim Dionysium Exiguum auctorem praefert, utique posterior est Innocentio I. Quae vero in collectione Isidori Mercatoris profertur, an vetustior sit sive recentior, nondum est exploratum. 623DQuae tandem est Rufini, ejusdem fere antiquitatis est cum Innocentio.

NOTICE.

623CIn that old collection of Lucca, of which I have treated more than once, there occurs, in an epitome of very many councils and of certain decretal letters, an extract from the Letter of Pope Innocent from the council of Nicaea, which letter no prudent man would doubt to be Pope Innocent I's, since the age of this codex, of at least nine hundred years, surpasses every other pontiff named Innocent. But what this letter of Innocent may be, from what codex it was copied, whether Innocent issued a single letter on the Nicene canons, or, as occasion was given, adduced and inculcated them in his various letters, and the author of this epitome then continued them into one series of canons, I plainly do not know. And although nothing new is presented in these, it is nevertheless useful to give this, such as it is, since this is the most ancient Latin edition of the Nicene canons. For the one which bears Dionysius Exiguus as its author is certainly later than Innocent I. And whether the one which is put forward in the collection of Isidore Mercator is older or more recent has not yet been investigated. 623DFinally, the one that is Rufinus's is of nearly the same antiquity as Innocent.

DISSSERTATIO In sequentem Epitomen epistolae Innocentii.

Novas Innocentii I epistolas, quamvis nullas habeo promendas, non abs re tamen puto cum lectoribus 625Cmeis communicari oportere ea quae breviarium nostrum canonum in ms. codice Lucensi annorum nongentorum exhibet. Exstant in eo canones e nonnullis Innocentii epistolis expressi, et in epitomen redacti. In ea vero quae ex epistola ad Exsuperium exprimitur epitome deest canon 3, de iis, qui post baptismum magistratum agentes, in causa sanguinis sententiam tulerunt. Cujus omissionis nullam aliam rationem excogitare possum, nisi quod Pontifex ea in re nihil judicat decernendum. Qui succedit canon 4, qui in nostra epitome est 3, male expressus est; neque enim communione privantur uxorati, si adulteraverint, sed innocens ab adultero segregatur. Caetera cum epistola congruunt. Non est negligendum additamentum illud de Lamentationibus Jeremiae, quod ab uno nostro manuscripto suppeditatur, nec in editis legitur.

Post hanc epitomen altera succedit epistola Innocentii ad episcopos Toletanos. Nonnulla plane in ea digna sunt animadversione. Primo enim in nostro 625Dbreviario perinde ac in omnibus antiquis codicibus, teste Harduino, legitur Tolosanos, non Toletanos.

Sequitur epistolae Innocentii ad Victricium ( Victorium habet codex noster) synopsis, in qua omnia plana sunt, et cum editis congruunt, unam si excipias canonis 3 partem secundam, in qua edita quidem epistola audiendas statuit causas ecclesiasticorum in suis singulas provinciis, sine praejudicio tamen appellationis ad Romanam ecclesiam, vel nisi causae gravitas ad sedem apostolicam judicium referat. Haec edita. Codex vero ms. non ita, sed: Romani clerici ad alios episcopos non audeant dicere causas suas. Longe profecto haec duo inter se distant, ut ex altero et altero codice petita videantur. Verum ea quae in editis leguntur Innocentii I papae esse nemo jure ambiget, cum a Nicolao I in gemina epistola in concilio Romano VII, apud Labeum, tom. X, legenda, tamquam ex Innocentio laudentur. Cum 626Avero nec ex epistola ad Victricium accepisse se illa scribat Nicolaus, nec breviator noster usque adeo semper in sensu canonum reddendo sit felix, alterutrum consequi necesse est; vel canon iste, prout in editis, ex alia quadam epistola Innocentii cum hac ejus ad Victricium male assutus est, uti aliis veterum Pontificum epistolis accidisse constat: vel anonymus noster Pontificis mentem minime est assecutus.

In epitome canonis 8, edicitur de monachis in clero non admittendis; id vero non mandat Innocentius, sed monet, ne ad altiorem gradum promoti modestiam, et virtutes reliquas quas in monasterio coluerant, abjiciant. Quare breviatoris nostri oscitantia castiganda est.

Epistolae Innocentii ad Decentium synopsis plane barbara est, sed ejus primum caput veterem quemdam liturgicum ritum exprimit, quem edita non tangunt, quamvis in sensu cum editis congruit. Vetat Pontifex canone 1, ne pax ante finem detur. Id vero 626Banonymus noster ita expressit, Ordo missae ad pacem dare post quartam orationem. Quartae orationis nomine designari hic censeo illam quam ordo Romanus vetus apud Mabillonium Musaei Ital. tom. II orationem ad complendum appellat. In missa enim oratio prior ea erat quae ante Apostolum, id est ante epistolam legebatur. Succedebat altera super oblata. Dein oratio Dominica tertia loco erat; quarta tandem oratio ad complendum habebatur. En ergo breviator noster cum Innocentio conciliatus.

Canon hujus epistolae quintus cur in duos sectus sit, cum praesertim ambo illi in unum et idem redeant, non aliam assignarem rationem, nisi breviatoris nostri oscitantiam. Reliqua plana sunt.

Postrema omnium legitur epitome litterarum Innocentii ad Aurelium (utique Carthaginiensem) episcopum. Exstat in collectione Labbeana num. 12, Innocentii ad Aurelium epistola, ad quam ea referri possunt, quae in hac synopsi habentur.

DISSERTATION On the following Epitome of the letter of Innocent.

Although I have no new letters of Innocent I to bring forth, yet I think it not beside the point that there should be communicated to my readers 625Cthe things which our breviary of the canons in the Lucca manuscript codex of nine hundred years presents. In it there are extant canons drawn from several letters of Innocent and reduced to an epitome. But in the epitome which is drawn from the letter to Exsuperius, canon 3 is lacking, concerning those who after baptism, holding magistracy, have pronounced sentence in a capital case. For this omission I can devise no other reason, except that the Pontiff judges that nothing is to be decreed in that matter. Canon 4, which follows, which is 3 in our epitome, is badly expressed; for the married are not deprived of communion if they have committed adultery, but the innocent party is separated from the adulterer. The rest agrees with the letter. The addition about the Lamentations of Jeremiah is not to be neglected, which is supplied by our manuscript alone and is not read in the printed editions.

After this epitome there follows another letter of Innocent, to the bishops of Toledo. Some things in it are plainly worthy of remark. For first, in our 625Dbreviary, just as in all the ancient codices, on the witness of Hardouin, is read Tolosanos, not Toletanos.

There follows a synopsis of the letter of Innocent to Victricius (our codex has Victorium), in which all is plain and agrees with the printed editions, if you except one, the second part of canon 3, in which the printed letter indeed rules that the cases of churchmen are to be heard each in their own provinces, without prejudice, however, to appeal to the Roman church, or unless the gravity of the case refer the judgment to the apostolic see. So the printed editions. But the manuscript codex not so, but: Let the Roman clerics not dare to plead their cases to other bishops. Assuredly these two are far apart from one another, so that they seem drawn from one codex and another. But that the things read in the printed editions are Pope Innocent I's no one will rightly doubt, since they are cited as from Innocent by Nicholas I in a twin letter in the Roman council VII, to be read in Labbe, vol. X. But 626Asince Nicholas neither writes that he took them from the letter to Victricius, nor is our abridger always so happy in rendering the sense of the canons, one or the other of two things necessarily follows: either that canon, as in the printed editions, has been badly stitched on from some other letter of Innocent to this one of his to Victricius, as it is agreed has happened to other letters of the ancient Pontiffs; or our anonymous author has by no means grasped the Pontiff's mind.

In the epitome of canon 8 it is proclaimed that monks are not to be admitted into the clergy; but this Innocent does not command, but warns that, promoted to a higher grade, they should not cast off the modesty and the other virtues which they had cultivated in the monastery. Wherefore the drowsiness of our abridger is to be rebuked.

The synopsis of the letter of Innocent to Decentius is plainly barbarous, but its first chapter expresses a certain old liturgical rite which the printed editions do not touch, although it agrees in sense with the printed editions. The Pontiff forbids in canon 1 that the peace be given before the end. But 626Bour anonymous author expressed this thus: The order of the mass: to give the peace after the fourth oration. By the name of the fourth oration I judge that here is designated that which the old Roman order in Mabillon, Museum Italicum, vol. II, calls the oration at the completion. For in the mass the first oration was that which was read before the Apostle, that is, before the epistle. The second followed, over the offerings. Then the Lord's prayer was in the third place; the fourth, finally, was held to be the oration at the completion. Behold, then, our abridger reconciled with Innocent.

Why the fifth canon of this letter has been cut into two, especially since both of them come back to one and the same thing, I would assign no other reason than the drowsiness of our abridger. The rest is plain.

Last of all is read the epitome of the letters of Innocent to Aurelius (the bishop of Carthage, certainly). There is extant in the Labbe collection, num. 12, a letter of Innocent to Aurelius, to which the things contained in this synopsis can be referred.

OBSERVATION OF PHILIPPE LABBE, S. J.

The following canons, sent long ago by some Roman synod to the bishops of Gaul, the Reverend Father Sirmond first brought to light from several manuscript codices in the appendix to volume I of the councils of Gaul; and the Royal collector threw the same, out of their place, into the end of the XXXVII volume, after the indices, with the treatise on the ancient promotions of bishops. We, however, following the not improbable conjecture of the same most illustrious editor, have placed them under the letters of Innocent the first, since neither the form of the decrees nor that of the writing is foreign to them. But in what year they were issued is plainly not established, although, as everyone 628Dsees, they bear on their face the greatest antiquity.

Cruces for this work — where the plate is defective or the reading uncertain →

Two short pieces of decretal material that Coustant did not place among Innocent I’s letters proper, both taken from manuscript epitomes. The first is an abridged, twenty-three-head version of the Nicene canons, headed From the Letter of Pope Innocent from the council of Nicaea, found in an old collection at Lucca of at least nine hundred years’ age; Coustant’s notice asks what the letter was, and from what codex it was copied, and leaves the question open. A dissertation then prints the codex’s epitomes of Innocent’s letters to Exsuperius, to the bishops at Toulouse, to Victricius, to Decentius, and others, where the codex departs from the printed letters. The last part, a run of canons sent by a Roman synod to the bishops of Gaul, is Innocent’s only on a conjecture. Philippe Labbe, printing them, says Sirmond had first published them from several manuscripts, that he places them under Innocent on Sirmond’s “not improbable conjecture”, because neither their form nor their style is foreign to him, and that “in what year they were issued is plainly not established”. This Latin has no foot notes; the plate was read for them and there are none.
