Ps.-Innocent I

Pseudo-Innocentius I

The name under which Migne prints four letters that Coustant refused to admit to his edition: sentences against Arcadius, Eudoxia, Arsacius and Theophilus, and a letter to the emperor, written as if after the death of John Chrysostom. Coustant judged them the work of one forger, drawing on Theodoret, and gave as proof that they keep Eudoxia alive after Chrysostom’s death, though she died three years before him.

Migne prints these letters as ascribed to Innocent I. Coustant, in the notice prefixed to them, argues that they are forgeries; three of the four survive in Greek (through Nicephorus Callistus and Glycas), one only in Latin.

NOTICE ON THE FOUR FOLLOWING LETTERS.

  • 627D

1.

E quatuor illis epistolis primam Nicephorus Callisti lib. XIII, cap. 34, integram, Glycas vero Annal. part. IV, nonnullis identidem resectis, mutilam Graece nobis transmiserunt; secundam autem et quartam solus Glycas ibid. pariter Graece asservavit: verum tertia tantum Latine ad nos pervenit, opera Baronii e Vaticano exemplari descripta. Idem Cardinalis 628Dad ann. 407 epistolis illis quintam adjecit Honorii imperatoris ad Arcadium, quam e veteri Guill. Sirleti Cardinalis codice eruerat.

1.

Of those four letters, Nicephorus Callisti, book XIII, chap. 34, has handed down to us the first entire in Greek, and Glycas, Annals, part IV, mutilated, with some things cut out here and there; the second and the fourth Glycas alone preserved, in the same place, likewise in Greek: but the third has come down to us only in Latin, copied by the labour of Baronius from a Vatican exemplar. The same Cardinal, 628Dat the year 407, added to those letters a fifth, of the emperor Honorius to Arcadius, which he had dug out of an old codex of Cardinal Guglielmo Sirleto.

2.

His omnibus scriptionibus ea communis est suppositionis nota, quod Joanni Chrysostomo superstitem ponant Eudoxiam, quam triennio ante illum in partu defunctam esse, Socratis, Sozomeni, Marcellini, Prosperi, Zosimi, factorumque Graecorum testimoniis constat. Sane cum his conferendus non est Georgius Alexandrinus, quo auctore Leo imperator 629Acognomento Philosophus, Cedrenus, Nicephorus aliique posteriores Graeci vitam Eudoxiae tribus annis produxerunt. Ad haec, nulla prodit historia, summam Imperatricis hujus in Arcadii animum potestatem atque gratiam ad mortem usque tantillum imminutam esse: quod tamen in secunda et quarta epistola fingitur. Neque minus ab historica fide, quam ab ecclesiastico more abhorret quod in epistola prima legitur, ubi post Chrysostomi mortem Innocentius Arcadium et Eudoxiam a sacramentorum perceptione segregare, Arsacium etiam post mortem ab episcopali gradu dejicere, eademque poena eos plectere, qui cum illo in ejus ordinatione communicarunt, ac Theophilum non excommunicatione tantum, sed etiam anathemate percellere, et ab omni Christianorum consortio removere narratur. Primo enim Romanos Pontifices cum principibus etiam haereticis longe alia ratione gerere se consuevisse testes sunt Liberii ad Constantium, Gelasii, Symmachi, Anastasii II et Hormisdae ad Anastasium 629Bimp. epistolae. Deinde nec privato cuique poenam adeo gravem imponere solent nisi monitionibus praemissis. Ubi vero illae monitiones? Si has adhibere voluisset Innocentius; Arcadii, qui vix septem menses Chrysostomo superstes fuit, ante didicisset obitum, quam extremo illo remedio uti valuisset. Praeterea ex ipsius Innocentii litteris certum est eum totius causae Chrysostomi judicium oecumenicae synodo servasse. Quocirca is papa (Innoc. epist. 5) Theophilum, communionem cum ipso non secus atque cum Joanne habere se testatus, iteratis litteris hortatur, « ut si judicio confidit, sistat se ad synodum. » Item ad Constantinopolitanos scribens, « Cogitamus, inquit (Epist. 7, n. 4), quomodo oecumenica synodus congreganda sit, ut turbulenti motus Dei nutu tandem conquiescant. » Et ipsius rogatu Honorius imperator tertio ad Arcadium scripsit (Epist. 9), nullas intentans ei minas, sed tantum obsecrans, « ut Theophilum Alexandriae episcopum, 629Cqui omnium malorum auctor esse dicitur vel nolentem se sistere jubeat » ad synodum. Istud vero dum cogitaret Innocentius, Joannis primum, ac paulo post Arcadii mors interveniens congregandae synodo moras attulit. Neque tamen idcirco primum abjecit consilium, neque etiam donec haberi posset desiderata synodus, cum adversariis Joannis omnino rupit pacem atque communionem, sed eam intermisit tantum atque suspendit. Qua de re Palladius Vitae Chrysostomi scriptor sub finem dialogi pag. 214 ita loquitur: « Scopus ecclesiae Romanae iste est, non communicare cum Orientalibus episcopis, praesertim 630Acum Theophilo, donec Dominus dederit locum universali synodo, quae putrefactis membris eorum qui haec patrarunt medeatur. » Ubi Romanae ecclesiae non judicium, sed scopus notatur seu consilium, quo sibi, donec synodus de hac re decidat, utendum censuit. Et vero Theophilus inter Alexandrinos antistites recenseri etiam a Romanis praesulibus numquam desiit. Idem dicendum de caeteris ejus sociis, si excipiatur Arsacius, quem, Theodoreto teste (lib. V, cap. 34), Occidentis episcopi « ne salutare quidem dignati sunt. » Verum cum Attico in ejus locum suffecto Innocentius ipse communionem suspensam tenere satis habuit, ad eam illi reddendam paratus, cum pactis conditionibus satisfecisset. Quocirca Maximiano episcopo eum amice roganti, ut pacificas ad Atticum litteras mitteret, rescripsit: « Communio suspensa restituitur demonstranti causas, quibus id acciderat, jam esse detersas, et profitenti conditiones pacis impletas. » Et eum quidem, postquam nomen Joannis in sacras tabulas retulisset, ab Occidentalibus 630Bsusceptum esse Theodoretus narrat.

2.

Common to all these writings is this mark of forgery, that they make Eudoxia survive John Chrysostom, though it is established by the testimonies of Socrates, Sozomen, Marcellinus, Prosper, Zosimus, and of the Greek acts, that she died in childbirth three years before him. Certainly George of Alexandria is not to be compared with these, on whose authority the emperor Leo, 629Asurnamed the Philosopher, Cedrenus, Nicephorus and other later Greeks prolonged the life of Eudoxia by three years. Besides, no history reports that the supreme power and favour of this Empress over the mind of Arcadius was diminished in the least, right up to her death: which is nevertheless feigned in the second and fourth letter. Nor is it less foreign to historical faith than to ecclesiastical custom, what is read in the first letter, where Innocent is related, after the death of Chrysostom, to separate Arcadius and Eudoxia from the receiving of the sacraments, to cast Arsacius down from the episcopal rank even after death, and to strike with the same penalty those who communicated with him in his ordination, and to smite Theophilus not only with excommunication but also with anathema, and to remove him from all fellowship of Christians. For in the first place, that the Roman Pontiffs were accustomed to conduct themselves by a far different method with princes, even heretical ones, the letters of Liberius to Constantius, and of Gelasius, Symmachus, Anastasius II and Hormisdas to the emperor Anastasius 629Bare witnesses. Next, they are not wont to impose so grave a penalty even on any private person, except after admonitions sent before. But where are those admonitions? If Innocent had wished to employ them, he would have learned of the death of Arcadius, who survived Chrysostom by scarcely seven months, before he could have used that extreme remedy. Moreover, it is certain from Innocent's own letters that he reserved the judgment of the whole cause of Chrysostom to an ecumenical synod. Wherefore this pope (Innoc. epist. 5), having testified that he held communion with Theophilus no otherwise than with John, exhorts him by repeated letters « that if he trusts in the judgment, he present himself at the synod. » Likewise, writing to the people of Constantinople, « We are considering, he says (Epist. 7, n. 4), how an ecumenical synod may be assembled, so that the turbulent commotions may at last, by the nod of God, be quieted. » And at his request the emperor Honorius wrote a third time to Arcadius (Epist. 9), threatening him with no threats, but only beseeching « that he order Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria, 629Cwho is said to be the author of all the evils, to present himself even against his will » at the synod. But while Innocent was considering this, the death first of John, and a little after of Arcadius, intervening, brought delays to the assembling of the synod. Yet he did not for that reason cast aside his first counsel, nor even, until the desired synod could be held, did he altogether break off peace and communion with the adversaries of John, but only interrupted and suspended it. Of which matter Palladius, the writer of the Life of Chrysostom, speaks thus toward the end of the dialogue, page 214: « This is the aim of the Roman church: not to communicate with the Eastern bishops, especially 630Awith Theophilus, until the Lord shall have given place to a universal synod, which may heal the putrefied members of those who perpetrated these things. » Where it is not the judgment of the Roman church that is noted, but its aim, or counsel, which it judged it should use until a synod should decide concerning this matter. And indeed Theophilus never ceased to be counted among the bishops of Alexandria even by the Roman prelates. The same is to be said of his other associates, if Arsacius be excepted, whom, as Theodoret witnesses (lib. V, cap. 34), the bishops of the West « did not deign even to greet. » But with Atticus, put into his place, Innocent himself held it enough to keep communion suspended, being ready to restore it to him when he had satisfied the agreed conditions. Wherefore, to the bishop Maximian, who amicably asked him to send letters of peace to Atticus, he wrote back: « Suspended communion is restored to one who shows that the causes by which it had happened are now wiped away, and who professes that the conditions of peace have been fulfilled. » And Theodoret relates that he was in fact, after he had put the name of John back into the sacred tablets, received by the Westerns 630B.

3.

Ex iis tamen, quae Theodoretus loco citato scribit, materiam et occasionem praebuisse videtur primae epistolae, adeoque et caeteris, quae cum prima sic congruunt, ut earum omnium unum artificem esse nihil prope dubitemus. Nec obstat quod cum Theodoretus de Arsacio, dum viveret narret, sic ab ejus communione abhorruisse episcopos Occidentis, ut eum ne salutare quidem dignarentur; prima tamen epistola eumdem post mortem, non ante, ordine suo movet. Ab impostore enim, qui veritate continere se nequit, in eis etiam quae imitatur exprimendis, summa fides nequaquam exspectanda est. Verum ex illo adjuncto, etiam post mortem, quod dedita opera insertum esse apparet, tempus expiscari licet, quo epistolae illae adornatae sunt. Certe si publicae tum fuissent, cum Justinianus imperator undique conquireret exempla, quibus Theodorum Mopsuestenum etiam post mortem excommunicari licere 630Cprobaretur, cum istud tam insigne Arsacii etiam post mortem ordine suo moti non fugisset. Illud tamen in medium protulisse nusquam legitur: quod non levi argumento est, etiam epistolas illas nusquam tum, hoc est medio VI saeculo, exstitisse. Sed et affectata illa locutio, etiam post mortem, non alia de causa videtur inserta, nisi ut iis, qui Theodorum Mopsuestenum excommunicari volebant, faveretur. Unde conjicere licet epistolas illas ipso saeculo sexto, cum praedicta controversia necdum sopita esset, fuisse confictas.

3.

Yet from those things which Theodoret writes in the place cited, he seems to have furnished the matter and occasion for the first letter, and so for the others as well, which so agree with the first that we scarcely doubt at all that there was one fabricator of them all. Nor does it stand in the way that, although Theodoret relates of Arsacius, while he was living, that the bishops of the West so shrank from communion with him that they did not deign even to greet him, the first letter nevertheless removes this same man from his rank after death, not before. For from an impostor, who cannot hold himself to the truth even in expressing the things he imitates, the highest fidelity is by no means to be expected. But from that addition, even after death, which appears to have been inserted of set purpose, one may fish out the time at which those letters were got up. Certainly, if they had been public at the time when the emperor Justinian was searching everywhere for examples by which it might be 630Cproved that Theodore of Mopsuestia could lawfully be excommunicated even after death, so notable a case of Arsacius, removed from his rank even after death, would not have escaped him. Yet it is nowhere read that he brought it forward: which is no light argument that those letters too did not yet exist anywhere at that time, that is, in the middle of the VI century. And that affected expression, even after death, seems to have been inserted for no other reason than to favour those who wished Theodore of Mopsuestia to be excommunicated. Whence one may conjecture that those letters were forged in the sixth century itself, when the aforesaid controversy was not yet laid to rest.

EPISTLE I. Sentence is pronounced against Arcadius, Eudoxia, Arsacius and Theophilus.

  • 629

OF INNOCENT, ARCHBISHOP OF ROME, Nicephorus judged this letter to be common, as it were, to Arcadius, Atticus and Theophilus. He, namely, states first that Innocent, because the legates whom he had sent to Constantinople had been ill-used and unworthily treated, was greatly indignant, and, when a little later he had learned of the death of John, gave a letter to Arcadius, Atticus and Theophilus, by which he subjected them to excommunication and deposition. Then he notes that this letter was written in words of this kind: The voice of the blood of my brother John, etc. In it, however, there is no word about Atticus. But, as if he wished to meet this observation, having copied out the whole letter he subjoins next: These things Innocent wrote, fighting for John. Nor yet did he absolve from the charge Atticus either, who after Arsacius inrasit into the see of that great man (John) (ἔξω αἰτίας ἐποίει) . But how that matter was composed, history will show in its place, namely in book XIV, chap. 25, where peace is said to have been restored, from the time Atticus ordered mention of John to be made in the prayers. Cruces for this work — where the plate is defective or the reading uncertain →

Four letters that Migne prints as Innocent I’s and that Coustant refused to admit to his edition: sentence against Arcadius, Eudoxia, Arsacius and Theophilus after the death of John Chrysostom, and letters to and about the emperor. Coustant’s notice argues that they are forgeries by a single hand. They keep Eudoxia alive after Chrysostom’s death though she died three years before him, and they were got up, he thinks, from Theodoret’s account. Three of the four survive in Greek, by way of Nicephorus Callistus and Glycas, and Migne prints the Greek and the Latin as separate blocks. The English of each block is made from its own language: the Greek from the Greek, the Latin from the Latin, and the one letter that exists only in Latin from the Latin. Where the two differ, the differences are logged on the cruces page. Coustant’s lettered notes are translated in full.
