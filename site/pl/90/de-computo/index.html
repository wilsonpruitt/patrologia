De computo

647Augustinus dixit de quatuor divisionibus scripturae: Quatuor necessaria sunt in Ecclesia Dei: Canon divinus, in quo narratur et praedicatur vita futura; Historia, in qua rerum gesta narrantur; Numerus, in quo facta futurorum et solemnitates divinae dinumerantur; Grammatica, in qua verborum scientia intelligitur. Igitur quatuor sunt partes Scripturae: Canon divinus, Historia, Numerus, Grammatica. Istae autem divisiones sunt quasi Scripturae quatuor fundamenta. Isidorus in Computi laude dicit: Ratio numerorum contemnenda non est. In multis locis sanctarum Scripturarum, quantum mysterium habet, elucet. Non enim frustra in laudibus Dei dictum est: Omnia in mensura et numero et pondere fecisti. Senarius namque numerus, qui partibus suis perfectus 648est, perfectionem mundi quadam numeri sui significatione declarat. Similiter et quadraginta dies, quibus Moses et Elias et ipse Dominus jejunaverunt, sine numerorum cognitione non intelliguntur. Sic et alii in Scripturis sacris numeri existunt, quorum figuras non nisi noti hujus artis scientiae solvere possunt. Datum etiam nobis est, ex aliqua parte sub numerorum consistere disciplina, quando mensium curricula disputamus, quando anni spatium redeuntis per numerum agnoscimus. Per numerum siquidem ne confundamur, instruimur. Tolle numerum a rebus omnibus, et omnia pereunt. Adime saeculo computum, et omnia caeca ignorantia complectitur. Nec differri possunt a caeteris animalibus, qui calculi nesciunt rationem.

649ADISCIPULUS: Haec ergo ratio numerorum unde primum processit, scire nos cupimus.

MAGISTER: A Deo scilicet, quia omnis sapientia et scientia a Domino Deo est, ex quo facta sunt omnia.

D. Dic ergo quando primum inventa est ista ratio?

M. Ex illo tempore ex quo factae sunt creaturae, hoc est ab origine saeculi. Tunc enim primum numerus initiavit; sicut in Genesi legitur: Et factum est ves ere et mane dies unus. Tunc ergo dixit de nocte et die, quando dixit vespere, id est nox; et mane, id est dies. De numero autem dixit, quando dixit, dies unus, sic secundus, sic tertius, sic quartus, sic quintus, sic sextus, sic septimus. Iterum de numero dixit Deus, quando dixit de sole et luna: Et sint in signa et tempora et dies et annos. Quis enim potest intelligere dies et tempora et annos, nisi per numerum? Inde dixit Boethius: Omnia quaecunque a prima rerum natura constructa sunt, numerorum videntur ratione formata. Hoc enim fuit principale exemplar in animo Conditoris. Hinc enim quatuor 649Belementorum multitudo mutuata est, hinc temporum vices, hinc motus astrorum coelique conversio intelligitur. Item Boethius dicit: Proprie ipsa natura numerorum, omnis astrorum cursus, omnisque astronomica ratio constituta est. Sic enim ortus occasusque colligimus: sic tarditates velocitatesque errantium siderum custodimus; sic defectus et multiplices lunae variationes agnoscimus.

D. Haec igitur ars, hoc est numerus, quod nomen generale habet?

M. Philosophia scilicet, quia omnis sapientia, philosophia nominatur.

D. Quid est philosophia? cujus linguae est?

M. Graecum nomen est, et interpretatur amor scientiae. Φίλος enim apud Graecos, Latine amor dicitur. Σοφία vero sapientia vel scientia interpretatur.

D. Philosophia quomodo diffinitur?

M. Isidorus eam sic definivit dicens: Philosophia est rerum humanarum divinarumque cognitio. In hoc 649Cexemplo ostenditur quod omnis sapientia, sive divina sive humana, philosophia nuncupatur. Item alia diffinitio philosophiae: Philosophia est divinarum humanarumque rerum, in quantum possibile est, probabilis scientia. Item alia diffinitio philosophiae: Philosophia est ars artium, et disciplina disciplinarum.

D. Quot sunt divisiones philosophiae?

M. Tres, id est, physica, ethica, logica; hoc est, naturalis, moralis, rationalis. Unde Isidorus dicit: Philosophiae species tripartita est. Una autem naturalis, quae Graece φυσικὴ, hoc est physica, in qua naturae inquisitio disseritur; altera vero moralis, quae apud Graecos ἠθικὴ dicitur, hoc est ethica, in qua de moribus agitur; et tertia rationalis, quae Graeco vocabulo appellatur λογικὴ, hoc est logica, in qua disputando quemadmodum in rerum causis vel vitae moribus veritas ipsa quaeratur. In physica igitur, causa quaerendi; in ethica, ordo inveniendi; in logica, ratio intelligendi versatur. Item, sunt ergo tres philosophiae partes, hoc est, tres divisiones sapientiae, Physica, 649DEthica, Logica. In his etiam tribus generibus philosophiae, eloquia divina consistunt. Nam aut de natura disputare solent, ut in Genesi, et in Ecclesiaste, aut in Proverbiis, et in Epistolis Pauli apostoli, aut in Cantica canticorum, et in Evangeliis, et in aliis multis libris; aut de moribus, aut de superioribus mysteriis coelestibus, hoc est de logica.

D. Quis ergo invenit istas tres partes philosophiae?

M. Physicam invenit Thales Milesius, unus ex septem sapientibus Graecorum. Ethicam invenit Socrates. Logicam invenit Plato philosophus.

D. Ad quam igitur partem de his tribus philosophiae pertinet computus?

M. Ad physicam, sine dubio.

D. Physica vero quot divisiones habet?

M. Quatuor, id est arithmeticam, geometriam, musicam, astronomiam.

D. Arithmetica quid est?

M. Numerabilis ars.

650AD. Geometrica quid est?

M. Terrae mensuratio.

D. Musica quid est?

M. Modulatio.

D. Astronomia quid est?

M. Astrorum lex.

D. Ex his itaque quatuor divisionibus physicae, id est, naturalis scientiae, quae prima discenda est?

M. Arithmetica procul dubio, quae principium matrisque quodammodo ad caeteras obtinet portionem. Arithmetica enim cunctis prior est, quia hanc ille hujus mundanae molis conditor Deus primum suae habuit ratiocinationis exemplar: ex hac cuncta constituit, hoc est, per numeros omnia elementa constituit et ordinavit.

D. Quis primus invenit numerum apud Hebraeos et Aegyptios?

M. Abraham primus invenit numerum apud Hebraeos, deinde Moses; et Abraham tradidit istam scientiam numeri ad Aegyptios, et docuit eos: deinde 650BJosephus.

D. Quis primus istam scientiam numeri habuit apud Graecos et Latinos?

M. Pythagoras apud Graecos, Apuleius et Boethius apud Latinos; unde Isidorus dicit: Numeri disciplinam apud Graecos primum Pythagoram nuncupant conscripsisse, ac deinde a Nicomacho diffusius esse dispositam; quam apud Latinos Apuleius et deinde Boetius transtulerunt.

D. Quomodo numerus nominatur apud Hebraeos, et Chaldaeos, et Syros, et Macedones?

M. Vocatur Nonnan apud Hebraeos, Chaldaeos et Syros. Apud Macedones calculus. Calculator nomen accepit a calculis, id est lapillis minutis, quos antiqui in manu tenentes componebant numerum. Apud alios Graecos cyclus, vel rhythmus; apud Aegyptios latercus, inde laterculus diminutive dicitur, et rima, unde rimarii appellantur.

D. Illud nomen quod dicitur numerus, simplexne 650Cest, an compositum?

M. Compositum, scilicet ex duobus corruptis. Nume enim ex eo nomine quod est nummus venit, rus autem ex eo nomine quod est rivus derivatur. Ex rivo enim nummorum, id est, ex multitudine census qui reddebatur regibus vel imperatoribus, numerus nomen accepit. Unde Isidorus dicit: Nummus numero nomen dedit, et a sui frequentatione vocabulum indidit.

D. Numerus, unde nomen accepit?

M. A Numeria. Hinc Augustinus ait: Numerus a Numeria quadam dea nominatur, cujus sacerdotes retro versis vultibus offerebant, et post oblata munera retro pergebant.

D. Numerus quomodo definitur?

M. Isidorus diffinivit dicens: Numerus est multitudo ex unitatibus constituta. Nam unum, semen numeri esse, non numerum, dicimus. Item alius dicit: Unus, non est numerus, sed ab eo crescunt numeri. Tamen Donatus etiam unum pro numero posuit, dicens: 650DNumerus est singularis, ut, Hic magister. Item Augustinus dicit: Numerus est singularis corporis ac vocis et significationis collectio. Item, Numerus dicitur a numerando, velut supra dictum est, a Numeria dea, quam antiqui dicebant deam esse numeri; sive numerus dicitur, quasi nummorum rivus. Antiqui enim nescientes numerare, ex lapillis sua tempora suosque dies numerabant, in prosperitate candidis, in adversitate nigris. Unde Persius ait: Hunc, Macrine, diem numera meliore lapillo.

D. Quomodo nominantur numeri ab uno usque ad mille, et myriadas?

M. Sic nominantur:--Unum, duo, tria, quatuor, quinque, sex, septem, octo, novem, decem;--undecim, duodecim, tredecim, quatuordecim, quindecim, sedecim vel sexdecim, septemdecim vel decemseptem, octodecim, novemdecim, viginti;--viginti unum, viginti duo, viginti tria, viginti quatuor, viginti 651Aquinque, viginti sex, viginti septem, viginti octo, viginti novem, triginta;--triginta unum, triginta duo, triginta tria, triginta quatuor, triginta quinque, triginta sex, triginta septem, triginta octo, triginta novem, quadraginta;--quadraginta unum, quadraginta duo, quadraginta tria, quadraginta quatuor, quadraginta quinque, quadraginta sex, quadraginta septem, quadraginta octo, quadraginta novem, quinquaginta;--quinquaginta unum, quiquaginta duo, quinquaginta tria, quinquaginta quatuor, quinquaginta quinque, quinquaginta sex, quinquaginta septem, quinquaginta octo, quinquaginta novem, sexaginta;--sexaginta unum, sexaginta duo, sexaginta tria, sexaginta quatuor, sexaginta quinque, sexaginta sex, sexaginta septem, sexaginta octo, sexaginta novem, septuaginta;--septuaginta unum, septuaginta duo, septuaginta tria, septuaginta quatuor, septuaginta quinque, septuaginta sex, septuaginta septem, septuaginta octo, septuaginta novem, octoginta;--octoginta unum, octoginta duo, octoginta tria, octoginta quatuor, 651Boctoginta quinque, octoginta sex, octoginta septem, octoginta octo, octoginta novem, nonaginta;--nonaginta unum, nonaginta duo, etc.;--centum, centum et unum, centum et duo, etc.;--ducenta, trecenta, quadringenta, quingenta, sexcenta, septingenta, octingenta, noningenta, mille;--duo millia, tria millia, quatuor millia, quinque millia, sex millia, septem millia, octo millia, novem millia, decem millia.--Sic constat quod decem decies multiplicatus, centum facit. Centum decies multiplicatus, mille facit. Mille decies multiplicatus, myriadam facit. Sic viginti, duas myriadas; et triginta, tres myriadas; et quadraginta, quatuor myriadas; et quinquaginta, quinque myriadas; et sexaginta, sex myriadas; et septuaginta, septem myriadas; et octuaginta, octo myriadas; et nonaginta, novem myriadas; et centum, decem myriadas; bis centum, viginti myriadas; ter centum, triginta myriadas; quater centum, quadraginta myriadas; quinquies 651Ccentum, quinquaginta myriadas; sexies centum, sexaginta myriadas; septies centum, septuaginta myriadas; octies centum, octuaginta myriadas; nonies centum, nonaginta myriadas; decies centena millia, centum myriadas.

D. Ista nomina numeri quae diximus, per quas notas significantur apud Latinos?

M. Per istas septem notas: hoc est I V X L D C M.

D. Quomodo istae notae significant et multiplicant numeros?

M. Aut simpliciter solae significant, aut compositae.

D. Alia cum aliis, aut multiplicatae per se?

M. Simpliciter significant, sicut est I unus. V sola quinque. X sola, decem. L per se, quinquaginta. C per se, tantum significat centum. D sola, quingentos. M sola, aut cum titulo supra, mille significat.

D. Quomodo autem multiplicatae, per se numerum generant?

M. Veluti I duplicata sic, II duos significat, triplicata, 651DIII tres, quadruplicata, IIII quatuor.

D. Quamobrem I unum significat?

M. Quid autem convenientius est quam illa littera, quae minima est litterarum in charactere, ut significaret illum numerum qui minimus est in numeris, hoc est unus. Nulla autem nota apud Latinos multiplicatur plus quam per quatuor vices. Deinde post I sequitur V, quae quinque significat?

D. Ad quid V quinque significat?

M. Quia quinta vocalis est in ordine vocalium; et qualis est in ordine vocalium, talis est in numero. Hinc dicitur de quinta littera, quinque virgulis notata; de X littera, decem. Nec V duplicatur, sed sequente V, I ponitur in numero. Si unum I, fiunt 652Asex; si duo, VII septem; si tres, VIII octo; si quatuor, VIIII novem: plus non crescit. Deinde sequitur X, quae decem significat.

D. Quis ostendit quod X decem significat?

M. Isidorus dicit: Nomina litterarum apud Graecos, et verba componunt, et numeros faciunt. Latini autem numeros ad litteras non componunt, excepta X littera, quae in figura crucem significat, et in numero decem demonstrat. Sequente X, I multiplicatur usque ad XIIII, quod quatuordecim denotat. Deinde sequitur V post X et fiunt XV. Sequente V, multiplicata I quater ita XVIIII, fiunt novemdecim.

D. Viginti quae nota significat?

M. Littera X duplicata. Bis enim decem habet, sic XX propterea dicuntur viginti, quia bis decem genita. Et sic XXX, triginta, ter decem genita.

D. Per qualem notam significantur triginta?

M. Per X ter multiplicatam. Et X non multiplicatur plus quam per tres vices.

D. Quadraginta, per qualem notam significantur?

652BM. Per X et L.

D. Quadraginta, quare dicuntur? M. Id est quater decem genita. (Omnis enim littera numeralis minus significans, si praecedit, tantum demit de sequenti, quantum in se habet; velut I, II, III, IV pro IIII, V, VI, VII, VIII, VIIII pro IX, X, XX, XXX, XL pro XXXX, ut quidam volunt, L, LX, LXX, LXXX, LXL vel XC pro LXXXX, C, CC, CCC, CD, pro CCCC, D pro CCCCC, DC, DCC, DCCC, CM pro DCCCC, M.)

D. Quae nota significat quinquaginta?

M. Littera L.

D. Quamobrem?

M. Quia numerus principalis in lege Domini est quinquagenarius. Sub illo enim numero cadebat Jubilaeus, hoc est in quinquagesimo anno. Conveniens autem erat ut ille numerus, qui principatum in lege veteri obtinet, hoc est, quinquagenarius, per illam litteram L, quae prima est in illo nomine, quod est lex, significaretur. Sexaginta vero, per L et X, septuaginta, 652Cper L et duo XX. Octoginta, per L et tria XXX.

D. Quae nota significat nonaginta?

M. Duae notae significant nonaginta, aut X inter duo L, ut LXL, aut X ante C, ut XC.

D. Quae nota significat centum?

M. Littera C.

D. Quare?

M. Quia prima est in illo nomine, quod est centum. Usus enim erat saepe apud veteres, per primam litteram nomina significare.

D. Unde dicitur centum?

M. A cantu, hoc est circulo, vel a choro cantantium.

Mos enim erat apud veteres, centum homines in choro cantantium habere. Centum ideo a cantu vel a circulo dicitur. C vero duplicata sic CC, significat ducentos; triplicata, CCC, trecentos; quadruplicata, CCCC, quadringentos.

D. Quae nota significat quingentos?

652DM. Ipsa D littera.

D. Quare?

M. Conveniens autem erat ut in numero prioris litterae, hoc est C, finito, illa littera quae sequitur in ordine litterarum, nempe D, teneret numerum sequentem, hoc est quingentos. Deinde C sequente D, sic DC, sexcentos. Duo CC, sequentes D, sic DCC, septingentos. Ter ducta C, sequente D, sic DCCC, fit octingentos. Quater C multiplicata, sequente D, sic DCCCC, nongentos significat. Mille autem signatur nota M (ut jam diximus), quoniam ipsa est prima littera in hac dictione mille. Mille vero a multitudine procedit, et usque ad centum centena millia pervenit.

On Reckoning

647Augustine said concerning the four divisions of scripture: Four things are necessary in the Church of God: the divine Canon, in which the life to come is recounted and preached; History, in which the deeds of things done are recounted; Number, by which the facts of things to come and the divine solemnities are numbered out; Grammar, in which the knowledge of words is understood. Therefore there are four parts of Scripture: the divine Canon, History, Number, Grammar. And these divisions are, as it were, the four foundations of Scripture. Isidore says in praise of Reckoning: The reckoning of numbers is not to be despised. In many places of the Holy Scriptures it shines out how great a mystery it holds. For not without cause was it said in the praises of God: Thou hast made all things in measure and number and weight. For the number six, which is perfect in its parts, 648declares the perfection of the world by a certain signification of its own number. Likewise also the forty days, in which Moses and Elijah and the Lord himself fasted, are not understood without the knowledge of numbers. So also other numbers occur in the sacred Scriptures, whose figures none but those acquainted with the knowledge of this art can unravel. It is granted to us also, in some part, to stand under the discipline of numbers, when we discuss the courses of the months, when we recognize by number the span of the returning year. Indeed, it is by number that we are instructed, lest we be confounded. Take number away from all things, and all things perish. Remove reckoning from the world, and blind ignorance embraces all things. Nor can those who do not know the reckoning of calculation be distinguished from the other animals.

649ADISCIPLE: We desire to know, then, whence this reckoning of numbers first proceeded.

MASTER: From God, clearly, since all wisdom and knowledge is from the Lord God, from whom all things were made.

D. Tell me then, when was this reckoning first discovered?

M. From that time from which the creatures were made, that is, from the origin of the world. For then number first began; as we read in Genesis: And there was ves ere and morning, one day. Then, therefore, he spoke of night and day, when he said evening, that is, night; and morning, that is, day. But of number he spoke when he said, one day, and so a second, so a third, so a fourth, so a fifth, so a sixth, so a seventh. Again God spoke of number, when he said of the sun and moon: And let them be for signs and seasons and days and years. For who can understand days and seasons and years, except through number? Whence Boethius said: All things whatsoever that have been constructed from the first nature of things appear to be formed by the reckoning of numbers. For this was the principal exemplar in the mind of the Creator. For hence the multitude of the four 649Belements was borrowed; hence the changes of the seasons; hence the motion of the stars and the revolution of the heaven are understood. Likewise Boethius says: Properly by the very nature of numbers has every course of the stars and every astronomical reckoning been established. For thus we gather risings and settings; thus we keep track of the slowness and the swiftness of the wandering stars; thus we recognize the eclipses and the manifold variations of the moon.

D. This art, then, that is, number — what general name does it have?

M. Philosophy, clearly, since all wisdom is named philosophy.

D. What is philosophy? Of what tongue is it?

M. It is a Greek name, and is interpreted as love of knowledge. For Φίλος among the Greeks is called love in Latin. And Σοφία is interpreted as wisdom or knowledge.

D. How is philosophy defined?

M. Isidore defined it thus, saying: Philosophy is the knowledge of things human and divine. In this 649Cinstance it is shown that all wisdom, whether divine or human, is termed philosophy. Likewise another definition of philosophy: Philosophy is the probable knowledge of things divine and human, in so far as is possible. Likewise another definition of philosophy: Philosophy is the art of arts, and the discipline of disciplines.

D. How many divisions of philosophy are there?

M. Three, that is, physics, ethics, logic; that is, natural, moral, rational. Whence Isidore says: The kind of philosophy is threefold. One is natural, which in Greek is φυσικὴ, that is, physics, in which the investigation of nature is discussed; another is moral, which among the Greeks is called ἠθικὴ, that is, ethics, in which there is treatment of morals; and the third is rational, which by a Greek word is called λογικὴ, that is, logic, in which, by disputing, it is sought how truth itself may be found in the causes of things or in the morals of life. In physics, therefore, is engaged the cause of seeking; in ethics, the order of finding; in logic, the reckoning of understanding. Likewise, there are then three parts of philosophy, that is, three divisions of wisdom: Physics, 649DEthics, Logic. In these three kinds of philosophy also the divine utterances consist. For they are wont to discuss either nature, as in Genesis, and in Ecclesiastes, or in Proverbs, and in the Epistles of Paul the apostle, or in the Song of Songs, and in the Gospels, and in many other books; or morals; or the higher heavenly mysteries, that is, logic.

D. Who then discovered these three parts of philosophy?

M. Thales of Miletus, one of the seven wise men of the Greeks, discovered physics. Socrates discovered ethics. Plato the philosopher discovered logic.

D. To which part, then, of these three of philosophy does reckoning belong?

M. To physics, without doubt.

D. And how many divisions has physics?

M. Four, that is, arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy.

D. What is arithmetic?

M. The art of numbering.

650AD. What is geometry?

M. The measuring of the earth.

D. What is music?

M. Modulation.

D. What is astronomy?

M. The law of the stars.

D. Of these four divisions of physics, that is, of natural knowledge, which is to be learned first?

M. Arithmetic beyond doubt, which holds, as it were, the portion of a beginning and of a mother toward the rest. For arithmetic is prior to all, since God, the founder of this worldly mass, had it first as the exemplar of his own reasoning: out of it he established all things, that is, through numbers he established and ordered all the elements.

D. Who first discovered number among the Hebrews and the Egyptians?

M. Abraham first discovered number among the Hebrews, then Moses; and Abraham handed on this knowledge of number to the Egyptians, and taught them: then 650BJosephus.

D. Who first had this knowledge of number among the Greeks and the Latins?

M. Pythagoras among the Greeks, Apuleius and Boethius among the Latins; whence Isidore says: They say that among the Greeks Pythagoras first wrote down the discipline of number, and that thereafter it was more fully set in order by Nicomachus; which among the Latins Apuleius and then Boethius translated.

D. How is number named among the Hebrews, and the Chaldeans, and the Syrians, and the Macedonians?

M. It is called Nonnan among the Hebrews, the Chaldeans, and the Syrians. Among the Macedonians, calculus. The calculator took its name from calculi, that is, small pebbles, which the ancients, holding in their hand, composed number with. Among other Greeks, cyclus, or rhythmus; among the Egyptians, latercus, whence laterculus is said as a diminutive, and rima, whence they are called rimarii.

D. That name which is called number — is it simple 650Cor compound?

M. Compound, namely from two corrupted words. For Nume comes from that noun which is nummus (coin), while rus is derived from that noun which is rivus (stream). For from the stream of coins, that is, from the multitude of the revenue which was paid to kings or emperors, number took its name. Whence Isidore says: Nummus gave its name to numerus, and from its frequent use bestowed the word.

D. Number — whence did it take its name?

M. From Numeria. Hence Augustine says: Number is named from a certain goddess Numeria, whose priests used to make offering with their faces turned backwards, and after the gifts had been offered went away backwards.

D. How is number defined?

M. Isidore defined it, saying: Number is a multitude constituted out of units. For we say that one is the seed of number, not a number. Likewise another says: One is not a number, but from it numbers grow. Yet Donatus also set down one as a number, saying: 650DThe number is singular, as, This master. Likewise Augustine says: Number is the gathering together of a single body and voice and signification. Likewise, number (numerus) is so called from numbering, as was said above, from the goddess Numeria, whom the ancients used to say was the goddess of number; or number is so called as though it were the stream of coins. For the ancients, not knowing how to count, used to number their times and their days by pebbles — white ones in prosperity, black ones in adversity. Whence Persius says: Number this day, Macrinus, with a better pebble.

D. How are the numbers named from one up to a thousand, and myriads?

M. They are named thus:--One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten;--eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen or six-and-ten, seventeen or ten-and-seven, eighteen, nineteen, twenty;--twenty-one, twenty-two, twenty-three, twenty-four, twenty-651Afive, twenty-six, twenty-seven, twenty-eight, twenty-nine, thirty;--thirty-one, thirty-two, thirty-three, thirty-four, thirty-five, thirty-six, thirty-seven, thirty-eight, thirty-nine, forty;--forty-one, forty-two, forty-three, forty-four, forty-five, forty-six, forty-seven, forty-eight, forty-nine, fifty;--fifty-one, quiquaginta two, fifty-three, fifty-four, fifty-five, fifty-six, fifty-seven, fifty-eight, fifty-nine, sixty;--sixty-one, sixty-two, sixty-three, sixty-four, sixty-five, sixty-six, sixty-seven, sixty-eight, sixty-nine, seventy;--seventy-one, seventy-two, seventy-three, seventy-four, seventy-five, seventy-six, seventy-seven, seventy-eight, seventy-nine, eighty;--eighty-one, eighty-two, eighty-three, eighty-four, 651Beighty-five, eighty-six, eighty-seven, eighty-eight, eighty-nine, ninety;--ninety-one, ninety-two, etc.;--a hundred, a hundred and one, a hundred and two, etc.;--two hundred, three hundred, four hundred, five hundred, six hundred, seven hundred, eight hundred, nine hundred, a thousand;--two thousand, three thousand, four thousand, five thousand, six thousand, seven thousand, eight thousand, nine thousand, ten thousand.--Thus it is established that ten multiplied ten times makes a hundred. A hundred multiplied ten times makes a thousand. A thousand multiplied ten times makes a myriad. So twenty makes two myriads; and thirty, three myriads; and forty, four myriads; and fifty, five myriads; and sixty, six myriads; and seventy, seven myriads; and eighty, eight myriads; and ninety, nine myriads; and a hundred, ten myriads; twice a hundred, twenty myriads; three times a hundred, thirty myriads; four times a hundred, forty myriads; five times 651Ca hundred, fifty myriads; six times a hundred, sixty myriads; seven times a hundred, seventy myriads; eight times a hundred, eighty myriads; nine times a hundred, ninety myriads; ten times a hundred thousands, a hundred myriads.

D. These names of number which we have spoken — by what marks are they signified among the Latins?

M. By these seven marks: that is, I V X L D C M.

D. How do these marks signify and multiply numbers?

M. Either they signify simply, alone, or in composition.

D. Some with others, or multiplied of themselves?

M. They signify simply, as I is one. V alone, five. X alone, ten. L by itself, fifty. C by itself, signifies only a hundred. D alone, five hundred. M alone, or with a stroke above, signifies a thousand.

D. But how, when multiplied, do they generate number of themselves?

M. As I doubled thus, II, signifies two; tripled, 651DIII, three; quadrupled, IIII, four.

D. Why does I signify one?

M. What is more fitting than that that letter, which is the smallest of the letters in its character, should signify that number which is the smallest among numbers, that is, one? And no mark among the Latins is multiplied more than four times over. Then after I follows V, which signifies five?

D. Why does V signify five?

M. Because it is the fifth vowel in the order of the vowels; and as it is in the order of the vowels, so it is in number. Hence it is said of the fifth letter, marked with five strokes; of the letter X, ten. Nor is V doubled, but with a following V, an I is placed in the number. If one I, there become 652Asix; if two, VII, seven; if three, VIII, eight; if four, VIIII, nine: it grows no more. Then follows X, which signifies ten.

D. Who shows that X signifies ten?

M. Isidore says: The names of the letters among the Greeks both compose words and make numbers. But the Latins do not compose numbers to letters, except the letter X, which in its figure signifies a cross, and in number denotes ten. With X following, I is multiplied up to XIIII, which denotes fourteen. Then follows V after X and there becomes XV. With V following, an I multiplied four times, thus XVIIII, there becomes nineteen.

D. What mark signifies twenty?

M. The letter X doubled. For it has twice ten, thus XX; they are therefore called twenty, because begotten twice ten. And thus XXX, thirty, begotten thrice ten.

D. By what kind of mark is thirty signified?

M. By X multiplied thrice. And X is not multiplied more than three times over.

D. Forty — by what kind of mark is it signified?

652BM. By X and L.

D. Forty — why is it so called? M. That is, begotten four times ten. (For every numeral letter signifying less, if it precedes, takes away from the one following only so much as it has in itself; as I, II, III, IV for IIII, V, VI, VII, VIII, VIIII for IX, X, XX, XXX, XL for XXXX, as some will have it, L, LX, LXX, LXXX, LXL or XC for LXXXX, C, CC, CCC, CD, for CCCC, D for CCCCC, DC, DCC, DCCC, CM for DCCCC, M.)

D. What mark signifies fifty?

M. The letter L.

D. Why?

M. Because the chief number in the law of the Lord is the fiftieth. For under that number fell the Jubilee, that is, in the fiftieth year. And it was fitting that that number which holds the primacy in the old law, that is, the fiftieth, should be signified by that letter L, which is the first in that name which is lex (law). But sixty by L and X; seventy, 652Cby L and two X's; eighty, by L and three X's.

D. What mark signifies ninety?

M. Two marks signify ninety, either an X between two L's, as LXL, or an X before C, as XC.

D. What mark signifies a hundred?

M. The letter C.

D. Why?

M. Because it is the first in that name which is centum (a hundred). For it was often the practice among the ancients to signify names by the first letter.

D. Whence is it called centum?

M. From cantus (song), that is, a circle, or from a choir of singers.

For it was the custom among the ancients to have a hundred men in a choir of singers. A hundred is therefore called from cantus (song) or from a circle. And C doubled thus, CC, signifies two hundred; tripled, CCC, three hundred; quadrupled, CCCC, four hundred.

D. What mark signifies five hundred?

652DM. The letter D itself.

D. Why?

M. It was fitting that, the number of the prior letter, that is, C, being finished, that letter which follows in the order of the letters, namely D, should hold the following number, that is, five hundred. Then C following D, thus DC, six hundred. Two C's following D, thus DCC, seven hundred. C drawn thrice, D following, thus DCCC, becomes eight hundred. C multiplied four times, D following, thus DCCCC, signifies nine hundred. But a thousand is marked by the mark M (as we have already said), since it is the first letter in this word mille (a thousand). And a thousand proceeds from a multitude, and reaches up to a hundred hundred-thousands.
