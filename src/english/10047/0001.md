## CHAPTER II. *The virgins' deliverance from captivity, their return to Rome, and their martyrdom together with the man who carried them off.*

[1028C]While the holy virgins, then, were living religiously and holily in the African region under king Eugegius, and the king was pursuing them with unbroken services and kindnesses, the twentieth year was already passing since they had been taken captive from their own city. After this space of time, the Lord Jesus, willing to draw his most faithful brides *504* out of the toilsome contest of the present world, and to lead them into the heavenly bridal chamber itself, and knowing them to be most ready to undergo martyrdom, did not will that they should be adorned with domestic triumphs only, but that they should go forth into the field, and, the contest being increased, end a glorious life by the triumph of a glorious martyrdom. For not in vain does our king gird his soldiers with the strongest arms — which he would surely then be doing, if he strengthened any for martyrdom and did not set the contest [1028D] before them, and allowed them to yield to nature in peace.

Their bridegroom therefore appears to them in a vision under the silence of the dead of night, and addresses them with these words: Beloved brides, make no further delay in these regions, but arise and return to your own country, where you must be consummated through martyrdom. Hearing this, the virgins, rejoicing with great joy for so great a message and benefit, gave the highest thanks [1029A] to Christ their bridegroom, and in the morning told their prince of it, asking that it might be permitted them to return to their country, according to the oracle made to them, to obtain the palm of martyrdom. Then indeed the king, hearing this and weighing that it was at once impious and impossible to resist the divine commands, and considering with deep reflection that up to then he had by the presence of the virgins been not merely preserved in matters of war but increased, and through them made glorious in all things, fearing destruction for himself and for his life through their absence, determined to give himself as a companion for their journey, and said to them: Since you must depart, and it is not whole and sound for me to linger in these regions after your going, if you permit it, I too will come with you. And when they assented and desired that this be done, [1029B] and asserted besides that he himself would most surely be joined to the Christian flock, then, as though moved by a divine answer, having left kingdom, sons, and wife, he is made the companion of the handmaids of Christ; and he who had once carried the virgins off from their own regions in barbarian fury, was leading them back like a most loving father. If you compare the one with the other, you behold the changing of the divine right hand. For there you find Eugegius a most ferocious wolf, here a most gentle lamb. Then he sought to seize what was another's, to corrupt virginity, to kill the innocent; but now he counts his own goods as nothing, defends chastity, desires to die for Christ, and of his own accord hastens from far-off regions to lay down his life for Christ. Let the gentiles and unbelievers marvel at these things; let those marvel who do not know that God is wonderful [1029C] in his saints [n: (Psal. LXVII)], as we said before. For we ought not to marvel at such things, but to love them, knowing that all things are possible to those who believe.

While M. Antonius Verus and Commodus Aurelius, therefore, were ruling at Rome — namely in the year of the Lord one hundred and eightieth or thereabouts — the holy virgins Flora and Lucilla, sisters, entering the city of Rome with king Eugegius to undertake martyrdom for Christ, find, *505* by the doing of the prefect of the city, Helius by name, the fury of the gentiles raging against the worshippers of Christ. Fearing him not at all, they showed themselves openly, and being seized by his officers, are presented to Helius the prefect of the city. Questioned by him about the Christian worship, they declare themselves Christians without hesitation, adding that after a captivity of twenty years they had returned to Rome [1029D] by Christ's command for martyrdom. To whom Helius asserts and presses that the emperors' decree is this: that Christians sacrifice to the gods, or be put to death by grievous punishments. To this the virgins answered that they were ready to lay down their own life for the living and true God, for which they had come by divine revelation from far-off regions of the world. Then the impious Helius, seeing that they could not be moved from their holy purpose, ordered them to be condemned to a happy shedding of blood. And in accordance with his command, with Eugegius standing by and [1030A] marveling at their steadfastness, they were struck in the head by the lictors and obtained the crown of martyrdom.

After their departure, king Eugegius, seeing that Flora and Lucilla, his mothers and mistresses, had been crowned through so great a steadfastness and had left him surviving them, burning with love of the martyrdom whose examples had already gone before in the female sex, offered himself unbidden to the prefect, saying: Put me away along with them, since both are my mistresses, and after their departure I refuse to live. And being asked who he was, he declares his name and his country, and makes known the whole history of the virgins and his own, adding that he sought death for the name of Jesus Christ. Helius drew back and called into doubt that this man, who was not a Christian, should wish to die for Christ. To whom king Eugegius said: [1030B] The pouring out of my blood will make me a worshipper of Christ, who will not hold me a stranger to his grace. After which confession of faith the venerable man is beheaded at the prefect's command. Thus the glorious virgins Flora and Lucilla, with king Eugegius, by the severing of the head and by death won immortality on the fourth day before the Kalends of August; and angelic spirits led them to the bridal chamber of the highest Bridegroom, whom while they lived, and in death, they followed with the whole affection and love of their mind, for whom they most gladly despised riches, pleasures, honors, and life itself, than which nothing is dearer to a man. Resting in him and blessed, in exchange for voluntary poverty they won inestimable and true riches; for temporal fastings, eternal feasts; for the contempt of a most foul itch, honor and perpetual [1030C] delights; for the ridiculous and childish things of this life, true, solid, and everlasting honors; and for a momentary and fragile life, they earned a perpetual one.
