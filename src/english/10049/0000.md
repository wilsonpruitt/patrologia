## PROLOGUE.

[0945A]

Desiring to write something to the glory of our God concerning blessed Maurus the bishop, we desire so to satisfy the longing of our hearers that, according to the measure of an ordered account, we may in all things take thought for the truth. For that cupbearer is to be despised who, while he boasts of an abundance of wine, serves up the dregs as well with a rashness deserving punishment. He is a worthless caterer who prepares a banquet for others with such ambition that he allows the burning of raging hunger to rage against himself. No less rash is that narrator who, while he strives to exalt others with the proclamations of praise, does not himself fear to be plunged into the pit of falsehood. For some think they render service to God if, to render him the marks of his praise, they put together arguments of falsehood; who assuredly, if [0945B] they diligently attended to the words of the excellent preacher, would not think such things. For he says to the Corinthians: *And if Christ has not risen, then our preaching is empty, your faith is vain; and we are found also false witnesses of God, because we have given testimony against God, that he raised up Christ, whom* *419* *he did not raise up [n: (I Cor. III)]*. According to these apostolic words, therefore, he is deservedly called a false witness of God who, desiring to praise him without discernment, lies; and whoever contrives a fabrication by perverse art in God's praise beyond doubt bears testimony against God. The Prophet too thunders terribly, saying: *Thou hast hated all who work iniquity, thou wilt destroy those who speak a lie [n: (Psal. V)]*. Plainly declaring, assuredly, that as it is a lighter thing to hate than to destroy, so also it is [0945C] more tolerable to carry out iniquity out of weakness than to fabricate lies out of zeal.

Hence it is that through Jeremiah it is said of liars: *They have taught their tongues to speak a lie; they have labored to act wickedly [n: (Jer. IX)]*. For they who could easily have brought forth the simple truth offered them unbidden, take pains to contrive lies with labor. Here, if anyone should perhaps reproach us for a long preface, and if a severe critic should set against us what is written in the book of Maccabees: *It is foolish,* he says, *to run on before the history, but in the history itself to be brief [n: (II Machab. II)]* — we render this account: that where [0946A] an abundance of matter is at hand, the very length of the writing forbids us to linger over such things. But here, where few things concerning the blessed man's virtues are at hand that ought to be written, we judge it more useful for the building up of the hearers — to which all ecclesiastical Scripture is directed — to write in the preface against lying, than, for want of narrative, to draw out the history itself to the borders of lies.

Therefore, what could come to our knowledge concerning the life or the miracles of blessed Maurus, according to what we have been able to gather from the account of certain monks not altogether to be despised, we faithfully make known to the faithful of Christ.

## CHAPTER ONE. *The episcopate of St. Maurus. His deeds in it.*

[0946B]At that time, then, when the Roman Church still extended its rights of possession much more widely than now, and among the rest possessed also the town of Cesena, it happened that this same Cesena was deprived of its bishop. And when the Roman pontiff saw his own nephew — namely blessed Maurus — climbing to the heights by the steps of the virtues, and keeping watch not sluggishly in the pursuit of a holy manner of life, he judged it fitting to promote such a man to the summit of the priestly office, so that the lamp of the Holy Spirit should not lie hidden under a bushel, but, set upon a lampstand, should illumine with the rays of holy teaching all who are in the house of God. What more? At length blessed Maurus received the chair of the Church of Cesena, divine providence so disposing. But we have passed over setting down here the names either of [0946C] the king who then was ruling, or of the apostolic *420* prelate himself, because along with those very men who dictate the rest to us, we are equally ignorant of these. For they recall that they saw those names in ancient volumes, but since the city had before this been burned up by flames, they report that the records too were consumed along with it.

When therefore the man of the Lord, Maurus, saw the burden of so arduous a care laid upon him, as is read of blessed Paul [n: (Gal. I)], he in no way yielded to flesh and blood, but began to render himself the more watchful in the divine services, the more he knew that the guardianship also of so many [0947A] subjects pressed upon him. For weighing that the governance of souls is the art of arts, he judged it fitting neither to lessen the care of inward things by occupation with outward ones, nor again wholly to turn aside from the provision of outward things by solicitude for the inward: lest, namely, either given over to outward things he should fall away entirely from the innermost, or, occupied with the inward alone, he should render more negligently to his neighbors what he owed them outwardly.

For often some, as though forgetful that they have been set over their brethren for the sake of souls, serve worldly cares with such straining of heart that they exult to be doing them when these are at hand; and to these also, when they are absent, they pant with the turbulent surgings of their thoughts. And when they are at rest from these, the opportunity perhaps having ceased, they are wearied worse by that very rest of theirs. For they judge it a pleasure [0947B] if they be weighed down with business, and think it a labor if they do not labor in earthly affairs. But on the contrary, some indeed undertake the care of the flock, but so desire to be free for spiritual things for themselves that they are in no way occupied with outward matters. And these, when they wholly neglect to care for bodily things, do not at all meet the needs of their subjects; and their preaching for the most part is despised, because, while they rebuke the deeds of offenders and yet do not grant them the necessities of the present life, they are by no means gladly heard. For the word of teaching does not penetrate the mind of a needy man, if the hand of mercy does not commend it to his heart. But then the seed of the word easily sprouts, when the compassion of the preacher waters it in the breast of the hearer. And we speak these things about the various [0947C] characters of shepherds for this reason: to show how prudent, how measured was the discretion in blessed Maurus. For he assuredly, since neither by pursuing the leisure of the contemplative life did he wholly desert the active, nor by running about freely through the weariness of the active did he withdraw from contemplation, made his way, so to speak, by a golden road into the height of the heavenly kingdom.

At length, while he was living in his bishopric and vigorously governing the Church committed to him, he began to search out with careful inquiry of mind such a place where he might for a time withdraw from the crowd of worldly men and cleave more particularly to God alone: being, namely, no sluggish imitator of his Lord, *421* who by day preached in the cities, but by night [0947D] pressed on in prayers upon the mountain [n: (Luc. XXI)]. And while the care of this anxiety grew more and more from day to day, at length he cast his eye upon a certain small promontory, set apart nearly four stadia from the little city. It was called the woodland of Spatianus: a place neither stretched up more loftily to the heights by sheer peaks, nor altogether sunk into the depths, seeming levelled to the flatness of a field-bottom marsh; [0948A] but it was so set in a kind of middle ground by a fitting measure that it both granted to those placed upon it free power of viewing almost the whole diocese, and yet, not being too high, did not weary those ascending it with steep stages. For that mountain was clothed with a wooded thickness of various trees. This mountain, then, so pleased the eyes of the blessed man, as if he beheld the Mount of Olives lying next to the city of Jerusalem; and he could not without reason have sung this psalm along with the Prophet: *I will be mindful,* he says, *of thee, O Lord, from the land of Jordan and Hermoniim, from the little mountain [n: (Psal. XLI, 7)]*. And this mountain he first asked for from the Roman pontiff, under whose right it was held; then he built himself a little cell there with a small church, where, persevering in the season of Lent, he gave himself to fastings and [0948B] prayers; and if ever he could steal time from ecclesiastical affairs, he ran back to that same cell as to a banquet of sweet delicacies. There at length he drank in the stream of divine contemplation with the eager throat of his mind, whence afterward he refreshed the parched breasts of his neighbors with the cup of holy preaching. That place he plainly used as a bath. For there he took care to wash away whatever stain of word or of thought he had contracted from conversation with worldly men; and the dust of worldly living, without which this road of human mortality is not travelled, he there washed clean with frequent springs of tears. That place, therefore, which was so beloved and familiar to the holy man in life, deserved after his death also to be marked out as his burial place. For [0948C] his body, laid away in a stone chest, was placed beside the little basilica which he himself had built.
