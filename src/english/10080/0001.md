## CHAPTER IV. *That the devil is detected by the sign of the cross.*

But on a certain night, when she had risen rather early before the [articulum, *vel* horam *vel aliquid simile* ] of the night office, and had called out to her companion repeatedly in the accustomed manner, and the other, weighed down by a heavy slumber, made no answer at all, at length, moved to anger, she did not heed what she was saying: Devil, she said, get up. *745* [0714A] At which word the devil was at hand and drew near, presenting the likeness of her who was sleeping; and, rendering the verses in turn, he began to chant the psalms together with her. And when in unbroken chanting they had come to that place of the psalter where it is said: « Let God arise, and let his enemies be scattered, and let them that hate him flee from before his face: as smoke faileth, let them fail [n: (Psal. LXVII)], » and the rest — here the malignant spirit at once stopped short, and did not presume to utter these verses. When the holy woman wondered at this within herself, she took fright; and, suspecting not idly what the case was, she set upon herself the sign of the holy cross, and immediately the malignant enemy vanished; and by fraudulently hiding himself he showed who it was that had stood as the holy woman's companion in the psalmody. To this venerable matron also the wicked [0714B] spirit brought, in the night hours, the corpse of a certain man who had been hanged for his crimes; and, that he might strike terror into her, he procured for her, so to speak, a guest-gift of this kind. She presently made known to the abbot of the place and to the convent of brethren what the most wicked enemy had done to her; and with submissive [ *f.* obnixis] prayers she obtained that all the brethren should pour out prayers in common for that wretched man. But afterward it was divinely revealed to a certain person that through the prayers of the brethren that man had obtained mercy. And so the ancient enemy was outwitted, who, while he contrives to mock the living, is compelled to suffer loss over the dead.

## CHAPTER V. *An example of Christian charity.*

To this end, therefore, are examples of diabolical temptation [0714C] of this kind set before you: that you yourself may keep skillful watch against the snares of the crafty and deceiving enemy; and that, while it delights you to hear of the victories of others wrestling with the enemy, you may not yourself grow slack through the sloth of a languid body. But let these things now suffice. Commend me to the brethren; humbly suggest to the lord prior on our behalf that he grow not lukewarm from his accustomed fervor of devotion and mercy, but that he fail not to persist in the receiving of guests and the relief of the needy, so far as the household's means allow. And for him too I bring forward a fitting example, which I am confident will profit not a little toward this matter which I am eager to urge upon him. For the same queen Agnes mentioned above said also that a certain man in the parts of Germany used to range, in his zeal for hunting, the trackless glades, the steeps of the mountains, the shadowy depths of the woods. [0714D] And so, while he ran anxiously this way and that, and with careful searching of his curiosity sought out the lairs of wild beasts, he found two women — a mother, namely, and a daughter — wandering miserably through those heaps of winter frost and the deep wastes of snow. He, straightway led by mercy, said to his companion, who alone was following him: Let us lift these women onto our horses, each of us one, and carry them down to the dwellings of men; for either they will perhaps be exposed to the bites of wandering wolves, or beyond doubt they will die by the force of excessive cold. This his companion utterly refuses, and testily protests that he cannot bear such an indignity. To whom his lord said: If you disdain it, I alone will lift them both, and will cheerfully bear this indignity, as you call it. [0715A] And when he had set one *746* behind him and, sitting in the middle, the other before him, at length his comrade, pricked in mind and put to shame, yielded one to his lord, and himself caught up the other to carry her. And so they bore them through most difficult places and long windings, not without grave peril, until they found a mill that met them by chance, and there set them down. The lord further directed that he should hand over a garment to one of them, whereby she might somewhat temper the force of the cold. Some while afterward this man became a monk, and thereafter, as his sickness grew heavy, he was brought to his last hour. Then he began to cry out with great alarm to the brethren who stood by, saying: Do you see the throng of innumerable demons which hems me in on every side, and does not cease to gaze upon me with terrible eyes [0715B] and to assail me? And when the brethren persisted in unbroken prayers and psalmody, while he nonetheless, trembling in his inmost parts, was still afraid, at length that man who, as has been said, had been his companion in the hunt, standing closer by, cried out: Do you not see that the woman whom together with you I carried to the watermill, holding in her hands that garment which I then gave her, is fanning it, and violently driving out all the demons in this house? A little after — behold, thanks be to God — they have been cast out, and at this woman's driving they have utterly vanished from my sight. And so that man died in the security of a good hope, and found offered to him, as he lay dying, the fruits of mercy which he had shown while alive. For it often happens that amid the many shadows of carnal deeds [0715C] one light of a good work breaks forth, which leads a man through to a good end and haven.

## CHAPTER VI. *Through one good work God's mercy saves sinners.*

Wherefore I judge it not idle if I weave in also that which Stephen, chaplain of the same queen, a man indeed honorable and prudent, related. For he says that Stedelandus, ruler of Galicia, reported what then came to the knowledge of one man, another afterward relating it: There were, he said, three harlots, given over to the obscene foulness of the brothels, and, so to speak, prostituted by base pandering to [0716A] every passer-by; but since that region was mingled by the common habitation of Saracens and Christians, they indeed exposed themselves shamefully to Christians, but utterly renounced the company of Saracens. And when those men grieved that they were scorned in this foul traffic, and complained that an injury was being inflicted upon their people, at length they dragged the women to the tribunal of the governor, and vehemently insisted that they should consent to their own people also, as to the Christians, in the commerce of lust. But when, plying them now with terrors, now with blandishments, they could in no degree obtain this crime from them, the governor's sentence was pronounced, and they were condemned to death. What more? Straightway the executioners approach, and drive their brandished daggers against the bared necks; but their strokes are made void, and they cannot cut [0716B] even the topmost skin. Meanwhile, while these women eagerly *747* wish to die, and those men are unable to kill, they are consigned to the custody of the prison. But in the night the Savior appeared to one of them, who was as it were the chief of the rest, and said to her: Fear not; today I will set an end to your contest, and will receive you, with the crown of martyrdom, into the delightfulness of my glory. But on the following day they are led to the tribunals of the judge; the inquiry being made, the same sentence nonetheless stands; their mind is the same as it had been; and, as before, they detest in all things the embraces of the Hagarenes. Straightway therefore the executioners, since they had proved that they could do nothing upon their necks, sever their throats with the sword [desecant], *748* and so make martyrs out of harlots. But since this pen of mine is now carried further than it had determined at the outset, [0716C] the reed must be checked, and a limit set to the compass of a letter. Finally I implore the clemency of the divine mercy, that among the other marks of the virtues and of all holy religion it may pour into you the inviolable strength of a modesty to be kept, and may guard the little vessel of your body in sanctification and honor; so that he who says in the Canticles: « I am the flower of the field, I am the lily of the valleys [n: (Cant. II)]; » even as he deigned for your sake to become the Son of the Virgin, so may also make you blossom with the flowers of chastity.

*Blessed be the name of the Lord.*
