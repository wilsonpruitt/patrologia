## Expositions on the Song of Songs

[1187A]At the beginnings of books the reasons must be stated, how and why the book itself was composed — reasons which Jerome calls the prooemium. The author, then, of this work of which we intend to treat had three names: he who, to the number of his names, composed three books, and distinguished their purpose by the interpretation of the names themselves. In the first book, then, tenderly and sweetly, after the manner of a father lovingly admonishing his son and, as it were, offering him milk, he instructs each of the faithful who is as yet less strong and not yet willing to renounce earthly things altogether. In which book, on account of this great sweetness, he is named Idida, which means *beloved*. For in it he teaches that the harlot must be fled, and the fellowship of the wicked, and the other things worthy to be done with honor and uprightness — one, however, as we have said, still [1187B] dwelling in the world. His second name is Coeleth, which in Greek is called *ecclesiastes*, in Latin *concionator*. Now we call that man a preacher who in the assembly of men, that is in the council, is a public orator, fearing no one in the utterance of truth, flattering no one: which this man does in his second book. For, as if by certain steps of ascent — in order to bring to perfection the one whom he would draw by his teaching, to whom first, mercifully yet lawfully, he had granted the use of these earthly things together with works of mercy (since he believed that a carnal and still weak mind could not hold heavenly and more perfect things) — now, as though [he were] stronger and able to take up higher things, he invites him in this second book to the contempt of all earthly things; and he shows how fleeting and how transitory [1187C] are all temporal things, saying thus: *Vanity of vanities, and all is vanity [n: (Eccle. I, 2)]*. This indeed pertains to the contemplative [life] — namely, to despise all worldly things and to gape after heavenly ones; but that which had been granted in the first book, to the active life. Moreover, for the sake of what thing he would urge [one] to undertake so great a labor, he shows in this his third book, to those who attend spiritually, by certain earthly similitudes — to this end indeed, that through the contempt of earthly things we might be able to attain to the glory [1188A] of eternal things. In which work he is called Solomon, that is, *the peaceable one*.

This book is called the Song of Songs, and that too in the plural, on account of the great excellence of its dignity. For Solomon first made parables, namely the book of Proverbs, in which he plainly shows how we should rightly use these worldly things; which pertains to the active life, and can be called simply a Song. Now Proverbs are called precepts concerning human life [given] through certain similitudes. Then he made Ecclesiastes, in which he announced that all worldly things are to be despised, as when he says: *All* things are *vanity*; and it is a certain step from the active to the contemplative, and can be called simply a song. After these he made this book, in which he teaches concerning the love of God, as though [1188B] the world had already been despised: which pertains to the contemplative life; hence it is called the Song of Songs, namely a song more excellent than the two aforesaid, and than the other like ones. The name of this book in Hebrew is *Sir hasirim*, which among us amounts to *the Song of Songs*. Solomon is said, according to these three books which he made, to have had thirty names: according to the parables he is called Idida, that is, *beloved*: for there, instructing the untaught, he began to be loved by them: by which name Christ is signified, who by excellence is called beloved: of whom God [says]: *This is my beloved Son [n: (Matth. XVII)]*. Ecclesiastes is the name both of the book and of the author, and is interpreted *preacher*, namely one making diverse people consent to the same thing. For diverse people are called together in that book that they may consent among themselves in the contempt of the world: which [1188C] is drawn from the similitude of a singer, who governs the voices of diverse people into one harmony. Likewise by this name Christ is signified, who is the true preacher, teaching that earthly, transitory things are to be spurned, and heavenly ones to be loved. He is also called Ecclesiastes, because he excellently governs the Church and calls it together to one faith. According to this book he is called Solomon, that is, *the peaceable one*, by which likewise he signifies Christ, who is the true peaceable one and calls all together to the love of himself. [1189A] The author is also called, in Proverbs, an ethicist, that is, one treating of morals. The same [is called], in Ecclesiastes, a physicist, that is, one treating of the nature of things. In the Song, a theologian, discoursing of divine things. In this book the subject-matter is the bridegroom and the bride, the friends of the bridegroom (who are also called paranymphs), and the friends of the bride. Now the bridegroom is God, since he pledges himself to the Church; namely, that if she keep faith with him, not committing fornication with the demon, he will at last join her to himself in marriage. The bride is said to be the Church, namely those firm in the faith, since, perfectly receiving the faith, she pledges herself to God and renounces the pomps of Satan. The friends of the bridegroom are said to be, as it were, the holy angels and the more perfect, such as the apostles, who help the bridegroom to retain his beloved, admonishing and helping the Church, that she may not commit fornication with a stranger, but cleave to God alone [1189B]. The friends of the bride are said to be those still tender in the faith, who do not fully know how to love the Bridegroom, but already show themselves apt to imitate the bride, so that, being thus well instructed by the bride, they too may become the bride. Now he treats of this subject-matter in this manner: showing how in this life the bridegroom addresses his beloved, both by himself and through his friends, and with how great affection she receives the words of the bridegroom, and how the friends of the bride show themselves ready to imitate the bride, and how they speak to the bride, and sometimes to the Bridegroom himself. For Solomon, still holding the gift of wisdom, illumined by the Holy Spirit, prophesies how the Church, before the coming of Christ, and thereafter according to her several states down to the end of the world, will conduct herself toward God. The end and purpose too is that he may impel his hearers to the love [1189C] of God. Now this author writes like a comic poet, namely saying nothing in his own person, but bringing in the very characters speaking: and because in no [other] love are words of love found so tender as between bridegroom and bride, he makes them speak, as it were, in the words of carnal love. And these are the first words of the Church now close to the coming of Christ, and now, with exceeding love, desiring his presence; like those who said: Do you think I shall see [it]? Do you think I shall last? Do you think that Nativity will find me? And as a carnal bride, to whom the bridegroom had already pleased exceedingly through messengers, now disdaining the envoys, longing for his presence, would say: Let him come himself, that being present he may speak with me and kiss me — so here those faithful just before Christ say:

[1189D] « Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth, for thy breasts are better than wine, fragrant with the best ointments; thy name is oil poured out, therefore the maidens have loved thee. »

God himself, as he was foretold through the patriarchs and through the prophets, this same one now — *let him kiss me*, that is, let him now delight me *with the kiss*, that is, with the presence, *of his mouth*, that is, of his Son, who is called the mouth of the Father just as also the Word, because through him the Father is made manifest to the world. *For thy breasts are better.* Here the Church now present speaks with Christ. As though she should say thus, turning herself to him out of exceeding affection: For *thy breasts*, the evangelical teaching, *are better than wine*; which teaching is a sweet refreshment and pleasant like milk. It is better than [1190A] *wine*, that is, than the teaching of the philosophers, which does not nourish but intoxicates, and makes those who receive it grow proud, and leads at last to destruction. Or by wine let us understand the carnal understanding of the Old Testament. We can also by the breasts understand the twofold love, or the two Testaments spiritually understood, or spiritual graces; and yet the meaning will be the same as the aforesaid. The breasts, I say, are *fragrant*, that is, sweet-smelling, and drawing men to themselves by their odor. For that teaching gives off a sweet smell, and draws to itself by its good repute and fame; *with the best ointments*, that is, through the best ointments. For in the evangelical teaching there are ointments, that is, spiritual gifts which are the best, compared to the carnal ointments with which lovers used to be anointed, that they might please the better — or also those dining. Now these [1190B] ointments are rightly called the best: for those whom they anoint they make true kings and priests. *Oil poured out.* Another reason why she had desired him: because *thy name* is poured out, like oil poured out. Oil indeed, while it is in the vessel, neither gives forth odor nor heals pains; but poured out it both emits odor and heals pains: so the name of God, while he was still held enclosed in the bosom of the Father, was little or not at all known: but after he took flesh, visiting the world by his presence, he set himself forth to be known by all; he sent forth an odor, that is, fame and good repute of himself: and, poured in, through the sufferings of those who well received his preaching, he healed [their] sins as well. *Therefore the maidens*, namely, because thou art so worthy and so sweet, therefore not only I, but also the [1190C] *maidens*, that is, the lesser and more fragile, still tender in the faith, *have loved thee*, that is, out of all things they have chosen thee alone as the one to be loved. For although they still take consolation for themselves from temporal things, yet they judge that God is to be preferred to those temporal things.

« Draw me after thee: we will run in the odor of thy ointments. The king has brought me into his storerooms. We will exult and rejoice in thee, mindful of thy breasts above wine. The upright love thee. »
