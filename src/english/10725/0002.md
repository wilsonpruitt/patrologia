## Expositions on the Song of Songs (cont.)

*If thou knowest not thyself.* The voice of the bridegroom. And here he does three things: he threatens, he coaxes the bride, and he also sets forth the means by which the Church may be able to obtain knowledge of herself. The connection. Dost thou seek knowledge of me? For which it is necessary for thee, *O fair one* — [fair] in virtues and in my choosing — that thou know thyself, namely thy dignity which I gave thee through faith, and by giving thee my body and blood, [1193C] and moreover the other spiritual gifts; because if thou knowest not thyself, in what dignity thou art from this, that thou art my bride, *go forth* from the fellowship of the Church, *and depart*, that is, be made far from the Church, so that thou even lose the virtues which thou hadst before. Depart, I say, *after the footsteps*, that is, by imitating the works, *of the flocks, and* thus *feed the kids*, that is, take delight in thy foul acts, and let them be food for thee; and this, *beside the tents of the shepherds*, by imitating those who call themselves shepherds and warriors for God in tents, and are not, but are heretics; or beside the tents of the true shepherds, and not within. For the heretics wish to draw the authority for their uncleannesses from the Holy Scriptures in which holy men do warfare for God — as certain men called Nicolaitans, whose heresy is condemned, Apocalypse II; and [condemned] by the Church in it. *But certain heretics* [1193D], 24, q. 3, who preach that women are to be held in common, and say that the Apostle said all things are to be held in common, and other things of this kind. *To my cavalry.* Having set down the threats, he sets down blandishments, as if he should say: If thou knowest not thyself, go forth; but thou oughtest not to go forth or to be ignorant of thyself, because I have so honored thee as to make thee my beloved, and to free thee from Egypt; which was prefigured in the liberation of the children of Israel from Egypt, Exodus XII. Now the Gentile world is led out of Egypt, that is, out of the darkness of unbelief, and out of the power of Pharaoh, that is, out of the yoke of the devil, through the Red Sea, because through baptism, where all the enemies are submerged, that is, all sins; and this he says, *I have likened thee to a cavalry*, that is, to that in which I did warfare, going among the chariots, [1194A] that is, among the chariots, or in the time of the chariots of Pharaoh. By the chariots in which the powerful sat is denoted the pride or the fickleness of this world. *Beautiful are thy cheeks.* Having set down threats and blandishments, he shows that there are in her means by which she might retain knowledge of herself. I said, if thou knowest not, go forth — which thou needest not do, because thou hast cheeks and a neck by which thou canst hold true knowledge. The cheeks are called that part of men which grinds food and sends it into the belly: so in the Church the higher ones are called cheeks, those who grind the Scriptures and make the lower ones capable of them; and as in the cheeks there is a sign of modesty, so they always preach modesty and chastity — which cheeks are *beautiful*, namely with many virtues, and are *like [those] of a turtledove*, because as the turtledove never has [1194B] but one mate, nor sings except by moaning, so these have Christ alone as bridegroom, and moan very often for their own faults and those of others. The office of the neck is that through it the ground food may descend into the belly, and that it may hold the body to the head; by which are signified the disciples of those higher ones, as Timothy was to Paul, through whom the Scriptures descend to the lower ones, and these by their preaching join the Church to Christ. *The neck*, I say, being *like necklaces*. A necklace warns some where they may take refuge, and protects the breast of the one wearing it, lest anyone lay hands on it; so these by their teaching both protect the Church, lest the adulterer devil lay hands on her, and warn the lesser ones of the Church where they may take refuge and whom they may follow. *Little chains of gold.* The voice of the friends of the bridegroom. Wishing to help the bridegroom, [1194C] as to how the Church may remain in the knowledge of herself, they say: The bridegroom gives thee cheeks and a neck, and we will give the ornaments of the ears. If the same ones who are the cheeks or the neck speak these things, it does no harm. A muraenula (little chain) is named from the muraena (moray), which coils itself into a circle; and by the murenae we signify rounded and perfect sentences, with which the saints adorn the inner ear, that is, the understanding; *of gold*, that is, seasoned with divine wisdom and charity, *inlaid with silver*, that is, distinguished with the best eloquence. Or the sentences and the wisdom which they have in themselves they know how to dispense to each one by honorable arrangements according to his measure — to the wise, as they are, to the humble likewise. Silver, which is a metal that rings better than the others, and by it eloquence is signified. And this we will do [1194D] while the king is in [his] repose, that is, in the confession of God, namely bodily removed from the Church; or this is carried over to what follows: *While the king was.* The voice of the Church answering thus: The bridegroom and his friends give me such ornaments, and therefore *my beloved [is] to me*, that is, to my honor, *he shall abide between my breasts*, that is, in my inmost love; and the cause is put first, as if she should say: Rightly ought I to love him, because he himself gave me nard, namely virtues, and this while he was *in his repose*, that is, in his passion, [in his] resurrection — namely because he himself suffered and rose again, and from this he gave me virtues which gave forth *their odor*, that is, [an odor] befitting them, to wit, good fame. Or, *while the king was in his repose*, that [1195A] is, because he chose the tranquillity of my mind in which to dwell, therefore *my nard gave forth its odor*. Nard is an ointment made from a lowly herb, and the herb itself is likewise called nard, by which humility is signified; and it is of a hot nature, by which charity is signified. The beloved, I say, *shall abide between the breasts*, and he himself will be to me both great sorrow and great joy — namely, for all the adversities which he himself suffered I shall grieve, and for those by which he was not burdened, I shall rejoice; and this he says: *A bundle of myrrh*. By myrrh is denoted bitterness, because it is a most bitter thing, and therefore bodies anointed with it remain whole, because on account of its great bitterness the worms do not approach. By the bundle is denoted a multitude of bitternesses. Likewise *my beloved shall be to me a cluster [1195B] of Cyprus*, that is, a great joy. By the cluster we have wine, in which is gladness. Cyprus is a place in which the best wine grows. The Church rejoices together with the bridegroom, as over the resurrection and the like. I said « my beloved shall abide between my breasts, » and this *in the vineyards of Engaddi*, that is, in the churches, where there is a fountain of the kid, because there the foul are washed in baptism, and become white. Now Engaddi is interpreted *fountain of the kid*, or *temptation of the eyes*. Now the Church is set in the temptation of the eyes in this world, in which all things are set before us for the tempting of the eyes.

« Behold, thou art beautiful, my beloved, behold, thou art beautiful, thy eyes [are those] of doves. »

« Behold, thou art beautiful, my beloved, and comely; our bed [1195C] is flowering, the beams of our houses are of cedar, our panels of cypress. »

*Behold, thou art beautiful.* The voice of the bridegroom, hearing toward himself so great an affection of the bride, in that she had said: « My beloved shall abide between my breasts, » answers her:

Because I abide with thee with so great love, « behold, thou art beautiful. » Which he sets down twice: note that she is beautiful both in body and in soul. *Thy eyes [are those] of doves*, that is, dove-like, namely gazing simply, and lacking gall and envy.

*Thy eyes*, that is, thy preachers, who provide for thee as the eyes for the body, shall be *of doves*. It is said intransitively, that is, they shall truly be doves, that is, having the seven virtues, which are denoted by the seven natures of the dove. For the dove dwells beside the streams, [1195D] so that, when a hawk is seen, it may plunge and escape; it chooses the better grains, it nourishes another's young, it does not tear with its beak, it lacks gall, it nests in the caverns of the rock, it has moaning for song. So the holy preachers dwell beside the streams of Holy Scripture, by which their hearts are watered, so that by their protection they may escape from the diabolical assault. The better grains, that is, the better sentences, namely not those of the heretics, they choose. Another's young, that is, men, at first estranged from Christ, who had been young ones, that is, sons of the devil, they nourish by their teaching and example. They do not tear with the beak, that is, they do not pervert good sentences by tearing them in the manner of the heretics. They lack gall, that is, they lack irrational anger. They nest in the caverns of the rock, that is, in the wounds [1196A] of the death of Christ, who is the most firm rock, they place their nest, that is, in faith in the death of Christ they place refuge, or hope. For by the nest refuge and hope are designated. For the dove, when rain comes on, flees to the nest, and has its hope in the nest. It has moaning for song, that is, as others take delight in song, so they [take delight] in tribulation and moaning. The dove has also this nature, that it recovers lost sight; so also the preachers and provisors of the Church, when some gift of the Holy Spirit has been lost through some fault, recover it, like David, who recovered the spirit of prophecy which he had lost. It has likewise this nature, that it flies in flocks. In the same way the preachers, in flocks, that is, holding the catholic faith, strive toward the heights by the steps of good works and virtues. For [1196B] as many good works as we do, by so many steps do we hasten to God. The dove defends itself with its wings; so good preachers protect and defend themselves with the sentences of the Fathers.

*Behold, thou.* The voice of the bride. Thou, O bridegroom, callest me beautiful, and such I must be because thou art beautiful, and therefore, unless I were beautiful, I could not be joined to thee. *Beautiful* is said according to [his] divinity, *comely* according to [his] humanity. *The bed*: the bridegroom is beautiful, and the bride beautiful, and therefore their coverings are beautiful. By the bed we have those in whom God [dwells], because, to whatever small degree he rests in those in whom he himself rests, the Church is not offended, but rests. Now such [persons] are flowering, that is, having the beginnings of good works. By the flower, therefore, we understand the beginning, since [1196C] the flower precedes the fruit. *The beams of the houses*: by the beams we understand those strong ones who bear the weight of the Church, who are of cedar. The cedar is a tree that does not rot, and by its odor kills the worms; so these, since they have eternity now at least in hope, both by their preaching and their good fame kill the vices in the hearts of others. *The panels* are things hanging from the beams, and are ornaments of the house, and by this are signified those who, hanging from the more perfect, adorn the Church by good instruction and by fame, who are of cypress. The same [is meant] of the cypress as was said of the cedar. When he says *of your houses*, he gives us to understand that Church of the Gentiles, now somewhat grown up.

[1196D] « I am the flower of the field and the lily of the valleys. As the lily among thorns, so is my beloved among the daughters. »

« As the apple tree among the trees of the woods, so is my beloved among the sons. Under the shadow of him whom I had desired I sat, and his fruit was sweet to my throat. The king brought me into the wine cellar, he set charity in order within me. Support me with flowers, surround me with apples, because I languish with love. His left hand is under my head, and his right hand shall embrace me. »
