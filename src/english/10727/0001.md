## Epistle (cont.)

Those who read the divine page and understand it, and yet do not live according to it, know how to build the house of God and to teach others about it, but they themselves are unwilling to enter. And while with zeal and great eagerness they store up that knowledge in memory, they gather it as it were manna — but it rots, because they have a tender and weak stomach, and the juice of that food is not diffused through the members [1591A] of the soul, nor can it be digested. The manna used to rot as it were from a certain faithlessness of those who kept back more than was necessary; and likewise these men, who are unwilling to live spiritually and to be formed, gather up more than is needful for them; nor do they diffuse it into their life and their conduct, but gather it for glory and for gain. And although it rots, still they do not feel the putrefaction and the stings of the worms, because they are occupied about vain and useless things, which are the flowers of the world holding their minds fast. But when these have passed away, they will feel the prickings and the stings of the worms proceeding from the aforesaid corruption.

Drunkenness, wholly feeble, first destroys the memory, scatters the senses, neglects the mind, confounds the understanding, [1591B] stirs up lust, entangles the tongue, tangles speech, corrupts the blood, dulls the sight, disturbs the veins, weakens the sinews, stops up the ears, troubles the inward parts, overturns the sense, makes the brain sodden, enfeebles the limbs, breaks sleep, hinders with the monthly courses. Drunkenness, moreover, is the mother of outrages, the matter of faults, the root of crimes, the origin of vices, a whirlwind captured, the overthrow of sense, the storm of the tongue, the tempest of the body, the shipwreck of chastity, an ignominious sickness, easily persuaded to evil, the foulness of conduct, the disgrace of life, the infamy of honor, the corrupting of the soul. Wine indeed is not rejected in its use, but condemned in excess.

Sobriety is wholly comely, wholly useful; it first [1591C] preserves the memory, sharpens the senses, keeps the mind pure, steadies the countenance, keeps modesty whole, mitigates vice, tends the face, polishes the ears, unburdens the brain, frees the tongue, unfolds speech, strengthens the blood, tends the veins, braces the sinews, despises lust, enjoys sleep, restores the body, prolongs old age. Without these every man becomes contemptible.

A stade has one hundred and twenty-five paces; eight stades make one mile; from one mile [1592A] and another half is made one league; three miles make two leagues.

AUGUSTINE. « The prophet Isaiah more openly designates holy Mary, saying: *There shall come forth a Rod from the root of Jesse, and a flower shall rise up from his root [n: (Isa. II, 1)]*. That this rod brought forth nuts was an image of the Lord's body; for the nut has in its body a threefold union of substance: the husk, the shell, and the kernel. In the husk, the flesh; in the shell, the bones; in the kernel, the inner soul is compared. In the husk of the nut it signifies the flesh of the Savior, which had in itself the harshness, or the bitterness, of the passion. In the inner kernel it declares the sweetness of the divinity, which bestows nourishment and supplies the service of light. In the shell, inserting the wood of the cross, which did not divide what was [1592B] without and within, but joined by the interposition of the wood those things of the Mediator which were earthly and heavenly — as the Apostle says: *For he by the blood of his cross made peace for the things that are in heaven and the things that are on earth (Colos. I, 20*. »

*For my yoke is sweet and my burden light [n: (Matth. XI, 20)]*. There is a burden which carries and lightens the one who bears it — that is, the light burden of the Gospel, and the yoke of Christ, sweet to those converted to Christ. He who before could not bear the precepts of the law, afterwards finds the precepts of the Gospel light, through cooperating grace. He who at first could not fulfill *thou shalt not kill,* afterwards finds it light to lay down his life for his brothers; and so with the rest. So, when a heavy burden is laid upon a beast of burden, and it shies away as though the load were unbearable, a rolling four-wheeled cart is brought up — that is, [1592C] the Gospel running through the whole world — and the burden which at first it shied from as heavy, it afterwards draws doubled without labor. So the little bird which, unfledged and without wings, cannot carry itself, once the weight of feathers and wings is added, flies away without labor. So too hard bread, which of itself cannot pass down, by the addition of milk or of some other liquid may be able to be strained down in the throat.
