## II. *To the same.--On the same subject.* [n: (Anno eod.)]

To his most excellent and most devoted lady Richenza, protected by God, the great and peace-making Augusta and empress of the Romans, PETER, the servant of your empire, wishes, beyond the power of the present kingdom, eternal happiness.

Although a severed sinew gives pain, and a sorrowful spirit [1139D] grinds the bones, yet our lord the emperor Lothar is not to be mourned as are those also who, as the Apostle says, have no hope. He has gone forth to his own, in whom is the highest good, in whom is all goodness, all justice, all mercy, and all holiness. He has gone forth to him who does not cease to cry out to those who keep his commandments: Come to me, all you who labor and are burdened, and I will make you to rest. Elijah in his haste asked that his soul might be taken from him.

*And after much more:*

Is there, then, cause to grieve at a departure? By no means. For what is more absurd than that you should bewail as something particular to yourself what you know has been laid down for all? This is to exalt the mind above its condition, not to accept the common law, to refuse fellowship, to be puffed up with the mind of the flesh, [1140A] and to be ignorant of the measure of that same flesh. What is more absurd than not to know who you are, and to aim at what you are not? Or what is more imprudent than to be unable to bear, when it has happened, the thing you knew was going to happen? Nature herself calls us back, and by a certain comparison with herself draws us out from mournings of this sort. For what mourning is so heavy, or what grief so sharp, that in it the mind is not now and then relaxed? Nature has this property, that although men are set among sad circumstances, yet, if only they are men, they draw the mind away from mourning for a little while. There are even said to have been certain peoples who mourned the births of men and celebrated their deaths — and not imprudently; for they thought that those who had come into this age of life were to be mourned over, while those who had emerged from the storms and waves of this world they judged were to be attended with a joy that was not [1140B] unjust. Death is equal for all men, making no distinction for the rich. This so great a Caesar had daily before his eyes — a man who, as we saw and learned by experience while we were engaged in his service, displayed beneath the imperial cloak the raw service of another warfare. For first, in the twilight, he would hear the Mass for the dead; then the Mass for the army; last the Mass of the day; and at length, distributing food and drink lavishly to widows, orphans, and the other poor, he would relieve the suits and oppressions brought against churches, and at the last would sweat over the care of the empire. For as long as he stayed at Cassino, every night he went about the cloister and all the workshops of the monastery just as an abbot or any dean would, wishing to know how each man was living under the mastership of blessed Benedict. And when [1140C] morning came, he would walk with unshod feet through all the churches of the monastery for the sake of prayer. And although he did this, he was never cut off from the company of archbishops, bishops, abbots, clerics, or monks, so that there was fulfilled in him what we read: And his conversation is with the wise and the religious. And who would not marvel at his gravity, when, in order to settle the dissensions of the brothers which had arisen over the election of an abbot, he came to the chapter and, from the first hour of the day until evening, indulging in neither food nor drink, would not go out from there? And with what affection, with what love he loved the church of Cassino, he showed when he said that he would sooner allow all the privileges and grants of the churches to be torn up [1140D] than allow anything of the dignities and privileges of the Church of Cassino to be diminished; and that he would sooner allow the election of all the bishops, archbishops, and abbots in the whole Roman world to be taken away than that of the abbot of Cassino alone. For he used to say: If an abbot has not been elected by the consent of the monks, he is not an abbot; and whoever takes away the election from the monks tears up all the rights of the monastery. And while he did all these things, he was nevertheless a staff to the blind, food to the hungry, the hope of the wretched, the solace of mourners; and he so stood out in each single virtue as though he did not possess the rest. He honored priests as lords, clerics as fathers, the poor as sons, widows as mothers; frequent in admonitions, sleepless in vigils, he offered his tears to God, not to men. Tears roll over my face, and though my mind is set hard [1141A] I cannot conceal the grief I suffer. For after he had made our littleness a disciple of Bertulf the chancellor; after he had rewarded me with the office of logothete, of recorder and of auditor; after he had granted me a seat at his feet; after he had made me his private secretary; after he had instructed me sufficiently in the things that pertain to eternal life — in your presence and with all the courtiers of the empire standing by, taking my hand and commending me to abbot Wibald, he said to him: If there are any traces of my esteem, any of my love, in your heart, show them upon this man; and for love of me hold him in the place of a most dear and only son. When the august emperor and Roman Caesar, named also father of his country, had said these things, while he was striving, [1141B] together with your excellency, to reconcile discordant clerics in peace, he rendered body and soul to heaven. Take comfort, therefore, most valiant lady, and according to that precept of Solomon: Receive consolation in place of sadness. But perhaps you say: How can I receive consolation, when I have lost him whom I loved? Are not these things common to us with the world itself and its elements, that we cannot hold in perpetuity what was created for a time? She has not lost her husband who displays chastity; she is not widowed of her marriage who has not changed her husband's name. Recall what David did: for when he had lost his son, he rose up, ate, drank, and said: I am going to him, but he will not return again to me. Considering these things, therefore, most excellent lady, so temper yourselves against the sadness that presses upon you, and so strive, [1141C] that you may be able to behold God, as is fitting, with a happy gaze. The congregation of Cassino also sends you the service of its greeting and consolation and prayer; and commending to you the boy Mino, son of the lord Ptolemy, who is with you, they ask that you hold him in the place of a son.
