## Brief Notes on Joel

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0321C] *[n: (JOEL. I.)] The word of the Lord that came to Joel the son of Phatuel.* ⟦s2⟧ HISTORICALLY. ⟦s3⟧ In a word three things are attended to: noise, form, meaning. ⟦s4⟧ The noise of persecution, the form of the voice, the meaning of the utterance. ⟦s5⟧ The striking is referred to vengeance, the voice to gladness, the utterance to grace. ⟦s6⟧ Since indeed vengeance strikes, the voice consoles, the utterance instructs. ⟦s7⟧ It instructs the son, it consoles the hired servant: it instructs the son who thirsts eagerly for his father's teaching; it consoles the hired servant who labors anxiously under judgment. ⟦s8⟧ The grace of obedience instructs the son, the wage of justice consoles the hired servant, the penalty of malice afflicts the slave. ⟦s9⟧ The noise is referred to punishment, the voice to reward, the meaning to grace. ⟦s10⟧ These three things, then, are considered in the word that came to Joel: because in the outcome of the prophecy that follows, [0321D] both the vengeance upon enemies, and the consolation of the afflicted people, and the instruction of the elect are foretold. ⟦s11⟧ The noise of the sound, then, is made toward the adversary; the form of the voice toward the afflicted people; the meaning of the word is made toward the prophetic mind and the choir of the elect. ⟦s12⟧ For whence the adversary [0322C] is afflicted, thence the people is consoled: in that the mind of the elect, understanding, glories in the Lord. ⟦s13⟧ The noise, then, is made to the carnal, the form is directed to the animal, the meaning to the spiritual. ⟦s14⟧ Now this word is the Lord's. ⟦s15⟧ In the Lord also three things are considered: vengeance, zeal, retribution. ⟦s16⟧ The vengeance of fault, the zeal of discipline, the reward of obedience. ⟦s17⟧ *Therefore the word of God is living, and effectual, and more piercing than any two-edged sword [n: (Hebr. IV)]*. ⟦s18⟧ Piercing, because it destroys malice; living, because it leads back to grace; effectual, because it brings through to glory. ⟦s19⟧ It is piercing in judgment, living in deed, effectual in promise. ⟦s20⟧ Piercing, because judgment is hidden. ⟦s21⟧ Living, because its deed bears fruit. ⟦s22⟧ Effectual, because its promise is not denied. ⟦s23⟧ Piercing, because it destroys mightily; living, because it quickens [0322D] powerfully; effectual, because it repays effectually. ⟦s24⟧ This is the word of the Lord that came to Joel the son of Phatuel.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ First it must be seen that the construction suffers an ellipsis. ⟦s2⟧ For in saying: the word of the Lord that came [0323A] to Joel the son of Phatuel, something must be supplied for the completeness of the sense, either thus: This is the word that came; or thus: The word that came shows these other things. ⟦s3⟧ There follows:

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *Hear this, ye old men, and give ear, all ye inhabitants of the land: whether this hath been done in your days, or in the days of your fathers. ⟦s2⟧ Tell ye of this to your children, and let your children tell it to their children, and their children to another generation.* ⟦s3⟧ From the greatness of the coming calamity he turns to the stupor of wonder. ⟦s4⟧ But lest these things seem to be held cheap, he calls together the authority of old men; lest they be thought of little moment or slight, he rouses the inhabitants of the land, saying: Hear, ye old men, etc. ⟦s5⟧ The wisdom of the old men gives authority to the wonder, the concern of the inhabitants gives credence to the stupor — namely, that of the misfortune to come it may in no way [0323B] be possible to doubt: to which the authority of the wise and the concern of the common people seem publicly to bear certain witness. ⟦s6⟧ There follows, expressing the parts of the misfortune, saying:

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *What the caterpillar hath left, the locust hath eaten: and what the locust hath left, the grub hath eaten: and what the grub hath left, the rust hath eaten. ⟦s2⟧ Awake, ye that are drunk, and weep: howl, all ye that drink wine in sweetness, for it is perished from your mouth.* ⟦s3⟧ The upper part of the misfortune having been shown, and the voice of lament having followed after, he undertakes to show another kind of misfortune, and strives to descend to that form of lament. ⟦s4⟧ For he says:

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *For a nation is come up upon my land, strong and without number. ⟦s2⟧ Its teeth are as the teeth of a lion: and its molars as a lion's whelp. ⟦s3⟧ It hath laid my vineyard into a desert: and hath barked my fig tree. ⟦s4⟧ Stripping it bare it hath despoiled [0323C] it, and cast it away; its branches are made white.* ⟦s5⟧ Behold the lament. ⟦s6⟧ Now he subjoins the misfortune, saying:

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *Lament, like a virgin girded with sackcloth for the husband of her youth.* ⟦s2⟧ And so, alternating misfortune with lament, he runs on as far as that place: *To thee, O Lord, will I cry.* ⟦s3⟧ Where there is made a suppliant invocation to God, and a pitiable setting forth of the calamity, and a humble and affectionate entreaty that it be blotted out. ⟦s4⟧ According to some he designates the terrible coming of the Babylonians into Judaea, their hostile onset, their fierce spirit: raging everywhere, laying all things waste, destroying all things. ⟦s5⟧ Or, what is truer, the pride of the Assyrians, and the bold ferocity of that nation, greedy for blood, [0323D] covetous of glory, exposed to disasters, prone to victory. ⟦s6⟧ Who under Sennacherib, laying waste all Judaea, besieged Jerusalem. ⟦s7⟧ And because they ascribed the fortune of the affair not to divine judgment but to their own strength, the king fleeing with a few, all perished in one night, struck by the angel. ⟦s8⟧ Joel designates by the caterpillar the Assyrians; by the locust the Babylonians; by the grub the Persians and Medes; by the mildew the Macedonians. ⟦s9⟧ All these, through the succession of the times, laid waste the people of God: and if one of them had left anything flowering, or green, or by chance unconsumed, at the coming of another it was trodden down and reduced to nothing. ⟦s10⟧ Let him read the ancient Josephus, and the traditions of the Jews, and the other histories pertaining to this, who desires to be made certain about the matter.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0324A]Allegorically, however, he speaks of the general persecution of the Church, which is read to be fourfold: because it is read to have been assailed first by idolaters, secondly by heretics, thirdly by false Christians, and fourthly to be assailed by Antichrist. ⟦s2⟧ Which Joel, foreseeing with a prophetic eye, on account of the greatness of the persecution calls the old men and the inhabitants of the land into fellowship of wonder. ⟦s3⟧ As if he said: I see the misfortunes to come upon the bride of Christ, I behold her calamities, I look out upon her various toils: but I call to witness the wisdom of spiritual men, and the concern of carnal men too, that they may pour on what they have heard, by succession, into after times: until the dawn arise from heaven, the bride of the Lamb come, the Church spring forth: that, forearmed by our oracle, she may remain firm: lest she receive a rival in place of a husband, lest, deceived, she choose a flower without [0324B] fruit, coin without silver, a sheaf without grain, a shadow without a body. ⟦s4⟧ By the caterpillar he designates idolaters; by the locusts, heretics; by the grubs, false and carnal Christians; by the mildew, the malicious solicitude of Antichrist: the first on account of the filthiness and the variety of idolatry; the second on account of the flight of knowledge, the agility of wit, the elation of pride; the third on account of the heat and the uncleanness of carnal concupiscence; the fourth on account of the monstrousness of its savagery. ⟦s5⟧ For after the Church escaped the shipwreck of idolatry, it fell into the waves and storms of the heretics: but while, with a spiritual west wind blowing, the storm was turned into a gentler breeze, again it labored long in enduring the heat of carnal men: at last, by the son of perdition, *who shall be lifted up above all [0324C] that is called God [n: (II Thes. II)]*, or that is worshipped: wearied in many ways, variously tormented, most grievously distressed: at length, fair weather being restored, it will return to the harbor of liberty: it will enter the dwelling of peace. ⟦s6⟧ And this is what he says: What the caterpillar hath left, the locust hath eaten, etc. ⟦s7⟧ This is the nation which is said to have come up upon the land, that is, upon the Church, strong and without number: strong in greatness, without number in multitude; strong in cruelty, without number in persistence; the lion is the devil, or a prince highest in earthly power: as some heresiarch, or Antichrist himself. ⟦s8⟧ The teeth are his satellites. ⟦s9⟧ The whelps, the nobles, or the petty kings. ⟦s10⟧ The molars, the executioners. ⟦s11⟧ That which above he called the land, he now calls the fig tree and the vineyard, that [0324D] is, the Church. ⟦s12⟧ The land, on account of the stability of faith: the vineyard, on account of the austerity of penance; the fig tree, on account of the sweetness of love. ⟦s13⟧ Or the land, on account of the firm ground of justice. ⟦s14⟧ The vineyard, on account of the gladness of spiritual understanding. ⟦s15⟧ The fig tree, on account of the sweetness of heavenly teaching. ⟦s16⟧ Or the land, on account of the fruit of spiritual exercises: the vineyard, on account of the grace of heavenly gifts; the fig tree, on account of the sweetness of eternal joys. ⟦s17⟧ These things Christ the Lamb conferred upon his bride, namely the Church. ⟦s18⟧ The first in the garden; the second in the storeroom; the third in the bed. ⟦s19⟧ For in the garden she is exercised; in the storeroom she is gladdened; in the bed she is made happy. ⟦s20⟧ First by the works of penance; secondly by the gifts of grace; thirdly by the kisses of wisdom. ⟦s21⟧ But the aforesaid nation barked it, stripped it cast it away. ⟦s22⟧ The bark [0325A] is the exercises, the leaves the words, the fruit the merits. ⟦s23⟧ The exercises of grace, the words of teaching, the merits of glory. ⟦s24⟧ But the aforesaid nation barked it, stripped it, cast it away, because, as the persecution grew heavier, the exercises of religion grew lukewarm, the words of preaching fell silent, the merits of the virtues were thinned in many.

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Tropologically, however, the prophet speaks of the temptations of the soul. ⟦s2⟧ He shows that a spiritual nation has come up upon it, that a troop of vices has by the just judgment of God invaded it, trodden it down, stormed it, saying: What the caterpillar hath left, the locust hath eaten: and what the locust hath left, the grub hath eaten: and what the grub hath left, the rust hath eaten. ⟦s3⟧ I know that certain men before me have said that these four signify the four perturbations of the mind, and have referred the caterpillar to fear, the locust to hope, the grub to joy, [0325B] the mildew to grief. ⟦s4⟧ Of which is that verse of Boethius: Drive off joys, and fear, and put hope to flight, and let not grief be present. ⟦s5⟧ Clouded is the mind, and bound with reins, where these reign. ⟦s6⟧ But we leave them their own interpretation, and hasten on to other things which seem more fitting. ⟦s7⟧ What the caterpillar hath left, the locust hath eaten, etc. ⟦s8⟧ The caterpillar, then, is lust; the locust, *cenodoxia*, that is, vainglory; the grub, *gastrimargia*; the mildew, anger or impatience. ⟦s9⟧ What the caterpillar leaves is chastity; what the locust leaves, humility; what the grub leaves, sobriety; what the mildew leaves, gentleness of mind, and patience. ⟦s10⟧ What the first leaves, therefore, the second eats; what the second leaves, the third; what the third leaves, the fourth: because it very often happens that from continence empty glory arises, that drunkenness and surfeit follow humility, and anger or [0325C] impatience follow sobriety. ⟦s11⟧ For not seldom it happens that the mind, still imperfect, while it reckons that it has conquered and has come to the summit of justice, falls from its victory; and while it sees that it has overcome one of the enemies, it reckons that it has sustained the whole business of the war, and supposes that it has overcome the whole army. ⟦s12⟧ But while the deceived mind exults over the triumph of one, it does not see, wretched thing, that another draws his sword and threatens death at close quarters. ⟦s13⟧ Hence it is that, insulting over the slaying of lust, now as it were secure and less provident, it is struck by the sword of *cenodoxia*. ⟦s14⟧ But if it should chance to have overcome this by humility of mind, *gastrimargia* at once thrusts itself in. ⟦s15⟧ For while the incautious soul sees chastity restraining the flesh, while it beholds humility repressing the mind, it straightway hastens to condescend to pleasure, speaking thus: [0325D] Since neither does the wind of empty glory stir the mind, nor does the enticement of carnal concupiscence tickle the flesh, it would be a kind of cruelty if I tortured my fellow citizen with the goads of dire necessity. ⟦s16⟧ The peace restored to me in both admonishes me, and allows me to minister pleasant things. ⟦s17⟧ Thus surfeit is brought in, and the citizen is slain. ⟦s18⟧ Further, if it happens to have overcome this too by divine help and its own zeal: now supposing that it has wholly escaped shipwreck, it falls back into that misfortune. ⟦s19⟧ For because the soul considers that continence is rising in the flesh, humility in the mind, abstinence in the body, now presuming upon perfection, and despising the rest in comparison with itself, if ever its endeavor is resisted, its onset opposed, its desire thwarted: straightway [0326A] it loosens the reins to anger, it leaps out into words of impatience, saying: *Thou wast born wholly in sins, and dost thou teach us? [n: (Joan. IX.)]* The order of things requires that the prior things hold the prior place, and that the lower lie lower. ⟦s20⟧ It is written: *The spiritual man judgeth all things; and he himself is judged by no man [n: (I Cor. II)]*. ⟦s21⟧ And in the law the mouth of the leper is commanded to be shut [n: (Levit. XIII)]. ⟦s22⟧ First, then, learn rightly, that you may teach soundly; first live well, that you may reprove justly. ⟦s23⟧ Hence the Lord through Job: *Remember the battle, and add no more to speak [n: (Job XL)]*. ⟦s24⟧ As if he said: The mind must show itself the more solicitous against the spiritual things of wickedness [n: (Ephes. VI)], the more subtle their arms prove to be. ⟦s25⟧ Nor ought you to become impatient over the grace of triumph, or elated over the victory over some few enemies, you who lie open to the ambushes of many and are hemmed in by many. [0326B] Whence Joel: What the caterpillar hath left, the locust hath eaten; and what the locust hath left, the grub hath eaten; and what the grub hath left, the rust hath eaten. ⟦s26⟧ There follows:
