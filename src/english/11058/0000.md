## Little Notes on Lamentations

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0255D] *[n: (THREN. I.)] How doth the city sit alone, that was full of people?* ⟦s2⟧ So far as concerns the letter, Jeremiah bewails the desolation of Jerusalem, and this is the voice of one wondering or grieving. ⟦s3⟧ And he begins from wonder for this reason, that he may show the greatness of the calamity, and by this make his hearers attentive: that they may recognize in what evils they are, and be turned to repentance. ⟦s4⟧ How doth the city sit alone, that was full of people? as if he should say: [0256D] The city which once, in the time of David and of the other good kings who pleased God, was full of people — mark why it has now remained alone. ⟦s5⟧ For why, unless because they offended God? ⟦s6⟧ But he says alone, that is, desolate, because of the people led away captive into Babylon. ⟦s7⟧ Or, if it be referred to the same time, she is full of people, and yet sits alone; because she has not God propitious, since [0257A] a multitude of people cannot profit where the help of God is wanting. ⟦s8⟧ But that he says, Sitteth, pertains to casting down and to humiliation.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *She is become as a widow,* that is, forsaken while her husband is yet living: and therefore not a widow, but as a widow; because if she shall have repented, she will still be able to be reconciled. ⟦s2⟧ And God is called the husband of that people for this reason; because he had joined her to himself in chaste love for his own worship, that she might not commit fornication through the various cults of idols.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *The mistress of the nations is become as a widow; the prince of the provinces is become under tribute.* ⟦s2⟧ So it was of old, that the peoples of foreigners served the Jews, and the provinces of the nations were subject to them; but now they themselves, forsaken by God, are made tributary to the nations. [0257B] The recollection therefore of the former glory is the heightening of the present misery.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ According to the sense of allegory, Jeremiah signifies in the Church any spiritual men whatever: who, when they see that a multitude of men has flowed together to the faith, and that the name of Christ has been spread through almost the whole world, but find none, or exceedingly few, who follow Christ in truth and sincerely hold his faith — all men seeking the things that are their own — grieving and groaning say: How doth the city sit alone, that was full of people? ⟦s2⟧ Why do we discern so great a people in the Church, and yet see the Church to be alone? because we can scarcely find anyone who is truly with the Church. ⟦s3⟧ I have found something similar in the Gospel, the Lord being set in the crowd, [0257C] the people hemming him in on every side and pressing upon him: *There came a woman with an issue of blood; and coming behind she touched the hem of his garment: and he straightway: Who,* he saith, *hath touched me? [n: (Marc. V.)]* Did someone touch me? ⟦s4⟧ For he who, when the woman touched the hem, asks as though he had suffered something new, who touched me, plainly declares that before (although all were jostling and pressing) he had not been touched. ⟦s5⟧ As therefore Christ remains untouched while the crowd presses, so the Church, the body of Christ, sits alone among many; because the catholic faith has many professors, few imitators, just as then too those who were near the Lord by bodily presence could not touch him; because they were far off by faith and [sic: *dilelectionem*]. [0257D] The spiritual Jeremiah therefore laments, and says: How doth the city sit alone, that was full of people? ⟦s6⟧ For wherever the servants of God are, they cannot see these things without grief and groaning. ⟦s7⟧ The mistress of the nations is become as a widow; the prince of the provinces is become under tribute. ⟦s8⟧ By the nations we rightly understand all carnal men placed within the Church; but by the provinces, all whosoever are established outside the Church, such as are pagans, Jews and heretics. ⟦s9⟧ Then therefore is holy Church mistress of the nations, when by the rigor of discipline she subjects all the carnal placed within to serve her: she is prince of the provinces, when by power she repels the unbelievers placed without, that they may not be able to harm. ⟦s10⟧ But if perchance, when for the demands of sins she is forsaken by God, so that she is able neither to restrain those who trouble her within, [0258A] nor to defend herself from those who assail her without: then the mistress of the nations is left as a widow, and the prince of the provinces is made tributary. ⟦s11⟧ For when she beholds the faithful drawn away to unbelief, whether deceived by heretics, or broken by the powers of this world through reward or through terror, what else does she do than pay tribute to foreigners?

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ According to the moral understanding, the city signifies the soul, which sits alone when it is forsaken by God; but full of a people of virtues, when it is inhabited by God. ⟦s2⟧ But if we have found the city whose desolation is lamented, where is Jeremiah to be found? ⟦s3⟧ Each one of us ought to be a Jeremiah, and to lament his own desolation just as he lamented the desolation of Jerusalem. ⟦s4⟧ And surely, if he so lamented [0258B] the ruin of stones, we much more ought to lament the desolation of our souls, and each one to say: How doth the city sit alone, that was full of people? ⟦s5⟧ How is my soul made desolate? ⟦s6⟧ How has it lost that good inhabitant, in whose presence it was once full of a people of virtues? ⟦s7⟧ The mistress of the nations is become as a widow. ⟦s8⟧ The nations are the desires of the flesh, which are inborn in us according to the corruption of our first nativity, and gainsay the law of the mind: over which the soul then rules well, when it is perfectly made subject to God. ⟦s9⟧ The prince of the provinces is become under tribute. ⟦s10⟧ By the provinces we can understand the bodily senses, because, as in one province there are many men, so each of the senses has diverse motions and diverse operations, through which it is poured out abroad [0258C] upon visible things; and while the appetites of the several senses are moved at the beck of reason, the soul holds rule as over certain provinces. ⟦s11⟧ If therefore the soul desires to rule over what is beneath it, that is, over sensuality, it is necessary that it make itself subject to what is above it, that is, to God; because it will in no wise be able to be troubled beneath itself by what is beneath it, so long as it shall have God above it as its governor. ⟦s12⟧ But if, forgetful of the fear of the Lord, it has followed its own concupiscences, God takes away his grace from it, and then of necessity it succumbs nervelessly to those desires which before, when it was subject to God and was ruled by him, it powerfully constrained — the appetite of its own senses — to the command of reason.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ And note that he says mistress, not prince, of the nations, [0258D] and prince, not mistress, of the provinces; because vices, which are not natural, ought to be repressed; for the senses, because they are natural, must not be repressed but ruled, so that in extirpating the former a man should study to be strict, in moderating and guarding the latter, discreet. ⟦s2⟧ But it happens sometimes that, while a man neglects to resist the lawful desires of his flesh and to guard the motions of his senses, he is at length so bound by depraved custom that afterwards, even willing it, he cannot resist those same desires. ⟦s3⟧ When therefore he is compelled to serve the vices to which before he consented of his own accord, what else does he do than pay tribute to depraved custom? ⟦s4⟧ Three goods therefore and three evils has he enumerated. ⟦s5⟧ The goods are: a city full of people, mistress of the nations, prince of the provinces; the evils: alone, a widow, tributary. ⟦s6⟧ But let us see [0259A] first how she obtains the goods, afterwards how she descends to the evils. ⟦s7⟧ Before the Holy Spirit comes to our heart, our soul is barren; but when he has come, he makes it fruitful, that it may bring forth and that virtues may be born in it. ⟦s8⟧ Which virtues, so long as they are still imperfect and beginning, and cannot yet go forth abroad, but are still nourished within by the grace of the same Spirit, that they may grow and become sturdy, what else are they than certain little ones brought up in the father's house, until they come to lawful age? ⟦s9⟧ But when they have come to full increase, and have begun to use a certain solid food of wisdom, then they no longer need to be nourished as little ones, but ought to live as a certain people in a city under the law of its own ruler. ⟦s10⟧ But when God, [0259B] presiding within, rules us, then the flesh, made subject, serves without; and the more humbly we are made subject to him within, the more sturdily we hold rule without. ⟦s11⟧ If therefore our soul was within full of a people of virtues, when it had God as king; it was also without the mistress of the nations, that is, of carnal desires, and prince of the provinces, that is, of the senses of the body. ⟦s12⟧ But now *alone,* because it has lost its king; *a widow,* because it has lost its husband; *tributary,* because, made subject to vices, it serves.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *Weeping she hath wept in the night.* ⟦s2⟧ The driving home of the word signifies the abundance of grief. ⟦s3⟧ Weeping she hath wept in the night, that is, in the time of rest, in the time of forgetfulness, when men are wont to forget their own evils. ⟦s4⟧ And mark how great is the misery of her from whom even then [0259C] griefs cannot be absent, when others rest from griefs.

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *And her tears are on her cheeks.* ⟦s2⟧ There is sometimes a grief which touches the heart in some fashion, but does not suffice to wring out tears. ⟦s3⟧ Not such is the grief of this woman; for her tears are on her cheeks.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *There is none to comfort her among all her dear ones.* ⟦s2⟧ Either because they despise her, set as she is in tribulation, or because in so great an evil they cannot come to her aid. ⟦s3⟧ And he calls the dear ones of that people the prophets and the princes, who had been wont to comfort them: the prophets, by promising help from God; and the princes, by purging away enemies and defending the fatherland. ⟦s4⟧ But now they in no wise comforted the people set in tribulation, because the one sort preached God angry, and the other could not resist the enemies [0259D] laying waste the country and taking the people captive. ⟦s5⟧ Or it can be read otherwise: weeping she hath wept in the night, that is, in secret, in hiding: which is a thing loved [amicum] by those who weep, who will not be comforted, but are fed upon their own griefs. ⟦s6⟧ Or the people set in captivity weeps in hiding for this reason, that it does not dare to make its sadness manifest, on account of the cruel lords to whom it is subject, lest it should stir up their greater wrath against itself, if it appeared sorrowful over its own servitude. ⟦s7⟧ But so great a grief cannot be hidden, because her tears are on her cheeks, that is, in the open, in the manifest, because from the constancy of weeping the face has swollen. ⟦s8⟧ And in these so great evils, what comfort could they look for, [0260A] to whom even their dearest bring no comfort!
