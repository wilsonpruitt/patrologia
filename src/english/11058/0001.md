## Little Notes on Lamentations (cont.)

*All her friends have despised her.* By friends he would have understood the neighboring nations once joined by treaty to the people of the Jews, who now have not only despised that people, set as it is in distresses, by bringing it no help, but have even become its enemies by persecuting it. According to the sense of allegory the night is sinners, but the cheeks are the preachers who by expounding it break small the food of the word of God, and so pass it on to the weak and to those dull of understanding. But when the Church, in this exile of the present life, weeps in her more perfect members for the falls of those who grow weak, she bears, like a people set in captivity, tears on her cheeks through the night. Or the night can signify this very present life in which we live, [0260B] when as yet we do not see one another's consciences. And, as we said above, the cheeks signify those who have the knowledge of the word of God. Then therefore does holy Church bear tears on her cheeks through the night, when all the perfect, the more nearly they behold by the illumination of the mind the day of eternity, so much the more lament the darkness of the present life, according to the sentence of Solomon, in which he says: *He that addeth knowledge, addeth sorrow* [laborem] There is none to comfort her among all her dear ones [n: (Eccles. I)]. By the dear ones of the Church we understand those same sinners whom above we said were signified by the night; whom assuredly, while she laments them, she loves, because she would in no wise lament over their perdition, unless she loved their salvation and their conversion. But then she receives no comfort from [0260C] them, when none of them returns to repentance, because the comfort of those who weep would be the conversion of sinners. Or the dear ones of the Church are those blessed angelic spirits, or the souls of the saints, to whose fellowship she sighs out of the night of this exile; who then bring her no comfort, while they still suffer her to be a pilgrim away from their company. Of whom it is further added. All her friends have despised her. Whom does he here call friends of the Church, save the powerful of this world, who sometimes, while they see her exalted in temporal things, honor her and feign to love her, but when they behold her set in distresses, persecute her and are despised? According to the moral sense the soul has its own day, has its own night. It has day, when it is raised up into the light of contemplation; [0260D] it has night when it is darkened by the gloom of temptations. But in the day it laughs, in the night it weeps, because the mind which groans, pressed by the weight of temptations, afterwards, being lifted up, grows glad in the joy of contemplation. The soul therefore weeps in the night, when it pricks its darkened conscience within with wholesome grief. It bears tears on its cheeks, when by strict chastisement without it afflicts the flesh. For then are the tears on the cheeks, when the griefs of the heart reach even to the maceration of the flesh. Whereupon, for a yet greater heightening of the grief, there is subjoined:

There is none to comfort her among all her dear ones. In three ways is a man forsaken by God: sometimes [0261A] within and not without, sometimes without and not within, sometimes both without and within. Job was forsaken without and not within, who outwardly endured the scourges of the flesh, but within did not lose constancy of mind. David was forsaken within and not without, whose mind lust subjected to itself within by consent, but the prophet's admonition without called him back to repentance. That prodigal son in the Gospel was forsaken within and without, who both, living luxuriously, ran to waste within, and, wasting away with hunger, found no comfort without. But of those whom God deserts in this manner, some he deserts for their testing, that they may be exercised by temptation; others he deserts for their overthrow, that they may be cast down by temptation. For which cause the Psalmist also prays: *Turn not aside in wrath from thy servant [n: (Psal. XXVI)]*; as if [0261B] he should say: Even if thou permittest me to be tempted, do not let me be led into temptation, that is, to be overcome by temptation. But because a man cannot penetrate the depth of the divine judgments, then most of all does each one set in temptation dread that he has been forsaken, when he beholds himself harried by temptations both within and without. But he bears adversities without more easily whose conscience within the delight of sin does not trouble. And again, he endures temptation within more easily who has comfort without. Whence also the great tribulation of this woman is shown, who is demonstrated to have been forsaken both within and without; for this which is said: Weeping she hath wept in the night, and her tears are on her cheeks, intimates that she was forsaken within; but that which is said: There is none to comfort her among all [0261C] her dear ones, declares that she was desolate without. There follows: All her friends have despised her, *and are become her enemies.* See how many are the tribulations of the just. Perhaps it was too little for her, set in distresses, that she received no comfort from her dear ones, unless she also endured persecution from her friends. But who are these friends who persecute us, save those of whom it shall be said in the Gospel: *A man's enemies are they of his own household? [n: (Matth. X.)]* These therefore are our enemies, our persecutors, our own household, our friends, near to us according to the affinity of the flesh, who love us for the present life by a love of the flesh, but gainsay us as we walk in the way of God. For when we desire to remove ourselves far from the love of this world, [0261D] straightway we find those who before seemed friends to be adversaries. First indeed, when we wish to be converted to God, they strive under the pretext of a pestilent affection to call us back with flattering persuasions. But if they see them fixed and immovable in their good purpose, presently they disown and spurn them as adversaries, and sometimes even, by hounding them with most savage hatreds and afflicting them with punishments, from false friends they become true persecutors. Let him say therefore, there is none to comfort her among all her dear ones, because in tribulation she does not deserve to receive the words of the just for her comfort; all her friends have despised her, and are become her enemies, because from the wicked she suffers insult and contempt.

*Judah hath departed.* He passed over, he withdrew, he fled from his own land into Babylon, because he could not endure the great [0262A] servitude with which he was afflicted by the nations, judging that it would be more tolerable for him if he served one nation in a foreign land than if he were a prey to all the nations in his own.

*He dwelt among the nations, nor hath he found rest.* It is proper to the afflicted that they always judge the present peril the heavier. As the sick in the night look for the day, and in the day desire the night, and while they always long for their pain to pass, always tend toward pain, so surely this people, while it was afflicted in its own land, sought flight; but when it was made desolate in exile, and, wandering as a vagabond, could not find rest, it sighed for return. And here he expresses the feeling of those who waver, not because they departed of their own accord, but because, set in straitness, they changed their wishes to a contrary [0262B] mind.

*All her persecutors have taken her in the midst of straits.* Hemmed in on every side, he could find no place of escape; fleeing the Chaldeans, he fell among the Egyptians; and when he was fleeing from the Egyptians, he ran into the Assyrians.

Mystically, Judah, who is interpreted *confessing,* signifies certain men in the Church who confess the name of Christ; but because they are not yet firm in the love of Christ they blush to suffer adversities for Christ. Of such it is said: *They believe for a time, and in the time of temptation they fall away [n: (Luc. VIII)]*. These therefore, on account of the affliction and the multitude of servitude, in which, while they will not share in the sufferings of the just, become strangers to the fellowship of the just. He dwelt among the nations. [0262C] To dwell among the nations is to imitate the life and conversation of the depraved. Not to find rest is to be entangled in the actions of this world; for because in the actions of this world there is no end, there can be no rest for those who pursue them. Yet often a man for love of the present life willingly bears the labors which for the love of God he was refusing to bear. There is made therefore a great reproach against those who depart from God, and at the same time excuse is taken away from them, since it is openly shown that they transgressed without cause, when by declining useful labors they came down to useless labors. All her persecutors have taken her in the midst of straits. So long as the heart of a man is enlarged by charity and by the hope of eternal goods, if perchance it endures without in tribulation, yet within it has no straitness. How great a good therefore [0262D] he loses who loses the confidence which is in God, a man can recognize from this, that the heart always suffers straitness in adversities, unless it be enlarged by the hope of future goods. In the midst of straits, he says, they have seized her. And consider what are the straits of those who depart from God: they are never secure, they always tremble; in prosperity they fear, in adversity they despair.
