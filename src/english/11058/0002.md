## Little Notes on Lamentations (cont.)

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Judah hath departed. ⟦s2⟧ There are some who, while they look upon their own sins, kindled by a certain transitory compunction, come as far as confession; they purpose to enter upon the ways of a better life, and promise that thereafter they will not return to the vices they have committed. ⟦s3⟧ But because they will not afflict themselves with a satisfaction worthy of what they have committed, [0263A] nor with the Apostle chastise the body and bring it into servitude [n: (I Cor. IX)], when temptation quickly comes upon them they return to the sins which they had forsaken; because they cannot easily resist vices who neglect to mortify in themselves the affections of the vices. ⟦s4⟧ Of whom it is here said: Judah hath departed *because of affliction and the multitude of servitude.* ⟦s5⟧ For what does Judah signify but those who confess their own sins? who depart because of affliction and the multitude of servitude, when, overcome, they go away after their own concupiscences; because they dread to mortify them by the affliction and servitude of their own flesh. ⟦s6⟧ Against whom Wisdom says: *Son, coming to the service of God, stand in fear, and prepare thy heart for temptations [n: (Eccle. II)]*. ⟦s7⟧ For those who confess their sins and resolve to amend their [0263B] life, these surely come to the service of God. ⟦s8⟧ But they neglect to stand there, when, overcome by adversities, they fall from their good purpose. ⟦s9⟧ This is what he admonished, prepare thy heart for temptations, that walking in the way of God we may be ready to bear adversities: we who, before we were in the way of God, swelled with pride at not having perpetrated unlawful things. ⟦s10⟧ He dwelt among the nations, nor hath he found rest. ⟦s11⟧ What is it to dwell among the nations, save to comply with carnal desires, where rest is not found — that rest, surely, which the Lord in the Gospel promises to those who labor, saying: *You shall find rest for your souls [n: (Matth. XI)]*; and of which it is said to the sinner: *Thou hast sinned, add not again; but be at rest [n: (Eccle. XXI)]*. ⟦s12⟧ And deservedly: those who would not endure labor in the body [0263C] come to the labor of the soul, and the fury of evil desires harries the mind of those whose flesh the due penalty does not chastise. ⟦s13⟧ So surely that Samson, his eyes plucked out, is set at the mill; because the spirit, having lost the light of truth, is carried round about by the appetite of earthly desires. ⟦s14⟧ Which Samson, so long as he had the hair of his head, was unconquerable; but after he fell asleep in a woman's lap, and, shaven as to his head, lost his hair, forthwith he is taken by his enemies, and is blinded, and is even made over to servitude. ⟦s15⟧ Samson is interpreted *their sun,* and signifies the spirit illuminated by divine knowledge. ⟦s16⟧ The head of Samson is the principal part of the mind. ⟦s17⟧ The hair of the head is the ray of contemplation. ⟦s18⟧ The woman's lap is the blandishment of the flesh. ⟦s19⟧ For so long as the spirit cleaves to contemplation, [0263D] it cannot be overcome by temptation. ⟦s20⟧ But if he shall have been dissolved [sic: *incarnis*] delight, and shall have fallen asleep there, forthwith he loses the light of inmost truth, and, the ray of contemplation being cut off, he does not suffice to fight back against depraved motions; and at length, his eyes plucked out, he is set at the mill, when, forgetful of the inward sweetness, he is scattered abroad through earthly desires. ⟦s21⟧ For what is the mill, save a mind unstable and unquiet which, while it always strives to grasp what it craves, driven round as it were by its own desires, never rests? ⟦s22⟧ Well therefore is it said: He dwelt among the nations; nor hath he found rest. ⟦s23⟧ Because the mind which follows the desires of the flesh is a stranger to inward quiet in the measure that it is scattered abroad without through things that slip away. ⟦s24⟧ All her persecutors [0264A] have taken her, in the midst of straits. ⟦s25⟧ Sometimes those who fall away from the purpose of the virtues are worse than those who have not yet been able to come to the purpose of virtue: because the latter hope that they can attain to that which they have not yet had; but the former are so much the farther from salvation as they have the more evidently lost, with despair, the virtue they had begun. ⟦s26⟧ More therefore are these open to the snares of the demons than those; because hope of future correction in some manner draws those back, but their own despair drives these to ruin. ⟦s27⟧ Therefore of those who, after the beginning of good, return to their vomit, it is here said: All her persecutors have taken her, in the midst of straits. ⟦s28⟧ For who are our persecutors, save malignant spirits and the desires of the flesh, which war against us in our [0264B] members? ⟦s29⟧ And what is the straitness, save the despair of a sinful conscience, which straitens the heart within? ⟦s30⟧ He therefore is seized by all his persecutors in the midst of straits, who for this reason does not gainsay the demons suggesting sin, and his own desires, in his fault; because from a preceding lapse he already despairs of pardon. ⟦s31⟧ Judah therefore hath departed because of affliction, when this man, who by confession had already begun to amend his life, broken by the trouble of temptations, falls from his purpose. ⟦s32⟧ He dwells among the nations, when he fixes his mind upon the delight of carnal desires. ⟦s33⟧ He does not find rest, when [sic: *concucupiscentia*] drags his heart apart through the precipices of the vices. ⟦s34⟧ He is seized by all his persecutors in the midst of straits, when, now pressed by despair, he struggles against no [0264C] depraved suggestions.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *The epilogue of the whole first alphabet.* ⟦s2+3⟧ Our physician, holding the sick man in his hands, behold how he proves the skill of his art. First by long stroking he calls the numbed limbs back to feeling, and by handling the painful wounds with a gentle touch he steadies them for the stroke; then he cuts, then he anoints, then he binds, then he cherishes, and restores him to full health. ⟦s4⟧ So this alphabet is divided into five partitions. ⟦s5⟧ The first is complaint; the second is rebuke; the third is consolation; the fourth is instruction; the fifth is entreaty. ⟦s6⟧ By the complaint he strokes; by the rebuke he cuts; by the consolation he anoints; by the instruction he binds; by the entreaty he cherishes. ⟦s7⟧ The complaint is from the beginning of the alphabet as far as that place where it is said: *To what shall I [0264D] compare thee [n: (Thren. II)]*, etc. ⟦s8⟧ There the rebuke begins, and reaches as far as that place where it is said: *The Lord hath done that which he purposed [n: (Ibid.)]*, etc. ⟦s9⟧ There the consolation begins, and reaches as far as that place where it is said: *Let tears run down like a torrent [n: (Ibid.)]*. ⟦s10⟧ In which instruction sinners are formed unto repentance, and it reaches as far as that place where it is said: *Behold, O Lord [n: (Ibid.)]*. ⟦s11⟧ And from thence to the end is the entreaty, in which the prophet beseeches the Lord for sinners. ⟦s12⟧ These things having been briefly tasted beforehand, let us now inspect the order of the complaint itself. ⟦s13⟧ First he laments them as though absent, because he ought not so quickly to have disturbed with direct address those who were in bitterness. ⟦s14⟧ He begins also to lament first those things which are least, [0265A] lest the weight of unforeseen grief overwhelm the minds of the mourners; and thus, going forward little by little in his enumeration from the lesser to the greater, he gradually rouses the minds of his hearers to mourning. ⟦s15⟧ First therefore he mourns the depopulation of the country; then the destruction of the common buildings; then the overthrow of the sacred edifices; then the contamination of holy things; then from inanimate things he goes forward to the misery of men, namely to the bewailing of their casting down and their want; and so at last he ends his complaint.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *[n: (THREN. II.)] How hath the Lord covered the daughter of Sion with gloom in his fury.* ⟦s2⟧ A lamentable beginning: it begins from wonder, that so suddenly was that people cast down which before seemed exalted even to heaven. ⟦s3⟧ The gloom signifies the sadness of tribulation. [0265B] The daughter, he says, of Sion — Sion herself, that is, the nation of the Jews, whom he loved and guarded with a father's affection; or the daughter of Sion herself, that is, the region of Judea. ⟦s4⟧ For the custom of the Scriptures holds that metropolitan cities be called mothers, and the surrounding regions, and the towns too, and the forts and the villages, daughters, Therefore he begins, as was said above, to lament her from afar, namely the region, that at last he may come to the very head of the region, Jerusalem. ⟦s5⟧ There follows:

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *And he remembered not the footstool of his feet.* ⟦s2⟧ That is, the people, which was humbly subject to his service.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *The Lord hath cast down headlong, and hath not spared.* ⟦s2⟧ Here distinguish.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *He hath destroyed all the beautiful things of Jacob in his fury.* ⟦s2⟧ Here [0265C] distinguish again. ⟦s3⟧ Then there follows:

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *He hath cast down to the ground the strongholds of the virgin of Judah.* ⟦s2⟧ Here once more distinguish. ⟦s3⟧ Then there follows:

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *He hath polluted the kingdom and its princes.* ⟦s2⟧ What in the preceding clause he had set forth generally under the name of the land, this he here pursues by parts — the beautiful things, the strongholds, the kingdom, the princes; and see how the lament grows: before he had named the soil only, then the land; but lest perchance that land itself should be thought barren and untilled and therefore less to be lamented, he thereafter strengthens his lament concerning its opulence and its loftiness. ⟦s3⟧ He hath cast down headlong, he says: it is more to cast down headlong than to cast away; and what is yet graver, and hath not spared. ⟦s4⟧ This was truly fulfilled in the last captivity, because they are now no more to be called back — he hath cast down headlong. [0265D] What: he hath destroyed all the beautiful things of Jacob, he hath cast down the strongholds of the virgin of Judah. ⟦s5⟧ By the beautiful things understand opulence: by the strongholds strength and power. ⟦s6⟧ By Jacob and the virgin of Judah the same people is signified. ⟦s7⟧ Yet Jacob is the general name of the twelve tribes: but Judah pertains to two tribes only. ⟦s8⟧ Therefore, the general name having been set first, he at once joined the special name, that he might show that he was lamenting the captivity of the two tribes. ⟦s9⟧ Jacob therefore and Judah are the same people: Jacob, because sturdy in power; the virgin of Judah, because delicate in opulence. ⟦s10⟧ What then are the beautiful things of Jacob, save the opulence of the strong? and what are the strongholds of the virgin of Judah, save the strength of the opulent. ⟦s11⟧ There follows:

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0266A] *He hath polluted the kingdom and its princes.* ⟦s2⟧ That is, of the virgin of Judah. ⟦s3⟧ He hath polluted, he says, because of the nations among whom they were scattered: by whose fellowship and rite they were profaned, who before reigned secure in the protection of God, and trusted in the strength of their own princes. ⟦s4⟧ But now the whole kingdom with its princes is polluted, because the people, with its protectors, is subjugated to the nations.

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *He hath broken in the anger of his fury every horn, and hath at the same time turned back his right hand.* ⟦s2⟧ By the horn is signified the strength of her princes; but by the right hand of God the divine protection is understood. ⟦s3⟧ What then is it that is said of God, he hath broken every horn of Israel, he hath turned back his right hand, save that in a transgressing people he both crushed all human strength, [0266B] and at length took away his own protection, so that those may be shown wholly desolate from whom, after human defenses, the divine help too is taken away. ⟦s4⟧ He hath turned back, he says, his right hand. ⟦s5⟧ He turned back the right hand, that he might not protect; and stretched out the left, that he might strike. ⟦s6⟧ Whence there follows:

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *And he kindled in Jacob as it were the fire of a devouring [sic: fiammae] round about.* ⟦s2⟧ As fire also, when kindled, ranges widely and consumes all things, so the enemies of the Jews, after all strength had failed in them and the divine help had been withdrawn, with none besieging them, laid all things waste.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *He hath bent his bow as an enemy.* ⟦s2⟧ In the bow a threatening is understood. ⟦s3⟧ To bend the bow therefore is to enlarge the threatening or to defer it; but he who strikes after [0266C] the threatening has bent the bow as an enemy. ⟦s4⟧ Whence there follows:

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *He hath made firm his right hand as a foe.* ⟦s2⟧ When in Scripture the right hand of God is set down, sometimes protection, sometimes a grievous striking is signified, in that the right hand is by natural aptitude the readier for striking. ⟦s3⟧ First therefore God turned back the right hand, that he might not protect; afterwards he made the right hand firm, that he might strike. ⟦s4⟧ But that it is said, he made firm his right hand, expresses the grievous indignation of him who strikes. ⟦s5⟧ Or he makes firm his right hand over the stricken, because the blow which once in anger he inflicted he does not purpose to heal any more; which is properly referred to the last captivity. ⟦s6⟧ Whence fittingly is joined, as a foe, who namely strikes unto utter destruction, [0266D] not unto correction. ⟦s7⟧ For he would not make firm his right hand as a foe, if, while that one strikes to destroy, he himself were striking to correct. ⟦s8⟧ Whence it is to be pronounced with grievous sorrow: as an enemy, as a foe. ⟦s9+10⟧ He, whose wrath the guilty cannot escape, nor can an enemy hinder his mercy.
