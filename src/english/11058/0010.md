## Little Notes on Lamentations (cont.)

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *They said to their mothers,* etc. ⟦s2⟧ MORAL. ⟦s3⟧ What are we to understand spiritually by the sons of mothers, unless a multitude of thoughts, which, while they come forth in one mind out of diverse affections, proceed as it were the offspring of many mothers [0290D] in one house, yet not from one origin? ⟦s4⟧ Now we have said that thoughts are born of the affections for this reason, that assuredly the memory of those things comes to us most frequently through thought, with the love of which we have been affected. ⟦s5⟧ This we easily prove to be true through the individual vices and virtues. ⟦s6⟧ For hence it is that it is said in the Gospel: *Where thy treasure is, there is thy heart [n: (Matth. VI)]*. ⟦s7⟧ As if it were said: Where thy love is, there is thy mind. ⟦s8⟧ Such as thy affection is, such also is thy thought. ⟦s9⟧ But it must be known that, just as the affections generate thoughts out of themselves, so those thoughts in turn the more inflame the affections from which they arise. ⟦s10⟧ Whence also in those who diligently guard their heart from unlawful thoughts, the roots of evil [0291A] affections at length wither away utterly. ⟦s11⟧ But on the contrary those who neglect to exercise their mind in holy meditations, even if they have first grown warm with some spark of divine love, gradually cool, and at last so far that, even if they should sometimes have thoughts of good affections, yet they perceive nothing of inward sweetness distilled from them into the bosom of the mind, not even thinly. ⟦s12⟧ For which reason it is rightly said in this place: They said to their mothers: Where is the wheat and the wine? ⟦s13⟧ For what is signified by the wheat, unless the food of wisdom; and what by the wine, unless inward joy? ⟦s14⟧ For as bodily bread refreshes the belly, so wisdom feeds the mind; and as wine eagerly drunk inebriates, so inward joy estranges the mind from carnal desires. ⟦s15⟧ What is it then that the famished sons seek from their mothers [0291B] wheat and wine, unless that thoughts born of good affections, but dried up through negligence, cannot find in them the savor of their former sweetness? ⟦s16⟧ Of whom it is rightly added: When they fainted as wounded in the streets of the city. ⟦s17⟧ For what are the streets of the city, unless the wanderings of the mind? ⟦s18⟧ In the streets of the city therefore they faint, because, while the wandering mind does not gather them to inward quiet, they do not reach as far as the taste of the innermost savor. ⟦s19⟧ To whom this also well agrees, that it is said as wounded. ⟦s20⟧ For depraved desires are the wounds of good thoughts, because, while they infect them by their admixture, they corrupt the integrity of their rectitude like certain blows. ⟦s21⟧ Rightly therefore are the barren thoughts of the soul said to faint as wounded, [0291C] because they remain as empty of the fruit of justice as those which have been corrupted through unlawful desires. ⟦s22⟧ There follows: When they breathed out their souls in the bosom of their mothers. ⟦s23⟧ The bosom of the mothers is the lukewarmness and sloth of good affections; in which the little thoughts are deservedly said to breathe out their souls, because, when the mind grows lukewarm from the fervor of divine love, presently all the vigor of good thoughts dies away

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *To what shall I compare thee?* etc. ⟦s2⟧ LITERAL. ⟦s3⟧ Little by little he advances the lament. ⟦s4⟧ First he mourned the buildings, and then the men, and these as though absent; now at last, their minds being as it were softened and made accustomed by long weeping, he turns his words to those present, saying: To what shall I compare thee, *and to what shall I liken thee?* as if he were to say: Because thy contrition is so great, to what shall I compare thee? and, because thou art so [0291D] grievous, to what shall I liken thee? ⟦s5⟧ Which is openly to say. ⟦s6⟧ Thy evils surpass all misery both in magnitude and in manner. ⟦s7⟧ But because great things too can sometimes be compared with the least by a certain differing likeness: he adds: *And I shall make thee equal,* etc. ⟦s8⟧ Which is to say. ⟦s9⟧ Other evils, even if they can be compared with thee by a certain difference, yet none can be so by equality. ⟦s10⟧ Why? ⟦s11⟧ There follows: *For great as the sea is thy contrition.* ⟦s12⟧ It is remarkable that he records that her calamity can be made equal to none, and at once makes the greatness of the sea equal to it in comparison. ⟦s13⟧ But it is to be understood thus, as if he were to say: Just as the sea excels all other waters both in bulk and in bitterness, so no calamity can be made equal to thy contrition. ⟦s14⟧ There follows: [0292A] *Who shall heal thee?* ⟦s15⟧ As the water of the sea cannot be dried up, so thy contrition will not be able to be reckoned by man, nor is there any in whom thou mayest hope save God alone. ⟦s16⟧ To what shall I compare thee? ⟦s17⟧ Those who, after the truth has been acknowledged, go back are worse than those who have never acknowledged the truth. ⟦s18⟧ And the more excellent each one's rank is, the more perilous is the fall. ⟦s19⟧ Whence the prophet, when he had lamented above the negligence of evil prelates in the Church and the ruin of their subjects, straightway following added.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *To what shall I compare thee, and to what shall I liken thee, and make thee equal, O virgin daughter of Sion?* ⟦s2⟧ The heaping up of words expresses the force of great grief. ⟦s3⟧ As if he were speaking to the Church herself concerning her withered members and saying: As formerly, when thou didst hold the faith by living rightly, thy glory [0292B] was comparable to none, so now, when by a reprobate life thou hast fallen from the faith, thy ignominy is comparable to none. ⟦s4⟧ There follows:

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *For great as the sea is thy contrition.* ⟦s2⟧ ALLEGORICAL. ⟦s3⟧ What is understood by the sea, unless the worldly life; which, while it is always drawn by an unstable motion now from adversity to prosperity, now from prosperity into adversity, is tossed as it were by certain surging storms? ⟦s3⟧ And because it never excludes either fear in prosperity or grief in adversity, like the sea, into whatever quarter it flows back, it does not lose its bitterness: let it be said therefore to her who grows bitter away from the sweetness of the spiritual life through carnal concupiscence and worldly cares, let it be said, I say: Great as the sea is thy contrition. ⟦s4⟧ As if it were openly said: Thou who, through contempt of the world and desire of eternal goods, from carnal love [0292C] wast made sweet, how art thou turned again into bitterness? ⟦s5⟧ There follows:

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *Who shall heal thee?* ⟦s2⟧ MORAL. ⟦s3⟧ Let the prelates of the Church hear this, who are appointed physicians of souls; let them hear what is said. ⟦s4⟧ Who shall heal thee? ⟦s5⟧ As if it were said: Physicians cure other sick men, but who shall cure ailing physicians? ⟦s6⟧ If the people sins, the priests pray for it. ⟦s7⟧ But for the priests, if they have sinned, who will pray? ⟦s8⟧ These things are utterly perilous, and yet true. ⟦s9⟧ And though we fear to hear them, yet we cannot deny them.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *To what shall I compare thee,* etc. ⟦s2⟧ The sinful soul is rebuked, whose so great evils have been enumerated above, which, having played the harlot away from the love of God, made her incest the more shameful in proportion as the integrity that fell was the more precious. [0292D] To whom it is well said. ⟦s3⟧ Great as the sea is thy contrition. ⟦s4⟧ For what is understood by the sea, unless a depraved conscience, which both the memory of past crimes and the delight of present ones toss about by driving it hither and thither? ⟦s5⟧ For like certain waves coming from opposite quarters they dash against one another, when in a certain way the penitence for her past errors always draws the unhappy soul back, and the love of present ones drives her on. ⟦s6⟧ And because in this so great contradiction that sweetness of wretched delight, such as it is, is always infected with grief mingled in, rightly is a depraved conscience called a sea not only for its unquietness but also for its bitterness. ⟦s7⟧ There follows: Who shall heal thee? ⟦s8⟧ He shows it to be utterly great and difficult, yet not for God [0293A] impossible: that such a one should return to health. ⟦s9⟧ Hence it is that John says of him that sins unto death: *I do not say for him that anyone should pray [n: (I Joan. V)]*. ⟦s10⟧ As if he were to say: Just as I do not forbid desiring his salvation, so I do not presume to command praying for him, because the cure of so mortal a wound, although it does not exceed the power of God, nevertheless surpasses all human estimation; and therefore it is done more considerately if meanwhile, with prayer humbly suppressed, desire only be offered to God, because perhaps in a case so difficult a humble and fearful devotion will obtain more quickly than a presumptuous petition. ⟦s11⟧ He says therefore: Who shall heal thee? ⟦s12⟧ As if he should say: Perilous is thy wound, which cannot be relieved by human industry, and which on account of past guilt is not worthy to be healed by God. [0293B]

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *Thy prophets,* etc. ⟦s2⟧ LITERAL. ⟦s3⟧ It is said with reproach: Thy prophets. ⟦s4⟧ As if it were said: Behold to what great misery thou hast sunk by hearkening to false prophets, whom thou didst love, while thou wouldst not hear the prophets of God who spoke true things. ⟦s5⟧ *They have seen for thee.* ⟦s6⟧ For thee they saw, because they were thy prophets. ⟦s7⟧ Therefore their lie redounded upon thee, because their malice pleased thee. ⟦s8⟧ Thy prophets have seen for thee. ⟦s9⟧ What did they see for thee? ⟦s10⟧ *False and foolish things.* ⟦s11⟧ False, in which thou wast deceived. ⟦s12⟧ Foolish, in which thou canst not be excused. ⟦s13⟧ If they had seen only false things, in which there would at least have been some likeness of truth, thy simplicity could perhaps have been excused through ignorance; but now, because they saw foolish things also, and yet thou wast not afraid to believe, [0293C] thy folly is deservedly punished. ⟦s14⟧ But what false things and what foolish things did they see? ⟦s15⟧ *False takings-up,* behold the foolish; *false castings-out,* behold the false. ⟦s16⟧ When and to whom they willed they promised prosperity. ⟦s17⟧ When and to whom they willed, they threatened adversity. ⟦s18⟧ But both were false, because to those who were to be cast out they promised takings-up, and to those who were to be taken up, castings-out. ⟦s19⟧ When God was angry, they announced that he would take thee up to protect thee; when he was appeased, they threatened that he would cast thee out to afflict thee. ⟦s20⟧ But the threatening was false, and the promise foolish. ⟦s21⟧ In the promise there was not only falsity but also folly, because, even if thou didst fear the terrors, it is utterly alien from reason that thou, conscious of so great a crime, couldst be deceived by empty promises. [0293D] Behold, thou didst not know what was to come; yet thou wast not ignorant of thy evil desert, which was present. ⟦s22⟧ But they, by seeing false and foolish things, did not open to thee thy iniquity, because, while they promised prosperity to the perverse in their crime, and troubled with empty terrors those who walked in simplicity, they did not open, nay rather they veiled thy iniquity, lest thou shouldst acknowledge it. ⟦s23⟧ Or it can be distinguished otherwise. ⟦s24⟧ What he said, false and foolish, this pertains to the promises; but what follows, false takings-up and false castings-out, both are to be referred to the terrors. ⟦s25⟧ For he calls tribulation both a casting-out, in that the people was to be cast out by God, and a taking-up, after the likeness of a burden, which when taken up weighs down the bearer. ⟦s26⟧ For hence it is that where the blessed Jerome [0294A] translated the burden of Babylon, or of Tyre, or of Damascus, etc. ⟦s27⟧ Symmachus and Theodotion rendered *assumptio*. ⟦s28⟧ And rightly, for the aforesaid reason, he calls the terrors only false; but the promises he names not only false but also foolish, because, even if a man cannot always know what is to come, yet it is exceedingly foolish to be ignorant of oneself in the present. ⟦s29⟧ Whence immediately after he had said, they have seen for thee false and foolish things, he subjoins. ⟦s30⟧ *Nor did they open to thee thy iniquity.* ⟦s31⟧ For he who promises to a sinner the reward of the just, as it were hides his iniquity, lest it be seen.
