## Little Notes on Lamentations (cont.)

See, O Lord, and consider whom thou hast gathered thus as a vintage. MORAL. By a fitting likeness the soul is said to be gathered as a vintage by God when, its own evil deserts requiring it, it is despoiled of the gifts of the virtues by the just judgment of God. But there is a certain vintaging more destructive than the rest. For it is less perilous to lose virtue than to be proud of virtue. For he who loses virtue at least acknowledges his own loss; but he who is proud of virtue bears a loss and does not know it. The prophet, bewailing a soul not only sinful but also emptily glorying in a false virtue — See, he says, O Lord, [0315D] and consider whom thou hast gathered thus as a vintage. And as though asking, How thus? he subjoins with a cry and says: Shall the women then eat their own fruit? For the women eat their own fruit when carnal souls feed themselves through empty glory upon the work of virtue. They eat the fruit, because the virtue itself is consumed from that very thing wherein the mind is delighted with an unlawful refreshment. Their own fruit, he says. What manner of fruit? Little ones of a span's measure. By the little ones we may understand tender virtues, not yet advanced to a manly strength; but by the span, the beginning of a good work. Little ones of a span's measure, then, are tender virtues, newly brought forth at the beginning of good working. What is it then that the women are said to eat little ones of a [0316A] span's measure, but that carnal minds glory even in the beginning of a good work? There follows: If the priest and the prophet be slain in the sanctuary of the Lord? The mind of a just man, which is the sanctuary of God, ought to have two things in itself: namely prudence and devotion — prudence, whereby it may trample down impending evils by foreseeing them; devotion, whereby it may wipe away past evils by supplication. What then do we understand by devotion but a kind of priest of the soul continually offering the sacrifice of expiation in the sight of the Lord; and what by prudence but a prophet? Because therefore the priest and the prophet is slain in the sanctuary of the Lord, the women eat their own little ones: for never would the mind of man exalt itself over its own virtue, unless first, through negligence, both the memory of [sic: *praeteritotorum*] [0316B] evils and the wariness of things to come had grown lukewarm within it.

*The child and the old men [senex] lay on the ground of fury: my virgins and my young men are fallen by the sword.* LITERAL. Or it is to be distinguished thus: The child and the old man lay on the ground, the virgins and the young men fell by the sword. Or thus: The child and the old man and the virgins lay on the ground, and the young men fell by the sword. Which perhaps agrees better for this reason: because virgins are not wont to be slain by the sword in war, but to be led away for plunder and spoil — unless by this very thing the fierceness of the enemy be marked out, who spared not even virgins. He says, then, that the warrior people fell by the sword together with their kings and princes, and that the rest of the multitude, of either sex and every age, was dragged away into captivity. [0316C] The child, he says, and the old man, and the virgins — the weak age, the weak sex — lay on the ground, cast down without mercy, without humanity, on the ground of fury. Not only on the ground, which of itself might suffice for such a punishment, but on the ground of fury. Either in their own land, which the Lord in his fury permitted to be laid waste and depopulated; or in a foreign land, to which the fury of the Lord drove them out. There follows:

*Thou hast slain in the day of fury, thou hast struck, and thou hast no mercy [misertus es* ]. As if he should say: To thee, O Lord, do we ascribe our calamity; concerning our misery we enter into a [sic: *cansam*] with thee. For the blow which the fury of the raging enemy inflicted, thy wrath, provoked first by our sins, dictated. Thou hast slain those who fell by the sword; thou hast struck those who lay on the ground; and thou hast [0316D] no mercy, in that, when thou seest so many already to have perished, thou dost not turn away the scourge of thy indignation at least from those who yet survive. I beseech thee, O Lord, I entreat thee, O Lord, have mercy: that the destruction into which we fell while thou wast angry, we may escape while thou art merciful.

The child and the old men lay on the ground. ALLEGORICAL. As the people, so also the priests. They lay on the ground: they took their rest in earthly desires. And not only did they rest (which was signified above, when it was said: The old men sat on the ground, they held their peace), but they also slept: for they lay down, so that now they know not their own selves; and, all reason being lulled asleep, they consider not whence they came or whither they tend. To whom it is said by Paul: *Arise, thou that sleepest: and rise up from the dead, and [0317A] shall enlighten thee [sic: illuminabi te] Christ [n: (Ephes. V)]*. Alas! how ill do they lie who lie on the ground! how ill do they sleep who sleep on the ground! who take their rest on the ground, and when they awake find nothing of riches in their hands! For whatever is loved in time is like a dream, and vanishes away when a man has begun to awake in death. On the ground, he says, of fury they lay. What ought we to understand by the ground of fury but this world, in which the human race, for the punishment of the first guilt, was cast out from the happiness of paradise and condemned to exile? How great a madness, then, that a man should seek rest in this pilgrimage, into which he doubts not that he has been cast in the wrath of divine vengeance? There follows: My virgins and my young men are fallen by the sword. By the sword [0317B] in this place is signified the teaching of heretics, of whom it is said in the psalm: *Their tongue is a sword [sic: aculus] [n: (Psal. LVI)]*; because, while they drag some away from the unity of the catholic faith to their own errors, they cut off, as it were, members from the body. And by the virgins and the young men the faithful are signified, who are both virgins, because they corrupt the integrity of faith by depraved works; and are young men, because through constancy of mind they do not yield when adversities press upon them. What is it then that virgins are said to fall by the sword, but that — the Apostle bearing witness — *evil communications corrupt good manners [n: (I Cor. XV)]*, and the doctrines of heretics, in which they prevail to violate the chastity of faith, afterward overthrow also the integrity of good works? Young men fall by the sword when those whose constancy could not be [0317C] overcome by adversities, cut down by the guiles of words, rush headlong into unbelief. Or if we follow the other distinction, the virgins lie on the ground when those men gape in mind after earthly desires who outwardly, by the showing forth of their work, feign a chastity of intention. By the young men, too, we may understand the proud and those presuming upon their own strength, because the life of man flourishes chiefly at this age both in heat and in vigor. Young men therefore fall by the sword, because since thou, O God, dost despise the proud, it is plain of those who are overthrown by the errors of heretics that they would by no means fall away from the knowledge of the truth had they not first been high-minded concerning themselves. There follows: Thou hast slain in the day of fury; thou hast struck, and thou hast no mercy. He slew those who fell headlong through unbelief; [0317D] he struck those who in the fall of the perishing were wounded through the suffering of charity. He has no mercy, because while he does not raise up the fallen, neither does he console the grief of those who stand.

The child and the old men lay on the ground of fury. MORAL. The ground of fury is our mortal flesh, which before the sin of the first man brought forth the shoots of the virtues; but afterward, by the sentence of the curse, began to bring forth the thorns and thistles of the vices But the little child is the sense of the flesh, because, so far as in it lies, it always seeks after certain childish and empty things. And as by the little child we understand the sense of the flesh, so by the old men we may not unfittingly understand the judgment and the appetite of the mind; because the mind ought both to be mature in discerning and not rash in desiring. [0318A] The little child therefore lies with the old men on the ground of fury when that very portion of our corruption which the punishment of the transgression brought in, the carnal mind spreads beneath itself for its delight not only according to sense but also according to judgment and appetite, and thinks to take its rest there where it has been cast down to be beaten. There follows: My virgins and my young men are fallen by the sword. By the virgins we may not unfittingly understand chaste thoughts; by the young men, the fortitude and constancy of the soul; by the sword, the temptation of the adversary. For because the age of youth flourishes with fuller vigor, what else are the fortitude of doing well and the constancy of persevering but a kind of young men in the rational soul? For they are both prompt to begin a work, and persist without failing [0318B] in the work begun. Rightly therefore are the old men said first to have lain on the ground, and afterward the virgins and the young men are recorded to have fallen by the sword; because from the moment that the judgment and appetite of the mind is corrupted by earthly delight, a temptation coming upon it easily both violates the integrity of good thoughts and unstrings the fortitude and constancy of the soul. For because, as has often been said, thoughts proceed from affections, while thoughts are corrupted from corrupt ones, they draw bitterness as it were from an infected root. And when the carnal soul desires or discerns nothing outside the flesh, all its fortitude and constancy soon grows soft in the temptation of the flesh. Nor is this to be passed over, that when the old men sit on the ground, the virgins sprinkle their heads with ashes; when the old men are clothed [0318C] with haircloth, the virgins cast their heads down to the ground; when the old men lie on the ground, the virgins fall by the sword — because assuredly the corruption of thoughts grows according to the corruption of affections. There follows: Thou hast slain in the day of fury, thou hast struck, and thou hast no mercy. God sometimes permits certain virtues in the soul to be destroyed by temptation, and certain others to be wounded: so that while the tempted man sees that he has kept some good things still, he may not fall utterly into despair; and while he sees that he has lost certain things, he may not presume upon his own strength, and may the more truly recover what he has lost the more subtly, taught by his own loss, he now sees that the very thing he has kept is not of himself. But because many are permitted to be tempted not for their correction but for their overthrow, [0318D] therefore a man, who cannot always know the cause of his own temptation, ought always to fear peril; and on this account it is rightly subjoined in the voice of those who tremble: And thou hast no mercy. Which sentence assuredly is one of fear, not of assertion. For because he does not quickly deliver from temptation, the tempted man therefore thinks his scourge to be of wrath and not of mercy; which nevertheless belongs not to one despairing but to one fearing, as we have said.

*Thou hast called as to a solemn day.* LITERAL. As if he should say: Enemies indeed are always ready to do harm; but nevertheless, unless they are called, they cannot come. This man therefore does not accuse God but honors him, who attributes nothing to the strength of enemies, but extols the divine power even in his own oppression. Thou hast called, [0319A] he says. As if he were saying: Theirs it was to will, thine to grant; that from this very thing thou mayest consider that it is thine also to deliver. Thou hast called as to a solemn day, that is, to a day of gladness and joy: not that thy goodness was delighted in our misery, but because their malice exulted in this, so that this very thing may provoke thee to have mercy, and thou mayest more quickly have compassion on us, while thou beholdest them wickedly rejoicing over us.

*Those that should terrify me round about.* Those who should hem me in on every side, that I might not escape the hands of my pursuers, after the likeness of hunters who by shouting drive wild beasts into the nets. He marks that time when, as the Jews were fleeing the Babylonians, the neighboring nations on every side blocked against them all defense of flight, that they might not escape the hands of their enemies when Sedecias, fleeing with an army [0319B] of warriors, was taken in the plain of Jericho, and brought back to Reblata to the king of Babylon, and there, his sons having been slain before him, was himself blinded and carried away to Babylon. Whence there follows:

*And there was none in the day of the fury of the Lord that escaped and was left.* Was left — that is, understood, alive; or was left in his own land, and not carried away captive.

*Whom I brought up and nourished.* I brought up unto discipline, and I nourished unto strength: who are the more to be bewailed.
