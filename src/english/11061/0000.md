## CHAP. I.-- *Which writings, etc., as has already been set down above.*

⟦p19⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0009A]

⟦p20⟧ ⟦s1⟧ The reader of the divine Scriptures must first be instructed, so that he may know which writings are worthy to be honored, in a special sense, with the name of divinity. ⟦s2⟧ For certain men, speaking through the spirit of this world, have written many things. ⟦s3⟧ We read the songs of the poets, in which, together with delight, there is also some usefulness; as a certain one says: *Poets wish either to profit or to delight.*
⟦p21⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [f: *HORATIUS, Art. poet., 333.*]

⟦p22⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Logic, mathematics, and physics teach a certain truth, but they do not reach that truth in which the salvation of the soul consists, without which whatever is, is in vain. ⟦s2⟧ The philosophers of the gentiles also wrote on ethics, [0010A] in which they painted, as it were, certain limbs of the virtues lopped off from the body of goodness; but the limbs of the virtues cannot be alive without the body of the charity of God. ⟦s3⟧ All the virtues make one body, and the head of that body is charity. ⟦s4⟧ Nor can the limbs of the body live unless they are given sensation from the head. ⟦s5⟧ Those writings, therefore, in which truth is not perceived without the contagion of error, and by which the soul is not restored to the true knowledge or love of God, are by no means worthy to be reckoned by the name of divinity. ⟦s6⟧ But that Scripture alone is rightly called divine which has been breathed by the Spirit of God and, administered through those who spoke by the Spirit of God, makes man divine, reforming him to the likeness of God by instructing him unto the knowledge, and by exhorting him unto the love, [0011A] of him. ⟦s7⟧ In it, whatever is taught is truth; whatever is commanded, goodness; whatever is promised, happiness. ⟦s8⟧ For God is truth without deceit, goodness without malice, happiness without misery. ⟦s9⟧ If, then, you wish to distinguish divine Scripture by right consideration from the others, which do not deserve this name, consider diligently the very matter about which and in which its treatment is engaged, since acquaintance with things makes for the opening up of words. ⟦s10⟧ For you will the more easily understand what is said; if it has been well known whence it is said.

## CHAP. II.-- *That divine Scripture is distinguished from others in its matter and in its manner of treatment.*

⟦p23⟧ ⟦s1⟧ There are two works of God, by which all things that have been made are brought to completion. ⟦s2⟧ The first is the work of founding, [0011B] by which those things were made which were not; the second is the work of restoration, by which those things were repaired which had perished. ⟦s3⟧ The work of founding is the creation of the world with all its [sic: *clementis*]. ⟦s4⟧ The work of restoration is the incarnation of the Word with all its sacraments, whether those which went before the incarnation from the beginning of the age, or those which follow after up to the end of the world. ⟦s5⟧ The first works, therefore, were made for service, that they might be subject to man while he stood by righteousness. ⟦s6⟧ But the second were made for salvation, that they might raise up man as he lay by fault: therefore these are the greater. ⟦s7⟧ For this reason the former, as being a slight and scanty token of the divine power, were completed in a short time, that is, in six days only. ⟦s8⟧ But the latter, as excelling in comparison with the former and having a greater effect of power, [0011C] cannot be brought to completion save in six ages. ⟦s9⟧ In these, then, consider the matter of the divine Scriptures, so that both in that of which they treat and in that manner in which they treat it — that is, in matter and in manner — you may be able to distinguish them from other writings. ⟦s10⟧ For the matter of all other writings lies in the works of founding; the matter of the divine Scriptures consists in the works of restoration. ⟦s11⟧ This, then, is the first distinction, in respect of that of which they treat. ⟦s12⟧ Again, if other writings teach any truth, it is not without the contagion of error; if they seem to commend any goodness, either it is mixed with malice, so that it is not pure, or it is without the knowledge and love of God, so that it is not perfect. ⟦s13⟧ Therefore, just as that in them which is thought to be called divine casts the reader's mind headlong down to earthly things [0011D] through the falsehood joined to it, so also that which in Holy Scripture seems to be earthly lifts him up, through the true acknowledgment of the Creator which is commended in all these things, to think upon and to love things divine and heavenly.

## CHAP. III.-- *On the threefold understanding of Holy Scripture.*

⟦p24⟧ ⟦s1⟧ The sacred utterance is expounded according to a threefold understanding. ⟦s2⟧ The first exposition is the historical, in which is considered the first signification of the words with respect to the things themselves of which the account treats. ⟦s3⟧ For the sacred utterance has [0012A] a certain property differing from other writings, in that in it first, through the words which are recited, certain things are treated of, which things again are set forth in place of words for the signification of other things. ⟦s4⟧ History is named from the Greek verb ἰςορέω, historeo, which is "I see" and "I narrate." ⟦s5⟧ This is because among the ancients no one was permitted to write deeds done except such as he had himself seen, lest falsehood be mingled with truth by the fault of a writer saying more, or less, or otherwise. ⟦s6⟧ According to this, history is spoken of properly and strictly; but it is wont to be taken more broadly, so that that sense is called history which is had in the first place from the signification of the words with respect to things. ⟦s7⟧ The second exposition is the allegorical. ⟦s8⟧ Now allegory is when, through that which is set forth as signified by the letter, some other thing done, whether in the past [0012B] or in the present or in the future, is signified. ⟦s9⟧ Allegory is so called as it were "other-speech," because one thing is said and another signified; and it is subdivided into simple allegory and anagogy. ⟦s10⟧ And simple allegory is when, through a visible deed, another invisible deed is signified. ⟦s11⟧ Anagogy, that is, a leading upward, is when through the visible an invisible deed is declared. ⟦s12⟧ Let one example of this threefold understanding be set down. ⟦s13⟧ There was a man in the land of Hus, by name Job, who, formerly rich, came to such misery that, sitting on the dunghill, he even scraped the discharge of his body with a potsherd. ⟦s14⟧ The sense of the history is plain. ⟦s15⟧ Let us come to the allegory, that we may consider how, through the things signified by the words, other things are signified, and through one deed another deed. ⟦s16⟧ Job, therefore, whose name is interpreted *grieving,* signifies Christ, [0012C] who, being before in the riches of the Father's glory coequal with him, condescended to our misery and sat humbled on the dunghill of this world, sharing in all our defects except sin. ⟦s17⟧ Let us also inquire what thing to be done — that is, what is worthy to be done — is signified by this deed. ⟦s18⟧ Job can signify any righteous man, or the penitent soul, which composes in its memory a dunghill out of all the sins it has committed, and, not for an hour but perseveringly, sitting over this and meditating, does not cease to weep. ⟦s19⟧ And these deeds according to the letter, which represent spiritual things of this kind, are called sacraments.

## CHAP. IV.-- *That not all things found in the divine utterance, but only certain ones, are to be forced to the said threefold interpretation.*

⟦p25⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0012D]

⟦p26⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Assuredly not all things that are found in the divine utterance are to be twisted to this threefold interpretation, so that individual passages should be believed to contain history, allegory, and tropology all together. ⟦s2⟧ And even if in many places this can be fittingly assigned; yet to observe it everywhere is either difficult or impossible. ⟦s3⟧ For just as in the lyre and in musical instruments of this kind not indeed all the things that are touched sound out anything tuneful, [0013A] but only the strings — the rest, however, in the whole body of the lyre were made for this, that there might be a place where those things should be fastened and something to which they should be stretched which the craftsman is about to tune for the sweetness of the song — so in the divine utterances certain things are set down which are meant to be understood only spiritually; certain others serve the seriousness of morals; certain others again are said according to the simple sense of the history; and some indeed there are which can be fittingly expounded according to history and allegory and tropology.
