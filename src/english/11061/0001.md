## CHAP. V.-- *That the literal and historical interpretation is necessary*.

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ Since, then, the mystical understanding is gathered only from those things which the letter sets forth in the first place; I wonder with what face certain men boast themselves teachers of allegories, [0013B] who are still ignorant of that very first signification of the letter. "We," they say, "read the Scripture, but we do not read the letter. ⟦s3⟧ We do not care about the letter; but we teach allegory." ⟦s4⟧ How, then, do you read the Scripture and not read the letter? ⟦s5+6⟧ For if the letter is taken away, what is Scripture? "We," they say, "do read the letter, but not according to the letter. ⟦s7⟧ For we read allegory, and we expound the letter not according to the letter, but according to allegory." ⟦s8+9⟧ What, then, is it to expound the letter, except to show that which the letter signifies? "But the letter," they say, "signifies one thing according to history, another according to allegory. ⟦s10⟧ For a lion according to history signifies a beast, according to allegory it signifies Christ: therefore that word, lion, signifies Christ." ⟦s11⟧ I therefore ask [0013C] you who approve this, why does a lion signify Christ? ⟦s12⟧ You answer perhaps, in the way in which answer is usually made in matters of this kind, on the ground of the [sic: *convenentia*] of the likeness set forth for the signification: because a lion sleeps with open eyes, or something else of the sort: therefore a lion signifies Christ, because he sleeps with open eyes. ⟦s13⟧ For so did you say, that lion, that word, signifies Christ, because he sleeps with open eyes. ⟦s14⟧ Either, then, take away the statement which you set forth, or change the reason which you subjoined. ⟦s15⟧ For either the statement is false by which you said that that word, lion, signifies Christ, or the reason which you subjoined is unfitting, that a lion signifies Christ for this cause, that he sleeps with open eyes. ⟦s16⟧ For it is not the word that sleeps with open eyes, but the animal itself [0013D] which the word signifies. ⟦s17⟧ Understand, therefore, that when he says that a lion signifies Christ, it is not the name of the animal, but the animal itself, that is signified. ⟦s18⟧ For this is what, as is said, sleeps with open eyes, according to which by some likeness it figures him who in the sleep of the death he took upon himself slept in his humanity, but kept his eyes open, watching in his divinity. ⟦s19⟧ Do not, therefore, glory in the understanding of the Scriptures so long as you are ignorant of the letter. ⟦s20⟧ But to be ignorant of the letter is to be ignorant of what the letter signifies, and of what is signified by the letter. ⟦s21⟧ For that which is signified by the first signifies a third. ⟦s22⟧ Since, then, those things which the letter signifies are signs of the spiritual understanding, [0014A] how can those things be signs to you which have not yet been signified to you? ⟦s23⟧ Do not, then, make a leap, lest you fall headlong. ⟦s24⟧ He walks most rightly who walks in order. ⟦s25+26⟧ First, therefore, be zealous by reading to acquire an acquaintance with those things which the sacred utterance sets before you, with a view to their mystical signification, so that, when these are known in their outward appearance, you may afterwards by meditation gather what you may bring forward by way of likeness either for the building up of faith or for the instruction of good morals. "But not all things," they say, "can be read according to the letter, or fittingly understood. ⟦s27⟧ For since the prophet says that he saw a fiery river going forth from beneath the throne of God, and testifies to certain winged and many-eyed living creatures flying about round about and crying out [n: (Dan. VII; Ezech. I)], and many [0014B] like things in this manner, there is no doubt that of those things which the sacred utterance narrates some are fittingly taken according to the letter, while others are understood as said only by figure." ⟦s28⟧ Thus, then, do they speak, as though we supposed that all things which are said through the letter are to be taken altogether so, and that nothing else is to be understood from those things which are said; for that which a thing is is not said through the letter, but is signified through that which the letter says. ⟦s29⟧ For even in that which is taken as said figuratively, it is not denied that the letter has its own signification, because, when we assert that what is said is not to be understood as it is said, we affirm that that very thing has in some manner been said. ⟦s30⟧ Something, therefore, is said and signified by the letter even then, when that which is said is not so [sic: *nonita*] understood as it is said, but [0014C] something else which is signified by what is thereby said. ⟦s31⟧ Thus, therefore, something is altogether said and signified by the letter, and that first thing which is signified by the letter must be understood, so that what is signified through it may afterwards be understood. ⟦s32⟧ In this manner we wish the reader to be admonished, lest perchance he despise these first rudiments of the teaching. ⟦s33⟧ Nor let him think that acquaintance with those things is to be despised which Holy Scripture sets before us through the first signification of the letter, since these are the very things which the Holy Spirit has painted for carnal senses — senses able to grasp invisible things only through visible ones — as it were certain images of mystical meanings, and, through the likenesses set forth, has figured a clear demonstration of those things which are to be understood spiritually. [0014D] But if, as these men say, one had to leap at once from the letter to that which is to be understood spiritually, then in vain would the figures and likenesses of things, by which the mind might be trained for spiritual things, have been interposed in the sacred utterance by the Holy Spirit. ⟦s34⟧ For, the Apostle being witness, *that which is carnal is first, then that which is spiritual [n: (I Cor. XV)]*. ⟦s35⟧ And the very wisdom of God, unless it had first been known bodily, could never have enlightened the vision of the bleary mind to contemplate it spiritually. ⟦s36⟧ Do not, therefore, despise the lowliness in the word of God, because through lowliness, you are enlightened unto divinity. ⟦s37⟧ All this [0015A] which the word of God has on the outside seems to you as mud, and therefore perhaps you tread it under foot, because it is mud, and you despise what the letter narrates as done bodily and visibly. ⟦s38⟧ But hear: by that mud which is trodden under your feet the eye of the blind man is enlightened to see [n: (Joan. IX)] Read, therefore, the Scripture, and learn first diligently the things which it narrates bodily. ⟦s39⟧ For if you have zealously impressed upon your mind the form of these things according to the order of the narrative set forth, then afterwards, by meditating, you will drive away, as from a certain honeycomb, the sweetness of spiritual understanding.

## CHAP. VI.-- *On the order, number, and authority of the books of Holy Scripture.*

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ All divine Scripture is contained in two Testaments, namely the Old and the New. ⟦s2⟧ Each Testament is divided into three orders: the Old [0015B] Testament contains the law, the prophets, the hagiographa. ⟦s3⟧ The New, the Gospel, the apostles, the fathers. ⟦s4⟧ The first order of the Old Testament, that is, the law, which the Hebrews name *thorath*, has the Pentateuch, that is, the five books of Moses. ⟦s5⟧ In this order the first is Beresith, which is Genesis. ⟦s6⟧ The second Hellesmoth, which is Exodus. ⟦s7⟧ The third Vagethra, which is Leviticus. ⟦s8⟧ The fourth Vagedaber, which is Numbers. ⟦s9⟧ The fifth Elleaddaberim, which is Deuteronomy. ⟦s10⟧ The second order is that of the prophets; this contains eight volumes. ⟦s11⟧ The first is Bennum, that is, the son of Nun, who is also called both Josue and Jesus, and Jesus Nave. ⟦s12⟧ The second is Sothim, which is the book of Judges. ⟦s13⟧ The third is Samuel, which is the first and second of Kings. ⟦s14⟧ The fourth Malachim, which is the third and fourth of Kings. ⟦s15⟧ The fifth [0015C] is Isaiah. ⟦s16⟧ The sixth Jeremiah, The seventh Ezekiel. ⟦s17⟧ The eighth Thereasra which is that of the twelve prophets. ⟦s18⟧ Then the third order has nine books. ⟦s19⟧ The first is Job. ⟦s20⟧ The second David. ⟦s21⟧ The third Masloth, which in Greek sounds Parabolae, in Latin Proverbia, namely of Solomon. ⟦s22⟧ The fourth Coeleth, which is Ecclesiastes. ⟦s23⟧ The fifth Sirasirim, that is, the Song of Songs. ⟦s24⟧ The sixth Daniel. ⟦s25⟧ The seventh Dabreiamin, which is Paralipomenon. ⟦s26⟧ The eighth Esdras. ⟦s27⟧ The ninth Esther. ⟦s28⟧ All, then, come to twenty-two in number. ⟦s29⟧ There are besides certain other books, such as the Wisdom of Solomon, the book of Jesus son of Sirach, and the book of Judith, and Tobias, and the books of the Maccabees, which are indeed read, but are not written in the canon. ⟦s30⟧ To these twenty-two books of the Old Testament [0015D] eight books of the New Testament are joined. ⟦s31⟧ In the first order of the New Testament there are four Gospels: of Matthew, of Mark, of Luke, and of John. ⟦s32⟧ In the second there are likewise four: namely the Acts of the apostles, the Epistles of Paul, fourteen in number, woven together under one volume. ⟦s33⟧ The Canonical Epistles, the Apocalypse. ⟦s34⟧ In the third order the first place is held by the decretals, which we call canonical, that is, regular. ⟦s35⟧ Then the writings of the holy Fathers, that is, of Jerome, Augustine, Ambrose, Gregory, Isidore, Origen, Bede, and [0016A] of other doctors, which are beyond number. ⟦s36⟧ These writings of the Fathers, however, are not reckoned in the text of the divine Scriptures, just as in the Old Testament, as we have said, there are certain books which are not written in the canon, and yet are read, such as the Wisdom of Solomon and the rest. ⟦s37⟧ The text, therefore, of the divine Scriptures, as it were the whole body, is contained principally in thirty books. ⟦s38⟧ Of these, twenty-two are comprised in the Old, and eight in the New Testament (as has been shown above). ⟦s39⟧ But the other writings are as it were adjuncts, flowing from these that precede. ⟦s40⟧ Now in these orders, especially of both Testaments, a correspondence appears: because just as after the law the prophets, and after the prophets the hagiographers, so after the Gospel the apostles, and after the apostles the doctors [0016B] followed in order. ⟦s41⟧ And it was brought about by a certain wondrous plan of the divine dispensation that, although in each of the Scriptures the full and perfect truth stands complete, yet none of them is superfluous.
