## HERE BEGINS THE EXPLANATION. (cont.)

Of these four fears two are evil, that is, servile and worldly; but two are good, that is, initial and filial. Servile fear fears the punishment which is inflicted by men, and therefore it suffices it to cease from evil deed: because it serves to the eye, and does not fear the guilt of conscience, seeing itself please men. But worldly fear, not seeking to please men, yet fearing to displease, itself also feigns what it is not, [0428B] as lying in denying the truth as the other is deceitful in covering falsehood, and each offends against the truth. The one, because it timidly denies what is; the other, because it perversely simulates what is not. But initial fear, because it strives to avoid the punishment which God threatens, in no way sees it to suffice for itself to restrain itself from unlawful action; because for him who beholds the heart it is not enough for proof if the action be innocent, unless also the very thought of the heart appear before his eyes sincere and unpolluted. Because, therefore, it fears to displease him who sees the whole, let it consider that for perfect innocence before him it is necessary that it cleanse the whole; and therefore this fear is called initial, because under it, through good will, both virtue takes its beginning and vice its end, yet it is not yet perfection: [0428C] because, while one thing is done and another intended, the good itself is not yet loved for its own sake. Then charity draws near, and enters through this fear, which, while it shows the danger we ought to flee, in a certain way makes us seek and desire a protection. It turns the heart, therefore, to God, so that in a certain way it flees from him to him: that is, while guarding against having him angry, it is eager to have him propitious. This is followed by filial fear, which is born of the charity that succeeds; so that this fearing is nothing other than to be now unwilling to lose the good tasted in charity. And this fear indeed has something of punishment joined to it, while we walk in uncertainty, and the state of a changeable life can still incline to either side. But when there shall be no mutability [0428D], then there shall be present no punishment of suspicion arising from uncertainty: and then fear will in a certain way be without fear, when we shall be both certain of our stability, and yet shall not cease to show reverence to the Creator. Therefore his mercy is from generation unto generations, to them that fear him. And not only to them that fear with perfect charity, but also to them that begin to fear, and that turn themselves through beginning wisdom, his mercy is from generation unto generations, to them that fear him. And then she begins to explain more fully that mercy which is afforded to them that fear God, and to weave together, in a clear narration, the very order and manner of human redemption, saying:

*He hath showed might in his arm; he hath scattered the proud in the mind of his heart.* This is that mercy which God showed to them that [0429A] fear him; because he sent his Word through assumed flesh into this world: that through him he might war down the aerial powers with mighty strength, and redeem the human race from their power. For these are the proud whom he scattered, casting them out from the hearts of men, and plundering their spoils. For the power by which they previously held rule in men, he dissipated. He hath showed, she says, might in his arm; because through the humility of his Son he conquered the devil. Therefore he showed might in his arm. His arm is his Son. He showed might in his arm; because through that which was made in him was redeemed that which was made by him. He showed might, he showed weakness, and that very weakness was might; because through it the devil was conquered, and man snatched from his power. He hath showed [0429B] might in his arm; he hath scattered the proud in the mind of his heart. What is, in the mind of his heart? In the mind of his heart he scattered them, by his profound counsel he scattered them. Profound was the counsel, that for man's sake God should become man, and the innocent one suffer, that the guilty might be redeemed: and in all these things the counsel was profound, nor could the devil foresee them. But Leviathan was caught with a hook, and the prudence of God struck the proud one. *Of the mystery,* says the Apostle, *kept silent through eternal times, known to God alone [n: (Rom. XVI)]*, *which none of the princes of this world knew;* for, *if they had known it, they would never have crucified the Lord of glory [n: (I Cor. II)]*. This is, in the mind of his heart: this God was turning over in his heart — nay, he was not turning it over, but holding it — and the devil knew it not. The foolish princes of Tanis, the wise counselors [0429C] of Pharaoh gave foolish counsel: *Where are now thy wise men? Let them tell thee, and let them declare what the Lord of hosts hath purposed upon Egypt. The princes of Tanis are become fools, the princes of Memphis are withered away: they have deceived Egypt, the corner of her peoples. The Lord hath mingled in the midst of her a spirit of giddiness [n: (Isa. XIX)]*. And there: He hath scattered the proud in the mind of his heart; and he hath showed might in his arm; because through the incarnation of his Son he both powerfully vanquished the demons, and prudently supplanted them. He hath showed might in his arm: and he hath scattered the proud in the mind of his heart. We can also, not unfittingly, understand by the proud the Jews, who gloried that they were of the race of Abraham, and presumed upon their own justice, and therefore were not subject to the justice of God. These [0429D] proud ones, therefore, God scattered in the mind of his heart, might having been showed in his arm; because through his Son coming in the flesh he taught that no one could be justified by the works of the law, but through the faith which is from God. But the Jews, who attempted to defend the works of the law against the justice of God, and proudly despised the humble coming of Christ, he cast off from his grace, in which they seemed to stand; and he took up the Gentiles, who humbly confessing their sins and preferring the justice of God. Whence there is fittingly added:

*He hath put down the mighty from their seat, and hath exalted the humble.* For he put down the mighty from their seat; because he cast off the Jews, who seemed to be the sons of the kingdom, and exalted the humble, namely the Gentile men; because the Gentiles, who had been cast off [0430A], he took up, through humble confession, into fellowship of the kingdom and into the adoption of sons. Or according to the earlier exposition, he put down the mighty from their seat; because he cast the malign spirits out of the hearts of men, and exalted the humble — namely those very men whom he had previously cast off on account of pride, restoring them once humbled. Therefore he hath showed might in his arm, he hath scattered the proud in the mind of his heart; he hath put down the mighty from their seat, and hath exalted the humble. But I see there is yet something which we may add to the things said concerning the scattering of the proud. For as to what she says: In the mind of his heart — if we expound it according to the former meaning — it is clear that it was done by the deep and unsearchable counsel of God, that the Jews who were first chosen should afterward be reprobated, and the Gentiles who previously [0430B] were reprobate should afterward be taken up. This was done by so profound and inscrutable a counsel, that God shut up all things under sin, and had mercy upon all. And therefore the Apostle, astonished in the consideration of this profundity, exclaimed: *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God! how incomprehensible are his judgments, and his ways unsearchable! [n: (Rom. XI.)]* This, therefore, we can consider in that which was said: In the mind of his heart.

And if we should wish to apply yet another exposition, the meaning will be not to be despised. For the mind of the heart of God is that living and abiding disposition of inward and hidden predestination. It is the book of life, in which are written the names of those who are saved and written unto life in Jerusalem. The same, therefore [0430C], is mind as is book, and, as it were, that is written in the book as destined to remain, which, retained in the mind through memory, is not deleted. That which, therefore, was said in this place: He hath scattered the proud in the mind of his heart — this same thing the Psalmist expressed in other words, saying: *Let them be blotted out of the book of the living [n: (Psal. LXVIII)]*. But because the foreknowledge of the divine predestination is not changed, and to be blotted out or scattered from it is not to cease to be there, but never to have been, he rightly added, saying: *And let them not be written with the just [n: (Ibid.)]*. It is to be known that in three ways someone is said to be written in the book of life: according to foreknowledge, according to cause, and according to operation. According to foreknowledge, those are written in the book of life who are predestined to life, who are never blotted out [0430D], because of those who were foreseen unto salvation, no one will be allowed to perish, although for a time he be permitted to err from the way of truth as though about to perish. According to cause, those are written in the book of life who for a time walk in justice: and such there sometimes are, who would prove worthy of salvation, if they remained such unto the end. But these are blotted out, when they abandon the justice they had begun, and, by declining to errors, withdraw from the way of truth by which they had begun to walk. According to operation, or rather according to human estimation, those are said to be written in the book of life whose works, according to human judgment, appear such that on their account they seem worthy to be written in the book of life: who again, when they abandon those good things which they seemed to do, are judged as though to be blotted out of the book of life. Those, therefore [0431A], who are so blotted out of the book of life that they do not again merit to be inscribed in it, even if for a time they seemed to be written either according to the cause of justice, or according to the judgment of human estimation, nevertheless were never written according to foreknowledge. Because, therefore, the rational creature, in angels and in men, had been inscribed in the book of life by its condition unto the title of the heavenly homeland and the supernal inheritance, but through the elation by which, in both, it perversely wished to compare itself to its Creator, was cast off and deprived of its honor: even then indeed he scattered the proud from the mind of his heart, when, casting out from inward stability those who swelled up around themselves, he permitted them to toss about outwardly through earthly desires. He hath scattered the proud in the mind of his heart: He hath put down the mighty from their seat, and hath exalted the humble. [0431B] First he cast the angel from heaven, and man, growing proud, from paradise; and afterward he restored man, humbled through penitence, to his pristine glory. But daily too he puts down and humbles all the proud, by withdrawing his grace, and afterward exalts those same humbled ones, restoring their former grace. To which also pertains that which follows:

[0432A] *He hath filled the hungry with good things, and the rich he hath sent empty away.* He calls hungry those who recognize that they lack the true good; by the rich he wishes to be understood those who are proud and elated, and think themselves to abound above others in the gifts of graces. Therefore, just as the humble, by thinking little of themselves, merit to receive greater grace, so the proud and elated, by presuming upon themselves, lose even those things which they have received.

*He hath received Israel his child.* He received him as a physician receives the sick, Israel his child, namely his people: Israel the child, that is, the humble and innocent one, he received, that he might heal the sick, redeem the captive, justify the ungodly, save the just. He received Israel, whom he did not find Israel, but that he might make him Israel. He hath received Israel his child.

[0432B] *Being mindful of his mercy.* That which he had once promised, but had long deferred, he at last was manifesting.

*As he spoke to our fathers, to Abraham and to his seed for ever.* Merciful in promising, truthful in fulfilling, because he promised without being in debt, and fulfilled without guile. As he spoke to our fathers, to Abraham and to his seed for ever.
