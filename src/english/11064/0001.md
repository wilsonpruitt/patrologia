## CHAPTER ONE *On the difference between worldly and divine theology, and on the demonstrations of each.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0923B] *The Jews seek signs, and the Greeks wisdom [n: (I Cor. I)]*. ⟦s2⟧ For there was a certain wisdom, which seemed wisdom to those who did not know the true wisdom. ⟦s3⟧ And the world found that wisdom, and began to be puffed up, and swelled, esteeming itself great in it. ⟦s4⟧ And it presumed, and resolved to press on further to the highest wisdom, trusting in its own wisdom, as though this could have been a way to that. ⟦s5⟧ And it climbed, and was lifted up, that it might reach the heights in heart. ⟦s6⟧ And it made itself a ladder, the outward form of the creature, striving toward the invisible things of the Creator. ⟦s7⟧ Then those things of God which [0924B] were manifest came forth to give light; and they were made known so as to be tested by a heart that was not pure. ⟦s8⟧ For the things that were seen were known, and there were others that were not known; and through the things that are manifest they thought to pass into those that were hidden, and they fell in mind beyond the truth that was possible into the lies of their own fabrications, where there was found nothing further that they might grasp. ⟦s9⟧ Therefore God makes foolish the wisdom of this world, because in it the wisdom of God could not be found; and he showed [0925A] another wisdom, which seemed foolishness and was not, that the true wisdom might be found through it. ⟦s10⟧ Christ crucified was preached, that truth might be sought through humility. ⟦s11⟧ But the world despised the physician [modicum], and could not recognize the truth. ⟦s12⟧ For it wished to contemplate the works of God which he had made to be wondered at, yet the things he had set forth to be imitated it would not venerate. ⟦s13⟧ For it did not attend to its own disease, so as to seek the medicine with devout devotion; but presuming upon a false health, it gave itself over to investigating things not its own with empty curiosity. ⟦s14⟧ And it seemed to make progress outside itself, but it failed within itself, and did not find him who was above it. ⟦s15⟧ And it set forth opinions about him which it had conceived with a proud heart, lest it should seem to be ignorant of the truth; and it followed, in the way of error, one thing after another, very many against the truth, and at the last things unseemly [0925B] to the Godhead and misshapen to his surpassing majesty, so that the error became manifest. ⟦s16⟧ For at first it was true, and seemed great, and at the last falsehood followed, when the truth ought to have been brought to completion. ⟦s17⟧ And there was shown, as it were, a certain light in one part, so that there something might be seen; and over against it were dense shadows and the deep gloom of ignorance, where the snare of error was set for the taking of the proud. ⟦s18⟧ And they saw where the light was, and where the shadows were they could not see; and there they were caught in the snare because of the boldness of their presumption, by which they cast themselves headlong to go into it: these things are manifest. ⟦s19⟧ For how great are the monuments of surpassing genius that they left, wherein with so much investigation they pursued the secrets of nature and the hidden things of created things, so that we judge their very efficacy [0925C] to be preferred to every study. ⟦s20⟧ We read of the arts, and the studies, and the disciplines, and very many precepts of the reasonings, which they, by the sense and genius given to them, searched out for this very purpose, and found; and they wrote down what they had found, and handed it down to posterity to be read — logic, and ethics, and mathematics, and physics — concerning the form of reasonings, and of life, and of morals befitting the ordinance of nature, concerning the disposition and order, and the causes, and the outcomes of all things. ⟦s21⟧ And they grew strong in this part, so as to grasp the truth; since this truth too was to be dispensed through them — a truth that was not unto life, since they were not the sons of life. ⟦s22⟧ Therefore it was given to them for our sake — for whom the consummation was being reserved and the beginning prepared — that they might find that truth [0925D] which the sons of life had to take up for the service of the highest truth. ⟦s23⟧ The labor of it was laid upon them, the fruit reserved for these others. ⟦s24⟧ And in all these things they received the light of understanding, and the keenness of genius, and the power of sense for the foregoing lessons; in which they subtly examined the force of creatures and the measure of the lower nature according to the form of the reason implanted in them. ⟦s25⟧ But at the last they devoted themselves to theology, as the account of divine things and the searching out of the invisible, as though to bring wisdom to completion — as they themselves supposed, about to bring it to completion; but in truth about to lose it, and not to find the true wisdom. ⟦s26⟧ For there they began to fall into the lies of fabrications, and they took visible forms as the images of divine things, so as to see the invisible through the things that were seen; and there was there something similar, but showing from [0926A] afar what was sought, and not bringing light to eyes that were darkened. ⟦s27⟧ For nature, established for service, showed forth its Creator; but it was a likeness foreign to his surpassing and sovereign majesty. ⟦s28⟧ Nor could that nature, which had to be taught, find an evident declaration in all these things, since it too was not sound, so as to grow much clear unto contemplation. ⟦s29⟧ For it did not have those exemplars which through grace were being formed for the health of the inner vision; nor did it know the ark of wisdom and of the treasures laid up — the flesh of the eternal Word in the humanity of Jesus; but using the natural lesson alone, with bleary sight, it contemplated a clouded and ambiguous light, bringing it to the beholder in the outward form of created things. ⟦s30⟧ Therefore they went astray and became vain, [0926B] when they wished to pass in mind beyond the things which alone they had received to know; and groping with their estimations toward the things that could not be seen, they were found blind who thought that they saw. ⟦s31⟧ These are the images of errors, which their theology (for so they themselves called the study by which they believed they were searching out divine things) proclaims as things to be venerated — images of their vanity and deception; in which they lie in so many things so far from the true, and the right, and what accords with good nature, that they too are compelled to blush over them. ⟦s32⟧ For it was fitting that those who were proud over the knowledge of the lowest things should be confounded in the highest, and that those who despise the humility of faith in the death of the Savior should marvel at his loftiness in the knowledge of the Creator. ⟦s33⟧ For two images were set before man, in which he might have been able to see the invisible: [0926C] one of nature, and one of grace. ⟦s34⟧ The image of nature was the outward form of this world. ⟦s35⟧ But the image of grace was the humanity of the Word. ⟦s36⟧ And in each God was shown, but not in each was he understood; for nature indeed by its outward form showed forth the Maker, but could not illumine the eyes of the beholder. ⟦s37⟧ But the humanity of the Savior was both a medicine, that the blind might receive light, and likewise a teaching, that those who see might recognize the truth. ⟦s38⟧ He made clay from spittle, and smeared the eyes of the blind man, and he washed and saw [n: (Joan. IX)]. ⟦s39⟧ And what afterward? ⟦s40⟧ Then to the man who saw and did not yet know, he says: I am he; and he who speaks with thee, he it is, the Son of God [n: (ibid.)]. ⟦s41⟧ First, therefore, he gave light; afterward he showed. ⟦s42⟧ For nature could show, but could not [0926D] give light. ⟦s43⟧ And the world proclaimed its Creator by outward form, but did not pour the understanding of truth into the hearts of men. ⟦s44⟧ Through the images of nature, therefore, the Creator was only signified; but in the images of grace God was shown present, because those things he wrought that he might be understood to be; but these he wrought that he might be acknowledged to be present. ⟦s45⟧ This is the difference of the theology of this world from that which is called divine theology. ⟦s46⟧ For it is impossible that the invisible be shown except through the visible; and therefore every theology must needs use visible demonstrations in the declaration of the invisible. ⟦s47⟧ But worldly theology, as we have said, took up the works of creation, and the elements of this world created according to their outward form, that it might make its demonstration in [0927A] them. ⟦s48⟧ But divine theology chose the works of restoration according to the humanity of Jesus, and his sacraments which are from the beginning — with natural things too subjoined in due measure — that in them it might shape its instruction. ⟦s49⟧ But a greater declaration of the divinity, as we have said, is shown in the sacraments of grace, and in the flesh of the Word, and in his mystical working, than is proclaimed by the natural outward form of things. ⟦s50⟧ And therefore worldly theology, using a demonstration of little evidence, had not the power to draw forth the incomprehensible truth without the contagion of error, whereas divine theology is known to proclaim it with a simple and pure assertion.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Now it must be said what theology is — beginning indeed somewhat higher, for the clarity of the things to be said. ⟦s2⟧ All philosophy is divided into three principal parts: [0927B] logic, ethics, and the theoretical. ⟦s3⟧ For the fourth, which we have added in its own place, to enumerate here would be superfluous. ⟦s4⟧ Philosophy, then, contains three parts: the first, that is, logic, which takes up the force and manner of reasonings, the judgment of the true and the false; the second, that is, ethics, which orders the right manner of living and the form of discipline according to the ordinances of the virtues; the third, that is, the theoretical, which alone has chosen to search out the true in all that is and is not. ⟦s5⟧ Of this, that is, of the theoretical, there are three parts: the first, mathematics; the second, physics; the third, theology — in which the contemplation of truth ascends to the summit as though by certain steps of contemplation. ⟦s6⟧ For the first, that is, mathematics, beholds the visible forms of visible things. [0928A] But the second, that is, physics, searches out the invisible causes of visible things. ⟦s7⟧ But the third alone, that is, theology, contemplates invisible substances, and the invisible natures of invisible substances. ⟦s8⟧ And there is in these, as it were, a certain progression, and an advance of the mind ascending to know the truth. ⟦s9⟧ For through the visible forms of visible things one comes to the invisible causes of visible things; and through the invisible causes of visible things one ascends to invisible substances, and to the knowing of their natures. ⟦s10⟧ And here is the summit of philosophy, and the perfection of truth, than which nothing can be higher for the contemplating mind. ⟦s11⟧ In this the wise men of this world were therefore, as we have already said, made foolish; because, walking by the natural lesson alone according to the elements and the outward form of the world, they did not have the exemplars [0928B] of grace, in which, although the outward form was humble, yet a more manifest demonstration of truth was afforded. ⟦s12⟧ Here, therefore, God made foolish the wisdom of this world [n: (I Cor. I)]; because it could not recognize the truth; because in its own learning it scorned to hold the form of humility. ⟦s13⟧ Let it now suffice to have said these things about theology, for the sake of the hierarchy of Dionysius, upon which, for the sake of explanation, we have undertaken to say some things. ⟦s14⟧ For every hierarchy is subordinated to theology; and it was necessary, for those being introduced to the reading of the hierarchy, to set down beforehand some things about theology, in order to define its subject-matter, which consists wholly in invisible substances, and in their natures likewise invisible — using a visible lesson for the demonstration of itself.
