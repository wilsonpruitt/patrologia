## CHAPTER IV. *Why Dionysius the Areopagite undertook to treat of theology, after he had received the catholic faith.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0929]

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0929D] The theologian Dionysius, seeing the wisdom of the world puffed up against the humility of the Christian faith — and seeing the cross of Christ, and the works of the humanity of the Word which in the flesh were wrought as a remedy, despised as though unworthy of God, and incongruous with the highest majesty, and impossible to the truth; and the Word of preaching, unto the redemption of the human race, esteemed foolish; and those walking according to the elements of this world, out of the lesson of nature, judging false the things that were accomplished for the salvation of man in the death of the Redeemer — sets himself against it, to empty out the glory which is according to the sense of the wisdom of this age. ⟦s2⟧ And he shows that the wisdom of the Christian faith, humble indeed in the death of the Redeemer, is yet [0930D] sublime in the knowledge of the Creator; and that the sacraments of redemption, which seemed despised according to the outward form of this world and the estimation of those who had known only what is worldly, are exalted and to be venerated, and above the wisdom of this world, and above reason, and efficacious in doctrine for demonstrating the highest truth. ⟦s3⟧ Therefore he shows that divine theology, which used these exemplars in the demonstration of the invisible, proclaims things worthy of God and consonant with the truth; but that the theology of the world, which by the reasoning of the flesh followed the elements of creation, could not grasp the truth of God. ⟦s4⟧ And so indeed, that it may be said as in a riddle, Goliath is struck upon his own head by one despised and [0931A] small, and laid low; because the wisdom of this world, swelling on high, is convicted at its very summit of manifest error by the humility of Christian truth. ⟦s5⟧ And it is shown that God is found by the humble, and revealed to the humble, and has bestowed the cross of Christ upon believers — which the wisdom of the world could not bestow upon the proud, and upon those who presume of themselves. ⟦s6⟧ Therefore we set our theology, and the knowledge of things exalted, and sublime, and invisible, against those who deride and reprove the humble faith — that they themselves may glory, if they can, over things like these which they despise. ⟦s7⟧ And if they have found such things, and known the like, by their own wisdom, and their own reason, and their own sense — which they do not fear to prefer to our faith — let them look upon their own theology, and the things they have said about God, things to be blushed at, [0932A] and ridiculous, and incongruous, and false, that they may acknowledge the faith and take up the truth. ⟦s8⟧ For on this account God willed to dwell in the nature of man, that the dwelling of man might be in heaven; and for this that One endured human things, that this one might deserve to know divine things. ⟦s9⟧ This is the mystery of the humility of God, and the mystery of faith, and the mystery of truth — which the proud in heart did not know, puffed up by their own sense, and by their own wisdom, which was of the flesh, and could not find the wisdom of God, so that it was found to be foolishness which was thought to be wisdom, and was not. ⟦s10⟧ In this we have been made sublime beyond them; because the wisdom of God, which by us is believed in the flesh, is despised by them; in glory and majesty it is acknowledged by us, and unknown by them.

## CHAPTER V. *What a hierarchy is, and its arrangement and origin.*

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0931]

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0931B] The things that it behooved me to set down beforehand, in the theology of Dionysius, for the description of the hierarchies and for the understanding of what is to be said, I have set forth in the foregoing chapters, as far as for the time was suggested to my mind. ⟦s2⟧ Now it remains that we examine what has been said about these things by the theologian. ⟦s3⟧ And because the very surface of the reading is covered with a great depth of words, and hidden by a certain lofty ambiguity of speech that carries and lifts itself up, and is folded up according to the greatness and excellence of the most secret matters, it is necessary that it first be unlocked by a measured, and common, and easy explanation for the understanding. ⟦s4⟧ And this will perhaps be the more suitable explanation for those being introduced to the great beginning, since it is not fitting that minds, to be exercised in the meditation of matters so great and so sublime, be [0931C] occupied with the wrappings of words. ⟦s5⟧ Which wrappings, although they are set as veils worthy of the venerable secrets for those entering in, nevertheless, when the face is unveiled for the contemplation of the things that are within in the liberty of the spirit, are to be taken out of the way, for the disclosing of the mysteries. ⟦s6⟧ This, then, we now undertake, and this is enough for us in the things that we shall say. ⟦s7⟧ For as to the rest, if anything be added, let it be from a superabundant gift. ⟦s8⟧ Hierarchy, interpreted from the Greek, is called a *sacred* *principality:* and there are three hierarchies, that is, sacred principalities, which we shall name, as we have recalled above. ⟦s9⟧ The first and highest hierarchy is the power of the Godhead. ⟦s10⟧ The second and middle is the angelic power, made to the likeness of the first power, and established beneath the first power. ⟦s11⟧ The third and last hierarchy is [0931D] the human power, made to the likeness of the angelic, and established beneath it, and through that middle one beneath the first and highest. ⟦s12⟧ By these hierarchies, that is, sacred principalities, the whole world is governed: among which there is the highest power, which only commands, and the lowest, which is only commanded; and the middle, which commands the lower, and is commanded by the higher. ⟦s13⟧ The highest, therefore, and first power established the second and third power after itself, in the angels and in men, that they might be both conformed to it by participation in virtue, and fellow-workers by fellowship [0932B] in power. ⟦s14⟧ And he divided the gifts of the virtues, and according to the divisions of the gifts distributed the offices of the powers; and he gave very many gifts, and established many powers, and all the gifts from one, and all power under one; and one in all, and all things toward one, and in one. ⟦s15⟧ But the angelic hierarchy the theologian shows first. ⟦s16⟧ Second, he treats of the human. ⟦s17⟧ Third, as at the end and consummation, of the divine and highest. ⟦s18⟧ But the angelic itself he subdivides into three hierarchies, and each of the three he distinguishes by three orders, so that the number of the nine angelic orders may be completed. ⟦s19⟧ And every hierarchy is of one power and one office and one dignity, and in each hierarchy he establishes both first, and middle, and last orders. ⟦s20⟧ And the first indeed [0932C] give light; the last are given light; but the middle both are given light by the first, and give light to the last. ⟦s21⟧ After these things he pursues, concerning the last hierarchy likewise, the divisions of gifts, and the distributions of powers and dignities, and the offices, and the orders, and the operations, ordered after the likeness of the angelic hierarchy. ⟦s22⟧ At the last, ascending to the divine, and simple, and super-excellent hierarchy (so far as is possible for one contemplating amid human things), and bringing them to consummation in it, this consummated whole is the summit. ⟦s23⟧ But the beginning (since it is the highest good), being one, is wondrously multiplied and varied for the comeliness and beauty of division and outpouring into all who are worthy of participation in it, so that in it all may be one; since its gifts are lights, and the lights make [0932D] shining things, and the things illumined are shining, and themselves illuminating, and they become light — shining things, and illuminating lights. ⟦s24⟧ They are light, and one light everywhere, and the shining things are one in the one light; and the one is multiplied in the many, and the many are united in the one. ⟦s25⟧ From this, therefore, theology begins, because the shining and illuminating lights are divine mirrors; and in them is seen the light shining and illuminating, which remains incomprehensible and inaccessible in itself. ⟦s26⟧ And for this reason, that it may be seen, it goes out into them, and pours itself into them, that they may grasp it, and [0933A] receive it, and see in themselves, shining from it. ⟦s27⟧ For those that do not shine from it cannot see or grasp it, because without light not even the light itself can be seen. ⟦s28⟧ And these are indeed beautiful images of the light unto the invisible outpouring, so that through visible things the invisible may be seen. ⟦s29⟧ For that good, and the light itself, is not this light; and yet according to something it is light, and can truly be called light; and when it is called light, the truth is spoken, because it is light, and it is the image of that light. ⟦s30⟧ And what is in this light is in that good; since the light itself is good from that good, and is wholly in it, because it is wholly from it: for it does not, when it began to be from it, cease [0934A] to be in it; but it proceeded from it, and remained in it. ⟦s31⟧ Therefore the image serves, and the creature shows the Maker, since it was made for this, that he might be seen in it. ⟦s32⟧ Therefore the gifts are illuminating lights, and the lights that partake are illumined, and themselves illuminating, and like to the illuminating light. ⟦s33⟧ In that they are illumined, they become partakers of grace. ⟦s34⟧ But in that they give light, they are made consorts of power. ⟦s35⟧ And every hierarchy consists of these two, and is perfected by grace and by office, by virtue and by ministry — all of which are shown theologically by likenesses, and figures, and various riddles taken from visible things.
