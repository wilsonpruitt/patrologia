## EXPOSITION.

[0935C]

The first book of Dionysius the Areopagite the theologian, which is entitled On the Celestial Hierarchy, that is, on the celestial principate, is woven together of fifteen chapters: in which he carefully enumerates the gifts of the celestial spirits, and their offices, powers, and operations, through their several orders and grades and distributions and differences. And the title of the first chapter is: That every divine illumination, coming forth in various unforeseen ways according to goodness, remains simple, and not this alone, but also unifies the things illuminated. For grace itself is a divine illumination, and the gifts of grace themselves are lights illuminating those who partake of them; and all grace [0935D] descends from one fount, and every illumination from one light; and there are many rays, and one light: and the one light scatters itself, that it may illuminate many; and the many illuminated shine, and nothing is seen but one light, and they become one light in one light. By such a likeness theology shows how the one good offers itself to be partaken by many, that they may be one in it — they who draw one likeness from it. Then the theologian goes on, and shows that the invisible operations of grace, and the distributions of the invisible gifts, cannot be demonstrated or understood except by visible signs and likenesses; and that every visible appearance and sensible nature holds some likeness for the demonstration of invisible things. For as every good is from the [0936A] highest good, so in every good, according to the emulation of participation, the highest good can be contemplated. And therefore it is fitting for theology to conform, out of every appearance and form and sensible quality, to the signification of invisible things. And this is the sense of the first chapter. « Every best gift, and every perfect gift, is from above, descending from the Father of lights. » By apostolic authority, and that of divine theology, he first proves that every good is from the highest good; then from his own opinion he adds that every good looks toward and is poured back to the highest good; because, as it is divided among many by participation, so the many are united in one likeness and imitation. The best gifts are the gifts of nature; the perfect gifts are the gifts of grace; the Father of lights is the author and bestower of the gifts.

[0936B] « Every best gift, therefore, and every perfect gift, is from above, descending from the Father of lights, » since all goods — whether those which nature, first well established, received, or those which afterward, glorified through grace, it merited to obtain — are given by one author of nature and bestower of grace. For which to descend, as it were, was to come from the fount of highest fullness to the participation of lower things. For every creature is by nature inferior to the excellence of the Creator; and therefore every good which by the Creator's gift is poured into the creature is rightly said, as it were, to descend to lower things. For it descends, because it pours itself into a nature subject and inferior in dignity. It descends also, because from a perfect and consummate fullness it divides itself into that participation [0936C] which is in part. Yet in such a way that neither in descending is it enslaved, nor in being divided does it decrease; but, remaining in itself what it is, and in that from which it came, it offers itself, so far as it may, to those to whom it comes; and what being they receive, and how much being in that which they are, they have merited. Thus from one good all goods are; and in one good all goods are; and the good itself, from which all goods are, is good, and is light; and of the things which are from it, it is the author of goods and Father of lights; and the goods themselves are lights, and illuminating those things which are able to shine, and to become shining lights from the illuminating light. For this good leaves nothing established by it estranged from it; and yet it illuminates only those things which it created toward itself and formed according to itself. For those [0936D] alone receive light which can shine from the light — which the coming light finds not unlike itself; and, poured in, it raises them to a greater likeness of itself and a more perfect outward image. These alone, therefore, receive the best gifts and the perfect gifts; because every creature (except that one which has the image of its founder and is capable of likeness), if in its own kind it is a good — which it received from the Creator to be — was neither best when it was given, nor perfect and consummate. But the rational creature, which alone was made to the image of its founder, has received both the given things and the gifts. The best, indeed, while it was being established toward the lowest; and the perfect, while it was being lifted toward the highest. For nothing better was given to any other nature than what it first received, nor anything more perfect than what it afterward [0937A] merited to be, since it was both first established good before all the rest, and afterward perfected to the image and likeness of him by whom it was made. Therefore the best gifts and the perfect gifts, descending from the Father of lights, reach only the loftiness of the rational creature, which alone was made sublime. First, while it was being granted them, the best; and afterward, while it was being glorified, it is made perfect, in a certain manner conformed and coequal to the highest. For the best gifts themselves and the perfect gifts are lights, which descend from the Father of lights; and he from whom they descend, the Father of lights, is himself light, and those into whom they descend themselves become lights; because neither could the Father of light beget another than he himself is, nor can the receiver of light become other than light. If therefore he who begot light is light [0937B], and he who received light is light, now in a certain manner both he who begot and he who received are found to be the same. Yet in such a way that the one is believed to be this by nature, but the other is acknowledged to be this by grace. For this reason, therefore, the one light poured itself out into many lights by participation, that it might reform the many illuminated toward the one light, so that, while many received it by partaking of it, all might appear one in its form. Therefore the theologian, when he had shown from divine authority that every good is from the highest good, at once adds from his own opinion: That every good is toward the highest good, and that it is both poured forth from it by the division of participation, and united to it by the likeness of conformation.

[0937C] « Every best gift, and every perfect gift, is from above, descending from the Father of lights. » In this, therefore, he proves that every good is from the highest good. After this he adds that every good has its conversion, and its leading-back, and its end toward the highest good. « But also, the Father being moved, every procession of the manifestation of lights, coming forth into us most excellently and largely, again, as a unifying power, restoring us, fills us, and converts us to the unity of the gathering Father, and to the deifying simplicity. » The movement of the Father is the affection of fatherly kindness; for by kindness alone and by mercy alone the Father is moved to pour out his lights upon us. He is moved not by any disturbance of himself, but by compassion for us. He is moved, not shaking himself, but gathering us; not emptying himself, but filling us. [0937D] He is moved, therefore, that he may come to us, and he is not moved so as to depart from himself. He is moved, that he may begin to be to us what he was not, and he is not moved so as to cease being to himself what he was. Thus, therefore, the Father of lights is moved upon us, and, the Father being moved, his lights descend into us, and by the things proceeding into us they are manifested through us. First to us, after this to others from us; and all this procession of the manifestation of lights — that is, through which the lights are manifested (for they would not be manifested unless they proceeded) — goes forth from the Father when he is moved. That is, all this procession of the manifestation of lights, coming forth into us, works this: namely, that it fills us — by nothing other, indeed, than itself, out of that with which it fills us — again restoring us by repairing, as it established us by punishments in creating. It restores us, namely, inasmuch as it is a unifying [0938A] power, which gathers what is scattered, composes what is diverse, and out of many makes one; and thus, restoring and reforming, it converts us, who before had been turned away by unlikeness, made diverse by multitude, made perverse by depravity. It converts us, I say, to the unity of the gathering Father, and to the deifying simplicity. For the light of the Father, invisible in itself, proceeding into us and going forth to manifestation, finds us empty and void of the true good; and, pouring itself into us, fills us according to the power and capacity of each one of us; and when it has filled us, it converts us, that we may not be at variance with the Father, but in the same likeness and image may look toward him, by which we are not in discord with him. It fills, indeed, by illuminating; and it converts by making us lights. For we are filled in that we receive light; but we are converted [0938B] in that, from the light received, we too are lights. For in this we are one with the light, and in the light we are one, that we are light; and there is unity in one likeness, in one light, in one brightness, and one illuminating and shining light. Since, therefore, every good is from the highest good, and every good is toward the highest good; for from it we receive that we are one with it; nor would there be in us anything by which we might look toward it, unless first what is its own became ours through it. « For from him are all things, and unto him, as the divine word says. » That is, as the authority of the divine word testifies, all things proceeding from him, converted unto him; from him as beginning unto him as end. But we can also, moreover, not unfittingly take the movement of the Father as the beginning of our [0938C] contemplation. For the mind, accustomed to its own darkness, when it strives to contemplate the inner brightness, as though with trembling and groping eyes not enduring the force of the unwonted radiance, is beaten back by the very first rays of the sight; and the light appears to it as though quivering; and the light itself seems to be moved, when rather its own darkness alone is moved and flees the presence of the flashing light. And this seems to be the movement of the light, when it is the light of the fleeing darkness; and after this movement the lights are poured out, and proceed to manifestation — lights which, while the darkness stood, could not be seen — and they fill us with light, that we may shine, and be lights, just as the light itself is which illuminates us, so that from it are all things [0938D] and unto it; things which receive the beginning of their subsistence from it, and the end of their consummation in it, as the divine word through Paul the apostle testifies: « For from him, and through him, and in him are all things: to him be glory forever. Amen [n: (Rom. XI)]. »
