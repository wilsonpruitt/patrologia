## EXPOSITION. (cont.)

As it follows: « judging the visible forms to be imaginations, or images, of invisible beauty, » namely our own mind; « and the sensible sweetnesses figures of the invisible distribution, » — understand, our own mind judging; « and the material lights an image of the immaterial brightness, » so that again it is understood, our own mind judging; « the sacred disciplines run through according to the understanding of contemplative fullness, » here more is to be understood, our mind judging them to be images; « and the ordered dispositions, gathered unto divine things and set in order, of those things which are here, the orders of dispositions, » again understand, our mind judging [0949A] them to be images; « the very reception of the Eucharist of the participation of Jesus, that most divine reception, » again understand it, our mind judging it to be an image. The sum, then, of the whole chapter is this: Since our mind cannot ascend to the imitation and contemplation of invisible things unless it be directed through visible things — in such a way, namely, that from the visible things themselves it may know how to judge and estimate the invisible, according as they bear a likeness to those and effect a signification. But which likenesses of visible things unto invisible things our own mind ought to judge and estimate, he shows by subjoining certain distinct examples, as if he were saying: For this reason the truth of invisible things has been demonstrated through visible things: because our mind cannot ascend to the truth of those invisible things themselves unless it be instructed through the [0949B] consideration of visible things — in such a way, namely, that it may judge the visible forms to be imaginations of invisible beauty. For since in the forms of visible things their beauty consists, he fittingly says that invisible beauty is demonstrated from visible forms, since visible beauty is an image of invisible beauty. Yet because in visible things form is one thing and essence another, therefore whatever things are visible are mutably beautiful, since whatever things are diverse in number and by nature mutable do not inseparably subsist together. But invisible things, in which form is not one thing and essence another — since everything that is, is one and simple, and the same being — are beautiful from the very fact that they are; and their beauty is not compacted out of [0949C] many things running together into one, as the visible nature is seen to be, whose form is unfolded according to the spaces of places and is arranged through figures fitted together out of many. Therefore the beauty of the visible nature is one thing, and that of the invisible nature another, since the latter is simple and uniform, but the former manifold and led together by a varied proportion. There is nevertheless some likeness of visible beauty to invisible beauty, according to the emulation which the invisible Artificer established between the two, in which, as it were, certain reflections of diverse proportions fashion one image. According to this, then, the human mind, fittingly roused, ascends from visible beauty to invisible beauty; as though led [0949D] from like to like, easily understanding within itself, invisibly, what kinship there is to invisible things in the things which it comprehends outwardly and visibly. For according to the invisible light implanted in it, our mind, looking to invisible things, easily judges the visible forms to be images of invisible beauty, corresponding by a certain friendly likeness to that which it itself has invisibly within, finding them according to its approval and affection. For what is in the mind is invisible, just as the mind itself is invisible; and yet it, which is invisible, conceives from those things which are visible joy, and love, and affection; and it loves out of these certain things as though like, and friendly, and akin, and yields itself to them willingly, and exults in them. But others it spurns, and hates, and shuns, and puts itself far [0950A] from them, in love and delight; and it judges them foreign to itself, and discordant, and having no likeness with itself. And in this manner our mind is taught, from its own nature, that visible things have kinship and likeness to invisible things; and that the visible things themselves are images and likenesses of those things which cannot be seen visibly, since from these are understood those things which are not seen; and because, according to something, the whole of that which the mutable nature has here received visibly and sensibly subsists there invisibly in the immutable nature, so that it may lead to invisible things. For here there is a shape and form which delights the sight; there is also the pleasantness of melody, which soothes the hearing; there is the sweetness of odor, which restores the smell; there is the sweetness of savor, which pours into [0950B] the taste; and the softness of bodies, which cherishes and gently receives the touch. But there the shape is virtue, and the form justice, the sweetness love, and the odor desire; the song indeed joy and exultation; and the touch the finding of the loved, and desired, and sought good. For all these things are there, and are true there, and have with these things, which are not true, some likeness according to which they are understood by us. For from these our mind ascends to the knowledge and imitation of those things, judging the visible forms — which either nature shows implanted according to the first condition, or the sacred word sets forth dispensatively as a signification, to make a declaration — to be images of invisible beauty; and the sensible sweetnesses, that is, the sensible delights, to be figures and likenesses [0950C] of the invisible distribution, that is, of the sweetness which is distributed invisibly, that is, bestowed in diverse ways: namely, that one may receive more, another less, according to measure, and the gifts of the bounteous Giver. And likewise, that the material, that is, corporeal lights are an image of the immaterial brightness, that is, of spiritual light; and again, that the sacred disciplines, that is, the things investigated and searched out by the intellect, are images of contemplative fullness, that is, of full and perfect contemplation, which is both perceived and ministered according to the understanding, that is, intellectually only and invisibly. For all that knowledge which we now learn through the sacred word by the study of reading or meditation is, as it were, only an image of that full [0950D] and perfect knowledge which we shall afterward draw from the contemplation that is to come. Whence the Apostle also says: « We see now through a glass, in a riddle; but then face to face [n: (I Cor. XIII)]. » What wonder is it, then, if those sensible things which appear outwardly are said to be images of invisible things, when our very knowledge, which in comparison with these is believed to be spiritual and invisible, is reckoned to hold the place of an image and likeness in relation to those things?

There follows: « and the ordered dispositions, gathered unto divine things, of those things which are here, the orders of dispositions. » Again understand, our mind judging the orders of dispositions: « which dispositions are here, » that is, exteriorly, to be images of a state gathered unto divine things. For there is a certain state of the good mind gathered [0951A] unto divine things, and, as it were, collected into one out of the various rents of desires, and set in order, so that it may go forward rightly. Gathered, namely, through love, and set in order through discretion. Gathered unto one, and ordered in one. Of which state, invisibly ordered and subsisting, the orders of dispositions are images, which are dispensed outwardly in the grades, and offices, and ministries in the holy Church. For just as in one Church diverse orders and dispositions effect one peace and concord in the whole, by ministering and cooperating, so in one soul many virtues, by cooperating and ministering to one another, compose one form of perfection. But also, in each one of us, the disposition of the outer man, and the order of living, and the manner of acting, demonstrate the form [0951B] and state of the inner man.
