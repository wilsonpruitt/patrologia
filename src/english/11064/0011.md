## EXPOSITION. (cont.)

There follows: « and the very reception of the most divine Eucharist of the participation of Jesus. » Again understand, as above, our mind judging the reception itself of the most divine Eucharist to be an image of the participation of Jesus. For the very reception of the most divine Eucharist, that is, of the most holy partaking of the body and blood of Jesus Christ, which we now handle sacramentally and visibly upon the altar, is an image and form of that participation of Jesus by which either now we are joined to him in spirit through love, or afterward, appearing in the same form of glory, we shall be united to him in full likeness. Now here it is to be noted that certain men have thought to allege from this passage a bulwark for their error, saying that in the sacrament of the altar there is not the truth [0951C] of the body and blood of Christ, but only an image and figure of it: for this reason, because the Scripture says that what is taken in the Eucharist of the altar is an image of that which will be received in the participation of Jesus. Who assuredly would not fall into this snare of error, if they either received the sacraments of God with a right and humble faith, or handled the sacred Scriptures with a fitting understanding. But now, because in the sacraments of God they prefer their own sense to faith, and in the sacred Scriptures scorn to hold the sound form of interpretation, it comes about that the very word of truth makes them grow yet more blind, while, not rightly understood, it ministers error in place of truth. Which, however, is not the fault of Scripture, but the blindness of those who read and do not understand; [0951D] nor a confusion of the sacraments of God, but the depravity of the presumptuous. Here, however, they have erred perilously, preferring one ambiguous thing to so many manifest sentences and undoubted assertions, and choosing in it the lie rather than the truth — not because this was more said there, but because this was more believed by them. For what? Is the sacrament of the altar therefore not truth, because it is a figure? Then neither is the death of Christ truth, because it is a figure; and the resurrection of Christ is not truth, because it is a figure. For that both the death of Christ and the resurrection are a figure, and are an image, and a likeness, and a sacrament, and an example, the Apostle plainly declares, saying: « Christ died for our sins, and rose again for our justification [0952A]: that, dead to sins, we might live to justice [n: (I Cor. XV)]. » And the apostle Peter said: « Christ suffered for us, leaving you an example, that you should follow his steps [n: (I Petr. II)]. » Therefore the death of Christ was an example, that we might die to sin, and his resurrection was an example, that we might live to justice. Was it therefore not truth? Then Christ did not truly die, and did not truly rise again, if his death, or resurrection, was not true. God forbid! For of him it is written: « Surely he himself hath borne our infirmities, and himself carried our sorrows » [n: (Isa. LIII)]. Therefore the death of Christ was true, and yet was an example; and the resurrection of Christ was true, and yet was an example. Why then can the sacrament of the altar not be both a likeness and a truth? In one thing indeed [0952B] a likeness, and in another a truth. For, since the sacrament is one, three distinct things are there set forth, namely: the visible appearance, and the truth of the body, and the virtue of spiritual grace. For the visible appearance, which is visibly discerned, is one thing. The truth of the body and blood, which under the visible appearance is invisibly believed, is another. And the spiritual grace, which is invisibly and spiritually received together with the body and blood, is yet another. For what we see is the appearance of bread and wine; but what we believe under that appearance is the true body and true blood of Christ Jesus, which hung upon the cross, and which flowed from his side. Nor is only the body and blood signified through the bread and wine; but under the appearance of bread and wine the true body and true blood is consecrated. And indeed the visible appearance [0952C] is a sacrament of the true body and true blood; but the body and blood is a sacrament of spiritual grace. And just as there the appearance is discerned, whose reality or substance is not believed to be there, so there the reality is believed to be truly and substantially present, whose appearance is not discerned. For the appearance of bread and wine is seen, and the substance of bread and wine is not believed. But the substance of the body and blood of Christ is believed, and yet its appearance is not discerned.

What, then, is seen according to the appearance is a sacrament, and an image of that which is believed according to the truth of the body; and what is believed according to the truth of the body is a sacrament of that which is received according to spiritual grace. The sacrament, then, [0952D] of the altar and the divine Eucharist is, in the true body and blood of our Lord Jesus Christ, both an image according to the appearance of bread and wine, in which it is discerned, and a reality according to the truth of its own substance, in which it is there believed and received. And again, that we now visibly, according to the appearance of the sacrament, and corporeally, according to the truth of flesh and blood, take Christ upon the altar, is a sacrament and image of the fact that we ought to take that same one invisibly and spiritually, according to the infusion of grace and the participation of his Spirit, in the heart. Therefore the most divine Eucharist, which upon the altar is handled visibly and corporeally both according to the appearance of bread and wine and according to the truth of the body and blood of Christ, is a sacrament, and a sign, and an image [0953A] of the invisible and spiritual participation of Jesus, which is accomplished within, in the heart, through faith and love. For the Wisdom of God, which manifests itself through the visible, willed to show that it is itself the food and refreshment of minds; and therefore it set forth the assumed flesh as food, that through the food of the flesh it might invite to the taste of the Godhead. But, lest again human weakness should shrink from the touch of flesh in the receiving, it veiled it with the appearance of the accustomed and principal food, and thus set it forth to be taken, so that the sense might be cherished in the one, and faith built up in the other. For the sense is cherished in the one, while it perceives only the usual and accustomed; but faith is built up in the other, while in that which it sees it recognizes what manner of thing that is which it does not see. The appearance of bread and wine is therefore set forth, that it may be taught [0953B] that there is full and perfect refreshment in the taking of the body and blood of Christ from the Godhead of Christ. Now full refreshment is food and drink; but of food and drink, bread and wine is the principal substance. And the appearance is set forth from the principal substance of refreshment, that in it may be taken, and through it may be signified, the truth of the body and blood, as he himself testifies, saying: « My flesh is meat indeed, and my blood is drink indeed [n: (Joan. VI)]. » Yet that this taking of the body and blood, alone and without a spiritual effect, does not confer salvation, the same Savior himself manifests, saying: « The flesh profiteth nothing; it is the spirit that quickeneth [n: (ibid.)]. » Therefore the virtue and fullness of the spiritual refreshment which is in the body and blood of Christ is signified indeed through the appearance of bread and wine; [0953C] but it is accomplished in the receiving of grace, and in the infusion of the inward and eternal refreshment. And thus indeed, since three things are there in one, in the first is found a sign of the second; in the second the cause of the third; but in the third the virtue of the second, and the truth of the first. And these three are in one, and are one sacrament. It is clear, therefore, that the reception of the most divine Eucharist is a sacrament, and an image of the participation of Jesus; because this, that we visibly receive the sacrament of it, is a sign that we ought to be united to him spiritually and invisibly. But the Eucharist itself, that is, the good grace, that very sacred host, is called most divine, [0953D] since it makes divine, and partakers of the Godhead, those who partake of it. And because it is a sign, and a truth in which the true flesh of Christ is taken under the appearance of bread, and in his flesh, worthily taken, the reception too, and participation, and fellowship of his very Godhead is granted. Therefore it is most worthy, and most holy, and sanctifying all things that sanctify, and holy.
