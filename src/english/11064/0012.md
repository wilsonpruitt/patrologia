## EXPOSITION. (cont.)

There follows: « And whatever other things have been handed down to the heavenly essences in a supramundane manner, but to us symbolically. » After he had set forth certain things, by way of example, to show the likeness of visible things to invisible, now he concludes generally about the whole, saying: « And whatever other things have been handed down to the heavenly essences in a supramundane manner, but to us symbolically, » as if he were saying: These things above mentioned, the visible things, our mind ought to judge [0954A] to have a likeness to invisible things according to the examples set forth; and not these alone, but whatever other things have been handed down to us symbolically, or to the heavenly essences supramundanely. For not only these visible things which have been set down — that is, the forms, the sweetnesses, the lights, the disciplines, the orders, the sacred Eucharist — have the manifestation and demonstration of invisible things, even if a multitude of them; but also all other visible things whatsoever, which have been handed down to us — who are to be instructed visibly — symbolically, that is, figuratively, have been set forth for the signification and declaration of invisible things. And not only these visible things which have been handed down to us symbolically have a demonstration of invisible things; but those too which have been handed down to the heavenly essences, that is, to the angelic spirits, supramundanely, that is, invisibly and spiritually, and not [0954B] according to the appearances of this world, are signs of invisible things, and images of those things which, in the excellent and incomprehensible nature of the Godhead, subsist above all understanding and sense. For this is what he says: that not only those things which have been handed down to us symbolically are signs of invisible things; but those too which have been handed down to the heavenly essences supramundanely. For the angelic spirits themselves also have their signs, and demonstrations, through which they are instructed concerning the invisible things of God, and things exceedingly hidden, and secrets concealed within, immaterially, and invisibly, and simply. Which signs indeed, as far as concerns us and those things which are visible among us, are reckoned altogether invisible; but as far as concerns that light, much invisible, and inaccessible, and incomprehensible, [0954C] of the Deity, they are as it were outside, and proceed from within into demonstration. On which account those very signs, which come upon minds, or upon souls divinely illuminated, are called theophanies, that is, divine apparitions; because in them, coming to manifestation, that which is altogether hidden of God is demonstrated. These, then, are the things which are handed down to the heavenly ones supramundanely, not according to that demonstration which is among us, but breathed in invisibly and simply. Many things indeed were here to be said about this kind of contemplation, by which theophanies, that is, divine apparitions divinely breathed in, come upon minds to be illuminated, and, by instructing them concerning the hidden and invisible things of God in a wondrous, and concealed, and secret, and [0954D] singular manner, make them wise: especially since here too certain men are found to have become vain in their thoughts, proclaiming God to be altogether incomprehensible and inaccessible to the rational mind, except that it is instructed concerning him by certain theophanies, that is, divine apparitions, or divine likenesses set forth for contemplation. But these they place, as it were, certain images of the hidden Godhead, as means between rational souls and God, higher indeed than the mind, but lower than the Godhead. And this alone, they say, is seen of God, and in this alone is God seen — inasmuch as he in himself can be seen by no mind or soul. But these are likenesses of them, and phantasms of vanity: in which, while they try to assert that only the light of the Godhead is visible and perceptible, [0955A] they are proved to take away from holy minds the true knowledge and vision of the Deity. For what is it, that God is seen in those things alone, and outside them is not seen, save that he is never truly seen, and the truth is never seen? For if the image alone is always seen, and the truth is never seen, since the image is not the truth, even when it is of the truth. Let them therefore take away their phantasies, by which they strive to overshadow the light of our minds; nor let them fence off from us our God by the likenesses of their own conjectures; because, just as nothing besides himself can satisfy us, so neither can anything come to a stand short of himself. Let us therefore reckon these theophanies in another manner, and one consonant with truth. For just as there are two things, the light and the body that receives the light — and out of these two is made one thing that [0955B] shines, and that shining thing is in a manner an image and likeness of the light, in that it shines as the light itself does — so also our God is light, and is true light, and rational souls, clean and pure, conceive that very light: and from it they become shining, and they themselves are not an image of the light in that they are; but in that they shine from the light, as the light itself shines; and those shining things are theophanies of the light, in which the light is seen, since by none would the light be seen, if none were illuminated by the light. For he too who sees the light in himself sees himself shining; who assuredly would not see, if he did not shine, and would not see himself shining. Thus, then, we set no other between our God and us, but make a way immediately, both for us to him, and for him even to us, that we may be in him, and [0955C] he in us: so that there be nothing outside himself in which we may be beatified, just as there could be nothing besides himself by which we might be created. On account of this, therefore, let us interpret the aforesaid sentence of theology to a more convenient understanding. For because he had said that certain invisible things are demonstrated through those visible images which are determinate, now he subjoins a general sentence concerning the invisible things themselves, saying: « And whatever other things, etc. » As if he were saying: Not only those invisible things, of which these signs have been set forth, came into manifestation; but also whatever other invisible things which have been handed down to us — namely to men — symbolically, that is, figuratively and through sensible demonstrations, that is, set forth and made manifest; [0956A] but to the heavenly essences, that is, to the angelic spirits, supramundanely and spiritually, revealed through a bare and simple truth impressed.

There follows: « On account of this, then, our co-rational theosis, » that is, divinity, which is to us co-rational, that is, congruous or apt. For it is congruous that through those things which are known to us we should be made partakers of divine things: « On account of this, then, etc., » as if he were saying: Because the human mind cannot be instructed concerning invisible things save through visible things, and things known and akin to it, therefore « the merciful Principle of perfection, » that is, God, from whom all perfection has its beginning, as in him it takes its consummation and end — by grace and mercy alone, that he might call us back to his own knowledge, and reform his likeness; [0956B] « manifesting to us the heavenly hierarchies, » that is, the angelic powers; « and perfecting also our hierarchy as fellow-minister of them, » namely of the heavenly hierarchies, that it might be both conformable to them in dignity, and like them in divine ministry, the deiform likeness of their sanctification being preserved in us, that we may be like the sanctification of them who are conformed to God, « according to our virtue, » that is, according to our possibility, that is, as far as we are able to be like — we who are men, and mortal, and still liable to sin; « he described the supercelestial intellects, » the invisible spirits, who were unknown to us, by sensible images, that through the known we might learn the unknown. And this description he made [0956C] « in the sacred compositions of the words, » that is, in the descriptions of the sacred word: which compositions set forth to us both figures and likenesses for the demonstration of invisible things. And this he did for this reason, « that he might lead us back through sensible things to intellectual things, » that is, through visible things to invisible things. And that he might also lead us back and « from sacredly figured symbols, » that is, from sacred figures and the figures of sacred things; « into the simple heights, » that is, into the simple and spiritual excellences to be known « of the heavenly hierarchies, » that is, of the angelic powers. For this reason he described them to us by visible signs, that he might make them intelligible to us, and conform us to their imitation.
