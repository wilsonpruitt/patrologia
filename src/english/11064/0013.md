## THE TEXT.

[0955C]

*It behooves us, then, as I judge, first to set forth what indeed we reckon the speculation of every hierarchy to be, and what each of them has profited the divine praisers of it. Then to praise the heavenly hierarchies according to their manifestation in the words, and, in what follows these, to say by what divine formations the sacred [0956C] descriptions of the words figure the heavenly orders, and to what truth one ought to ascend through the forms. That we too may not, in the same manner as the many, uncleanly reckon the heavenly and deiform minds to be certain many-footed things, and of many faces, and formed to oxen and to a cattle-like beastliness [pecuinitatem], or to the bestial imagination of lions, or figured to the appearance of eagles with curved beak, or to the tripartite motion of the wings of flying things: and imagine certain fiery [0957A] wheels above the heaven, and material thrones necessary for the Divinity to recline upon; and certain many-colored horses, and arms-bearing chief-commanders; and whatever other things have been handed down to us from the sacred words, and formatively, in the variety of manifesting symbols [n: (Ezech. I; Apoc. IV; Isa. VI; Dan. VII; Zach. I; Apoc. VI; Ezech. XXIII; Job. XVI; Sap. V; Josue V; II Machab. III)]*. *For indeed most artfully has theology used poetic sacred formations in un-figured intellects: revealing, as has been said, our mind, and providing for it by a proper and connatural reduction, and reforming unto itself the anagogical holy Scriptures. But if to anyone it seems that the sacred compositions ought indeed to be received as of things simple in themselves and unknown to us, and of things incontemplable [0957B] in their subsisting: but he reckons unfitting the descriptions of the holy intellects in the sacred words, and all such speaking hard — this theater of angelic names; and that the theologians ought, he says, coming universally to a corporeal fashioning among corporeal things, to form and manifest these by their proper figurations, and, as far as possible, akin, from the most precious things among us, and in any way immaterial, and supereminent essences — and not to set the earthly, most base, manifold forms round about the heavenly and deiform simplicities. This indeed would be both more sublime for us, and would not lead down the supramundane manifestations into unfitting dissimilitudes. This too would at once not unjustly do injury to the divine virtues, and likewise would not seduce our [0957C] mind, thrusting itself into unclean compositions. And perhaps also the supercelestial things will be reckoned to be filled with certain leonine and equine multitudes; and with a bellowing utterance of praises, and with the flying headlong rush of angels, and with other animals, and with more ignoble matters, as though shut up unto the inconsequent, and ignoble, and passible, while there are described throughout as deiform the clear likenesses of the manifesting words. But the inquiry of truth, as I judge, shows that the most sacred wisdom of the words [n: (Ezech. XXVIII; Job. XXXVIII)] has in the formations of the heavenly minds provided exceedingly for both: so that neither would it (so perhaps someone might say) do injury to the divine virtues; nor would it fix you, in a passible manner, into the vile lownesses of images. For because indeed there have been beautifully procured [0957D] forms of the formless, and figures of things lacking figures, one might say that it is not one single cause — that our analogy, not being able to be extended immediately into invisible contemplations, and desiring its proper and connatural reductions: which hold out to us the passible formations of formless and supernatural speculations. But because this too is most seemly for mystical words, to conceal by incomprehensible divine *aenigmat*, and to set trackless to the many the sacred and hidden truth of the supramundane intellects. For not every one is holy, nor is knowledge, as the words say, of all [n: (Matth. VII; I Cor. II)]*. *But if anyone should reckon the deformed causes of the description of images dishonorable, saying that base [0958A] formations are thus referred to deiform and most holy dispositions, it suffices to say to him: How is the mode of holy manifestation twofold? One indeed as it were consequent, on account of the like images of the sacred figures that come forth; but the other, on account of the dissimilar makings of forms, conformed to what is altogether inconsequent and unseemly. And so the mystical traditions of the manifesting words sometimes praise the blessedness of the Godhead, which is to be venerated above essence, as reason, and intellect, and essence; declaring the divine rationality and its wisdom, and the truly existing substance, and the true cause of those things which are substance; and they form it as though light, and call it life, so many wondrous, more chaste formations abiding, and the material formations [0958B] being proved in some way to fall short of excelling; and thus by a likeness of the divine unto truth. For it is above all essence and life, no light indeed characterizing it, all reason and intellect being incomparably left behind by any likeness of it. But sometimes it is praised supramundanely by dissimilar manifestations from the very words [n: (Rom. XI; I Tim. VI; Psalm. CXL)], which call it invisible, and infinite, and incomprehensible: and by which is signified not what it is, but what it is not. For this, as I judge, is more powerful in its case. Since indeed, as the hidden and priestly tradition has introduced, we truly say that it is not according to anything of those things which are. But we do not know its superessential, and invisible, and ineffable infinitude. [0958C] If, therefore, negations in divine matters are true, but affirmations incompact, the manifestation through dissimilar formations is more apt to the obscurity of the arcane things. And so now the sacred descriptions of the words do not fill up the heavenly ornaments with base things, manifesting them by dissimilar makings of forms, and through these showing, together with all material things, the excellences of the supramundane things. Nay rather, that dissimilar likenesses lead our mind back more — I do not think any of the well-wise contradict. For through the more precious sacred formations it is consequent to be seduced, reckoning the heavenly essences to be certain gold-formed things, and certain resplendent men clothed in comely garments, sprinkling harmlessly a white and fiery brightness, and by whatever other like imaginal forms theology has [0958D] figured the heavenly intellects. And lest they who understand nothing higher than visible goods should suffer this, the restorative wisdom of the holy theologians has wondrously descended to unseemly dissimilitudes: not granting our material part, remaining in base images, to rest; but purging, and bearing upward, and suggesting to the soul by the deformity of the compositions — as neither the just nor the true approving — that the supercelestial and divine spectacles are by no means, according to truth, like to things very material, because thus base. But so this too it behooves to understand: That nothing of those things which are, is universally deprived of the participation of the good: since indeed, as the Truth of the words says: « All things very good [n: (Gen. I)]*. » *It is possible, then, from all things to understand good [0959A] speculations, and, of both invisible and intellectual things, to form from material things the said dissimilar likenesses: the intellectual things having in another way those things which are otherwise distributed to sensible things. For fury indeed in irrational things is from a passible motion: and the furious motion of them is filled with all irrationality; but in intellectual things one must understand the irascible in another way, declaring, as I judge, their manly reasonableness, and their immense [immanentem] rest in divine and immutable foundations. In the same way we say that concupiscence indeed is in irrational things a certain inconsiderate and material thing, from a natural motion, or from custom incontinently engendered in mutable things — an impassibility, and an irrational continuity of bodily pleasure; at once compelling every animal [0959B] according to sense to what is unfit to be desired. But when, setting dissimilar likenesses about intellectual things, we form concupiscence about them, we must understand it to be divine love — above reason, and above the intellect of immateriality, and the inflexible and needless desire of a superessentially chaste and impassible contemplation, and that pure and most sublime brightness, and the invisible and form-making beauty of eternal, true, and invisible fellowship. And as it were it receives power in sufficiency and in convertibility; and it is afflicted by no virtue, through the unconfused and immutable love of the divine beauty, and the universal recalling into that which is truly to be sought. But that very irrationality and insensibility [0959C], in irrational animals indeed, or in animate matters, we properly call a defect of reason and sense; but in immaterial and intellectual essences we confess it, holily and fittingly, to be their supereminences, as of supramundane things, excelling our transitory and corporeal reason, and the material sense of the mind, alienated from incorporeal things. And so it is possible to form for the heavenly things forms not dissonant, even from vile parts of matter. Since it too, possessing subsistence from the truly good, has through all its material disposition certain images of intellectual beauty; and it is possible through them to be led back to the immaterial first forms — the likenesses being received dissimilarly, as has been said, and not in like manner with them: but compactly and beautifully, the intellectual [0959D] and sensible properties being defined. These mystical theologians we shall find wondrously conforming not only to the declarations of the heavenly dispositions, but sometimes to the divine manifestations themselves. And sometimes indeed they praise it from precious lights, as the Sun of justice, as the morning star holily rising in the mind, and as a light shining unenveloped and invisibly [n: (Malach. I; Apoc. II)]*. *But sometimes from middle things, as fire shining harmlessly, as water the giver of vital fullness, and, as it must be said symbolically, entering into the belly, and pouring back rivers that flow again immeasurably. But sometimes from the lowest things, as a sweet ointment, as a corner-stone [n: (Exod. III; Joan. I)]*. » *But they set about it also a bestial form; and fit to it the special property of a lion and of a panther [0960A], and clothe it with a leopard's skin, and a raging she-bear [n: (Cant. II; Isa, XXVIII; Osee V)]*. *But to a doe indeed, and to that which seemed to be the vilest of all, and to signify more; since the divinely wise [divine wisdom] handed it down as forming itself even into the appearance of a worm [n: (Psal. XXI)]*. *So too all the theosophers, and the prophets by hidden inspiration, distinguish the Holy of Holies from the undefiled saints, and honor the dissimilar holy figuration, so that neither may divine things be rightly received by the unclean, nor may the students of wondrous images remain in the figures of contemplation as though in true things. And so they honor divine things by true negations, and by diverse likenesses of images compacted unto the lowest things. Nothing, therefore, is inconsequent, if they form the heavenly essences too from unfitting, dissimilar likenesses [0960B] according to the said causes. For perhaps we would not indeed come to the question, out of need, unto the anagogy, through the diligent scrutiny of divine things, unless the deformity of the manifesting deformation of the angels forced us out: not suffering our mind to remain in the dissimilar makings of forms, but making it reluctant to deny the material passibilities, and accustoming it to extend itself purely through visible things into the supramundane heights. So much, then, has been said by us on account of the material and unfitting angelic descriptions of the images of the divine words. But then it behooves to set apart what indeed we reckon the hierarchy itself to be, and what those who obtain a hierarchy profit from the hierarchy itself. But let Christ be my leader (if indeed it is lawful for me to say), the [0960C] inspiration of the whole hierarchical manifestation. But thou, O boy, according to the holy legislation of our priestly tradition, do thou holily and fittingly hearken, made divine in the divine doctrine of the things wondrously said, covering round in the secret of the mind the things that are holy, and, from the unclean multitude, guard them as uniform. For it is not lawful, as the words say, to cast to swine the unconfused, and light-formed, and beneficent ornament of the invisible pearls [n: (Matth. VII)]*.
