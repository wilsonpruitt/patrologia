## EXPOSITION.

First I said, and I say now — that I may not detain you in expectation — that I undertook your petition upon the Hierarchy of Dionysius, not that I should pursue the searching-out of the deep things of the matters, but that I should only uncover, and bring into the light [0960D], the coverings of the words. For this is more fitting, first, for those who are to be introduced: especially because those things which we judged were to be discussed we recognize to be exceedingly great, and above our possibility. The title of the second chapter is this: « That divine and heavenly things are beautifully manifested even through dissimilar symbols. » Above we have already said what a symbol is: namely a collation, that is, a fitting-together of visible forms set forth for the demonstration of an invisible thing. For example, when we wish to express visibly the natures of the heavenly spirits, we fit human faces indeed, but the wings of a bird, into one appearance of composition, so that, by the face of a man — who alone among visible things uses reason — the invisible spirits themselves too may be understood to be rational and wise; but by the wings the agility [0961A] of their nature, and their swift motion toward all things, may be expressed. Since, then, in the first chapter he discussed generally concerning every hierarchy, as though briefly foretasting the sum of the following work in order to make instruction — first, that every good is multiplied from the highest good by participation, and every good is united to the highest good by likeness and conversion; then, that Scripture fittingly took up visible signs for the declaration of invisible things: now in the following chapter he shows, as the title itself declares beforehand, that beautifully — that is, aptly and fittingly — are manifested, that is, represented and signified, that they may be made manifest: divine things, namely those which are in the highest hierarchy, and heavenly things, which are in the angelic hierarchy. Both these are fittingly manifested, [0961B] not only through like symbols, that is, not only through beautiful and comely figures and forms, congruous or like to their majesty and purity; but also through dissimilar symbols, that is, through such forms and descriptions as seem foreign to their excellence and unworthy of their purity. Which indeed may seem to some to be less fitting. But to those who consider well, it is found to be ordered by a great reason and a necessary dispensation. By reason indeed, so that while we discern these foreign things taken up for demonstration, we may recognize that those things too, which seemed to be proper according to something, are foreign and dissimilar from the highest truth. But by dispensation, so that while the pious mind sees those things, which it does not endure to be placed among divine things according to their propriety, attributed by the signification [0961C] of them, it may in no way be able to doubt that other things too, which seemed worthy, and through this could be believed to be true and proper, are figurative and led to truth by likeness. Therefore the like symbols are set forth for demonstration to this end, that they might show the truth of invisible things by a like appearance; but the dissimilar ones, that by signifying they might demonstrate that one must go out from the figure to the truth, and not remain in those things which could not be true. Therefore, as much as the like symbols excel in appearance, yet the dissimilar symbols transcend in signification; since, even if the former have a more beautiful appearance, yet the latter hold a more manifest signification. The former show the truth; the latter compel us to go out from falsity. The former so signify the truth that [0961D] the untrained mind can easily be detained in them; the latter so instruct that they do not suffer the minds of those who consider to be deceived in their veneration. The former, though they are signs of the truth, would yet perhaps sometimes cause themselves to be received in place of the truth on account of their excellent appearance, unless these were signs in which the truth is not believed, even though through them the truth is signified. For the truth is one thing, and the sign of the truth another; because the sign is not the truth, even when it is a sign of the truth, and is true. Those signs, therefore, have a more evident demonstration, which both, through the likeness by which they approach the truth, manifest the truth itself; and, through the dissimilitude by which they are far removed from the truth, demonstrate that they are not the truth, but signs nevertheless, and an image of the truth. [0962A] This, then, is what he intends to demonstrate in this chapter, as is expressed through its title: that divine and heavenly things are beautifully and fittingly demonstrated not only through like, but also through dissimilar forms and figurations. And that no injury, as some have reckoned, is done to those divine and heavenly things, if they are sometimes figured in the sacred Scriptures through humble formations, and, as it were, removed from their excellence, and unworthy — as in Scripture God is compared to a wagon, and the angels to oxen: and the rest in this manner.

« It behooves us, then, as I judge, first to set forth what indeed we reckon the speculation of every hierarchy to be, etc. » Modestly he tempers his assertion, as befits a prudent man; nor in excellent matters, [0962B] and remote from the human sense, does he seem to presume beyond man's possibility, saying: « As I judge. » For he himself knew this which he said, since he had been taught by him who had seen and knew. But he kept the modesty of speech, that he might show that these things are to be sought and found by humility, not by elation. It behooves us, then, as I judge, first to set forth what indeed we reckon the speculation « of every hierarchy to be. » Five things here he first sets forth, generally, by way of introduction to the readers: which through the following chapters he will set forth one by one. First, the general definition of every hierarchy. Second, the utility of each hierarchy. Third, the praise of the heavenly hierarchies according to the visible formations which are found in the sacred word. [0962C] Fourth, the quality of those very formations and descriptions by which the heavenly virtues are signified. Fifth, what truth the human mind ought to conceive, in those very invisible things, from those things which are visibly set forth for the declaration and praise of invisible things. That is: to consider, first, what a hierarchy is. Secondly, what it profits, that is, what utility consists in each one. Thirdly, how the excellence of the invisible hierarchies is shown through visible signs. Fourthly, to know the quality of the mystical signs and demonstrations. Fifthly, to separate the thing signified from the sign, the truth from the figure. It behooves us first to set forth what speculation, that is, what definition we reckon to be of every hierarchy, that is, how generally [0962D] or universally a hierarchy is to be defined. But he calls the definition a speculation for this reason; since the definition of a thing is as it were a mirror, in which the nature of the thing itself is discerned, just as in a mirror the image of the nature of the body placed before it is seen. A general definition is that which fits universally, and is found in the whole. For he who says that the angelic principalities are a hierarchy, says truly; but he does not define universally, since a hierarchy is found among men also. Again, he who says that a hierarchy is an order, says truly; since where there is power, there is order. But he does not say the whole; since not every order is a hierarchy, because not every order is a power. And so a general definition is that which fits every one and contains the whole. [0963A] This general definition of a hierarchy, therefore, the author named the speculation of every hierarchy: which both fits every one, and contains the whole. But because he himself is about to place and set forth this in what follows, we ought not to anticipate it at present. Then he subjoins another of those things which one ought to search out or set forth, namely, what each hierarchy has profited its divine praisers, that is, what or how much each hierarchy, that is, each sacred power, has profited its divine praisers, that is, those who, established and ordained in it, praise God, or celebrate and frequent the divine praises. For all the hierarchies are ordained and instituted unto the divine praise, whether the higher or the lower, so that by all God may be praised: by whom and through whom are all things, and [0963B] in all things all things [n: (I Cor. XV)]. And those who are more excellent praise more; and those who praise more are more sublimely rewarded. And so every hierarchy profits its own divine praisers, in proportion to what it has received according to the distribution of grace and the gifts of bounty, so that it may be either more sublime in knowledge, or more fervent in love. For according to grace are the gifts, and according to the gifts are the merits, and according to the diversity of the merits the diversity of the rewards is established. Every hierarchy, therefore, has profited its praisers of God, in reward, in proportion to what it conferred in gift; because every dignity and power, according to the grace of ministry and office by which it was able to proclaim and amplify the praise, and honor, and glory of God, grows greater in the fruit of retribution. But the heavenly virtues themselves also, [0963C] to which this very thing is a reward — to praise God — receive, each according to their orders and dignities in their principalities and hierarchies, so much of utility as they rise up, more or less, in the praise of the Creator through the gift of cooperating grace. Then, he says, « it behooves, » that is, after the divine definition of every hierarchy and its utility has been set forth, it behooves to praise the heavenly hierarchies, that is, the angelic principalities, that is, to describe and demonstrate their praises according to the manifestation of them, namely of the hierarchies, in the words, that is, according to this, that their praises are manifested in the words, that is, in the divine Scriptures. For there are set forth in the sacred word figures and demonstrations by which the virtues and praises [0963D] of the angelic powers are declared: which the author says are to be inspected and considered, unto the manifestation of the angelic hierarchy.

There follows: « And in what follows these, » that is, consequently after those things aforesaid, or in those things which follow: It behooves « to say by what divine formations the sacred descriptions of the words, namely the divine words, figure the heavenly orders. » As if he were saying: First it behooves to consider how the divine words proclaim the heavenly virtues as praiseworthy through the formations of the descriptions. Then, of what quality these very descriptions and formations are, which by a certain divine speech they figure for the declaration of those heavenly virtues. But last of all it behooves to consider to what truth one ought to ascend through [0964A] the forms. For it is not enough to attend to this alone, that it is visible, unless we also know of what quality we ought, from that which is visibly demonstrated, invisibly to think the truth. For if we think that this alone is what is visibly discerned in the demonstration, we are never made partakers of the truth. Let us therefore discern the sign from the truth, and let us know that one thing is that which is set outwardly for the instructing of the sense; but another that which is reserved within for the beatifying of the mind. Therefore it behooves to say to what truth we ought to ascend from these forms of the visible figurations, lest we too, like many, should reckon all these things, which are set forth figuratively in the Scriptures as signs of the heavenly ones, to be there just as here they are mystically figured in likenesses and forms [0964B] and figures pertaining nevertheless to corporeal and sensible things: which by no means fall into the divine and spiritual things.
