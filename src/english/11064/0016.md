## EXPOSITION. (cont.)

This is what follows: « But if to anyone it seems that the sacred compositions ought indeed to be received, as of things simple in themselves, and unknown to us, and uncontemplable in their subsistence. » This is, that it is worthy that the sacred compositions be received, that is, the sacred figurations or formations which are made concerning the divine and invisible things themselves, inasmuch as they are of those things which are simple and uncompounded, without corporeal figuration and form, and thereby, to us who know only how to contemplate and think corporeal things, subsist unknown and uncontemplable, and cannot be shown, nor perceived by the human heart, save through visible signs and visible demonstrations. If, then, it seems to anyone worthy [0967B] and necessary, on account of causes of this sort, that the sacred compositions be received — provided only that they be formed beautifully and seemly, and befitting so great a purity — but now judges them to be unseemly « and unfitting, » namely whatever, « of the holy intellects, » that is, the descriptions of spirits, that are made « in the sacred words »; and he judges it also to be unfitting, all this figment of angelic names, as it seems to him theatrical — namely, that angels, oxen, and lions, and eagles, and horses, and wheels, and chariots, and thrones, and the rest of this sort should be introduced and named: which according to his supposition seems theatrical, and worthy of theatrical recitation and derision. [0967C] But to us, thinking piously and believing rightly, to speak thus is hard, or, every such speaking is hard, that is, all such hard and unfitting sayings and descriptions, which is as it were a certain theater of angelic names; or, if he supposes that to speak thus, that is, that it must so be spoken, that which is feigned in Scripture, is as it were hard, that is, an inconsonant and unfitting theater of angelic names to be: and if he also says that the theologians, coming or descending to a corporeal fashioning, that is, a material figuration, of things universally, that is, altogether incorporeal — namely the heavenly and invisible — ought to have formed them, namely the invisible things, and to have made them manifest, as far as was possible for them, by proper and cognate figurations, that is, by unfitting and like figurations [0967D] taken from among us, that is, from among these visible things, out of the most precious ones — or taken out of the most precious things among us, that is, out of those things which are most precious among us; and out of things in some way immaterial, that is, out of those things which in any, that is, in some, way seem to be immaterial and incorporeal, such namely as light, and fire, and splendor, and heat, and the rest of this sort: which, though they be material and corporeal, nevertheless approach closely to a spiritual nature by their subtlety and purity. If then he say that out of these most precious things, and in some way immaterial and supereminent essences, the theologians ought to have taken the figures of those things which are altogether incorporeal, so that through those things which are almost immaterial and incorporeal, truly incorporeal things might be signified. And if he say further that there ought not to be set round about [0968A] earthly and lowest, that is, last or least, or abject multiformities, that is, figures composed of many and various forms, upon the deiform simplicities, that is, the spiritual natures: which, because they are deiform in glory, earthly and lowest things, and because they are simple in essence, multiformities ought by no means to have been attributed to them. And if he say further: This indeed — that is, if out of precious and supereminent essences the figures of the invisible things were taken, and earthly and lowest formednesses were not set round about, or fitted to, the deiform simplicities — this indeed both would be our own more sublimely, that is, would advance us more sublimely to the knowledge of spiritual things. Or, this indeed both would be ours, because it would be corporeal and material; and would be more sublime, [0968B] that is, nearer to divine and spiritual things, so that on either part it would become reasonable: so that through that which was ours, that is, familiar and known to us, it could instruct; and through that which was more sublime and more worthy, it would befit the excellence and dignity of spiritual things. And it would also not lead the supermundane, that is, the heavenly and spiritual manifestations into unfitting unlikenesses, as these descriptions do; because out of earthly and lowest essences they bring likenesses unfitting and unlike to spiritual things. This also — that is, if only out of beautiful and seemly forms the figures of spiritual things were formed — would do no injury to the divine powers by attributing to them unfitting forms: [0968C] which to do is unjust. And equally, that is, likewise, it would not seduce our mind, as this base and unseemly formation seduces it, into thinking things alien and unworthy of those powers, while it inserts, or intrudes, itself into these impure compositions: which have been made unseemly concerning them. And perhaps yet another evil may come from these base compositions, namely, that the supercelestial things will be supposed to be filled, or to have been filled, with certain leonine and equine multitudes, that is, of horses and lions, and with a lowing utterance of praises, and with a flying headlong of angels; because the figures of lions, and horses, and oxen, and birds, whose office is to roar, and to neigh, and to fly, and which cannot praise God save by lowing, or roaring, are ascribed to the angels. And the heavenly things themselves will be supposed [0968D] to be filled not only with these, but also with other animals and baser materials, such as worms, and coals, and other things of this sort, which are found said by figure of spiritual things — as though the heavenly things themselves were laid open, that is, disclosed and opened, unto that which is inconsequent, that is, unfitting and ignoble, and it be possible above that things of this sort be admitted into them, or even passible, that is, corruptible. And all these things indeed will be supposed, while they are described, likenesses of the manifesting words: which the words set forth for manifestation clearly, that is, manifestly, as deformed. If then to anyone this whole thing seem so (so that up to this point the sentence hangs, and at last it is thus inferred) — to him indeed it so seems, namely that only beautiful and excellent appearances are to be set to the signification of spiritual [0969A] and divine things. But nevertheless, if anyone diligently inquire into the truth, he recognizes that the wisdom of the divine words has fittingly set forth both.

This is what follows: « But the inquiry of truth, as I suppose, shows that the most sacred wisdom of the words, in the formation of the minds, or spirits, of the heavenly ones, has provided for both exceedingly, so that neither (as perhaps someone would say) should it do injury to the divine powers, » that is, so that neither through those lowly figurations should it do injury to the divine powers, that is, to the spirits, who are divine and sacred and incorporeal: which nevertheless perhaps someone would call injury, and would think ought to be called so, when yet it is no injury, « neither should it fix us » through the same figurations « passibly [0969B] in the vile lownesses of images, » that is, in the vileness of lowly, or in the lowness of vile, images. For the wisdom of the divine word provided and took care of both: that through these lowly figurations it should neither do injury to the divine things, nor inform our mind to vile thoughts or false suppositions. And first, indeed, why corporeal and visible figures and forms have been set to the manifesting of divine and invisible things, a necessary and fitting cause is demonstrated — which perhaps someone would say is not one, but twofold, that is, not only because those things could not otherwise be made manifest to our mind save through these, but also because in the figures and enigmas of mystical descriptions the divine secrets were to be covered and concealed from impure minds and from the malevolent. [0969C] Thus, he says, someone will state the cause of the mystical descriptions: which, though he himself could have said it indubitably and truly, he modestly attributes to another, lest perchance he should seem to commend his own authority.
