## EXPOSITION. (cont.)

This, then, is what he says: « For because indeed the forms of the formless, and the figures of things lacking figures, have been beautifully provided: one would not say the cause thereof to be one, our analogy. For because indeed. » Contrary to the usage of Latinity, according to the idiom of the Greek tongue, he has heaped up conjunctions, or *quia* is to be read for *quod*, so that this is the sense: That indeed one, that is, someone, would say the cause not to be one — namely this our analogy, that is, that not only our [0969D] analogy is the cause of this thing, namely that the forms of the formless and the figures of things lacking figures have been beautifully, that is, fittingly, provided by the sacred word — that is, that in the sacred word forms and figures have been attributed to those heavenly spirits, which in their own nature have neither corporeal forms nor figures. Of this thing, I say, one would say the cause to be not only the analogy, that is, our condition, otherwise unable to attain to the knowledge of invisible things, nor able immediately, that is, without some intermediary, to extend itself through the intellect into the invisible contemplations, that is, into the contemplations of invisible things. Our analogy, I say, which also desires its own and connatural leadings-back, this is, to be led back to the knowledge and contemplation of invisible things [0970A] through those things which are its own and connatural, that is, visible and corporeal — which corporeal things, namely, put forth, that is, set forth, the formations of the formless, that is, of spiritual speculations, and the supernatural, that is, of speculations exceeding our nature, and altogether incomprehensible to us, unless through these passible formations they were demonstrated and insinuated to us. He calls analogy the human condition; because analogy is a property, according to reason and the agreement of several like things in one, just as grammarians too are wont to assign analogies of words according to the likeness of several falling under one property. The analogy, then, of human nature, that is, the condition or property, or [0970B] agreement, is this: to be able and to know those things which pertain to man, and which man received to be and to be able. But above our analogy, that is, above our agreement and equality, is it to scrutinize heavenly things, unless we be instructed through those things which are with us visible and known to us. This analogy, then, that is, the human condition, was the cause why the sacred word, for the instructing of human minds, set forth visible signs of invisible things. And not this cause only, but also « because this also is most seemly for the mystical words, » namely to hide, « and to set as trackless to the many the sacred and hidden, » that is, the concealed truth « of the supermundane intellects, » that is, of the invisible spirits, « through incomprehensible divine enigmas. » [0970C] For enigmas, and parables, and figures are set to the mystical word of the Scriptures for this reason: that the truth of spiritual things be not laid open to carnal and impure spirits, and that at once it may exercise the studious and devout by its very profundity. Which cause, however, the author here judges to mention out of superabundance. Therefore, then, the divine sacraments are covered lest they lie open to all, because not all are worthy of the recognition of truth. « For not everyone is holy, neither is knowledge of all, as the words say. » For because not every man is holy, therefore the truth is not to be made manifest to all; since, if it were made manifest to all, many would gnaw at it by contradicting through malice, or would defile it by living impurely. Whence it is said: « Give not the holy thing [0970D] to dogs [n: (Matth. VII)], » namely to those who gnaw the truth set forth with the tooth of malice; « neither cast pearls before swine [n: (ibid.)], » namely before those who, as far as in them lies, pollute what is offered by living wickedly. For these are the unholy, that is, unworthy of holy things, whose knowledge it is not, « as the words say, » of the sacred Scripture. For the Apostle says that « faith is not of all [n: (II Thess. III)], » since that knowledge of truth, by which God is piously believed by the holy and just, is either not received by any of the perverse, or not worthily held.

« But if anyone should suppose the causes of the deformed descriptions of images to be dishonorable, saying that such base formations are thus referred to the deiform and most holy dispositions, it suffices to say to him: How [0971A] is the mode of holy manifestation twofold? » Now at length he responds to the question objected above, in which it was contained that vile and abject formations are unfittingly set to sacred and divine things in the sacred word, saying: « If anyone should suppose the causes of the description of images to be deformed, » that is, if anyone should suppose the causes of describing images to be deformed, that is, unfitting, saying it to be dishonorable that such base formations be referred, that is, fitted, to the deiform and most holy dispositions, that is, orders. If anyone, I say, should so suppose, and so say: « It suffices to say to him how the mode of holy manifestation is twofold: » that is, to a supposition and objection of this sort it is sufficiently answered in this, that the holy manifestation which is made [0971B] through the Scriptures is shown to have a twofold mode. « One indeed as it were consequent, » that is, fitting and seemly, in which the signs follow the signified truth by a like property: on account of like images of sacred figures agreeing, that is, proceeding to make the manifestation, or coming forth, that is, apt and concordant with that which they signify. « But the other mode is conformed unto the altogether inconsequent, » that is, discrepant, and unfitting, and unseemly, for the reason that the very figures and the very signs manifesting the truth seem not to say it. This is what he says: « On account of unlike fashionings of forms. »

There follows: « And so the mystical traditions of the manifesting words praise the venerable [0971C] beatitude of the superessential divinity — sometimes indeed as reason, and intellect, and essence, declaring the divine rationality and its wisdom, and the truly existing subsistence, and the true cause of those things which are subsistences. » As if he said: Since the mode of manifestation in the sacred word is twofold — one, namely, formed through like signs, the other through unlike signs — and so the sacred words themselves also sometimes represent the majesty of the divine nature through like, sometimes through unlike formations. Through like ones, sometimes indeed taken from corporeal things, sometimes from incorporeal. From incorporeal things, as when they name it reason and intellect; from corporeal, as when they name it light and [0971D] splendor, and the rest that are done in this manner. But sometimes through unlike formations, likewise taken from incorporeal, or from corporeal things, they manifest the divine nature. From incorporeal indeed, as when they attribute to it anger, zeal, repentance; but from corporeal, when they set to it, in signification, the forms or figures of beasts, or of any other corporeal things whatsoever. This, then, is what he says: « And so the mystical traditions of the manifesting words praise the venerable beatitude of the superessential divinity; sometimes indeed as reason, and intellect, and essence, declaring its divine subsistence truly existing, and the true cause of the subsistence of all those things which are; » since it both truly subsists in itself, [0972A] and makes all subsistences to subsist, in that they call it essence — although it itself nevertheless, above all reason, and intellect, and essence, subsists in its majesty, neither intelligible nor comprehensible, above all subsistences.

There follows: « And they form it as light, and call it life. » Namely, the venerable beatitude the mystical traditions form, although it itself is light above all light and subsists as life above all life. By so many, then, and so great modes do the sacred words form the divine majesty in figuration. « These so great and wondrous formations remaining chaster than the rest, which seem incongruous, and unworthy, and these so great and wondrous formations being approved in some way to excel the other material formations. » As if he said: [0972B] Although unto that excellence every formation, or representation, is found inferior, yet in comparison with the other formations such beautiful and such seemly formations are proved to excel. When, namely, the nature of the divinity is named reason, and intellect, and essence, and light, and life — although even in these formations too the likeness fails in manifestation for the expressing of the ineffable truth. Whence he says: « These divine things also thus failing of likeness unto the truth. » Namely, even these more excellent likenesses thus failing, that is, even in so great excellence failing of the divine likeness — failing, namely, unto the truth, it is understood, to be expressed. For everything that here is in the sign is less than that which there is in the truth. « For it is, [0972C] » namely the divine nature, « above all essence and life, no light indeed characterizing, » that is, figuring, or expressing, it: and it is excellent, all reason and intellect being incomparably left behind, or beneath the likeness of itself. Whence it appears that if its likeness is so far superior to reason and intellect, that neither reason nor intellect can advance to equal its likeness.

There follows: « But sometimes it is praised supermundanely by the words themselves through unlike manifestations, they calling it invisible, and infinite, and incomprehensible, and by those things from which is signified not what it is, but what it is not. » As if he said: Not only is it manifested by like and excellent manifestations, [0972D] and by those approaching to its image: but sometimes also by unlike manifestations, and ones foreign to its nature, it is praised by the words « supermundanely, » that is, mystically and spiritually, and above the appearance of this world. For when it is praised through beautiful forms, it is praised according to the appearance of this world, that is, it is said according to something which is the very thing through which it is praised. But when it is praised through unlike formations, and ones alien from itself, it is praised supermundanely; since it is neither said to be the same, nor according to that, but above that, wholly other, through which it is praised. Therefore, then, it is praised supermundanely by the words themselves through unlike formations. The words, I say, or the formations themselves, calling it, namely the divine nature, invisible, and [0973A] infinite, and incomprehended: first, infinite in itself; then, invisible to us; after, incomprehended by us; and many other things also the words themselves calling the divine nature, which are such that from them is signified not what it is, but what it is not. For when God is said to be invisible, and infinite, and incomprehended: it is not said what he is, but what he is not — for he is not visible, because he cannot be seen, nor is he bounded, because he is not enclosed by place, nor terminated by time; nor is he comprehensible, because, even if it is believed what he is, how great he is is not grasped. Whoever, then, says him invisible, does not say to be that which is, but not to be that which is not. Likewise also whoever says him infinite, and incomprehended, does not say to be that which is, because he affirms nothing to be. But he says not to be that which is not, [0973B] because he denies something to be; since both what he does not affirm is that which cannot be said, and what he denies is that which can be understood.
