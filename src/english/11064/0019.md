## EXPOSITION. (cont.)

There follows: « But that unlike likenesses lead our mind back more, I do not suppose that any of those who are well-wise contradicts. » As if he said: Not only for this reason are the unlike figurations approvable, [0975A] that they show the excellences of the supermundane things; but also for this reason, that they lead our mind back from material and corporeal things more than like figurations do, nor suffer it to rest in itself. Hear a great mystery. That which God is, is above all; and when it is asked what he is, this cannot be said, because it cannot be thought. For that which can be thought ascends into the heart of man, and is grasped by the heart of man, either in those things which are seen according to appearance, or according to those things which are through imagination, or in those things which are perceived within by experience and truth; and the heart of man grasps only what it knows, or according to those things which it knows. But it knows those things which it conceives without by sense, and those things which it feels within by experience; and everything that it grasps, it either grasps in these, or conjectures according to [0975B] these. But that which is neither in these, nor according to these, the human heart cannot grasp. But that which God is, is neither any of these, because he is not a creature; nor is it according to these, because he is the Creator. That which God is, then, can neither be found in these, nor understood according to these what sort it is. For if it were understood according to these, it would be led down into the same likeness unto these, and there would be this in these which is in him. But whatever things are in creatures are more near and akin to one another, because they are made; than the work is to the artificer, and the thing fashioned to the fashioner. For all time compared to eternity, and all space composed to immensity, is found to have less, than any length of time set beside a moment, or an extension of quantity [0975C] however much increasing, referred to the proportion of an atom. So that which God is, compared to a creature, is found to excel more amply, than that which is highest among created things, compared to those things which are lowest, or made most extreme. God, then, cannot be thought according to these things, what he is; since he is other, and is otherwise, and far, and remotely, and unlikely; and what he is cannot be said. For if any of these things is said, he is other. If according to any of these things it is said, he is otherwise. What then is to be said, which God is? If he is called heaven, he is other. If he is called earth, he is other; and whatever is in heaven, and is on earth, is not this which God is. Therefore this which God is, is other. And what is this? Only this can be said, that he is other, [0975D] and what he is cannot be said. We have, then, what we may say, God is not this; but we have not what we may say, this is God; because everything that we have, this is not God, and we have not in all these things, nor do we find, what God is. For all this is other than God; because God is not everything that is made by God, and the eye does not see, nor does the mind grasp, save this, or according to this, which is not God, but from God. For man has the sense of man, and feels according to the sense of man, either that which is without according to the flesh, or that which is within according to the mind, and man has no more. The eye of flesh those things which are unto the flesh, the eye of the mind those things which are unto the mind. What more? « No man knows the things which are of a man, save the spirit of a man, which is in the man. » [0976A] [n: (I Cor. II)]. » So the things which are of God no one knows, save the Spirit of God; and he who has the Spirit of God knows through the Spirit of God the things which are of God. But the eye is threefold: the eye of flesh, the eye of reason, the eye of contemplation. The eye of flesh is open, the eye of reason bleary, the eye of contemplation closed and blind. By the eye of flesh is seen the world, and the things which are in the world. By the eye of reason the mind, and the things which are in the mind. By the eye of contemplation God, and the things which are in God. By the eye of flesh man sees those things which are outside himself; by the eye of reason those things which are in himself; by the eye of contemplation those things which are within himself and above himself. Therefore God, that which he is, is unthinkable, but to men, and to human reason: which perceives nothing save what it knows, or according to that which it knows, which is in itself or outside itself. But those who [0976B] have the Spirit of God in themselves, have God also: these see God, because they have an illumined eye by which God can be seen, and they feel him not in another, or according to another which he himself is not, but himself and in himself that which he is, which is present. Nor yet can it be said, because it is ineffable, because it is unthinkable; and it is felt, and not expressed. Therefore, everything that is said of God, that he is, is said according to that which can be said and thought, since it cannot be said otherwise; and everything that can be said and thought is less and is below than that which God is. This very thing which is said, is said as less, and this very thing is not said which God is, because he who says something says according to something, and thinks that which he says, and according to that which he says. You name [0976C] God, and you form two syllables; and you think you have said the whole of that which he is. What have you thought? For that which you have thought, this you have said. I have thought, you say, that which is above all things: what is this? If you can think what it is, this you can say. But if you cannot think it, you cannot say it; because that which cannot be thought, cannot be said. You have said God: and what is God? What do you think, or of what sort do you think, when you say God? For that which sounds is this, either one beholding or running, or fear, or whatever else you shall have supposed of him as you are able. Therefore when you say God, you say one beholding and contemplating, and considering all things. And what is this? How does God behold, and how does he see? What is his seeing, save his being? And of what sort is this? But if you understand one running, because he penetrates [0976D] all things, and apprehends, and contains everything that is, for him to run is this: to stand. And who may grasp this? But if you interpret it fear; and you assert that this itself is to be thought under this name when it is said God: who could explain how fear is God? But if for this reason you suppose fear to be said, because he is feared, how is that feared which is not seen? how can that be seen which cannot be thought? and how can that be feared which cannot be known? See therefore what you say, when you say God; or what you think, when you say God. The Creator, you say, of all things I think, when I say God, who made all things, and himself was not made. Therefore when you say God, you think that which made all things. You think that which he made, and you do not think that which he himself is who made. Not yet have you attained that which you had promised, that you should think, and understand [0977A] what God is. Less is this whole which you say; and this whole is not himself of whom you say it; and yet of him you say this, not that you may reach unto him, but that you may draw near to him. For it is a great thing for man now to go unto him, even if it be not granted to arrive. But it will be granted afterward, when that which is perfect shall have come; and man shall have begun to see as he is seen, not the image through a glass, but the truth face to face. But now meanwhile the whole is image, and the image itself is far from the truth; and yet it does what it can as an image; and it turns the mind, but does not lead it through. For this alone it can do in us, and we in it this alone, because neither can it show more, nor we grasp another, and yet there is a certain image more sublime, and more approaching to the truth, [0977B] so that it can be more called truth by us, because there is nothing else above it, by which truth could be more expressly demonstrated. For it is said that God is fire; and the figure is manifest; since God, according to property, is not fire; since fire is a body, God is not a body. It is said also that God is light; and here likewise appears an image of the truth, other than the truth, since God is not light according to property, which is named according to figure. For all these things are visible, and are far from God by the property of nature, even if they are fitted to him according to likeness alone, which itself too is scanty in relation to the excellence of his majesty. But there is another incorporeal nature more near to God, between which and God there is no other intermediary nature; and this approaches more to likeness, [0977C] although it too is far from the truth. According to this, then, the highest nature to us, a sublime likeness to God is formed for us, when God is called spirit, and wisdom, and reason, and love; because the soul is spirit, and the angel is spirit, and in the spirit itself is reason, wisdom, and love. And we know what spirit is, as far as we know the soul, and know the angel; and through the soul we know the angel, as far as we know our own selves; although even this is little, and can scarcely be called knowledge. When therefore we hear that God is spirit, we think the soul, and the angel, and we suppose a likeness, since God is some such thing as the soul is, and the angel, because the soul and the angel [0977D] are spirit. And we know not how far this is from the truth of the incomprehensible excellence. For whoever should call a body spirit, would say a falsehood, since a body is not spirit nor spirit a body. Whoever, then, should say this, would rightly be reprehended; and would be judged contrary to the truth; and yet whoever says God to be spirit is supposed to have said a truth. No one accuses him of falsity, although yet a body and a spirit are more near in nature and condition, than a spirit and God. For here each is a creature, and each is comprehensible, and each mutable, and finite. But there the one indeed is eternal, the other temporal; the one immense, the other comprehensible; the one always remaining the same, the other mutable; the one falling under knowledge, the other [0978A] unthinkable. And yet, because another cannot be said, this is said; lest nothing be said, where another is to be said, and what is cannot be said; or if it can be said, it cannot be understood. This, then, is said, and the truth tolerates this of itself, and commends this to us for truth — to us who cannot yet grasp the truth itself, until the figure pass away, and the truth be manifested, above all this, and outside all this, nakedly and openly as it itself is. Now therefore up to this point the figures remain, and of these some are far, and appear to be likeness only; but some indeed are proper, and are received as it were for truth, though they be only signs of the truth and not the truth, in which indeed, if nothing higher shall have been unto it, the truth concedes these to us, and does not reckon the impossibility. But if [0978B] they shall have been near and closely alike, yet shall not have been nearest, and something more sublime appear for the manifesting of the truth; the truth does not suffer itself to be led down to them according to property, since in the other it demonstrates itself more perfectly, in which it proves itself here to be only through likeness. But in that supreme thing, than which nothing is higher, no other appears unto it by which the figure may be proved; and therefore it behooves to receive it so as it is, since no other is given, until that which is perfect shall come. Every figure, then, the more evidently demonstrates the truth, the more openly it proves itself, through an unlike likeness, to be a figure, and not the truth; and in this the unlike likenesses lead our mind back more unto the truth, in that they do not permit it to remain in likeness alone. [0978C]
