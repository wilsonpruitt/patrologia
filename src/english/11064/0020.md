## EXPOSITION. (cont.)

Wherefore, he says, « I do not think that any of the well-wise would contradict » this — that dissimilar likenesses lead our mind back to the truth. For indeed « it follows, that men are led astray by the more precious sacred formations, » that is, it can follow, or come about, or easily happen, that through those formations of sacred things which are represented as more precious in the sacred discourse, the thoughts of men are led astray, « thinking certain celestial essences to be gold-formed, » just as in certain places of the Scriptures they are represented by likeness; and thinking also that there are in heaven certain flashing men clothed in comely garments, just as [0978D] angels are read to have appeared in splendid garments and with flashing countenances, « sprinkling forth, harmlessly, what is white and fiery, » that is, emitting, or pouring out, brightness and light; the white indeed as to the garments, and the fiery as to the flaming and burning countenances; harmlessly, that is, without injury — this itself holding firm in the very divine miracle, because in the divine and celestial natures, which are here shown by appearance, they are otherwise there according to truth: in which the appearance of visible nature is discerned, but the effect is not found.

There follows: « And by whatever other similar imagined forms, » that is, expressed according to imaginable things, « Theology has figured the celestial intellects, » that is, has represented the spiritual natures — so that from the things above [0979A] (understand) here it follows that men are led astray, thinking the celestial essences to exist in their own nature such as these. « Now that they might not suffer this — those who understand nothing higher than visible goods, » that is, that those might not think this who do not suppose there to be other goods higher, or better, than these visible goods; therefore « the restorative wisdom of the holy theologians wondrously descended to unseemly likenesses, » that is, therefore the holy theologians, who by their wisdom — which restores and reforms us unto the knowledge of truth — handed down to us the divine discourses, by a wondrous consideration descended to the taking up of unseemly likenesses, that they might fit them to the divine and celestial natures; and through these they might show that the other things too, which seem to be said of them magnificently and becomingly, are to be referred to likeness, and not to [0979B] property. This, then, the wisdom of the holy theologians did, « not granting that our material part, remaining in base images, should rest; but purging, bearing upward the powers of the soul, and suggesting by the deformity of the compositions that these are approved by neither the just nor the true; and that the super-celestial and divine spectacles are not, according to truth, so very like to things material and thus base. » As if he were saying: The wisdom of the theologians, descending to unseemly likenesses, in this very thing wondrously provided for our restoration, not granting that our material part — that is, our carnal sense, which cleaves to material things — should rest; the material part, I say, which remains in base images, that is, which so far as in it lies wills to remain, so that it may think only these things, [0979C] and accept only these as though true, if it were permitted to rest in them, and were not compelled by the very baseness of the images to go out to seek other things beautiful and true; or, not granting that our material part should rest, remaining in base images — that is, not granting that it either rest or remain, but by the very baseness of the images expelling it, and advancing it to higher things; but purging, bearing upward, namely, the power of the soul, that is, the intellectual force of the soul, which bears it upward and strains toward higher things; purging it from the admixture of images, that it may grow accustomed to contemplate spiritual and invisible things purely and simply; and suggesting, that is, admonishing and persuading the soul from the very deformity of the compositions, [0979D] that the super-celestial and divine spectacles are not, according to truth, very like to material things — especially to things so base — as neither the just nor the true approves, namely that those are like to these; or, suggesting by the deformity of the compositions that it is approved by neither the just nor the true — understand, in that manner, in the property of spiritual and invisible natures, as it is shown in the appearance and image of visible things; and suggesting also, that the super-celestial and divine spectacles are not very — that is, not much — like, according to their own truth, to material things thus base.

There follows: « But so too this must be understood: that none of the things which are is universally deprived of the participation of the good. For indeed, as the truth of the discourses [0980A] says, all things are very good. » Above he demonstrated that dissimilar representations are for this reason fittingly brought forward for the manifestation of divine things: that from those which evidently appear dissimilar, those too which seemed similar may be recognized to be outside the property of the thing. But now he demonstrates that for this reason also dissimilarities are not unfittingly taken up; because those too which seem to be dissimilar from the truth of divine and spiritual things have something in common with them, in which they can be called similar, since there is nothing in the universe which does not draw participation from the highest good; and through this — in that it participates with it — it fittingly represents its image and likeness. And so it behooves us not only to consider the former cause, but [0980B] also to understand this: that none of the things which universally are is deprived of the participation of the good. For « as the truth of the discourses says, » that is, the true discourses say, « all things are very good. » For thus it is written in Genesis: « God saw all the things that he had made, and they were very good [n: (Gen. I)]. » If therefore all things were good, in all things there was the good, and all things participated in the good, and from the good all things had something similar to the good; from which the good itself may be understood and known in them. Whence there follows: « It is possible, then, » that is, it happens, « from all » namely things « to understand good speculations; and to form, » or fit, « to invisible and intellectual things the likenesses called dissimilar; » namely those which above we called dissimilar: « to form, » I say, « from these very [0980C] material things, » that is, corporeal and visible ones. Yet in such a way that those things which, according to the likeness of visible things, are attributed to invisible things, be recognized to subsist otherwise in the visible things themselves, and otherwise in the invisible. For this indeed he says: « the intellectual things having them in another manner, » namely those things which are distributed otherwise to sensible things.

There follows: « For indeed anger is present in irrational beings from a passible motion; and their raging motion is full of all irrationality. » Now, certain examples having been set forth, he proves that those things which are referred from the nature of visible things to invisible things subsist otherwise here and otherwise there; as anger, and concupiscence, and the rest, which are referred from visible things to invisible things [0980D] by likeness; as if he were saying: Those things which are fitted from visible things to invisible things must be believed to be otherwise in the visible things themselves, and otherwise in the invisible; as can be plainly understood in the things which follow. For « anger » « in irrational beings, » that is, in those proceeding and acting irrationally — whether they have no reason, or, having reason, do not proceed according to reason. In these, indeed, anger is present from a passible motion, that is, one impetuous and fervent, and born according to a dominating passion; and their raging motion is full of all irrationality. For that motion is named anger which, seething wholly outside reason, is borne by the impulse of passion alone. « But in intellectual beings the irascible must be understood in another manner; » [0981A] in this manner, namely, that the irascible itself be understood in them as declaring their manly rationality, and their vast quiet in the divine and immutable foundations. For when anger is named in the spiritual and celestial nature, it is not an impulse or a motion that is named, but quiet and immutability that is understood. By this reasoning — these things being considered among the dissimilar and contrary: that just as in these beings anger strives by impulse and vehemence to drive off a supervening annoyance, so there an immovable quiet, persisting, is disturbed by no supervening shock — for this reason he calls that quiet itself "vast," that is, strong, and robust, and imperturbable; repelling every motion without motion, and suppressing every violence without shock and disturbance of itself; which quiet, however, [0981B] is not from themselves, but from the divine and immutable foundations to which they cleave, that is, from divine love and contemplation, by which they are made firm unto eternity, that they can in no way henceforth be moved. But this quiet, since it is not of necessity — as though they were held unwilling — but of will — as though, loving indefatigably, they are not forsaken — therefore he joined manly rationality to the quiet, showing that through illuminating reason they manfully reject the evils which they discern, and through affecting love they are at rest in the goods which they perceive, so that the quiet may be fortified by reason, lest it take on an affection for evils; and reason may be guarded by the quiet, lest, setting itself in opposition in hatred of evil, it overstep the bounds of tranquillity. This manly reason, then, and vast quiet, free and [0981C] absolute, and receiving no perturbation, is what anger — when fitted to divine things — signifies; because, as we have said, just as here anger repels an assailing annoyance through madness, so there a rational and voluntary quiet receives no shock, through its immutability.
