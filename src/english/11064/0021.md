## EXPOSITION. (cont.)

There follows: « In the same manner we say that concupiscence in irrational beings is a certain ill-considered and material passibility, engendered incontinently from a natural motion or from custom in mutable things, and an irrational continuity of bodily pleasure; at once compelling every animal toward the concupiscible according to sense. » After he demonstrated what anger signifies when attributed to sensible and material [0981D] things, and likewise what it signifies when fitted to immaterial and invisible natures, now he consequently shows the difference of concupiscence — whether when it is said of corporeal things, or when it is named in spiritual and divine things. Defining the concupiscence of corporeal beings, indeed, to be a certain passibility, or passion, that is, a dominating affection — ill-considered indeed, because it is not borne by reason, but is drawn by rash appetite into those things by whose delight it is affected; and material, that is, rising from the flesh and from the carnal sense, and showing forth things carnal and sensible — a passibility, I say, engendered either from a natural motion, namely when its appetite is according to nature; or from custom incontinently held in those mutable things, when its desire is borne outside nature, [0982A] or against it: and an irrational continuity of bodily pleasure; understand, we say that concupiscence itself is an irrational continuity, that is, a prolongation, or straining, or effusion of bodily pleasure — that is, of pleasure rising from the body, and tending toward corporeal things, and even compelling every animal, namely by the violence of its appetite, toward that which is concupiscible according to sense. And such indeed we understand the concupiscence of corporeal things to be. « But when, placing dissimilar likenesses about the intellectual and spiritual things, » or clothing them: « being formed about them, » that is, we adapt « to them concupiscence; » then we must understand that concupiscence itself not such as before, but rather divine love, [0982B] and a desire of immateriality, or incorporeality, and of divinity, existing above reason and intellect; since it can be loved more than investigated or understood; and inflexible, since it is always toward the one; and not needy, since what is loved is present. A desire, I say, of contemplation superessentially chaste and impassible — that is, of that thing which they contemplate superessentially, passing over in mind, namely, every essence and nature — the love of which thing is both chaste, since it brings no corruption upon the lover, and impassible, since, sweetly refreshing the one who desires, it does not afflict him. « And it must also be understood that concupiscence itself is a desire, tending toward that pure and most sublime brightness; and toward the invisible [0982C] and form-making beauty of the eternal, true, and invisible fellowship » — which beauty, namely, is called form-making, because it conforms to itself those turned toward it, that they may become beautiful, loving the true beauty, not as in the flesh and according to the flesh, where a lover of beauty can be base, and a possessor of beauty be found not good; but there he who loves, possesses; and he who loves to have, begins to be it. Such a love, then, and such a desire, we must understand in the concupiscence of spiritual and divine things.

There follows: « And it receives, as it were, a power in its sufficiency and in its convertibility; and is afflicted by none; by virtue, through the unconfused and immutable love of divine [0982D] beauty, and the universal recalling unto that which is truly to be desired. » As if he were saying: Concupiscence itself, or the chaste desire of eternal and invisible goods, because it is inflexible, as was said, and not needy, therefore has received, or receives, in its sufficiency and its convertibility toward God and toward divine things, as it were a certain power, or excellence and firmness, so that it cannot at all be afflicted by any contrary power, or violence, or strength. For no one can be hurt or afflicted, except in that which he loves; and therefore he who loves that alone which cannot be taken away can in no way be hurt, because he neither desires anything else outside it, having his sufficiency in it, nor is he anxious about its loss, holding it immutably. Because, then, [0983A] it is sufficient, it suffers no need; and because it is inflexible, it can undergo no violence. And this indeed is conferred on it through the unconfused and immutable love of divine beauty, and the universal recalling unto that which is truly to be desired. For because in them the love of God is unconfused, or, to say it otherwise, unmingled and pure, therefore their desire is not split toward diverse things. And because it is immutable, it is not bent away from the same object. And because they are universally recalled and gathered together unto that which is truly to be desired, it is disturbed by no exterior violence. For if with their whole desire they were not recalled to loving the truth, nor gathered together, without doubt they could be hurt and afflicted, wherever they cleaved in mind to mutable and transitory things. But now, because they are wholly there where all things [0983B] immutably stand fast, it comes about that they too, through the cohesion of love, cannot feel the mutability of affliction.

There follows: « But irrationality itself too, and insensibility, in irrational animals or in animated matters, we properly call a defect of reason and sense; but in immaterial and intellectual essences we confess, holily and becomingly, their supereminences as of super-mundane beings, surpassing our transitory and corporeal and material reason, and the mind's sense that is estranged from incorporeal things. » Other examples being set forth, he shows that the same things which are said of visible and invisible things are not said similarly, nor in the same manner. As if he were saying: Not only anger and concupiscence must be understood otherwise [0983C] here and otherwise there. For irrationality, which signifies the loftiness of reason; and insensibility, which demonstrates the excess of sensibility, when they are named here, that is, in irrational things and things not having sense, show a defect of reason and sensibility; but when they are said there, that is, in divine and intellectual natures, something above reason and sense is signified — not by defect, but by advance. For an excess of reason and sensibility happens either upward or downward: namely when something, either falling short of reason and sense, sinks down, so that it has not this; or, advancing beyond, transcends it, so that it has more. Whence also in Holy Scripture we find that holy men, breathed upon by the Spirit of God, at times suffered ecstasy, that is, an excess of mind [0983D]; since, led above human reason and sense, they went beyond reason and sense in this: that, reaching to that which was higher than reason, they began to be vivified in it and illumined by it. For them, then, the excess was in that they received more, just as for these others the excess came about in that they lost that very thing. Therefore, then, irrationality and insensibility in irrational animals and in inanimate matters we properly call a defect of reason and sense; but in immaterial and intellectual essences, as often as we name irrationality and insensibility, we confess not a defect of them, but rather their supereminences, inasmuch as they are super-mundane. We confess, I say, holily and becomingly, [0984A] that is, attributing nothing to them irreverently or unbecomingly; but, as befits the supereminences or excellences of super-mundane beings, that is, of spiritual essences, which surpass our transitory and corporeal reason — that is, a reason which can neither ascend above transitory things, nor comprehend anything at all outside corporeal things; and surpassing the material sense of our mind, estranged among corporeal things — that is, a sense flourishing only toward material things, and, through material and corporeal things, estranged from immaterial and incorporeal things, unable to perceive the same. Because, then, they are established above such reason, above such sense, through the excellence of contemplation, they are rightly reported to be estranged from such reason and sense — so that this very insensibility and irrationality of theirs [0984B] be understood not as stupor or ignorance, but to be something other — both a manly knowledge and a perception of the good; and not to be such as it is with us, or according to us.

There follows: « It is possible, therefore, to form for celestial things forms not discordant, even from the base parts of matter; since matter itself too, possessing its subsistence from the truly good, has, throughout its every material disposition, certain images of intellectual beauty; and it is possible through them to be led back to the immaterial first forms — the likenesses being taken, as was said, dissimilarly, and not similarly for the same things; yet compactly and beautifully, the intellectual and sensible properties being defined. » He subjoins another cause to the former, for which likenesses are fittingly drawn from visible things to invisible ones [0984C], even according to those things which seem contrary; namely not only because the things which are said are understood to subsist otherwise here and otherwise there, but for this reason too: because whatever things are here have, according to something, a likeness to those which subsist there, since both these and those are, whatever they are, from the one good; and according to the image which these possess toward that good, they also put forth the figure and likeness of those there. This is what he says: « It is possible, » that is, it happens, « to form for celestial things, » namely for the essences, forms not discordant, even « from the base parts of matter, » that is, of corporeal substance, since matter itself too, namely, possessing its subsistence from the truly good, has certain [0984D] images of intellectual beauty throughout its every material disposition: and through them, namely the images, it is possible for the mind to be led back to those first immaterial forms, that is, to the forms of immaterial and spiritual essences, which are prior to these — because these exist according to those, and these tend toward those, and these exist on account of those. It is possible, I say, to be led back from these — yet to those — the likenesses themselves being taken dissimilarly, so that, namely, the things which are here, and are said of those, be understood to be and subsist otherwise there. And the same properties being not similarly defined. But yet compactly, that is, fittingly, and aptly, and beautifully, that is, becomingly — understand, the same properties being defined, on both sides namely, both intellectual and insensible. He speaks of likeness [0985A] when, through extrinsic forms or figures or qualities, descriptions of invisible things are taken from visible ones; but of property, when the likeness is drawn from the inner nature. Whether, then, likenesses from visible things be set forth to be transferred extrinsically to the nature of invisible things, or properties be taken up intrinsically from those same visible and material things for the demonstration of invisible ones, the comparison and adaptation on both sides must be so tempered, that the likenesses themselves, which seem to be set forth similarly on both sides, be nevertheless taken dissimilarly, and be understood to be otherwise here, otherwise there; and the properties themselves, which seem to be attributed the same to both, be defined otherwise in these, and otherwise in those, and be understood to be different — so that to each may be attributed what is [0985B] fitting and apt to it according to its nature.
