## EXPOSITION. (cont.)

There follows: « We shall find the mystical theologians wondrously conforming these things not only to the declarations of the celestial dispositions, but sometimes also to the very divine manifestations. » He recalled the same thing above: namely that the mystical theologians — that is, theologians who narrate mystical and secret things — conform and fit these things, that is, these likenesses and properties taken from visible things, not only to the declarations of the celestial dispositions, that is, of the celestial orders, namely of the angelic spirits — that is, they set forth these likenesses and properties of visible things not only to declare or demonstrate the celestial dispositions and angelic orderings; [0985C] but they sometimes bring them also to the very divine manifestations, that is, to manifesting and indicating the divinity itself. « And sometimes indeed they praise it » namely the divinity, « from precious lights, » by comparing it to precious lights, and by demonstrating its praise through that thing which, among the other visible things, is precious and comely, as, for example, when they call it the sun — not this visible one, which illumines even the bodily eyes for perpetrating iniquity; but the sun of justice, which enlightens the spiritual eyes for knowing truth and virtue. And as when they call it the « morning star, » whose rising puts darkness to flight — not rising to illumine this world visibly, [0985D] but rising into the rational mind, which alone can receive this light, holily, that is, for making sanctification. And as also when they call it « light shining without being wrapped about, and invisibly; » not as this visible light, which can be both darkened by shadows, and wrapped about, and enclosed in place, and constrained by a bound. If, then, mystical theology praises the excellence of the divine majesty through the appearances and forms of the highest and most precious things. « But sometimes from the middle things, » understand, which in the order of creation are neither highest nor lowest, that theology praises the divine majesty, namely by calling it fire; not such as this corporeal fire is, which, though it profit by illumining, harms by burning and consuming; but « fire harmlessly shining; » [0986A] namely illumining, and not burning up; kindling, and not consuming. And as when it calls it « water, giver of vital fullness; » which gives, namely, the fullness of life, and fills with life those who draw and carry it; and water also, « that it be so said symbolically, » that is, figuratively, « entering into the belly; and overflowing with rivers immeasurably flowing back. » For all these things are said of it not in property, but in figure alone. In all these things mystical theology praises the majesty and goodness of the divinity. « But sometimes also from the lowest things » — as from the inferior and earthly ones — it praises it, and manifests its praise figuratively, as when it names it « sweet ointment, » and likewise when it calls it « corner [0986B] stone. » And to such a degree does it fit the appearances of lowly things to it by likeness, that sometimes it seems even to descend, in describing it, to unfitting and contrary forms. Which nevertheless, according to the cause mentioned above, is carried through by a fitting and necessary dispensation. « But they even set a bestial form about it. » As if he were saying: Not only in the declaration of the divinity do the theologians take up likenesses from inferior things, but (what seems marvelous!) they even descend to contrary and dissimilar formations — ones which, if they were understood according to property, would be wholly unworthy of its majesty. « For they even set a bestial form about it; and to it the particularity of a lion and of a panther, » that is, the appearance, or form, or figure, « they fit; [0986C] and they clothe it, » namely the divinity: « with a leopard-like form, » understand form, that is, the form of a leopard. « And they clothe it, » understand again the divinity; a raging she-bear, that is, with the appearance of a raging she-bear; or they call it « a raging she-bear, » so that in both the incongruity may appear, since they attribute even deformity to the beautiful, and cruelty to the mild.

There follows: « But I will add also that which has seemed to be, of all things, among the base, and to signify more. » As if he were saying: Although in the preceding things which have been mentioned, and in the rest of this kind, a great unworthiness may seem to appear, nevertheless in this which I subjoin a much greater affront appears. « But I will add, » I will indeed presume, and I shall seem to speak boldly, [0986D] I who add this too to the divine significations, « what has seemed to be the basest of all; » and through this, according to the aforesaid reasoning by which dissimilar symbols make the declaration greater, it is proved to signify more than the rest. Which nevertheless perhaps a man would not dare to fit to the divine majesty, if the wisdom of God itself did not take these things to itself by spontaneous condescension. « For those wise in divine things have handed it down as forming itself even under the appearance of a worm. » As it is written: « I am a worm, and no man; the reproach of men, and the outcast of the people [n: (Psal. XXI)]. » For unless it had first said this of itself, who would dare to say this of it? For since nothing seems to be baser and lowlier than a worm, who would presume to bring the highest majesty down to this abasement, [0987A] unless it had first, by its own dispensation, formed itself about with such an appearance, signifying itself thereby. Therefore it deigned first to say this of itself, because it knew that human conscience would not, of itself, dare this against its own Creator. « Thus all the theosophists, and the prophets by hidden inspiration, distinguish the Holy of Holies from the undefiled holy things. » Theosophists, that is, those wise in divine things and who have been made prophets by hidden inspiration. All these, when they wish to designate invisible things through visible appearances and the properties of visible things, distinguish, as we demonstrated above, « the Holy of Holies from the undefiled holy things. » The undefiled holy things are the symbols of divine things taken from beautiful and becoming forms; the Holy of Holies are the divine things themselves, which [0987B] are figured through these. The theosophists, then, by the fact that they attribute dissimilar figures to divine things, also separate the similar formations — even those which seemed worthy — from the excellence and majesty of those divine things. For because they fit even those figures which are not doubted to be foreign to the signification of them, they show that the others too, which could seem true, are not, according to property, similar. Thus, then, in one and the same thing, both in the similar figures they separate the figure from the truth, and in the dissimilar ones, by the fellowship of truth, they honor the figure of truth — since those figures, when they are shown to be foreign, are distinguished, and these, when they are fitted to likeness, are honored. For thus it was fitting that all that was made, compared to the excellence of the Creator, should be shown both in sublime things not [0987C] to be equal, and in the lowest things not to be dissimilar. For because it was made, it cannot be comparable unto equality; and because it was made by him, it cannot be wholly dissimilar to him by whom it was made.

There follows: « So that neither may divine things be rightly received by the unclean, nor may the zealous of the contemplation of marvelous images remain in the figures as though they were true. » This, then, is effected through dissimilar figurations: that in the consideration of them the unclean of heart, and those unworthy of the knowledge of truth, may be the more blinded; and those who are zealous, in the contemplation of marvelous images — that is, of sacred representations marvelously made — may be the more exercised. For thus on both sides [0987D] a just design is accomplished: that while the truth, in its manifestation, covers itself as with a base garment, it both provokes the unworthy to contempt of it, and invites the worthy to desire and to seek that appearance of its own which lies hidden, so that they be not content with what they look upon outwardly, but, repelled by the very deformity of the exterior demonstration, do not remain in the figures as though they were true — since a sign cannot be the truth, even when it is a sign of the truth.

There follows: « They therefore honor divine things by true negations, and by the diverse likenesses of imaginations compacted unto the lowest things. » As if he were saying: Since negations made concerning divine things signify their truth more expressly, and dissimilar formations of images demonstrate their purity more evidently, [0988A] therefore the holy theologians do no affront to the divine things themselves, both by making true negations concerning them, and by attributing dissimilar formations to them, but rather they honor the divine things themselves by true negations, by which they show their excellence to be so great that what they are can in no way be expressed, even if in some way what they are not can be said; and they honor them also by likenesses diverse from their sublimity through a humble formation; and by the likenesses of imaginations compacted unto the lowest things — that is, composed and joined together out of the lowest things, that is, out of the basest and vile appearances of corruptible things.

There follows: « Nothing therefore is inconsequent, if they also form the celestial essences from unfitting, dissimilar [0988B] likenesses, according to the causes stated. » As if he says: Since divine things are honored by true negations and by diverse formations, nothing is inconsequent — that is, unfitting — if the theologians form, that is, represent, the celestial essences from dissimilar and unfitting likenesses; that is, through representations of figures dissimilar from their nature, and in a certain way unfitting. It is not inconsequent, I say, according to the causes stated above.
