## EXPOSITION.

Up to this point the theologian has discoursed on the things that had to be said in a general way for those being introduced into the knowledge of the hierarchies, in proportion to the visible demonstrations set forth for the signifying of visible things. Then now, entering upon the principal exposition, he first defines what a hierarchy is; not universally, however, but with a restricted meaning, only according to that hierarchy which consists in angels and [0992B] men. « A hierarchy, » he says, « is, in my view, a divine order, and knowledge, and activity, likening the deiform as far as is possible; and ascending, in proportion to the illuminations divinely implanted in it, into the likeness of God. » What he says, « in my view, » is to be taken as though he had said, according to my own estimation. For he modestly tempers his assertion, lest he seem to presume upon his own estimation more than is right. Then he sets forth three principal things in the definition of a hierarchy, which complete the very definition of a hierarchy. And these are: order, knowledge, and activity. If any one of all these three be lacking, there is no hierarchy. The first is a divine order; because there is no power, if it is not ordained [0992C] by God; for this reason he says, a divine order. For « all » « power is from God; and the things that are from God are all good and ordained; therefore he who resists the power ordained by God, resists God [n: (Rom. XIII)]. » For this reason, then, a hierarchy is a divine order, that is, a power ordained by God and disposed according to God. For it is from God by ordination; and according to God by imitation; and therefore a divine order, because it is from God, that it may be; and it is according to God, as to what kind and how great it is. Then, because every power that is ordained by God is ordained for the accomplishing and completing of something, there follows in the definition, after order, « knowledge and activity. » Knowledge, indeed, by which they may understand what is to be done; [0992D] but activity, by which they may accomplish what they have understood must be done. In order, duty; in knowledge, discernment; in activity, ministry. Without order, activity is presumption; without activity, order is negligence; but without knowledge, both activity is blameworthy and order is useless. For these reasons, then, a hierarchy is a divine order, and knowledge, and activity. A hierarchy, I say, both in order and in knowledge and in activity, likening the deiform, that is, imitating conformity to God as far as is possible for it; and ascending into the likeness of God in proportion to the illuminations, that is, according to the illuminations divinely implanted in it: each one, namely, according to the manner and measure of the grace divinely infused into it, accomplishing its task in its own order, and ascending to the imitation of God, so that by discerning rightly and working well [0993A] it may imitate him. For in both respects every hierarchy emulates the divine likeness: namely, both in that it is disposed by him, and in that it works according to him. But this definition, as we have said, embraces only the angelic and human hierarchy, which were made after the likeness of the supreme and eternal hierarchy; and, being disposed according to it, they imitate it.

There follows: « The divine beauty, as simple, and as best because consummative; it is pure, indeed, universally from all unlikeness; but distributive, according to the dignity of each, of its proper light; and perfective in the most divine sacrifice, according to the unchangeable formation of those made perfect, closely joined unto it. » The sense is this. That the divine beauty, [0993B] which we name the supreme hierarchy, according to which the other hierarchies were made, is universally pure, that is, wholly so, inasmuch as it is always simple in unity, best in goodness, consummative in perfection. For where there is unity, there is no diversity; and where there is no diversity, there can be no unlikeness. Likewise, where there is perfection, there is no degree; where there is no degree, there is no difference; where there is no difference, there is no unlikeness. Therefore the divine beauty, which is the form and exemplar of all things well and beautifully disposed — because it is one, it does not admit plurality; and because it is best, and consummate, and not only consummate but also consummative of all things to be consummated, and the cause of consummation, it does not admit diversity, and thereby [0993C] knows no unlikeness at all; it that is both one in simplicity and the same in perfection. And although in itself it is such that it is neither divided by plurality nor made lesser by diversity, yet in those who become partakers of grace it is distributive of its proper light, bestowing its proper light in diverse ways, according to the dignity, namely, of each of the participants — granting to the higher greater, but to the lower lesser, gifts of graces, so that it may be beautifully multiplied in them, it which in itself truly abides one. The divine beauty itself is also perfective, since it perfects and makes the partakers of its lights consummate; perfective, I say, in the most divine sacrifice, by which it perfects those to be perfected according to the unchangeable formation of those closely joined and made perfect unto it. By the most divine [0993D] sacrifice he means the divine illumination itself, and grace, and propitiation; by which all who are to be purged and to be saved are purged and cleansed, not only from the corruption of evil, that they may become good, but they are purged also from the defect of good, that they may stand present as better. It is therefore the supreme offering, and the *teletargis*, that is, the victim of the principal purgation; namely, the divine grace itself which is offered to us and offered for us. It is offered to us for purgation; it is offered for us for propitiation. It is offered to us, that we may have it; it is offered for us, that through it we may be pleasing. It is offered to us by infusion; it is offered for us by cleansing. It is offered to us while we begin to be what we were not; it is offered by us, while we exhibit and present [0994A] what we are. It is therefore the *teletargis* itself, that is, the victim of the principal purgation, and the most divine sacrifice, without which all victims and all sacrifices can have neither effect nor profit; it is that by which the divine beauty perfects, and makes perfect those who are perfect unto it — that is, reformed unto its likeness, that is, harmoniously, so that, namely, they may not diverge from it — according to the unchangeable formation of those very ones made perfect, unto it, that is, according to its likeness, which, once received, they keep unchangeably, so that they do not flow away from it. For the divine beauty, which in itself is one and perfect, purges and perfects those to be perfected unto itself through the victim of the principal purgation — that is, the infusion of its grace, which from its own fullness it poured out into participation for the sake of those to be purged and [0994B] perfected — according to the manner and measure and capacity of each, which they received by the gift of that same grace, according to the unchangeable formation of them unto it; which formation, namely, is called unchangeable, as we have said, either because it turns those who are formed and reformed toward unchangeability; or because it abides unchangeable in him from whom it is, even if it be changed for those for whom it is and in whom it is.

There follows: « The interpretation [ *intentio* ], therefore, of a hierarchy is likeness and unity with God, as far as is possible. » What in Greek is called σκοπός *scopos,* and what the translator calls interpretation, is more properly named *intentio* or *directio*. For the intention [0994C] or direction which is called *scopos* is a fixed aiming toward some end. For every action tends toward some end, and tends by some direction. But *scopas*, that is, the direction or aim, is that by which it tends. Every hierarchy, therefore, has a *scopon*, that is, a direction according to which it may proceed in fulfilling its ministry; namely, the imitation and divine likeness, so that, just as it has been ordained by him in office and dignity, so according to him it may proceed in ministry and operation, so that it presume to do nothing beyond the manner and measure assigned by him and owed to its own order. The interpretation, then — that is, the definition, or rather the direction and contemplation of a hierarchy, toward which every hierarchy ought to aim and does direct itself — is likeness and unity, that is, [0994D] imitation of and identity with God, so that in nothing it may deviate or decline from his likeness, in which it has been placed, but may emulate him in all things as far as is possible. Likeness to God is to imitate him; but unity is to follow him alone. And this is the *scopos*, that is, the direction or aim of every hierarchy: that according to him it may proceed toward him, having him as the leader of all holy knowledge and activity, following him in judgment and in action — illumined by him unto the knowledge of truth, and aided unto the carrying out of good work. « Having him as leader, » presiding within in the demonstration of truth, and going before without in the example of good action. « And unto its own most divine comeliness defining unchangeably indeed; [0995A] but, as far as is possible, it reforms, and perfects its own praisers as divine images. » The hierarchy itself, namely, defining unchangeably — that is, invariably or inflexibly turned, or aiming through its *scopos* toward the most divine comeliness of God himself, by imitating and following him, so that it may be conformed to his beauty and most divine comeliness, according to which every hierarchy is beautifully and becomingly disposed in its own order and rank: as far as is possible it reforms its own praisers, that is, those who in it praise God, and are disposed and ordained unto the praise of God: it reforms them, I say, in this very thing, that it makes them imitators of God, and turns them unto his likeness in their ministry; and by turning and reforming them it perfects them as divine *agalmata*, so that they themselves may be divine [0995B] *agalmata*, that is, holy images and receptacles of divinity, and most bright mirrors; so that they themselves may be divine *agalmata*, indeed, by perfecting the divine light, but most bright mirrors, to shine from the light received. He also perfects those praisers of his as « pure receptacles, » or vessels « of the principal light, and of the divine ray » — that is, of the light immediately illuminating, and descending to illuminate those first things — so that, after the light is received, they may be filled with the sacred brightness implanted in them, that is, infused; and so that they may also be things declaring it, namely the brightness — « again, » that is, in the second place, « in those things which follow, declaring it » abundantly, according to « the divine laws. » The sense is this: Since a hierarchy, according to that disposition by which it imitates the divine beauty, [0995C] makes its praisers universally — that is, whether the higher or the lower — such that they are worthy both to receive the divine light and to shine from the light: but in particular it makes certain ones so pure that they are capable of the principal light, and are immediately illumined by God, and then transfuse their light to those who follow after them and are set beneath them in dignity: administering, indeed, abundantly, out of the abundance of the first reception, the second participation — provided only that the divine law is observed, in which it is prescribed for each one what, or how much, or to whom he ought to impart out of the gift of grace granted to him. For that it is not without the divine law — that is, that divine dispensation by which the gifts of grace are bestowed upon the partakers of the largess according to [0995D] a fixed measure and proportion, and duties are assigned, so that each one may know how much is permitted to him according to the ministry assigned, or is permitted to be done — he makes clear when he adds:
