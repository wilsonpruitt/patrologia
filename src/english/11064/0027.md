## EXPOSITION. (cont.)

[0998D]There follows: « The divine beatitude (as far as it must be said among men) is indeed pure at once from all unlikeness; but full of eternal light: perfect, and at once not lacking any perfection; purging, and illuminating, and perfecting. » Now he wishes to demonstrate that the good which, in the creature — whether purged, or illumined, or perfected — stands by grace, in the Creator himself, from whom it is, subsists by nature: whose divine beatitude is both pure without purgation, and shining without illumination, and perfect without reception. For it is not purged in order that it may be pure; nor is it illumined in order that it may shine; nor does it receive in order that it may be perfected. But having the whole in itself, it does not lack what is another's; and, administering its own, it fills those things which of themselves are in need. It is indeed pure, he says, from all unlikeness equally; [0999A] this, then, is the reason for which it is rightly named pure, and has no unlikeness whatsoever. For where there is purity, there is no unlikeness; because it is the very same throughout, and a concordant one. For corruption introduces unlikeness, and makes other that which withdraws from its own being, and fails as though it were other than it was. That, therefore, which is always the same admits no unlikeness; and that which perseveres unchangeable admits no corruption. Therefore the divine beatitude is pure, and incorrupt, and foreign to all unlikeness, abiding in that which it is, and keeping incorrupt what it has. It is also full of eternal light, yet not as if illumined, since it is itself light; nor beginning to shine, since it is eternal; nor growing in light, since it is [0999B] full. It is also perfect, and at once not lacking any perfection, that is, lacking nothing that pertains to perfection; because it has and possesses the whole, and it is itself the whole which it possesses: and therefore it can be neither greater, because it has the whole; nor lesser, since it has it unchangeably and eternally. And although in itself it is such that it needs neither to be purged, being pure; nor to be illumined, being full of light; nor to be perfected, being consummate: yet it purges, and illumines, and perfects all who merit to be purged, and illumined, and perfected — whether receiving grace immediately in the first place, or partaking mediately through those who first merited to receive it. But this whole, namely that the divine beatitude is called full, and full of light, and perfect: is to be said as far as among men, that is, [0999C] according to that mode by which that which is ineffable can be said by men. For as regards that ineffable purity of the supreme truth, the divine nature itself is rather to be called holy purgation, and illumination, and perfection, so that it may be understood to be little that it is called pure, but rather purgation itself; that it is called illumined, but rather illumination itself; that it is called perfect, but rather perfection itself: which itself, however, is still found to be too little, unless there be thought purgation above all purgation, illumination above all illumination, perfection above all perfection. For this reason he says: « Purgation, illumination, perfection, above purgation, above light, before perfect, » or more than perfect, that is, above perfection; because all this that is said is said according to [0999D] something, from which he who supremely is is far removed, and therefore he is above all this, which is. Therefore it is purgation, since it receives nothing defiled into itself, and takes away defilement or corruption from those receiving and suffering corruption: and yet above purgation, since it touches no corruption. And it is illumination, since it shines in itself, and makes dark things grow bright from itself, and above illumination, since, irradiating and penetrating all things, it does not go out from itself; and it is perfection, since it has nothing less in itself, and to those having less it grants what is lacking, and supplies it out of itself; and yet before perfect, or more than perfect, since it stands wholly in the singularity of good, and undivided in participation, so that its good is neither less [1000A] because it is one, nor manifold because it is participated. For this reason, « it is perfect through itself, » because what it has it did not receive from elsewhere; and « through itself the principle of perfection, » because, by granting to those to be perfected that which it has, it itself does not lose it. And it is « the cause of every hierarchy, and of every holy thing, according to its supereminent loftiness. » It is a principle, since it is from it; and a cause, since it is through it, and on account of it; and a form, since according to it every hierarchy in the order of dignity, and every holy thing in the ministry of action, is dispensed. From it, by predestination; through it, by creation; on account of it, by glorification; unto it, by conversion, that it may imitate it and be beatified in it.

There follows: « It behooves, therefore, as I suppose, etc. » [1000B] After he has demonstrated that the divine beatitude is the form and cause of every sacred power and disposition; and that it is both pure, and shining, and perfect: pure indeed and clean in that it lacks all unlikeness and confusion; shining in that it contains in itself the fullness of eternal light; but perfect in that, having all things, it lacks nothing. Now he infers, proving that those too who are ordained unto its likeness and imitation in the sacred dispositions ought likewise to be pure from all contagion and confusion, and shining with truth, and perfect in goodness, so that they too may be able to cleanse, and illumine, and perfect others by doctrine and by example, so that in both respects they may rise up unto the image and likeness of their Creator, [1000C] namely, both in that they themselves are pure, and clear, and perfect, and in that they cleanse, and illumine, and perfect others. « It behooves, » he says, « as I suppose, that those to be purged indeed » — namely, whether those who in the first place, or those who, with others mediating, receive purgation — « be made altogether pure, and be freed from all confusion of unlikeness, » which either ignorance of the true, or concupiscence of evil, has introduced. « But those to be illumined it behooves to be filled with the divine light, » inasmuch as they are to be led back, in the most chaste eyes of the mind, « unto a contemplative habitude and power » — that is, so that, the eyes of the mind being chaste and pure from the preceding purgation of sin and of error, they « may have a contemplative habitude and power. » A habitude, namely, through purity of mind; but a power through [1000D] the stability of contemplation, so that they may be able to contemplate divine things, which they both truly apprehend, and retain perseveringly. « But those to be restored from the imperfect it behooves to become partakers of the perfective knowledge of the explored holy things, » so that, namely, they may apprehend the true goods, and those holy things which they explore through perfect knowledge, by following them with love and partaking of them with holiness; so that, just as through perfect knowledge they are perfected in cognition, so through perfect goodness they may be perfected in participation. « But the purgers, by the greatness of purgation, to hand on to others out of their proper chastity, » that is, the purgers themselves, whose ministry it is, namely, that through them others be purged from error and fault: it behooves them to be such that, by the greatness [1001A] of their own purgation, that is, of their own cleanness which they have in themselves, they may hand on purgation to others by their proper chastity, chastening the life of those to be purged by their own, not another's, example. « But the illuminators it behooves to convey their supereminent light upon those who are worthy of light, » inasmuch as they have more luminous souls for the participation of light, by which they themselves receive light, and for the distribution of light, by which they transfuse the light received to others to be illumined: « having » I say « properly, » that is, singularly and excellently, because those who ought to illumine others ought to shine more than others: « and most richly, » that is, they ought to be abundantly filled with the sacred brightness, so that out of the abundance of reception they may be able to bestow upon individuals what is needful. For he who ought to teach all ought to have the knowledge of all; [1001B] because he cannot administer to each one what is expedient, who does not know the causes of all things, and their utilities. And they ought to convey their light aloft, so that what they teach by word they may commend by the excellence of life, as though shining on high, and pouring lights upon those who remain lower in their manner of life. But the perfecters it behooves to perfect those to be perfected by most sacred doctrine, through the knowledge of the holy things beheld, as teachers of the perfective knowledge. He truly beholds holy things who knows the habit through experience; he truly beholds holy things who has perceived the interior goods by tasting. He therefore has most sacred doctrine who teaches what he savors; who instructs in what he feels; who teaches not only to know the true, but to apprehend the good [1001C] and to love the just. Which handing-on is for this reason called perfective, because this alone leads a man to the perfect, when he apprehends, through the pursuit of good work, the goods which he could not know through the understanding. By such doctrine, therefore, the perfecters themselves ought to perfect those to be perfected, so that they may be holy and perfect in goodness, imitating him unto whose form and likeness they are reformed — the superiors by bestowing, and the inferiors by receiving, the participation of divine grace.
