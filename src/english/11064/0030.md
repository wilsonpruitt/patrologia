## EXPOSITION.

After he has shown — the general definition having been given and set forth — how the hierarchy is to be understood according to the angelic and human ordering (for the divine hierarchy, which is infinite, cannot be defined), he now begins to treat especially of the angelic hierarchy, first showing how the names of the angels, which have been given by men and after the manner of men, are to be understood in that spiritual and heavenly nature. He links what precedes to the narrative that follows, saying: « Therefore, what the hierarchy is, as [1005D] I think, the angelic hierarchy having been well defined by us, is next to be praised; and its wondrous fashionings of forms in the sacred words are to be beheld with supramundane eyes. » As if he should say: After we have, according to our estimation, well shown what the hierarchy is from the definition of it set forth, now it follows that we praise the angelic hierarchy — that is, that we show how greatly it has been praised in the Holy Scriptures, or that we demonstrate how praiseworthy it appears from the things that have been said about it. And to demonstrate this it is fitting that we behold with supramundane eyes, that is, with spiritual eyes and eyes that see spiritually, the wondrous fashionings, that is, the compositions or adaptations of forms, which in the sacred words are [1006A] attributed to it, in order to demonstrate its invisible nature. For the things that are set forth visibly are to be beheld with spiritual eyes for this reason: lest what is seen be thought to be the only thing that is, lest the mind remain in that which it looks upon outwardly. But through that which is set forth outwardly for demonstration, it is invited to contemplate that which is truer and more sublime.

Therefore there follows: « That we may ascend to the most deiform » — namely of the angels — « simplicity through the mystical formations. » Therefore, he says, « the wondrous fashionings of forms are to be beheld with supramundane eyes, so that through those very mystical formations » — considered outwardly — being roused, we may inwardly « ascend to the most deiform » « simplicity » of the angels themselves. For it is as though a certain multiplicity belongs to the angels [1006B], through which the truth of the mystical formations is demonstrated outwardly. But their simplicity is the unity of their spiritual and unvarying nature. Therefore in their multiplicity they do not have deiformity, but in their simplicity; because in that part in which they are shown through visible forms, they take on the likeness of corporeal nature. But in that part in which they themselves are understood as spiritual and as an incorporeal nature, they look toward the image and likeness of God. Which likeness is called not only deiform but even most deiform; because, although the very same is found in men too, yet in the angels it is believed to be more excellent.

There follows: « And at the same time we shall praise the principle of the knowledge of every hierarchy in divine religiousness, and [1006C] in most perfect acts of thanksgiving. » In this, he says, that we shall praise the angelic hierarchy, at the same time we shall also praise the divine and highest hierarchy, which is the principle of all the knowledge of the hierarchy — that is, the knowledge which every hierarchy has, and through which every hierarchy is ordered, because every hierarchy is both ordered through it and illumined by it. « We shall praise, I say, the principle of every hierarchy, » of knowledge — yet not by definition and demonstration as we praise the angelic hierarchy, nor by definition and comprehension as we praise the human, of which the one, invisible, is demonstrated through visible things, the other, visible, is known in itself. But we shall praise it with divine religiousness and most perfect acts of thanksgiving. [1006D] For that which is unthinkable is not praised by demonstration, nor is that which is incomprehensible praised by comprehension. Therefore God, who cannot be searched out and comprehended, can be praised by religion and thanksgiving alone. If, then, you cannot praise by speaking, praise by living. What the tongue does not unfold, a good life commends; he who is not searched out through knowledge is attained by good will. If, then, you do not comprehend him, by living according to him, reach toward him; this is to praise with divine religion. Again, if you cannot speak him, you can speak the things that have been given by him; to commend his best gifts, to proclaim him ineffably good — this is to praise with a most perfect act of thanksgiving. Thus, then, we shall praise our principle with divine religiousness [1007A] and most perfect acts of thanksgiving. But in the very praising of our principle, first of all this must be called to mind: how the divine goodness, which is poured out upon all things, first brought into being the things to be created; then established beneath itself the things to be governed, and reformed unto itself the things to be nourished. This is what he says: « First of all together, » that is, first before all things, one must « speak that true thing, » that is, speak that truth, « namely how, » that is in what manner, the superessential divinity « by its universal goodness brought into being the essences of the things that are; substituting, » after creation, namely disposing and ordering, or establishing beneath itself, and ordering according to itself. « For it is, » etc. As if he should say: For this reason the divine goodness recalls to itself and reforms the things which it created, [1007B] because it is proper to it, out of its innate kindness, to call to its own communion the things which have received being from it, so that they may be able to be blessed — namely, as far as each one, according to the order of its condition and its measure, can be capable of that participation. « For this, he says, is proper to the cause of all things, and to the goodness that is above all things, » that is, to the goodness which is the cause of all things, because through it all things were made, and is above all things, because it draws to itself the things made by it; this, namely, is proper to it: to call the things that are to its own communion — that is, as it is defined or dispensed to each one of the things that are according to its proper analogy, that is, its measure, and mode, and order. For in this very thing the beauty [1007C] of the universe is perfected: that not all things in one and the same way, but each single thing according to its own order and grade, are variously and manifoldly recalled to the communion of the divine goodness — so that, in that they are not forsaken, the work of goodness may be completed; but in that they are variously disposed unto the comeliness and beauty of all, the work of wisdom may be perfected. « All things, therefore, that are, participate in the providence flowing from the superessential and most causal divinity. » Since, he says, this is proper to the divine goodness — that it recall all things to participation in itself, so that, turned back to it, the things which proceeded from it in order to receive being may subsist — therefore « all things that are participate in providence, » that is, in the provided good and the provided grace flowing from [1007D] the divinity itself, as from a fountain and the first principle of all goodness, which is superessential, because in the excellence of its nature it is supereminent over all essences and subsisting natures; and is most causal, that is, the cause of all causes and the first cause, since from its goodness proceeds every good which it offers to be participated by all the things founded by it, that they may subsist. « For perhaps they would not be, except by the assumption of the essences of the things that are, and of their principle. » From this, he says, it can be proved that all things that are participate in divine providence, because they could not otherwise subsist, unless they subsisted by participation in the divine goodness itself, from which all things received being, and in which all things subsist. For all things that are, unless they had received their principle from the divine goodness, [1008A] would not have begun; and unless they had their essence in it, they would not remain in that which they are. Therefore it is the principle of all things, by assuming and participating in which they begin; and it is the essence of all things, by assuming and participating in which they subsist.

Whence there follows: « All existing things, therefore, participate in its being. » As if he should say: Because without it nothing can subsist, it is manifest that all things which subsist participate in its being. Therefore the divine nature, being one and the same in itself, as regards effect and power and operation, is, to all subsisting essences and natures created by it, both the principle from which they receive being, and the essence of all in which they subsist; and it is the life, not [1008B] of all indeed (because not all things live), but is the life of all living things, from which and through which they are made alive and live; and it is the wisdom, not of all existing or living things — because not all existing or living things are wise — but is the wisdom of all wise things; and it is the intellect and reason, from which and through which they are illumined and are wise and understand and discern.

This is what he says: « For the being of all things is the super-being of the divinity, » because through the being of the divinity, which is above all being, whatever is has being and subsists. « But living things, » which now receive from it not only being but also life, by participating in that same life which is above all life, and [1008C] by participating in that same life-giving power, are made alive and live. « But rational and intellectual things, » by participating in « that same » wisdom itself, existing « above all reason and intellect, perfect through itself and pre-perfect, » that is, by participating in the wisdom perfect above all things, reason and understand and are wise. They reason indeed by searching out, they understand by knowing, they are wise by participating — so that one and the same divine goodness is both essence to subsisting things, and life to living things, and wisdom to wise things. From which it is clear that those created things are nearer to the creating nature in likeness and in truth which have received more from it as a gift.

[1008D]This is what follows: « And it is clear that those of the essences are around it which have received innumerably from it. » Around it, namely the divine nature — that is, they are near and neighboring to it, and immediately joined to it, those of the essences, that is those essences or natures, whichever, after being itself was given, have received innumerably or manifoldly from it the gifts of powers. Among which in the first place are reckoned those heavenly and spiritual natures of the angels, which are rational not only through the subtlety of wisdom, because they discern by intellect; but are also intellectual through the subtlety of their spiritual nature, because they are perceived in their nature by intellect alone, and do not touch the sense of the body; and through this they have been made above all things that are, because they live; and above [1009A] all things that live irrationally, because they discern.
