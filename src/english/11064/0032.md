## EXPOSITION. (cont.)

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1012D]This is what he says: To the holy ones there have been made theophanies according to certain sacred manifestations befitting God, and through manifestations of proportional visions to those who see. ⟦s2⟧ Which, namely, were so proportionally tempered to the capacity of those seeing, that the more imperfect indeed perceived the truth of heavenly things by an inferior and more imperfect kind of vision; but the more excellent came to know the divine secrets in a more sublime manner. ⟦s3⟧ And yet, whether in these or in those, whatever could come from God unto human knowledge was less than himself, and in a certain manner beneath himself. ⟦s4⟧ For hence it is written: « The things that were under him filled the temple [n: (Isai. VI)]: » because everything that the human mind in this life can grasp of the knowledge [1013A] of him cannot be made equal to his ineffable majesty. ⟦s5⟧ Nevertheless Holy Scripture has been accustomed to call those manifestations, by which God reveals himself to human minds, [sic: *theopbanias*], that is, divine apparitions; because, although that which is discerned is not God by nature, according to demonstration it is, since through it and in it God is manifested.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ This is what follows: « That most wise theology, therefore, revealed that vision which is described in it to be a divine one, and to be beautifully called a theophany, as a likeness of formless things in a form, from the leading-back of those who see unto the divine. » « That most wise theology itself, he says, » that is, Holy Scripture, which wisely brings forth the divine secrets unto human knowledge, « revealed that that vision which is described in it is beautifully, » that is, fittingly, called [1013B] a theophany — inasmuch as it is « a divine likeness of formless things, » that is, of spiritual natures, and of natures not having corporeal form, expressed in a visible form, or taken according to visible things. ⟦s2⟧ It revealed, I say, that it is beautifully called a theophany from the leading-back of those who see unto divine things — that is, because through it those who see are led back to knowing divine things, as though, through it, divine things were made illumination to those who see, and indeed the divine things themselves holily perfecting them; that is, not only because it manifested divine things to those who see, but because it also made the seers themselves divine.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ There follows: « But these divine visions our glorious fathers accomplished, through the mediating heavenly powers, » [1013C] that is, by the heavenly powers mediating, through which they were brought down to the knowledge of men: our glorious fathers attained, and obtained, and perfectly comprehended those visions.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ There follows: « Does not the tradition of the words also say that the sacred legislation was given to Moses by God, as it were through himself, so that it may also truly teach us that they are divine and sacred characters? » Does not, he says, « the tradition of the words, » that is, the authority of the Scriptures, « say that the sacred legislation, » that is, the giving of the sacred law, « was given to Moses by God through himself, » namely commemorating no mediator in the giving of the law between God and Moses. ⟦s2⟧ But testifying that God himself, through himself, spoke to Moses: so that it may also teach us that it, namely the law, are sacred and divine [1013D] characters, that is, signs of sacred and divine things, so that through the things which were done visibly in the legislation it may show certain other invisible things signified and demonstrated, as it is written: « Thou shalt therefore make the tabernacle according to the pattern which was shown thee in the mount [n: (Exod. XXVI)]. » According to this manner, therefore, the visible manifestation itself and the material address, by which the invisible God willed to show himself to men, is said to be God, or to be of God, because in it the invisible God came forth unto manifestation, and brought forth to knowledge the things of his own which were hidden. ⟦s3⟧ In which visible manifestation, nevertheless, he worked all things by the mediating ministry of the angels, so that that which was made visible might be said to be both of God, by the first [1014A] authority, and of the angels, by the subordinate operation.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ For hence it is that there follows: « But theology also teaches this wisely: that it came unto us through the angels. » Not only, he says, does theology testify that the legislation was given to Moses by God immediately, but it also teaches that it, namely the law, came unto us — that is, was set forth to our knowledge through the angels — so that it may be openly demonstrated that that legislation was both of God, from whose authority it proceeded, and of the angels, whose ministry too it employed. ⟦s2⟧ « As though, he says, the divine legal order itself was setting that down legally, that is, that through the first things the second are led back unto the divine. » Thus, he says, Scripture shows the legislation to have been ordained by God through the angels among men, as though [1014B] the legal order itself, divinely made, established that legally, that is, instituted or sanctioned it — namely, that the second things are led back unto the divine through the first. ⟦s3⟧ For when the divine law is declared to have proceeded first from God unto the angels, and then through the angels unto men, it is manifestly shown that through the first and superior things, the second and inferior things are led back unto the divine knowledge: and this must be understood not only between God and the angels, or between the angels and men, but also among the angels themselves, since the superior lead back the inferior unto the divine knowledge. ⟦s4⟧ Since among the angels themselves too there are orders, some superior, some inferior; and those who are superior draw more copiously the light of the divinity, and transfuse it to those who follow after them, to be illumined. ⟦s5⟧ And in those very orders too, [1014C] in which, according to an equal disposition, many are equal, this definition of the law is kept: that in the perception of divine grace some are first, some second, some last; and even those who are equal in order are not equal in the perception of grace. ⟦s6⟧ This is what he says: « For indeed not only among the placed-above and among the subject minds, that is, spirits, but even among the equally powerful, the very law is defined from the superessential principle of the ordering of all things, that is, » namely this, « that through each hierarchy, » that is, sacred ordination, « there are both first, and middle, and last ordinations and powers: » and that in those very ordinations and powers, the more divine are always the teachers « and hand-leaders » of divine things, [1014D] so that they may teach them and lead them « into the divine leading, and illumination, and communication. ⟦s7⟧ Into the leading » by conversion, « into illumination » by knowledge, « into communication » by perception.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ There follows: « But I see that the angels first taught the divine mystery of Christ's humanity. ⟦s2⟧ Then through them the grace of that knowledge descended unto us. » From this, he says, it is clear that the things which are commanded by God to men are announced through the angels, since the angels taught even the divine mystery of Christ's humanity itself — both those who foretold and believed, and those who saw and received. ⟦s3⟧ And then, that is, consequently, through those very angels the grace of this knowledge descended unto us. ⟦s4⟧ Thus, therefore, just as [1015A] Christ's nativity was announced through the angels, so too the nativity of his forerunner is read to have been announced through the angel Gabriel. ⟦s5⟧ « Thus, he says, the most divine Gabriel taught Zachariah, the high priest, the mysteries, that is, the boy to be born of him against hope, » because of a barren mother and an aged father: by divine grace « that he would be a Prophet, to manifest to the world the manly divine operation of Jesus, divinely and salvifically. » As if he should say: The most divine Gabriel taught Zachariah the high priest that the boy to be born of him would be a prophet of the operation of Jesus — a manly operation, because it was displayed in the nature of humanity, in which he took on the sex of flesh; and a divine operation, because it was accomplished through the power of the divinity, in which also he founded his own flesh. ⟦s6⟧ An operation, I say, to be manifested to the world through him [1015B] divinely and salvifically, that is, one to come forth divinely unto the salvation of the world. ⟦s7⟧ For the same most divine Gabriel taught « Mary how, » namely, « in her the divine mystery of the ineffable divine formation was to be completed. » Namely, that the divine operation, ineffably, above the use and law of nature, would take flesh from the flesh of the Virgin herself without the admixture of male seed, and would wondrously form it as the garment of the eternal Word. ⟦s8⟧ « But another of the angels was instructing Joseph, how the things divinely promised to his forefather David were truly fulfilled. » That is, how those things were fulfilled which had been promised divinely, that is, by God, to his forefather David.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ « But another evangelized the shepherds, as those purged by the withdrawal of the many and by silence: and with him the multitude [1015C] of the heavenly host were delivering to those who are on earth that most praiseworthy doxology. ⟦s2⟧ Another angel again, he says, evangelized the shepherds, as those purged and made pure by the withdrawal of the many, » that is, by the separation or segregation from the multitude and the tumult and the din; and purged also by silence, that is, by quiet of mind and inner peace, so that in this very thing they might become worthy of the Gospel and of the evangelic address, in that, returning apart from the multitude unto themselves, established in silence and quiet of mind, they had shown themselves capable of the spiritual announcement. ⟦s3⟧ Whence, the grace gradually growing, after the evangelization of the one, the multitude of the heavenly host is heard, who were delivering that most praiseworthy doxology, that is, hymn of glory, to those who are on earth, namely to men of good will.

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1015D]There follows: « And let me look also to the most excellent apparitions of the light of the words. » As if he should say: Not only in the things which were announced to men through the angels concerning the incarnation of the Word does it appear that the knowledge of divine things descends to men by the mediation and ministry of the angels themselves; but the same can also be seen in the very person of the incarnate Word: who, although by the majesty of his divinity he commanded the angels, nevertheless willed that the things which had to be done temporally about his humanity should be dispensed through the angels: in this very thing deigning to take up the condition of humanity, in that, lacking all necessity, by dispensation alone, for an example of humanity and a lesson of truth, [1016A] he inclined himself to the angelic dispositions. ⟦s2⟧ « I shall look, he says, also to the most excellent apparitions of the light of the words, » that is, to the apparitions of Jesus Christ himself, namely when Jesus himself, who is the true light, appeared visibly in the flesh he had assumed. ⟦s3⟧ Which apparitions of the light are the most excellent, beyond all the other apparitions [sic: *eloquiorom*], that is, beyond all the other apparitions which are reported in the sacred words; because never before did God manifest himself to the world so excellently as when he himself appeared visibly in the flesh he had assumed. ⟦s4⟧ To these apparitions of the light, therefore, he says, the most lofty above all the apparitions of the words, I shall look, so that in them too I may prove this: that the divine secrets are dispensed to men through the angels. ⟦s5⟧ For if, about that [1016B] man who is above men, the angelic dispensation was not repudiated, how much more is it proved to be necessary in those who are subject to sin and established in the darkness of ignorance. ⟦s6⟧ In that man, therefore, who is the head of men, we can recognize what we ought to think about other men, his members.
