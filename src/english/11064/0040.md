## EXPOSITION. (cont.)

Consider now what keenness of love they had, of whom it was said: « Where the impulse of the spirit was, thither they went [n: (Ezech. I)]. » For that very impulse was keen, just as in a certain other place the liquid too is named as the keenness of love. And I think that it was the bride herself who was speaking; and it was not fitting that anything hard or harsh be brought to one who was fearful and timid. Therefore the liquid was named instead of the keen, in the blandishment of love. For the liquid too penetrates like the keen, and does not cease until it has reached the inmost parts. Therefore she says: « My soul melted when the beloved spoke; I sought him [n: (Cant. III)]. » For she sought him for this reason, that [1038B] she had been melted toward him. For unless she had been melted toward him, she would not have run after him; but she would have stood hard, and would not have entered. But now she has been melted, and has begun to run; yet she did not straightway find him, until she arrived. Therefore here too the unceasing was necessary, that she might enter, and penetrate, and say: « I held him, nor will I let him go, until I bring him, she says, into the house of my mother, and into the chamber of her that bore me [n: (Cant. V)]. I will bring him, she says, into the house of my mother, into the chamber of her that bore me. » Therefore he himself will enter in to you, that you may go in to him. For then you enter in to him, when he enters in to you. When his love enters your heart, and penetrates it, and his love reaches to the inmost part of your heart, then he himself enters into you, and you too enter [1038C] into yourself, that you may go in to him. Therefore do you bring him in to yourself; and bring him in to yourself in no such way that he should merely remain or subsist outside, near you, or in your gates, or in your courts; or before the door of your house, or even only in your house — because this is not much, nor is it great for a great love, unless it reach as far as the marriage-chamber, and enter the inner room, and penetrate to the inmost parts, and rest in your innermost places. I will say something further still: that even the chamber of him who begot perhaps she does not love, unless she be brought into the chamber of her that bore, where love is more tender and the blandishments sweeter, so that it may find in you nothing hard or rigid of manly fierceness. But let the whole be melted, and grow soft with the fire of love. For then nothing of hardness will withstand, so that charity may reach to the [1038D] inmost parts, and love may have the keenness to penetrate all things; this it was we had to say, concerning the keen of love and the liquid, that you may understand the force of love and affection, how great it is. If, however, this can be understood, since love surpasses knowledge, and is greater than understanding. For more is loved than is understood, and love enters and draws near where knowledge is outside. Nor is this to be wondered at: for love always presumes further, and always trusts; love brings itself in without hesitation. Therefore it has the keen, and the liquid that penetrates all things, and follows the impulse of its own ardent desire, unable to dissemble until it reach the beloved; and by that very fact thirsting still more to enter into him, and to be with him, and so near that, [1039A] if it could be, it might be the very same thing that he is. Is it not exceedingly keen, to penetrate all these things, and to enter into the inmost parts, so that it cannot be repelled by any power, until it reach where it loves? « Who, says the Apostle, shall separate us from the charity of Christ? Persecution, or famine, or the sword? [n: (Rom. VIII)] » and many other things, which could have been a hindrance, had love not withstood them. But love, because it had the keen, could not be held; but it passed through, and penetrated, escaping freely, and running to its own desire. If, then, such is the hot and the keen of love, of what sort do you think is that which follows, the « superfervent »? For that must have something more, which has been added to the increase of the preceding powers. And the very matter admonishes us to think something greater in that [1039B] which is superfervent, than in that which is hot and keen.

For you know how that which boils is cast beyond itself by a certain violence of its heat and burning, and is lifted above itself, and makes a great movement out of the underlying and invisible seething of the fervor conceived. And the heat of the hidden burning, which is within and moves, is not seen; but that which is moved is seen by it: and from that which is seen, we conceive and understand the great force, and the robust power, and the strong violence of that which lies hidden and is not seen. Who will be able worthily to bring the emulation of visible things to the majesty of invisible things? Spectacles are set before us, and there is shown to us fervor from heat and moisture, or rather in moisture from heat: and we see how the heat [1039C] without tumult gradually enters into the moisture, so that, having entered, it may powerfully and violently cast it out. It brings itself in invisibly, that it may manifestly raise it up, as though it would not have it there, the thing which it hastens to cast out with so vehement, as it were, an indignation of a certain impulse. The hot, therefore, is moved to the keen, then the keen is advanced to the superfervent. For that which was first keen, and liquid in love, able to penetrate other things that stand in its way, now becomes superfervent, and boiling, unable to stand still in itself. For the keen belongs to love, when by passing through it despises all things; but the superfervent, when by despising it forsakes even its own self. For he who desires this alone which he loves despises even his own self in comparison with it. For neither would he truly desire that alone, if he loved [1039D] even his own self along with it. But this a great and singular love alone can do: that for love of that which alone is loved, even he who loves should be despised, as it were, by his own self. It comes to pass, therefore, in a wondrous manner, that while through the fire of love he is lifted up into him who is above himself, by the force of love he begins to be driven out, and even to go out of himself. How, then, does he grow fervent, and how does he boil in heart, who through the fire of the supernal love conceived, while by thought and desire he is borne toward the desiring of him alone who is above, is cast beyond his own self, and is lifted above himself, and does not think of himself, while he loves him alone? Thus we understand the mobile, and the unceasing, and the hot, and the keen, and the superfervent of love.

[1040A]But how shall we be able to assign these things in those supercelestial natures, in which the same thing is life as is essence, because they have no other essence than life? What is that unceasing movement of theirs about divine things, and the hot, and the keen, and the superfervent? What are those divine things, about which they are unceasingly moved, and grow hot, and are sharpened, and are superfervent? For he who is in a circuit does not yet enter; because either he is lukewarm from the hot, or he is numb from the keen. If, then, they have the keen, how are they in a circuit? Perhaps because those divine things, of which Scripture has spoken, are within every creature, and so utterly secret and hidden, that even if they can be touched, they cannot be penetrated. But there are certain other divine things, which come into manifestation, and in some manner set themselves forth to knowledge, [1040B] either while they come forth inwardly to the mind, or while they proceed outwardly as far as the sense. For the Apostle intimates that certain divine things are utterly within, and hidden, and latent, but that certain things have gone forth outwardly, and have been manifested, saying: « What is known of God is manifest in them [n: (Rom. I)]. » For when he says, « what is known of God, » that is, the knowable concerning God, he plainly shows that of the things which are of God, and are in God, something is manifest, something hidden. And that indeed which is manifest can be touched through knowledge; but that which is utterly hidden can by no reasoning be penetrated. There are, therefore, certain divine things, and certain things of God set forth for manifestation, which according to something can be penetrated and comprehended; but certain things so deep, and hidden, [1040C] and exceedingly inward, and altogether impenetrable, that no intellect can search them out, nor any wisdom investigate them: concerning which it is a great thing, when it is granted to touch them, even if it be not granted to penetrate them; and when by penetrating one arrives at them, they nevertheless are not penetrated, but remain impenetrable and incomprehensible, in which this alone, which is outside, is shown to the arriving understanding for knowledge, and that which is always within is not opened for comprehension.
