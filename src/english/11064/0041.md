## EXPOSITION. (cont.)

Consider now and see whether the things which have been made visible by God do not instruct you concerning the invisible things of God. For the things which by reason alone are sometimes less investigated, are not seldom made knowable by the light of examples. See, therefore, what the sense of the flesh can do in [1040D] the world, that from it you may understand, as far as it can be understood, what the sense of the mind can do in God. When we touch the just visible world with the eye of the flesh, we perceive the things which are outside it; and to the things which lie hidden within, by that same sense we are not able to penetrate. And even if certain things which lay hidden are sometimes opened, still many others lie hidden which cannot be comprehended, either by their immensity, because they exceed the sense; or by their subtlety, because they escape the sense; or by their obscurity, because they do not admit the sense to themselves. So consider that the sense of the rational mind, that by which we perceive divine things, if ever it be admitted to touch God, perceives those things only which are, as it were, outside him; and the things which lie hidden within, secret and concealed, it does not comprehend. [1041A] But he said that it is outside him, and not outside us, for this reason: because everything that is in God is within to every creature; but yet, in comparison with those things which cannot at all be comprehended, that which even of the most secret and most subtle understanding is manifested concerning him is said in some manner to be to him, or in him, outside. Through the keen of love, therefore, they penetrate to him; and yet, through his incomprehensible majesty, they remain about him, so that they do not enter into the whole, even though they penetrate as far as something. But this very thing also must be considered, that they are said to be about him, and not in some one part. For they encompass with desire that which they do not penetrate with the intellect, so that they may leave nothing unconsidered of all the things that can be known, [1041B] always seeing, and always thirsting to see. Stable, that they may not depart; mobile, that they may unceasingly desire. In a circuit, because they do not enter into the whole of that which is. In a circuit, because they draw near immediately. In a circuit, because they survey by contemplation and love all that is knowable in him. Thus, therefore, the mobile of them about divine things, and the unceasing, and the hot, and the keen, and the superfervent of that unceasing, and intent, and perhaps inmost, and inflexible movement itself, can fittingly be understood. This, however, must not be passed over, that he named the movement of the invisible natures unceasing, and intent, and perhaps inmost, and inflexible, using the voice of one doubting in this one thing alone, when he said "perhaps," as though he asserted the rest without hesitation, and this one thing alone he would not venture to affirm except with the qualification [1041C] of doubt. The movement, therefore, of those spirits that draw near to the supreme divinity is called unceasing, and inmost, and inflexible; because, tending from itself toward God through the desire of love, it is both always mobile, so that it never rests in itself; and intent, that it may press on toward him; and inmost, that it may not flow out to outward things; and inflexible, that it may not turn aside to other things outside itself and beside him. Why, then, does he not say absolutely "inmost," but, as if doubting, and as if he knew not whether it were so, or whether it were so to be said, did he add "perhaps"? Perhaps because he judged that this alone was to be understood and called truly inmost, than which nothing is more inward. An inmost movement, therefore, there is not, except that which is either from the inmost, or is unto the inmost. Because, therefore, the divine nature [1041D] alone is within to every nature, it alone is inmost to every nature; whose movement, without movement toward the creature, is alone called inmost in reality, because it is from that than which nothing more inmost is found. But the movement of the creature toward the Creator, however secret and penetrating, cannot properly be called inmost; because it is from that which is outside, toward which it is: and when it is led to touch him who is inmost, the way is not opened to it as far as his inmost parts. Because, therefore, it comes in some respect and outwardly, going out from the creature, and subsists outwardly, not reaching to the penetrating of the inmost parts of the Creator, it ought by no means to be called inmost; although, however, for the reason that it is from an inner nature unto the inmost, it can fittingly be called inmost: on account of this he says well, "perhaps inmost," that he might intimate [1042A] that it could according to something be called inmost, which according to every mode was not inmost. For it can be called inmost by property, not by comparison. Inmost to that from which it is; but not to that toward which it is.

There follows: « And the exemplative of the reductive and active of the subjects. » In the enumeration above he expressed the power of true love toward God, whether how great it is, I know not; but I think, as great as it can be told. Now, in what follows, he shows the force and effect of the same love toward the neighbor. There he demonstrated its movement and its turning to higher things, by which they thirst after their Creator: but here he sets forth its movement, and its turning to lower and nearer things, by which they do not withdraw from one another. The movement, therefore, of love, which there toward higher things [1042B] is called intent, and inmost, and inflexible, here toward the subject and lower things is named the exemplative of the reductive and active. For that movement, which in the higher is contemplation, in the lower is operation. It tends to higher things, that it may rest in them; it tends to lower things, that it may bring them back to itself. Upward, therefore, charity is moved, that it may abide there; downward, that it may return. Therefore the movement of charity in the higher beings is called reductive and active toward the lower. Reductive in this, that, kindling those beings with the same fire of charity, it turns them to their Creator. Active in this, that, illumining them with the brightness received, it composes them to his will. It is reductive, therefore, of the subjects, because it draws them to higher things. Active, because it disposes them in lower things. Reductive, that they may tend to God. [1042C] Active, that they may walk according to God. Of such a reductive and active movement of the subjects, therefore — that is, which brings back the subjects to those things which are above them, and disposes them to acting in themselves — the exemplative is that form of love, in which by the example of the higher beings it is shown to the subjects with how great an affection of charity they ought both to walk according to God, and to pant after him. Or so that it be read thus, the exemplative of the subjects, that is, that which is set before the subjects as an example: "reductively" and "actively" being pronounced adverbially, the same sense remaining. See, therefore, how charity spreads itself out, desiring to embrace all things in those blessed spirits, and those near to God, as though burning from close at hand, and fervent the more. The fire of love [1042D] is indeed brought back to higher things, while, thirsting for its own good through the love of God, it is moved also to lower and subject beings, wishing to gather to itself partakers and companions of its good. Loving, therefore, they bestow upon the subjects the form of loving, and, burning in themselves, they kindle others also with the flame of love. On account of this, therefore, their love has been made an example to the subjects of tending to higher things, and of walking according to the higher things, as though rewarming those, namely the subjects, and re-arousing them into a like warmth, so that they may similarly burn, though not equally. But that he says « rewarming, and re-arousing, » it is not so to be understood as though, being first extinguished and dead, they are again kindled and made alive; but that through the love coming from above they are aroused again to the loving and seeking of those things which are above. [1043A] Through the fire of love, therefore, they are as it were rewarmed and re-aroused, in whom the kindled flame of love is brought back again to the loving of those things from which, in the first and principal lovers, it arose upon the following and subject lovers. All these things, therefore, the surname of the Seraphim, or its manifestation, teaches. For in that they are surnamed Seraphim, that is, "the enkindling," or "the warming," it is both a surname to them, and a manifestation to us; because both to them the surname is expressed in the word, and to us the property of the surname is manifested in the interpretation of the word, since they would not be manifested unless they were surnamed. For those who are known to themselves by contemplation become known to us by surname: and therefore to them, as regards the word, it is called a surname; [1043B] to us, as regards the interpretation of the word, it is called a manifestation. That very surname, therefore, or manifestation of the Seraphim teaches all these things, that is, namely, their mobile about divine things, and unceasing; and it teaches also the hot, and the keen, and the superfervent of their intent, and inmost, and inflexible movement; and it teaches also the exemplative of the subjects, reductively and actively, which is in them, and is furnished by them to the subjects for reduction and action, that they may be rewarmed and re-aroused into a warmth like the warmth and fervor of the higher beings. The surname of the Seraphim teaches also the « fiery from heaven, and the purgative by holocaust, and the unveiled, and the inextinguishable. » Which is in them first from heaven, or descending divinely into them: then from them, [1043C] that they may burn and enkindle, be purged and purge, be revealed and reveal.
