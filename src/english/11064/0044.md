## BOOK SEVEN. *Exposition of the remaining part of the seventh chapter of the divine Dionysius the Areopagite on the celestial hierarchy, the text of which has been set out above.* (cont.)

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ There follows: « And by every extremity ineffably unto the most sublime: and about the truly lofty placed with all its powers unchangeably, and stably. [1048C]" Namely, the surname of the Thrones teaches the placed, that is, their placing, or their establishment, made unchangeably, and stably, with all its powers, that is, with every kind of power, and unshaken strength. ⟦s2⟧ Made, I say, ineffably far from all extremity, that is, beyond every end, unto the most sublime, and about the truly lofty. ⟦s3⟧ For where would the seat of God be, except where God sits? ⟦s4⟧ Where God dwells, where he reigns, where he sits, where he rests, where his thrones are, and his seats. ⟦s5⟧ See how far they are from us. ⟦s6⟧ How far is the highest majesty from all subjection, the eternal unchangeableness from all corruption. ⟦s7⟧ Where majesty is, there is a throne; where unchangeableness is, there is a seat. ⟦s8⟧ A throne signifies unchangeableness. ⟦s9⟧ Therefore in that very eternity, in that very unchangeableness, the seats of God [1048D] have been placed. ⟦s10⟧ And how far this is from all extremity, who can tell? ⟦s11⟧ What is extremity? ⟦s12⟧ An end: where an end is, there is extremity. ⟦s13⟧ An end at the top, an end at the bottom. ⟦s14⟧ In both the creature has an end. ⟦s15⟧ The end is at the bottom, where the falling-away ceases, lest that which is something go into nothing. ⟦s16⟧ The end is at the top, where the advancing sets a limit to itself, lest that which is great extend itself beyond measure. ⟦s17⟧ By how much, then, do those surpass the lowest things, who ineffably transcend the highest? ⟦s18⟧ We can still, not unfittingly, interpret those extremities in another mode. ⟦s19⟧ For all visible things are extremes; because, as to one ascending upward from the lowest there is a highest, than which nothing is higher, so to one coming forth outward from the inmost there is an extreme, than which nothing is more outward. ⟦s20⟧ The divine benches, therefore, as, in that they are called thrones, [1049A] they are shown to be excellently raised up, through dignity, in the summit, above the subjection of the lowest things, so also, in that they are called seats, they are demonstrated to be placed unchangeably, through stability, in the inmost, above all fluctuation of the extremes. ⟦s21⟧ And yet neither, raised up on high, can they be made equal to the ineffable loftiness under which they are; nor, gathered to the inner parts, can they be compared to the simple unity about which they are. ⟦s22⟧ On account of this, therefore, they are called seats, because they are both underneath, through the ineffable majesty, to him who presides above, and are in a circuit, through the unchangeable unity, to him who rests within. ⟦s23⟧ This, therefore, the surname of the Thrones teaches: and not only this, but it teaches also « the receptive of the divine coming-upon, » that is, that they receive the divinity coming upon them from above in all impassibility and [1049B] immateriality, that is, incorruptly and purely. ⟦s24⟧ For because they receive it in purity, they receive it immaterially; and again, because they receive it flowing in sweetly, without labor and weariness, they receive it impassibly. ⟦s25⟧ Those who advance to the knowledge of the hidden divinity through study and labor, these receive the divine coming-upon passibly. ⟦s26⟧ Again, those to whom the hidden divinity presses itself in to be known through outward signs and corporeal figures, to these it comes in some manner as it were materially. ⟦s27⟧ Because, therefore, those highest spirits, in perceiving the knowledge of the divinity, neither advance by study, nor are taught by material figures: rightly are they declared to receive the divine coming-upon both impassibly and immaterially.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1049C]There follows: « And God-bearing, and in a servant's manner opened unto the divine receptions. » From what precedes it must again be supplied, "the surname of the Thrones teaches," namely their God-bearing, that is, that they bear God presiding over them; and "opened in a servant's manner unto the divine receptions," that is, that in a servant's manner, that is, obediently, they open themselves and voluntarily fit themselves, that they may receive into themselves God himself coming, inasmuch as the will subject to the divine operation may respond, and the sacred ministry may be proved to be not of constraint, but of love. ⟦s2⟧ A right order. ⟦s3⟧ First the benches of God are raised up through dignity; then they are placed on account of stability; afterward they receive God coming into themselves, and bear him. ⟦s4⟧ Lastly, because they are rational vehicles, that the work may be one of felicity, to the ministry they join desire, [1049D] and will.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ There follows: « This indeed is the declaration of their names, as far as it concerns us. » He continues himself unto what follows. ⟦s2⟧ For, the surnames of the three orders having been told, he now passes to defining and setting forth their hierarchy, that is, their sacred power. ⟦s3⟧ « This indeed, » namely this which we have said above, « is the declaration of their names, » namely of the three orders. ⟦s4⟧ It is, I say, as far as it concerns us, that is, as far as it seems to us, or as far as it concerns us it is the declaration of their names, that is, for our sake, that they may be declared to us through names, not for their own sake, who can be known to themselves, and without names.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ There follows: « But it must be told, what hierarchy [1050A] of theirs we deem it to be. » As if he should say: Thus far we have spoken concerning their surname; but now we ought to speak concerning their sacred power, of what sort we deem it to be. ⟦s2⟧ « For that the speculation of every hierarchy, depending ineffably upon a deiformity imitating God, is; and that every hierarchic action is divided into a sacred participation, and a handing-down of pure purgation, and of divine light, and of perfective knowledge: I think has already been sufficiently said by us. » The sense is this: I think it has already been sufficiently said by us, that the speculation of every hierarchy, that is, the general definition of the hierarchy, depends ineffably upon deiformity, that is, upon likeness to God: a likeness, I say, imitating God, that is, upon a likeness of the imitation of God. ⟦s3⟧ Enough, he says, [1050B] I think has already been said, what the hierarchy is, defined generally, namely deiformity, that is, a conformation or likeness to God, which is in those who imitate God. ⟦s4⟧ For above, in the third chapter, he defines the hierarchy universally thus. ⟦s5⟧ The hierarchy is likeness and unity to God, as far as is possible. ⟦s6⟧ On account of this, therefore, I think it has now been sufficiently said what the hierarchy is, namely as far as pertains to the general definition. ⟦s7⟧ Likewise I think it has now been sufficiently said, that every hierarchic action is divided, that is, that every hierarchic action is divided into a sacred participation, and a handing-down of pure purgation, and of divine light, and of perfective knowledge. ⟦s8⟧ For the action of every sacred power consists either in this, [1050C] that they participate from a higher; or in this, that they hand down to the lower purgation, and illumination, and perfection. ⟦s9⟧ Threefold is the grace, which they exercise in two modes, by receiving, and by imparting: first is purgation unto purity; then illumination unto truth; then perfection unto goodness. ⟦s10⟧ For this is the perfective knowledge, which perfects, and makes perfect, when from the habit of virtue truth is perceived. ⟦s11⟧ « But now let us worthily go on to tell of the most excellent intellects, how already according to them the hierarchy is manifested by the sacred words. » As if he should say: Since above the definition and the division of the hierarchy have been universally and sufficiently given by us; now let us go on worthily to tell of the most excellent intellects, that is, the highest spirits, [1050D] namely the Seraphim, the Cherubim, and the Thrones, in whom the first angelic hierarchy is ordered. ⟦s12⟧ Let us go on, I say, to tell how the hierarchy according to them, that is, how their hierarchy is manifested by the sacred words. ⟦s13⟧ For this the order of reason demands, that after the general definition and division of the hierarchy, the discourse descend to the special treatment of it. ⟦s14⟧ First indeed, concerning the first hierarchy, which consists in those three highest orders, it must be asked of what sort the sacred words manifest it to be. ⟦s15⟧ And lest perhaps anyone should think their sacred power to be diverse for this reason, that their orders are found to be differing, he says that in all three there is one and altogether like hierarchy, [1051A] inasmuch, namely, as they are all in the first place united to the highest and principal hierarchy, and immediately obtain from it the dignity of their sacred power.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ For this is what he subjoins, saying: « In the first essences, which, placed after their substantifying divinity, and ordered as it were in its vestibules, are made to be above all invisible and visible power, it must be deemed that there is a proper and altogether uniform hierarchy. » The first essences are those highest and principal three orders of angels, which among all creatures are first, and, after their substantifying divinity, that is, that which makes them subsist, are first constituted, and ordered as it were in its vestibules, that is [1051B] so near, that whatever is beyond, is only in it, and is itself the divinity. ⟦s2⟧ In those first essences, therefore, which, so placed and ordered, are next to the divinity, superexcellent, and transgressing every made power, invisible and visible, that is, the power of every making, or creature, invisible and visible; in those essences, I say, it must be deemed that there is a hierarchy, that is, a sacred power; proper, that is, distinct, and differing from others in itself, and altogether uniform, that is, like and equal among themselves. ⟦s3⟧ For in that sacred power which they have, as they are more excellent than all others, so among themselves they exist altogether equal, so that each in that which they receive immediately from the highest divinity have not others superior; and in that which to the lower beings [1051C] they bestow out of the divine participation, all alike exist as first dispensers. ⟦s4⟧ In this, therefore, the hierarchy is one, and alike, in the three.
