## BOOK SEVEN. *Exposition of the remaining part of the seventh chapter of the divine Dionysius the Areopagite on the celestial hierarchy, the text of which has been set out above.* (cont.)

There follows: « But I marvel that even the first of the celestial essences, and [those] alone at once supereminent above all in the divine illuminations, seek questions reverently, as though mediated. » This looks back to what is said, that the highest essences are immediately illumined and instructed by God; for the contrary seems to be the case, that in this passage they are found inquiring not only of their superior, but also of one another, and as it were wishing to learn. But it must be known that this questioning is not [1059A] an inquiry after teaching, but a profession of ignorance. For they question, showing that they do not know, and that they have need to be taught. But where all question, they assuredly show that they ought to be taught from elsewhere. They question, therefore, among themselves, [being] to be taught from above themselves. Because so mutual a questioning seems to be an inquiry of teaching and knowledge from one another, whereas it is established that they are instructed by their superior alone, therefore he says: I marvel that those first and principal of the celestial essences — that is, among the celestial essences, and so far supereminent above all the other celestial essences at once in the divine illuminations — I marvel, I say, that they seek questions, that is, that they make questions seeking knowledge, reverently, that is, humbly like the humble and lower, and as though not attending to the excellence of their own illumination; and [1059B] this is what he says, « as mediated, » that is, like those who have something in between themselves and God, and need to be instructed by others; or 'as mediated,' because they are not truly mediated, since they make no question to others; nor yet truly immediate, since they refer their question not to their own principle, but to one another.

There follows: « For indeed they do not thence ask: Why thy red garments? » He proves that they truly make their question 'as mediated,' because they direct their words not first to Jesus himself, but to one another, saying: « Who is this that comes from Edom, with dyed garments from Bosra? » For they do not, he says, thence ask — that is, they do not begin their questioning from him — 'Why thy red garments?' where they would direct their speech immediately to Jesus himself; [1059C] but first among themselves, as though mediately: « Who is this, » and the rest. Whence it is a marvel that those supreme essences, though they are first, and nearest to the Divinity, make their questions as though mediated.

There follows: « But among themselves they deliberate before asking, showing that they learn, and seek the deiform knowledge. » He now resolves the question, namely why those highest essences, though they are nearest to God, make their question among themselves; namely, because among themselves they deliberate about asking, lest perhaps by too eager a questioning they leap ahead of, or anticipate, that illumination which comes to be in them through the divine procession — that is, through the divine grace [1059D] proceeding to illumine them; showing also, by the very deliberation of their questioning, that they seek the deiform knowledge. For in that they ask, they signify that they seek knowledge; but in that they first confer among themselves, questioning and deliberating, they demonstrate that they do not dare to anticipate the divine procession into themselves.

For this is what follows: « But not leaping ahead of the illumination implanted through the divine procession. » For they seek knowledge, and therefore they ask, but not leaping ahead of the divine illumination, until it offers itself, proceeding of its own accord into them; therefore they deliberate among themselves, and do not first dare to question Jesus until he himself offers himself, saying: « I am he who speaks justice. » Then at last, [1060A] having taken confidence, they direct their question to him: « Why is thy raiment red, and thy garment like theirs that tread in the winepress? »

There follows: « Is not, then, the first hierarchy of the celestial intellects, sanctified by the very Prince of perfection in that it is extended immediately into it, purged, and illumined, and perfected — with a most holy purgation, with much light, with a fore-perfected consummation filling it proportionally? » The sense is this: Is not, then — 'num' for 'nonne' — the first hierarchy of the celestial intellects purged, and illumined, and perfected? Sanctified by the very Prince of perfection — that is, by the divinity itself, which is the prince and principle of all sanctification. Sanctified, I say, in that the divinity itself is extended immediately into it, [1060B] illuminating, or irradiating, or filling it proportionally — that is, by a participation differing from the illumination of the rest — with a most holy purgation, and with much light, and with a fore-perfected — that is, super-excellent — consummation or perfection. « It is purged, » I say, and « illumined, and perfected, that it may be pure from all lessening or imperfection, and full of the first light, » that is, of the [light] shining into it first before the others; and that it may be « perfect, participating the perfect knowledge and cognition first given to it. » But when he says « sanctified » — or, as perhaps it is more fittingly, though less usually, translated, 'made pontiff' by the very Prince of purgation — he signifies, as we said, the divinity itself, which in Greek is called teletarchia, that is, the prince of purgation, or [1060C] of sanctification.

There follows: « But, comprehending, this too I would perhaps not undeservedly have said. » As though it were asked what this purgation is, he answers that the taking-up of the divine knowledge in the rational soul is both purgation, and illumination, and perfection. Purgation, because it purges ignorance; illumination, because it illumines with divine cognition; perfection, because, by illuminating, it consummates by the knowledge of perfect teachings, or disciplines, according to the illumined habit — this is what he says: « Comprehending, » that is, briefly gathering into one the things said above; this too I would perhaps not undeservedly have said, that the taking-up of the divine knowledge is both purgation, and illumination, and perfection. [1060D] And he gives the reason why it is called purgation, and illumination, and perfection. « Purging, indeed, ignorance, inasmuch as the knowledge of perfect teachings is implanted according to order. » For this reason it is called purgation, inasmuch as it purges ignorance by implanted knowledge — that is, through the implanted knowledge of perfect teachings. Implanted, I say, according to order — that is, according to what the dignity and excellence of each order demands. « But illuminating by that very divine cognition, through which it also purges [the hierarchy] that does not contemplate beforehand. » For this reason it is called illumination, because it illumines each hierarchy with divine cognition, through which divine cognition, namely, it also purges that hierarchy. The hierarchy, I say, that does not contemplate before it is purged, as it is written: [1061A] « Blessed are the clean of heart, for they shall see God [n: (Matth. V)]. »

There follows: « Which it manifests through a higher illumination, and again perfecting, by that very light, according to habit, with the knowledge of the most lucid teachings. » Which hierarchy, namely, the very taking-up of the divine knowledge manifests through a higher illumination. For in this it declares it to be higher than all the rest: that it illuminates it with a higher and more excellent light of divine cognition.

There follows: « And again perfecting, by that very light, according to habit, with the knowledge of the most lucid teachings. » He does not distinguish illumination and perfection clearly enough, in that he seems to assign both to cognition and knowledge. Yet this [1061B] is the difference: that illumination properly pertains to that cognition which builds up knowledge; but perfection to that cognition which shows the form of good morals and the habit of the virtues. For there are certain things which are investigated only for the cognition of understanding; but certain things pertain especially to the understanding of doing. For the former, illumination is necessary; for the latter, perfection. For the cognition of truth does not make [a man] perfect, unless the habit of virtue follows. Therefore he calls 'most lucid teaching' that which consists in the habit of virtue, because experience is the mistress of understanding; and he knows virtue best who has learned it not by hearing alone, but also by tasting and by doing. In the experience and habit of virtue, the cognition of truth is perfected, which is begun in the illumination [1061C] of understanding alone. All of which, because it is acquired through the taking-up of the divine knowledge, is therefore fittingly called both purgation, and illumination, and perfection.
