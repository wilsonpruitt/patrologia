## BOOK SEVEN. *Exposition of the remaining part of the seventh chapter of the divine Dionysius the Areopagite on the celestial hierarchy, the text of which has been set out above.* (cont.)

Hear what follows: « Seeing indeed many and blessed contemplations purely, and simple and immediate splendors, illumined, and filled with divine nourishment. » Do not, then, marvel if they go about [it] thus, and do not suffer themselves to be drawn far off. There is something there that draws [them]; and what is this? You are waiting for it to be said what that is which so incites the affection of those who desire, and provokes their love. But how do you think it will be told by us, which is not penetrated by them? They themselves have not yet entered, but [1063B] stand in the encircling; and it is a great thing for them to draw near to it, that they may see, and touch, and taste, and experience what the sweetness of the good is — [the good] set forth to its lovers without obstacle; yet they neither enter nor penetrate so as to comprehend it, and to grasp even to the whole how great is the immensity of the hidden. How, then, do you think to set it forth outwardly, from which even those who are within remain outside? That good, then, cannot be told by us — [the good] which holds those blessed spirits immovable beside itself through the joy of eternal contemplation, that they may not flow away toward perishable things; and again, moving [them] by desire, draws them to itself, that they may seek it with unceasing love. But lest that be altogether passed over in silence which cannot be told at all, hear what it is that both holds them beside itself and draws them to itself. A certain good, and great above [1063C] all those goods that you know, quite other — not only in difference, but in supereminence; yet, that it may be conveyed to you and made known according to these goods that you know, as far as can be, let us say: that good is light and sweetness. Why light? Because it makes the eyes grow bright. Why sweetness? Because it refreshes. These are two great goods among us, and no others are found greater than these, nor any that work more toward our joy or our happiness: light and sweetness. The one is for illumination, the other for refreshment. If you are illumined and not satisfied, it is a great good, but not a full one. If you are satisfied and not illumined, it is again a great good, but not a perfect one. Refreshment makes pleasant what is within; illumination [1063D] presents as pleasant what is without; both are required for full joy. For if you are refreshed in the one and afflicted in the other, the gladness is not perfect, to which sadness is mixed. Seek, therefore, refreshment, that what is in you may be pleasant to you; seek also illumination, that what is outside you may be pleasant to you. Refreshment indeed would seem to be more necessary, just as the good which is in you is more properly your own than the good which is outside you. Nevertheless, how much pleasantness does the illumination of contemplation add? Sweet is the light, and delightful for the eyes to see the sun. Behold this world, which offers many spectacles of pleasantness. And all these things were made through the wisdom of God. All that you see went forth from there, where is the reason and cause of all things. If, then, it is so beautiful [1064A] to see the disposition and form of the work, how pleasant and delightful do you think it is to contemplate the wisdom of the craftsman? But do not think, because it is named one wisdom, [that it offers] a certain solitary and wearisome contemplation to those who see it: it is one, but not one in that way. For how do you think the wisdom of God is one? Perhaps as you call one essence, one species and one form, one place and one time: all these are one in number. What is [it] to be one in number? By distinction. What is [it] to be one in number? By smallness. What is [it] to be one in number? By imperfection. For thus you number this and that. For when you number, you say this and not that. What, therefore, is numbered by unity is separated by unity; and by that same unity it is proved to be diminished from all those from which it is shown, [1064B] by that very unity, to be distinct. Do you think the wisdom of God to be one in that way? Then you number it, and say, rightly, this and not that. If, then, it is [this] and not that, then it is not the whole; and there is something outside it which is not in it. If the whole is in it, it is the whole; how do you say [that] it is this and not that? Do not, then, number. Scripture tells you: « His wisdom is not number. All things that are from it were made in number, and weight, and measure. But it itself is not under number; » in which there is true unity without smallness, and universality without multiplicity; nor is it under weight, [being] inestimable; nor under measure, [being] incomprehensible. What, then, do you think it is to see the wisdom of God? When you see this world, how many things do you see in it? And all [1064C] this comes from there; and the whole is there, where the wisdom of God is. And why do I say 'all this'? For it would be too little a thing, were there in the wisdom of God nothing else [but this]. If God understands his own work, and does not understand himself, what wisdom is that? For what God is is more than what is made by God. If, then, he knows what he made, and does not know what he himself who made [it] is, he is ignorant of what is greater, and there is not perfect wisdom in him. If your heart can grasp and comprehend the things that you do and that you unfold in work — you dispose all things by reason dictating within, and all that you are about to do you first conceive in reason; and when they go forth into work, they do not withdraw from reason — how can it be that in the eternal reason of the Founder there is not everything [1064D] that has been made? Again, if it is a small thing for a rational soul to see and comprehend the things that are outside, unless it contemplate those things that are within — [things] far greater and more wonderful — and recognizes that what it itself is is greater than everything that is outside it: [then] the eternal craftsman cannot, in that wisdom by which he sees all that he himself made, be ignorant of what is greater than all things — which is he himself who made [them]. Consider, then, what that wisdom is, in which are all things that have been made, and — beyond all things and above all things — that which is greater than all things: he by whom all things were made. What kind of form do you think that is, in which there is such great beauty? When, therefore, you hear the wisdom of God named, think of all this: all this is in it; and to see it is to see all this. In it are seen all things that have been made; [1065A] and in it is seen he by whom all things were made. Therefore those highest spirits, who through the excellence of supereminent grace draw near immediately, are illumined by it to contemplate it, and in the contemplation of it they see both what was made through it, and what is in it, and him who through it made [all things], in whom it [itself] is. These, then, are the contemplations which the first hierarchy sees purely, immediately illumined unto the simple splendors — which contemplations are both many, inasmuch as all things are seen there; and blessed, because, with all things, he too who made all things is seen. For what is seen in him is not seen apart from him, because that which is seen is one, which is he himself; and in that one all things are seen, in which all things are one and one is all things. See how [1065B] many are the contemplations, where nothing of all things is hidden; how blessed the contemplations, where in the highest good all things are seen; where to see and to have, to love and to taste the good, is the same; where nothing is seen but truth, nothing is loved but goodness. For there are two things: cognition and love. The one pertains to illumination; the other to refreshment. Cognition illumines, love satisfies. Cognition of truth, love of goodness: in these blessedness consists — to know and to love the good. « Taste, says Scripture, and see, for the Lord is sweet [n: (Psal. XXXIII)]. » In « taste » is love; in « see » is cognition. These two he commends distinctly, namely cognition and love. « Seeing indeed many and blessed contemplations purely, and simple and immediate splendors, [1065C] illumined. » This has been said concerning illumination and cognition.

There follows: « Filled with divine nourishment. [Filled] with many things, indeed, by the first given outpouring, and by the unity — sole, domestic, and unifying — of the divine refreshment. » This has been said concerning love and refreshment. He commends both in many ways. First, cognition, in the many and blessed contemplations seen purely through the illumination of the simple splendors. What are the simple splendors? Pure illuminations. Through pure illuminations one comes to pure contemplations. Pure illumination is when the truth is conceived through itself; pure contemplation is when the truth is seen in itself. When the truth is learned through an underlying image or figure, [1065D] it is not pure illumination; when the truth is known in an underlying image or figure, it is not pure contemplation. For there can be neither simplicity except where there is [only] the one thing, nor purity except where there is the true. Simplicity in the one alone; purity in the true. Therefore those who contemplate immediately, and draw near to the truth through themselves, alone have simple illuminations and pure contemplations.
