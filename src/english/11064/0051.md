## BOOK SEVEN. *Exposition of the remaining part of the seventh chapter of the divine Dionysius the Areopagite on the celestial hierarchy, the text of which has been set out above.* (cont.)

Hear: « That most holy, and supremely blessed, and altogether blessed divinity, it is lawful that it be blessed by those receiving God, as far as it is possible for it to be known and praised by the intellects. For they are, as it were, deiform divine places, of the divine repose, as the oracles say. » He expounds the aforementioned testimony taken from Ezekiel. « Blessed be the glory of the Lord from his place. » This first hierarchy, he says, crying out, « Blessed be the glory of the Lord from his [1069A] place, » teaches that it is lawful — that is, just, or due, or worthy — that this most holy and supremely blessed and altogether blessed divinity be blessed by the intellects that receive God, and blessed to just such a degree as it is possible for it to be known and praised. It is just, he says, that this divinity, which is in itself ever most holy — ready, namely, to bestow, and not needing to receive — and which is supremely blessed, and altogether, or most strongly, blessed, that is, whose glory in itself is so great that it can neither be increased by another's benefit nor be further commended by another's praise. It is lawful, he says, that although it be in itself so perfect that it needs not to be blessed or praised, yet that the intellects — that is, rational hearts — should bless and praise it, as far, namely, as they can know it [1069B] and praise it according to the knowledge received. For although his glory be perfect of itself, yet we ought not to cease from blessing and praising him: we who, even if by blessing and praising we add no perfection to him, are nevertheless perfected by him. Or what he says can be understood otherwise: that it is just that the divinity, which is in itself supremely blessed, be blessed by the intellects that receive God. As if he said: Although the praise of God be in itself supremely perfect, yet this is by right assigned to his praise, that through grace he deigns to inhabit the hearts made by him. Thus, then, it is just that the divinity, in itself supremely blessed, be blessed also for this, and be blessed, that it offers itself to be received by the minds to be beatified, and deigns to inhabit them as its own [1069C] place. For it does not so dwell in them as though seeking a support, and as though about to fall if it had no prop. For God does not seek a place for himself, as though he could not exist unless he were contained; but he seeks, as it were, a receptacle into which to pour himself: which certainly could not be blessed, unless it were filled by him. Therefore it is added, above all his praise, that he who is supremely blessed in himself makes others blessed from himself. Let him be blessed by them, let him be blessed also for them. Let him be blessed by them, in so far as he is revealed to them to be known and praised; let him be blessed for them, in so far as he is made manifest in them.

There follows: « For they are, as it were, deiform divine places, of the divine repose, as the oracles say. » He gives the reason why, from that which has been said, « Blessed be the [1069D] glory of the Lord from his place, » he understands it to be signified that the glory ought to be blessed by the intellects that receive God: because, he says, these intellects that receive God, they themselves are the divine places. For "Theodocos" is said in Greek, that is, "receiver of God." By which name every pure mind, whether human or angelic, can be signified. For in these two God dwells and rests. They, then, are as it were deiform divine places. He gives, as it were, the reason why they are called divine places. For the cause is the same why they are places and why they are deiform. For from the fact that they receive light, they become a place of light. And again from the very fact that they receive light, they become shining, and exist conformed to the light. They are, then, divine places, inasmuch as [1070A] they are deiform, and have the likeness of God from the God who dwells within. Again, because they are divine places, it is clear that they are places of the divine repose, because they could not be divine places at all unless they were places of repose and peace, as the oracles say: « In peace is his place made [n: (Psal. LXXV)]. »

There follows: « And that it is a monad, and a unity three substantially: and, over the celestial essences down to the uttermost parts of the earth, extending its goodness into all things that are — a providence, as the super-principal principle of all essence, and the cause, and binding all things about, superessentially, with an immeasurable containment. » This he adds concerning the exposition of what follows, which had been taken from Isaiah. As if he said: Just as that first celestial hierarchy, crying out, [1070B] « Blessed be the glory of the Lord from his place, » taught that God is to be blessed and glorified not only in himself, but also in his saints, so too, by proclaiming him thrice holy, and by adding not "lords" but "the Lord of Sabaoth," it signified that there are three persons in one Godhead — not three gods, but one God. This is what he says. And because it is a monad, and a unity three substantially, as if he said: Not only this, which was said above concerning the glory of the Lord to be blessed from his place, did this first celestial hierarchy introduce to the hierarchies following after it; but also this, that the monad and unity — that is, the divinity, which is truly one and a simple nature — is three persons substantially, that is, that the three persons are one essence or substance, and the one essence is three persons; [1070C] because God is one in essence, and three in persons. Truly, that "monad" and "unity," though they seem to be the same, are set down doubled, is done on account of the Greek: in which there are two names, "monas" and "henas": which, although they have one interpretation with us (for both sound "unity"), yet this difference seems to exist, that "monas" signifies rather that unity which is spoken of according to distinction, while "henas" signifies that which is marked according to simplicity. For everything that is simple is one. But not everything that is one is simple; since a thing is called one by collection, and one by composition, and one by likeness. All of which, because it is not the whole of what they are that is one, are not truly one, and borrow the name of unity by likeness only, not by property. [1070D] But to the divine nature, for which to be one and to be simple is the same, its whole being is truly to be one; and therefore rightly it had to be called not only "monas," but "henas" also; because its unity subsists ever in the true simplicity of the same and undivided substance. Let him say, then, that in the true Godhead the monad and unity are substantially; because, as the trinity of persons in God does not divide the unity of essence, so the unity of nature does not confound the trinity of persons. But the three are substantially one, of which three the substance is one.

There follows: « And, over the celestial essences down to the uttermost parts of the earth, extending its goodness into all things that are — a providence. » And this too, he says, [1071A] the first hierarchy insinuated to those following after it: that the true unity and supreme trinity extends, or makes to penetrate, its goodness from the supercelestial essences down to the uttermost parts of the earth, that is, from the highest things to the lowest; its goodness, I say, being a providence into all things, because it provides for all and leaves nothing foreign to itself, inasmuch as it is above the principal, or super-essential, principle of all essence, and the cause of all; because from it and unto it all things were made: and which is the binding-about of all things [1072A] — that is, the encirclement, because it binds about all things, and embraces, and contains them superessentially with an immeasurable containment, which comprehends the whole, and in it itself remains incomprehensible. Because, then, it is the principle from which all things were made, and the cause through which all things were made, and the binding-about containment in which all things subsist, therefore all the earth is full of his glory, since from that goodness all things are filled and nourished, that they may subsist in it, which were made through it.
