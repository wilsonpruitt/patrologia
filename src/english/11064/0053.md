## THE EXPOSITION.

The eighth chapter is on the dominations, and the virtues, and the powers, and on the middle hierarchy, which is theirs. For in the preceding chapter he treated [1073D] of the three first orders — the seraphim, and the cherubim, and the thrones — and of the hierarchy, that is, their sacred power, which in the celestial ordering is first and principal, and ordered immediately after God. Now, therefore, he proceeds consequently to treat of the three following orders, that is, the dominations, virtues, and powers; and of the hierarchy, that is, their sacred power, which in the celestial ordering is constituted second, or middle, that is, after the first and before the last. Of this hierarchy, then, he treats in the same manner as he does of the higher order: first by showing the cause or reason of the appellation which is attributed to the orders themselves of this hierarchy, then by expounding more fully the power of that sacred authority.

[1074A] « We must now, he says, pass over into the middle ordering of the celestial intellects: exploring those dominations, as far as is possible, with supermundane eyes, and the truly powerful contemplations of the divine powers and virtues. » Join it together: himself to the following statement, as if he said. After the exposition of the supreme hierarchy, the order demands that we pass over to the middle, that is, to the exposition of the middle hierarchy of the celestial intellects, that is, of the angelic spirits. Afterward he sets forth by name which are those orders of angels in which that middle sacred power consists: to whose exposition he says he ought to pass over, adding: « The dominations: exploring those, as far as is possible, with supermundane eyes, and the truly powerful [1074B] contemplations of the divine powers and virtues. We must pass over, he says, » he says, « as we explore in that » — namely hierarchy — « the dominations, and the truly powerful contemplations of the powers and virtues, » that is, both the powers and the virtues. For these are the three orders of the second hierarchy, whose power and offices he must diligently explore who desires to expound their sacred power. Therefore, he says, « with supermundane eyes, » that is, with spiritual ones, it must be explored. For these things are not seen with bodily eyes. « As we explore, » therefore, « those, » namely the celestial dominations, and « as we explore the contemplations, » that is, the speculations or contemplations « of the powers and virtues. » As to his saying that he will explore the speculations or contemplations [1074C] of the powers and virtues; either it is to be understood thus, as if he said, that through the speculation or contemplation had concerning them he will explore their dignity, or he testifies that he will explore and investigate their very contemplation, by which they contemplate God and perceive divine things. He calls them "truly powerful contemplations," or "powerful speculations," for this reason, that they explore those who are truly powerful. For by "powerful speculation" he understands "speculation of the powerful." He names them "truly powerful" for this reason, that they are powerful in goodness and in truth. For to be able to do evil is not truly to be able, but to be unable, as it is written: « Why dost thou glory in malice, thou that art mighty in iniquity [n: (Psal. LI)]: » Wherefore he also calls those [1074D] powers divine, because from God and in God they can do all that they can.

There follows: « For each appellation of the essences which are above us signifies their deiform properties, imitative of God. As if he said: Rightly have I named the powers and virtues divine, because their very appellation also teaches this. Just as each appellation of the essences which are above us, that is, the celestial ones, signifies their deiform properties: and they are deiform for this reason, that they are imitative of God. For that those celestial essences have conformity, they have it not from quality, but from imitation, because it is not nature that confers on them the likeness of God, but grace.

There follows: « Therefore I judge that the manifesting naming of the holy dominations declares a certain absolute [1075A] anagogy, free from all pedestrian diminishment; and in no way whatsoever universally inclined by any of the tyrannical dissimilitudes, a dominion liberally severe, set above all diminishing servitude: superior to every subjection, and remote from every dissimulation, and unceasingly desirous of dominion; and conforming, as far as is possible, both itself and the things that are after it, in the best and most beautiful way, to the likeness of that very virtue subsisting naturally; turned toward none of the things seen as vain, but toward the properly ὄν on universal, and made, according to what is possible for it, ever in participation of the Lordly deiformity. » All these things the naming of the dominations teaches; for in that they are called dominations, they are signified to have and to do all these things [1075B] which have been said of them. This is what he says: « I judge the manifesting naming of the holy dominations, » that is, the naming by which their power and excellence and dignity is manifested and declared: I judge, he says, that that naming declares all these things. All what things? Hear. « For they are many. » Therefore I said "all these things": because they are many with respect to us, but few with respect to them. « I judge, he says, that the naming of the holy dominations declares a certain absolute, » that is, free, and estranged from all depression, « anagogy, » that is, their excellence, or sublimity, or upward leading, by which they are raised to the highest, so that they are subjected to none below the highest. And I judge that this naming also [1075C] declares that this anagogy, or their excellence, is not universally inclined in any way — that is, that it is in no way at all inclined by any of the tyrannical dissimilitudes, that is, through any, or according to any, tyrannical dissimilitude, that is, according to any tyranny, which is wholly dissimilar from their dominion. As if he said: Just as by their universal excellence they are raised above all subjection, so toward the oppression of their subjects they are inclined by no tyranny.

There follows: « A dominion liberally severe; set above all diminishing servitude. » From what precedes (it is to be understood) "I judge the naming of the dominations to declare" their dominion "liberally severe," that is, kindly severe. Having severity [1075D] in power, liberality in kindness. Severe, because power is exercised from the superior; yet liberty is not taken away from the inferior. Therefore he calls it a dominion liberally severe, set above all diminishing servitude, that is, not subject to anyone unto a servitude which would diminish liberty, but superior to every subjection, and remote from every dissimilitude, that is, from all inferiority or imperfection which would make it dissimilar to its Creator, and unceasingly desirous of dominion, that is, looking toward — or tending toward, or clinging to — the Creator through likeness of dominion and conformity, and conforming both itself and the things that are after it, so that each, as far as is possible for it, may [1076A] imitate the divine virtue in the best and most beautiful way. Therefore he says, « I judge their naming to declare their dominion conforming itself, and the things that are after it, to the divine virtue; and turned toward none of the things seen as vain, but toward the properly on universal. » He puts "of those seeing" (videntium) for what he ought to have said, "of things seen" (visorum), by the most usual manner of the Greeks; since it is derived from the verb δοκοῦμαι, that is, "I see" or "I am seen," because it is common: so that the sense is: I judge it to declare her turned not toward any of those things which seem vain, that is, which, considered in themselves, appear lowly and transitory. But rather turned toward the properly on universal, that is, turned toward that which is properly and universally; namely toward God, to whom being is both proper, because he subsists through himself, and universal, [1076B] because he grants being to all. But it must be known that ὄν on among the Greeks, when it is written with ὄντῶν μικρὸν, through omicron — that is, a short or small "o" — is neuter, and is interpreted *that which is.* But when ὤν is written with ω τὸν μέγα, that is, a long or great "ω," it is masculine, and is interpreted *who is.* "On," therefore, is the Creator of all himself, and the principle, who is, and that which is properly in himself, and universal toward all: toward which principle he says this dominion is turned, and made in participation, that is, made a partaker of the Lordly deiformity, that is, of the divine likeness, which it retains always, that is, unchangeably: made, I say, according to what is possible for it. Thus far he has shown what the naming of the dominations signifies; now he passes on to show what the naming [1076C] of the virtues signifies.
