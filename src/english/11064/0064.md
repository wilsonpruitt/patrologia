## EXPOSITION.

The title of the eleventh chapter is: Why all the celestial essences are commonly called virtues. This is the question: since only one order among the celestial spirits is called virtues, why is this name commonly attributed to all. And he says:

« But these things being defined, it behooves to understand that which is worthy: for what cause we are wont to call all the angelic essences alike celestial virtues. » [1105C] This indeed is worthy of inquiry; because it is not established that the cause here is the one which we render when we call all the celestial essences angels: namely, that the order of the angels is the last, and therefore its name is assumed to the higher orders, because its property is partaken by them; since all the higher, just as they partake all the properties of the lower, so also fittingly sometimes assume their name. But here we cannot say likewise, when we name all the celestial spirits virtues; because, since certain orders are lower than the virtues themselves, they, just as they do not partake in the property of the higher, so also it seems that they ought not to partake in the name. This is what he says: « For it is not [1105D] to be said, as in the angels, namely, that the disposition of the holy virtues itself is the last of all. » For if this were true, it would be no wonder if all the other orders partook their name, because they would partake their property as being higher. This is what follows: « And indeed of the last essences, » that is, of the last spirits, « the dispositions of the superposed essences partake the holy and comely illumination; but the last, » understand: dispositions, « in no wise, » namely, partake the illumination of the first essences.

There follows: « And for whose sake are all the divine intellects indeed named celestial virtues; but the seraphim, and thrones, and dominations in no wise. » [1106A] It is worthy, he says, to understand for what — understand: thing — cause all the divine intellects are named celestial virtues, whereas the seraphim, and thrones, and dominations are in no wise named. For that they are named angels is no wonder, because the angels are last, and their property is universally partaken by the higher. This is what he says: « For the extremes, » understand: essences, such as are the angels, « are partaken of by the most excellent virtues » in all their properties; but not otherwise, the properties of the higher come universally to the participation of the lower. Therefore it is a wonder how the angels, and those who are before the angels, the archangels, and the principalities, and the powers, assume the name of virtues, since they do not partake the property; because « after the virtues [1106B] they are ordered by theology itself, » that is, they are reported to be ordered after the virtues by the divine Scripture. And yet « together with the other holy essences they are called by us celestial virtues. »

There follows: « But we say that, using commonly in all the naming of celestial virtues, we do not introduce a certain confusion of the properties of each disposition. » Now he resolves the question, why the name of virtues is attributed to the lower orders. « We say, » he says, « that we, using the naming of celestial virtues, commonly also for all, do not bring in any confusion of the properties of each disposition, » that is, by this, that we make the name common, we do not confound the property of distinction. But rather, when [1106C] we name the lower orders virtues, we signify not the property of that order, but the virtue common to all. For all the divine intellects are divided into three, not among themselves, one, namely, in relation to another, but each according to itself, or in itself, « by a supermundane reason, » that is, spiritual. For that they are divided is not made from a consideration of parts, where there is not simplicity of essence, but there is a spiritual distinction, where not the whole is discerned into parts, but the nature into properties. Afterward he adds into what three each spirit is divided within itself, namely « into essence, and virtue, and operation. » For in every spirit these three are. First, essence, in which it subsists; then virtue, according to which it is strong; then operation, [1106D] through which it effects. « When therefore we call all together, » namely spirits, « or certain of them, unobservantly, » that is, indistinctly or commonly, « either celestial essences, or celestial virtues, it is to be supposed that we signify those, of whom the discourse is, periphrastically, » that is, by circumlocution, « from that essence, or virtue, which is through each of them, » that is, which is common to all, not from that which is properly singular, which discerns the dignity of its order. But why he is unwilling to attribute the singular property of one order to the other subject orders, he shows, saying: « For neither, » he says, « is it easy for us to annex the superposed property of the holy virtues, already distinguished by us, » that is, properly and singularly attributed to them, « to the lesser essences, » or to bestow it, [1107A] « in a turning-about of the unconfused ordination of the angelic adornments; » because if I should do this, I should turn about, or pervert, the ordination of the angelic adornments, which is unconfused. But how the ordination of the angelic adornments, that is, of the angelic orders beautifully or comely disposed, is unconfused, he shows, namely, because they keep their own distinction, and do not mingle themselves with the higher, in whose properties they have no part. « The superfirmed dispositions indeed, » that is, the higher orders, « have abundantly, » or fully, « even the sacred properties of the lesser; but the last do not have the superposed universalities of the greater. » A little above we already said how, there, where God is all in all, anything proper can belong to any one, which [1107B] is not common to all. Here too the author has not altogether kept silence how this is to be understood, in that he says: « They have abundantly. » In this, then, is the property to be understood, which befits the higher, [1108A] does not befit the lower; that the higher have abundantly whatever the lower have; but the lower do not have abundantly whatever the higher have. For what can be so great there, which does not pass into the communion of all — there, where charity is common, which is greater than all? Therefore all things are of all. But this alone is proper there, that it is abundantly to any one what is not to all; because the higher virtues indeed have universally and abundantly those of the lower; but the lower do not equally have the virtues of the higher universally, that is, according to all fullness, of the divine illuminations, « only particularly first appearing into them. » Not first appearing into them, but first particularly into them. For into others, that is, [1108B] into the higher, they first appear universally, and then afterward through those first ones they are distributed proportionally, that is, according to proportion, not according to all its fullness.

## TITLE OF CHAPTER XII. *Why hierarchies according to men are called angels.*



## THE TEXT.

[1107B]

*But this also is inquired by those studiously beholding the intelligible oracles. For if the participants of the more excellent universalities of the virtues are not the last, for what cause is the highest priest according to us named an angel of God almighty by the oracles? [n: (Malach. II; Apoc. II.)] But there is not a contrary reason, as I suppose, to the things before defined. For we say that [1107C] by the universal and superposed virtue of the greater adornments the last are left behind. For they partake the middle and proportional according to the one conjunctive society of all at once: such as is that the order of the holy cherubim partakes wisdom and a higher knowledge. But under them the dispositions of the essences do indeed themselves also partake wisdom and knowledge, particular, however, in relation to those, and subject. And indeed to be altogether in participation of wisdom and of knowledge is common to all deiform intellects. But attentively, and firstly, or secondly, or below, is by no means common; but as for each one before, its proper analogy is defined. And this, of all the divine minds, one perhaps not erring will define. For as the first have more abundantly the holy and beautiful [1107D] properties of the lesser, so the last have those of the first; not however alike, but below. Nothing therefore, as I suppose, is inordinate, if theology also calls the highest priest according to us an angel, according to the proper virtue of the angels partaking of the prophetic property, and, unto a manifestative likeness of them, extended as far as is possible for men. But thou wilt find that theology calls both gods, and the celestial essences above us, and those among us most beloved of God, and wonderful men. And indeed the divine secret, superessentially at once remote from all, and set above, and none of those things which are from it can properly, or at all, be named like to it. Nevertheless whatsoever, both of the intellectual [1108B] and of the rational, is universally converted to the unity of it, and whatever virtue, and is unceasingly extended to its divine illuminations, as far as is possible, according to virtue — if it is just to say it — is made worthy by divine imitation, and by a divine univocation.*
