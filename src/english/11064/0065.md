## EXPOSITION.

The title of the twelfth chapter is: Why the hierarchies according to men are called angels. The sense of it is: [1108C] Why those who are hierarchies, that is, the sacred princes according to men or among men — such as are the highest pontiffs, or any priests and the other ministers of the word of God — why, namely, those are called angels, since it was said above that the lower, just as they do not partake the properties of the higher, so also ought not to assume their names. For if the order of the angels, because it is lower than the virtues, cannot partake their name according to their property, neither can men (because they are undoubtedly lower than the angels) usurp their name according to their property. But this question is resolved by that which we said above: that the gifts of spiritual grace, which pass into the communion of all, [1108D] also bring their appellation into the participation of all; and although, by the fact that they are possessed singularly through excellence by certain ones, they make a distinct appellation, yet by the fact that in some manner they are common, they also sometimes, according to the requirement of the causes, bring their names to a common appellation.

« But this also is inquired by those who studiously behold the intelligible oracles. » By those studiously beholding, that is, by the studious inspectors of the intelligible oracles, that is, of the divine oracles — which are intelligible, that is, spiritual and profound, made concerning intelligible things — is inquired, I say, this, namely, why men set over others are called angels. [1109A] But why this is inquired he subjoins the reason: « For if the last, » that is, the lower orders, « are not partaking of the virtues of the higher universalities, » that is, of the properties which are in the higher universally, or, to say it more expressly, universally, that is, fully and perfectly: « for what cause then is the priest, who according to us » or among us, namely men, « is the highest, named by the oracles an angel of God almighty? » For if he does not have the singularity of the property, why does he assume the expression of the appellation? It seems contrary that a name of distinction should be brought to communion. But yet, he says, « this reason, » or assertion, « is not contrary, as I suppose, to the things before defined, » that is, to those which were defined before, namely concerning the distinction of the several ones. For in [1109B] this, that we concede such a communion of appellation to the many, we do not take away the distinction of the several ones. « For we truly say that the last, » or lowest, namely orders, « are left behind by the universal and superposed virtue of the greater adornments, » that is, of the more excellent orders, so that, namely, they do not partake the virtues of the higher according to that universality, or totality, or fullness by which those partake. And yet they can sometimes fittingly partake their name; because, although they do not partake their virtue according to an equal fullness, they nevertheless partake it according to a like imitation.

« For they partake, » he says, « the middle and the proportional, » understand: virtue — that is, they partake [1109C] their virtue, and though not according to the highest participation, in which they would be equal, yet they partake according to a middle, or moderate and lower, participation, in which they can be alike; but this participation is made not according to the singular perfection of each one, but « according to the one and conjunctive society of all at once, » that is, not according to that which is singular to each one, but according to that which is common to all. And because it is one, that is, common to all, in it all are at once joined and associated. And so, according to that which is common to all, the singular names are drawn to a common signification; because, as we said, even that which through excellence [1109D] is proper to certain ones is, through participation, common to all. « Such as is that the order of the holy cherubim partakes wisdom, and a higher knowledge, » etc. By the subjoined example he proves what he said: that that which certain ones alone excellently possess comes, according to a lower communion, into the participation of all; such as is this, that the order of the holy cherubim partakes wisdom and a higher knowledge. « But the dispositions of the essences, » that is, the orders of spirits which are under the cherubim themselves, « do indeed themselves also partake wisdom and knowledge; but particular, however, and subject in relation to those, » that is, not so perfectly nor so excellently as they. And truly not the cherubim alone partake wisdom and knowledge, but also others lower; [1110A] because « to be altogether, » that is, universally, « in the participation of wisdom and knowledge is common to all deiform intellects, » that is, to all spirits conformable and like to God through the reason implanted in them. To partake wisdom in some manner is indeed common to all. But « to partake it attentively and firstly, » that is, expressly and principally, « or secondly, or even to partake it below, is by no means common to all. » For those who do not partake firstly partake secondly, or below, that is, they partake more lowly and more imperfectly; but they do not also partake firstly; because whoever have perfection have also the beginning; but not whoever have the beginning can straightway have perfection. « But » they have each thing only [1110B] « as it is defined to each one, » or dispensed, or as some one thing is determinately assigned, « according to its proper analogy, » that is, according to the rule, or manner, or proper measure, which was predestined and foreseen for it before all things in the eternal providence of God. « But this, » namely that the lower partake the virtues of the higher not according to fullness, yet in some manner, perhaps « someone will define, » or affirm, « of all the divine minds, » that is, of the rational spirits, « not erring » in this, because it is true. « For indeed as the first, » and the higher orders, « have more abundantly the holy and beautiful properties of the lesser, so the last themselves and the lesser have those properties which are of the former: not however alike, but below, » that is, more imperfectly than they. Therefore, [1110C] he says, « as I suppose, nothing is inordinate, » understand: was or is, « if theology calls the highest priest according to us an angel, » that is, him who according to us men discharges the highest priesthood, or calls him an angel according to us, because, namely, he announces to us the word of God; and therefore an angel, because « partaking according to his proper virtue, » that is, according to the grace given to him from his proper office: « partaking, » I say, « of the prophetic property » of the angels, because it belongs to him to prophesy, and to announce the word of God as the angels announce. And him, I say, also « extended, » or advanced, or exalted, unto the manifestative likeness of them, namely of the angels, because in this he is like the angels, that he manifests [1110D] or reveals the secrets of God for the instruction of his subjects. Nor is it a wonder if men are called angels, since angels and men alike have been called gods.

This is what he says: « But thou wilt find that theology calls even gods the celestial and higher essences themselves, » that is, the angels, and not only those, but « also certain ones » among us, that is, men of our kind, namely « wonderful, and most beloved of God. » But why men are called angels, or men gods, he subjoins the cause: namely, because the divinity, although it is secret and remote, according to the excellence of its nature, from every creature, is nevertheless in some manner conceived and partaken by the rational minds which turn themselves through virtue to its unity and illumination; and therefore, inasmuch as they are [1111A] partakers of the divinity, they can in some manner not unfittingly be called gods.

This is what he says: « And indeed the divine secret, » that is, the secret of the divinity, « both is remote and is set above, superessentially, at once above all things; and none of those which are from it, » understand: created, « can at all and properly be named like to it; » because the Creator and the creature cannot expressly be alike. « Nevertheless whatsoever virtue of the intellectual and the rational, » that is, of the angels, « and, » to say it universally, « whatsoever [1112A] virtue, » whether of angels or of men, « is converted to the unity of it, » namely of the divinity; and whatsoever virtue is unceasingly extended to receive the divine illuminations of that same Deity, that virtue is made worthy of divine imitation through likeness, and of divine univocation through appellation: made worthy, I say, is that virtue according to virtue, that is, worthy in so far as it is virtue. Yet only if it is just to say this, that any virtue, however great, can be worthy of « divine imitation, and divine univocation. »
