## EXPOSITION.

« Come, let us consider this also according to virtue. » Come now, he says, O Timothy, after the other things which have been said, [1114D] let us consider this also according to our virtue and possibility, « for what reason, namely, it is said by the theologians that one of the seraphim was sent » — in Isaiah, namely, when, the coal having been taken up from the altar with the tongs, he purged the lips of the prophet [n: (Isa. VII)]. And it is necessary that we inspect this question, and discuss it, because it can become doubtful whether by 'seraph' here some one of the higher order is to be understood, on account of the property of the name; or one of the lower, on account of the property of administration, because it is proper to the weak and the lowest to administer our hierarchy. Therefore the question is to be inspected, that the ambiguity of the estimation may be taken away. For perhaps « someone may answer that it is not one of the subordinate ones, » that is, some one of the Angels, « that purges the priest, » [1115A] namely Isaiah; « but a certain one of the innermost and greatest essences, » that is, the supreme spirits. But this again can be doubtful to someone, for the reason that the supreme orders are not sent to dispense, or administer, exterior things.

Therefore « certain ones say that, according to the definition of the society of all the intellects already rendered before » by us, that is, according to that which we defined above — they say that all spirits have among themselves a certain society of properties and names — that the oracle « does not name, » that is, « does not » say « that some one of the first minds came unto the purgation of the theologian, » that is, of the first spirits about God, understand: established. But they say « that a certain one rather of those excelling us, » who [1115B] are set only over us men, « performing the sacrifice of the prophet's purgation, » that is, making it holy, and offering as it were a sacrifice pleasing to God, « was called seraph, by equivocation, » that is, of the name, not by the excellence of the property. But by what likeness an angel of the lowest order was called seraph, he subjoins: « On account of the fiery and celestial washing away, » or cleansing, « of the » sins « aforesaid » already above, of the prophet himself, and on account of the « raising up » also, or vivification, of that same one « purged into the divine obedience; » because just as it belongs to fire to purge rust by burning, and to vivify dead and extinguished things by heating, so by that divine fire he was both purged from sins, and inflamed [1115C] to the love of God. And because this was done by the ministry of an angel, « certain ones say » that the sacred « oracle said 'one of the seraphim' simply, not one » of the virtues « set about God, » but rather one « of the purgative virtues excelling us, » that is, one of the angels set over us, that is, over men — yet him called 'seraph' on account of the likeness of the action.

There follows: « But another furnished me a certain not too unfitting apology concerning this kind of state. » Certain ones, he says, hold that the angel who purged the prophet was of the supreme spirits, on account of this, that he was called seraph. But others say that he was of the lower ones, and called seraph only on account of the likeness of the action: [1115D] thus each one defining probably according to his own opinion. But a certain « other, » agreeing wholly neither with those nor with these, « furnished me a certain apology, » that is, an excuse, or satisfaction, or defense, « not too unfitting, » concerning this kind of state, that is, definition, by which the state of the matter is described; or concerning this kind of instance, that is, question, which in some manner presses, and is importunate until it is resolved. But what satisfaction or defense he himself furnished concerning this question, he subjoins: « For he says (understand: he who furnishes the apology) that that great angel, who then was forming the vision for teaching the theologian divine things, » that is, in the mind of the theologian, whom that angel was bound to teach divine things through that vision [1116A] — namely, his own purgative sacrifice, that is, the operation by which he then properly and singularly purged the lips of the prophet, and made them holy, that operation of his he referred back « unto God, » that is, attributed to God, « and after God referred it back unto the pre-operative hierarchy, » that is, unto the hierarchy in which and through which God first, that is, before all the others, operates. But he attributed his action to that hierarchy for this reason, that from it, after God, he principally received that he should do this. And this is the reason why the seraph is said to be the highest, namely because the angel who did this, just as he principally received it, after God, from the seraph, so also, after God, attributed it to the seraph itself. This reason therefore he rendered concerning this kind of question. « And is not this reason then [1116B] truthful? » These are the words of the author, who seems to accommodate his own estimation to this opinion.

This reason seems to be truthful because that man, « who said this, » says, showing « how the divine virtue, coming into all things, fills all » — and not only comes, but also penetrates, and passes through all, immeasurably crossing over them, « and is invisible to all. » And again this is invisible, thus transcending all without end, as it is invisible to all, subsisting before all without beginning. Or « again it is invisible » through immensity, as it is invisible through eternity, because just as temporal intelligence does not grasp eternity, so a circumscribable nature does not comprehend immensity. Or again « it is invisible, » that is, not only in itself, according as [1116C] it is essentially and ineffably remote from all, but also to us, according as it is incomprehensibly hidden from all. In itself therefore it is invisible, and in us it is invisible, and in both ways it remains incomprehensible. This is what he says: « Because it is not only invisible in itself, as being superessentially remote from all, but » it is also invisible in us, « as secretly letting, » or causing to penetrate, « into all things its provident operations. » Thus therefore the divine virtue is both invisible in itself as remote, and invisible in us as hidden, through operation, because according to its providence it operates in us. « But nevertheless, » although it is thus invisible, it shines above, pouring itself out [1116D] unto manifestation « to all the intellectual beings, » that is, to the rational minds. « It shines above, » I say, « proportionally, » that is, to some more, to others less, namely according to the measure and capacity of each one. And though it shines above commonly to all, yet excellently « bringing its own illumination into the most precious essences, » that is, into the subtlest and most excellent spirits: « through those » essences, namely, « as the first ones » it afterward distributes itself in well-adorned fashion « into others, » namely essences, « established under those » first ones, that is, made firm, or collocated, or disposed. « It distributes, » I say, « according to the contemplative commensuration of each disposition, » that is, according as each disposition, or order, is capable of that contemplation according to its proper [1117A] measure: « which, » understand: distribution of the divine illumination, arriving thus differently according to the measure of the receivers, « that I may say it more openly, also through examples proper; » proper, I say, « though » that is, although, yet « deficient in relation to God, remote from all; » that is, deficient in comparison with God, who is remote from all things, and can properly be demonstrated by no likeness. Therefore deficient, and not proper. « Nevertheless » proper, because « more manifest to us. » Not proper to the divine majesty, proper to human possibility; not proper as it is in him, proper as it can be shown to us. That therefore I may show, through examples not proper to God but proper to us, the distribution of the divine illumination to be dispensed, [1117B] I set forth this likeness. « The distributions of the solar ray, well distributed into the first matter, fill it, the most lucid of all, and through it it more manifestly declares its own splendors. » The distributions, he says, or outpourings of the solar ray, well distributed into the first matter, that is, coming into that matter which is first, that is, best and purest, and most apt for illumination; coming, I say, to such a matter, they fill it, finding in no part any obstacle to the light, and by filling they make it more lucid, most lucid of all; and thus through it the ray itself more manifestly declares its own splendors, because the more perfectly it radiates, the more perfectly it will be illuminated; and the more perfectly it is poured into it, the more brightly [1117C] it is poured back from it. « But approaching grosser matters, » that is, the grosser matters, and those which are less apt for illumination, there « it has a dimmer distributive over-appearance. » For just as, when it pours itself into them, it is received imperfectly, so also, when it pours itself out through them, over this it appears dimly from without: which comes from « the inopportuneness of the matters to be illuminated; » the inopportuneness harms, and is a hindrance to « the distributive habit of illumination, » that is, to this, that the illumination itself be either distributed, or possessed. Thus therefore in pure and polished bodies it shines more perfectly, in the grosser and coarser ones it is lessened; and a little afterward, descending to others still less apt, from this defect, [1117D] that is, after this defect, it is contracted almost to the perfectly undistributed, that is, to this, that it be in no way distributed. For from the subtlest matters, by which it is perfectly comprehended, descending to the less apt, by which it is imperfectly received, it comes at last to others so dreggy, and gross, and dull, that they almost wholly repel the light, and can receive nothing of the illumination. In this manner the spiritual light, coming to the rational minds, perfectly illuminates those which it finds pure and purified, but those which it finds less apt it illumines with a lesser brightness, and, so to speak, with a dimmer light. But those which it meets as wholly contrary and dreggy, it leaves altogether free from its participation and communion. Thus through the likeness of visible things the truth of invisible things is demonstrated. [1118A] Not only in light, but also in material heat, is there an image of the invisible truth. For heat itself more kindles those bodies which it finds more apt for its reception, but others less, until at last, meeting things wholly contrary and dissimilar, it shows in them no effect of its operation.
