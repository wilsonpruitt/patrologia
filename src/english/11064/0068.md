## EXPOSITION. (cont.)

This is what he says: « Again the heat of fire, » as if he said: Not only in the splendor of light, but also in the heat of fire, can the aforesaid likeness be seen. « For the heat of fire distributes itself more into the more capacious ones » — understand: bodies — and into those which are well suited to its likeness, so that, namely, they may be heated by the heat, because between the hot and heat there stands a likeness, as between the white and whiteness, and the good and goodness, and, in short, of every [1118B] property to its own affection. But coming, namely the heat, « to the essences contrary to its re-formings, » because, namely, they resist by a contrary quality the heating through which they ought to have been re-formed unto the likeness of heat, in them the heat itself « manifests no trace, » or at least « hidden, » or obscure, « of its primal operation; » that is, it shows there no effect, in which it could even faintly be recognized that it has there at least some beginning of operation. In this manner the rational substances either receive the fire of celestial love, if they have been apt, and dwelling-places prepared and suited to its reception, or they wholly repel it, if they disagree by a contrary quality, or cannot [1118C] be kindled. I desire that my soul repel not, by any dregginess of earthly filth, the light of the inner brightness, that it shut not out, by any coldness of malice, the heat of holy devotion, but that it grow bright and grow warm, re-formed from heaven unto the divine likeness. O what an essence, joined to the superessential good! A blessed nature, full of the supernatural good! Happily has it been made, which merits thus to be refreshed. If this be granted to me, I have nothing further to complain of concerning all these things which have passed before. The creature shows the craftsman, and the form of wonderful works commends the beauty of the author. There is one good and one beauty, and the good itself is the very beauty. The highest good and the highest beauty, and in the highest good every good, one good, and in the highest beauty every beauty, [1118D] one beauty. But the visible nature could not contain all things in that one; and by God many good things were made, that they might show forth one highest good, and likewise many beautiful things, that they might demonstrate the image of one highest beauty. But many lives too were established in those whose condition merited to have something more, beyond those which were made indeed good and beautiful, and yet cannot receive this good which is life. And all these lives emulate one highest life: each in its own kind, in that which it is, and inasmuch as it is from her, and according to her who alone is true. But of every corporeal life there are two principles, heat and moisture: the one, namely moisture, furnishes the nourishment of life; the other, that is, heat, makes life to feel. Without heat [1119A] that which subsists does not live; without moisture that which lives does not subsist. Therefore these two, by a rational tempering, pour themselves into all things to be vivified, so that from them each life may receive the measure of its own capacity, as much as is fitting, to the end that the lower lives may advance, through the steps of their increments, unto the image of the highest life. For the first step of corporeal life is sense-making; the second, entering through sense, imagination; the third, through the imagination of the things conceived, memory; the fourth, according to the passible application of sense, a certain foresight without the discernment of understanding. In which there is indeed as it were an image of reason, but there is no reason. According to this, certain brute animals too seem craftier than others of their kind, and, by a certain readiness as it were of sense, imitating the foresight of a rational [1119B] mind. Which nevertheless is proved to be rather a passion of sense than an operation of the understanding. But in all these the corporeal life imitates the spiritual life. First, namely, in that it feels; secondly, in that it conceives sense; thirdly, in that it retains the things conceived; fourthly, in that, whether in things imagined or in things felt, through the passion of sense, according to a certain likeness of reason, it bends itself either to seeking or to fleeing. And so in all these the corporeal lives hold the image of the spiritual lives, and through them as intermediaries emulate, as far as they can, the highest life. For the spiritual lives themselves too all receive from the highest life that they are lives, partaking the spiritual heat and moisture descending thence, whereby they may be nourished and vivified that they may live. [1119C] These two, spiritually conceived, perfect the spiritual life: the spiritual moisture nourishing through joy; and there stands no life which has not its own heat and moisture nourishing and making to feel, that it may be able to subsist in that which is life; and that which has these more is deservedly called more a life. As there are many lives, so there are many participations, and every participation is from one, and every life from one; and so the highest good, diffusing itself into all things, constitutes every life, and by forming leads back every life unto the highest life.

There follows: « And this the more. » As if he said: Not only is this an argument of the invisible truth, that some bodies receive corporeal light and material heat, while some [1119D] wholly repel them, but this also, that through those which are more warm the other less warm ones are kindled: this, he says, is a still further argument. Further after the first, not further above the first: this is an argument of the invisible truth. Because the heat itself of material fire is admitted, and poured into those bodies, namely, « which are not cognate » to it, that is, apt to receive it: poured, I say, into them « through others having what is opportune, » that is, which stand opportunely toward it. « First, » they receive it, « inasmuch as it makes things easily movable ignited from the fiery ones. » For the heat itself, from the fiery things which, more warm, are ignited, makes others, which are secondarily and less heated, which yet themselves become ignited from the fiery ones for this reason, that they are easily movable [1120A] and apt for the reception of heat. And so from the fiery things first and principally warm it makes things ignited, and through these ignited things, namely afterward, it heats still colder and more remote ones, that is, either water, or something else, that is, « something other of the things not easily catching fire, » that is, of those which do not easily catch fire. « It heats, » I say, « proportionally, » that is, each thing according to its manner, and its measure, and its capacity for receiving heat.

There follows: « According to this then, » etc. He adapts the likeness. « According to this, he says, reason, » that is, likeness « of natural ordination, » that is, of the ordination of natural and visible things, « the [sic: *supernatur alis*] ordination itself also, » that is, the [1120B] distribution of divine grace, which is « the ordination of every good adornment, » that is, of every order well and comelily disposed, whether « visible » as in men, or « invisible » as in angels; that, I say, supernatural ordination fittingly manifests first « the brightness of illumination, » that is, of manifestation, or revelation, « to the most exalted essences, » that is, to the supreme spirits: that « brightness, » I say, « first appearing » in them, inasmuch as « in the most copious outpourings, » that is, as it befits it to appear when it copiously pours itself out, or to those into whom it has most copiously poured itself. « And through those, » namely the most exalted essences, those essences « which are after » them « partake the divine ray, » that is, the divine brightness. « For these, » [1120C] namely the most exalted essences, « are the first knowing God, and supereminently desiring the divine virtue. » And because they principally know and love, they have been made worthy « to become pre-operative, as far as is possible, with virtue and action like to God; » that is, that before all the others, first after God, they may operate into all subject things, by bestowing the divine virtue; and in that very thing become like to God, because the virtue which they receive from above they expend upon their subjects. For they « extend the essences which are after them, or subject to them, unto a like virtue, » namely to their own virtue, inasmuch as it is a virtue holding itself deiformly, because in that they do this, by their virtue they imitate God, « copiously delivering to them, » namely to the subject essences, « from the brightness supervening upon them. And those, » [1120D] namely the subject essences, « again » deliver « to their own subjects; » « and » thus « through each one, » the outpouring of the divine gift running on, « the first delivers to that which is after it, » and so onward unto the end.

There follows: « The water itself, » etc. After the likeness which he set forth concerning fire according to brightness and heat, he sets forth again another likeness concerning water. « The water itself, he says, does it not proportionally reach into all? » The moisture itself of water, which diffuses itself through all the parts of earthly bodies, proportionally fills all, moistening some more, some less, according to this, namely, that each one is more or less capable of moistening. In this example therefore is fittingly demonstrated, likewise, the distribution of divine grace, coming forth to all in no equal [1121A] manner.

There follows: « It is therefore, » etc. Since, he says, just as one visible light illuminates many things, and from that light many shining things shine, so the invisible light illumines all things invisibly shining. « Therefore God is the principle of illuminating, » or of illumination, « at once to all the illuminated. » Nor is it a wonder: since he too is light, and not light in any manner whatsoever, but by nature, that is, naturally, and truly, and properly. Naturally, because from himself; and truly, because in himself; and properly, because through himself. Naturally, because from that which he is he is light; and truly, because that very thing which he is is light; and properly, because through that which he is he is light. For that is natural which is not taken from elsewhere, [1121B] that is truly which is essentially possessed, that is properly which is not conferred through another. And so thus God is light: and I say too little when I say that he is light, inasmuch as he is the essence of the light itself. For the rest, who shine, are not the essence of light, but its effect. They therefore, in that they are lights, are from light; but he is light from that which he is. « And it is also causal of being and seeing, that is, of the essence and vision of the light itself; » as if it were said thus: God is the essence of light, and is also the cause of that essence, because that which is light, is essentially; and that which is essentially, is from itself. Thus therefore he is the cause of the essence of light: and not only of the essence of light, but also of the vision itself, or manifestation, of the light, because he has from himself not only that he is essentially light [1121C] shining by nature, but this also, that he temporally shines, illuminating by grace.
