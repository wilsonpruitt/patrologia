## EXPOSITION. (cont.)

There follows: « He says therefore these things, saying, » etc. He, he says, of whom I spoke above, who furnished me a not unfitting apology. He, saying these things which have thus far been said concerning the divine illumination descending through some to others, says that the aforesaid vision — about which the question arose concerning the purification of the prophet's lips — « was received by the theologian himself, » namely Isaiah, « through one of the holy and blessed » angels « ruling over us, » that is, over men, « descending from that, » understand: the first hierarchy. For he says this, that that vision which Isaiah saw descended from the first hierarchy, and was brought to the prophet himself [1124A] through one angel of the last order of them, who are the lowest among the angels and preside over us alone: and therefore he says that that angel « before its own illuminative hand-leading, » that is, that revelation by which he himself led the prophet by the hand and instructed him, and by which he himself had before been illuminated by the first hierarchy: « had reposed in, » that is, had referred to « that holy contemplation, » that is, to the first hierarchy itself, from which he contemplated that he had had what he had, and had done what he did. « He sees also, » Isaiah himself, « sitting above, » that is, sitting on high and aloft, « the most exalted essences, placed after God, and about God, and with God, as far as, in symbols, » that is, in figures and likenesses, it « is to be said. » For all this is symbolic and figurative, [1124B] that God, according to a corporeal likeness, is said to sit upon a lofty throne; and the seraphim are reported to stand about him and beside him. And Isaiah himself sees also a summit more sublime than « all these, » namely than the most exalted essences of the blessed spirits; « even super-secretly, » that is, in a most excellent manner: « the summit, » I say, « above the principal, » that is, God, who is the highest and principal of all, superplaced « in the midst of the superfirmed virtues. » For in the midst of the virtues, that is, of the holy spirits, who are firmed and established by an eternal placing about God above all things, God himself, the highest and principal, is seen sitting upon a throne lofty and elevated. « In these visions therefore the theologian himself learned, » namely Isaiah this, « that the divine, » that is, the divinity, [1124C] « is superplaced incomparably above every superessential superexcellence, above every invisible and visible virtue, » that is, that the divinity incomparably transcends, according to every mode of excelling, every virtue, visible and invisible. And no wonder, if the divine itself transcends all things: « and yet, » that is, assuredly, « because it is removed from all, » namely by excellence, inasmuch as that which is universal is removed from those things which are all in part, and so far removed and excelling that « it is not like even to the first essences of those » which are, but far distant and transcending. But that he says « to the first essences of those which are, » it is as if he should say, to the essences of those who are first, or to the essences which [1124D] are first, that is, to the supreme spirits of the angels. Nor did he learn only that the divine is removed from all, and like to none. « But still, » or moreover, he learned this also, that it « is the principle of all, » because from it are all things; and the sanctifying cause, because through it all things subsist; and the immutable foundation of those things which are, by a secret singularity, because all things persevere subsisting in it: which, containing all things by its secret and hidden divinity, as a unity gathers together the universe, and as a singularity binds together multiplicity. From which both being and well-being belongs even to those most highly fortified virtues, that is, to those spirits who are firmed and established in the highest virtue and felicity: who have from it not only that they are, but also that they are blessed. All this therefore he learned in [1125A] that vision concerning the excellence of the divinity. « Then, » he also learned, and « was taught, the same deiform virtues of the most holy seraphim, » or, to speak more expressly, of the same most holy seraphim, « the deiform virtues, » or the same virtues which he had learned to be in God, he learned also to be in the seraphim out of conformity to God; because, what God has by nature, they have by grace, being conformed to God. But which and what sort of virtues, or also from what he learned the virtues to be in the seraphim, he subjoins, saying: « From their sacred surname indeed, which, » that is, which surname, « is fiery. » Because seraphim is interpreted *burning,* or *kindling,* and signifies fiery: « concerning which » fiery thing, he says, we « shall a little later, as far as it will be possible for us, introduce the anagogies, » [1125B] that is, the upward-leadings « of the fiery virtue, » namely of the charity of the seraphim themselves, « into the deiform: » that is, into a thing so worthy and altogether like to God. Or thus: From this sacred surname, I say, the prophet himself learned the anagogies, that is, the upward-leadings, or ascents, or advancements of the fiery virtue, that is, of the charity of the seraphim themselves. « But in the expanded sacred formation of the wings, » that is, in the sacred formation of the expanded wings, the prophet himself learned « an absolute and most lofty extension into the divine, » which is « in the first, and in the middle, and in the last intellects. » Through the surname therefore, which sounds "fiery," he learned the fiery virtue; that is, charity ascending to the heights by desire; but through the expansion of the wings, he learned knowledge stretching itself by subtlety [1125C] into far-off things, and extending itself even unto the divine to be grasped, in the first, and middle, and last intellects, that is, in the spirits first, and middle, and last in that order: or in the first intellects by which they grasp the divine above themselves, the middle by which they grasp the divine in themselves, the last by which they grasp the divine below themselves. But the extension itself, or the stretching-forth of knowledge, is both absolute, because, diffusing itself unto immensity, it is enwrapped by no darkness of ignorance; and most lofty, because, pressing on to the highest things, it is weighed down by no infirmity. And not only from these things which have been said did he recognize the virtue of their love and knowledge, « but also, seeing their manifold and multiform character, the intellectual theologian himself » [1125D] Isaiah, « and seeing also that vision distinguished by wings beneath the feet, and it distinguished by wings beneath the faces: and seeing also the ever-moved, » that is, that which ever is, in the middle wings: through all these things « was led back, » and instructed « to the invisible knowledge of the things which were seen. » For through this, that he saw the distinction of the wings which downward beneath the feet; and of the wings which beneath the faces of the one sitting were stretched upward; and of the wings which in the middle were ever moved: through this, namely, he himself saw the manifold virtue of them, which is both multiplied in operation, signified through the motion, and multiform in knowledge and love, signified through the stretching-forth. And in all these things he was led back and admonished, that he might invisibly recognize what he saw according to the appearance of visible things: [1126A] « the manifold-wayed and manifold-seeing virtue of those most lofty intellects being manifested to him through all these things. » Which virtue, namely, is both manifold-wayed, inasmuch as it is moved in manifold ways by desiring; and manifold-seeing, inasmuch as it is beatified in manifold ways by possessing. Manifold-wayed in that by which it presses on by searching; manifold-seeing in that which it finds by penetrating. Many are the ways by which one goes to the one good: therefore rightly manifold-wayed. But why manifold-seeing, since it is one that is seen, unless because in the one all things are seen? Thus therefore the prophet was led back to the knowledge of truth, the manifold-wayed and manifold-seeing virtue of the most lofty intellects being shown to him through sacred images. There being shown to him also « their sacred dread, » or reverence, « which they have supermundanely, » that is, impassibly and [1126B] purely, « against a proud and audacious and impossible searching-out of higher and lower things, » that is, lest they should proudly, and audaciously, and impossibly search out, beyond the measure of their possibility, the secrets of God, which are higher than they by majesty, and lower by profundity. For this reason indeed they veil the head, that they may profess the higher things to be covered from them: for this reason they veil the feet, that they may testify the lower and deeper things to be impenetrable. On this account they dread to be proud and audacious toward that which is impossible to be searched out. Proud toward the heights, audacious toward the depths; proud, lest they be too much lifted up; audacious, lest they be cast down headlong. In this therefore they have a sacred dread, because it is a sacred thing to fear that which, if presumed, would do harm; and they have it supermundanely, because they dread without passion and affliction. For they fear, [1126C] and are not affected; they tremble, and are not shaken. They are afraid, yet secure, and without any trouble, or corruption of their rest, they are wary of passing, toward the incomprehensible majesty, beyond the measure of their possibility. To the knowledge of all these things the prophet was led back, seeing the aforesaid things, in the extension of the wings. And seeing also the unceasing and ever-high-flying character, namely of the persevering motion in the commotion of the wings: which commotion consists in the commensuration of actions imitating God, that is, in their actions by which they imitate God, commensurating themselves to their possibility, so that they presume to do nothing beyond reason and measure.

There follows: « But he was instructed also in that divine and much [1126D] precious hymnody. » As if he should say: Not only in the habit of the seraphim did he recognize their virtue; but also in their voice he learned the hymnody, that is, the divine praise, « the angel forming the vision for the theologian himself according to the virtue » of his capacity, « and handing on his own sacred knowledge: » because what the angel himself knew, through this sacred vision he made the theologian to know. « He taught therefore » the angel himself « him, » namely the theologian, in this, that he purged his lips — of him who already seemed just — with a coal taken from the altar, « because the very participation of the unknown divine brightness, received as far as is possible, is a purgation to however perfect ones. » Because however much anyone has been purged, he becomes more purged when that is received.
