## EXPOSITION. (cont.)

[1127A]There follows: « But these things, » etc. He said that the perception of the divine brightness is a purgation: now consequently he shows that this brightness, proceeding from the hidden depth of the highest divinity, unto the perfecting and illuminating of all the sacred intellects, first manifests itself to the most lofty virtues placed more openly about it: and he shows how it is in its own and true being; then after the first ones it shows itself to the second; and afterward to the last; and at the very last also to our human intellects, and so, descending, it conducts its illumination to each virtue, according as each has been made deiform by it, this is what he says: « This, » namely « brightness proceeding from the remote causes of the divinity, going forth from which divinity it perfects all the sacred intellects, » that [1127B] is, the rational minds, illuminating them « by a superessential hiddenness, » that is, by a very hidden and secret inspiration: proceeding, I say, from those remote causes unto manifestation, it first becomes more manifest as it is, that is, according to its true being, to the most lofty virtues placed about it: and it manifests, « and distributes itself to those more » than to others; « and then » after those « it manifests itself to the second, » « then to the last, » lastly also « to our intellectual virtues, » that is, to the rational minds; « and so, » descending from the first even to the last, « it conducts its illumination through the several virtues: so, » that is, manifest in the several ones to just so great a degree as each virtue exists from that same brightness according to the deiform, that is, according to [1127C] conformity to God. For it illuminates each virtue the more, the more it fits it to deiformity. But why this brightness proceeds to manifestation he subjoins: « Unto the praising of the unknown thing of its own hiddenness. » For we perceive something of it for this reason, that in that, and through that which is hidden, and unknown, and altogether incomprehensible in it, we may praise it.

There follows: « But it shines through the several ones, to the second through the first. » For everywhere it shines to the second through the first, or through the first ones. « And if it behooves to say it briefly, » this can be said in sum, that « the first is led from the hidden to the manifest through the first virtues. » For that which is in itself is altogether hidden; and it then [1127D] first begins to be seen when it first begins to be had. For neither is it ever seen by anyone, except by him by whom it is had. « This therefore the theologian learned from the light-leading angel. » From the angel that is leader of the brightness he learned this. A new composition, "light-leading," as if he should say "light-leader." From him therefore the theologian learned this. What is this? this, namely, is what he learned: « That the purgation, and all the divine operations shining back through the first essences, are distributed through them into all the rest, according to the analogy of each of the rest, » that is, the mode and measure, « unto the deifying participations. Wherefore also he reposed the fierily-purgative property » — that angel, that is, attributed it — « to the seraphim themselves consequently after God: » and therefore « nothing inordinate » is said « if the seraphim is said to purge [1128A] the theologian. For so also God purges all, of whose whole purgation, » or of whose purgation of all, « he is the cause; » and not only God, but rather in this example it can be seen how not only he through whom something is done, but also he from whom it is done, is said to do it. Because « we shall use an example more nearly at hand. » Thus the seraphim is said to purge him whom the inferior angel purges, the virtue and mandate having been received from the seraphim: « Just as, according to us, the highest priest, purging or illuminating through his ministers, or priests, is himself said to purge and to illuminate, the orders purged through him reposing their own sacred operations upon him, » that is, attributing them to him of themselves. For what he works through them, is through them attributed to him. « So also his own purgative knowledge [1128B] and virtue, the angel himself perfecting the purgation of the theologian, reposes upon God indeed as upon the causal one, then upon the seraphim itself as upon the highest priest that first acts. » So therefore the angel attributed what he did, first to God, then to the seraphim, through whom he did it. « As perhaps someone would say, » that is, for example: « If anyone with angelic reverence, » that is, under the person of an angel, which is to be revered: « teaching » the prophet « to be purged » by him, « should say » to him: O Isaiah, « of the purgation to be perfected in thee, » namely through me, « I am not the principle, but before me the principle is exalted indeed, » that is, God, who is both « essence, and Creator, and causal » of all and bringing the first essences to be present; « and containing them about himself by a placing, » that [1128C] is, by stable firmness; « and preserving them unchangeable, and lacking a fall, » that is, lest they be together turned from themselves, and fall; « and moving himself into the first ones of the participation of his own provident operations, » that is, of the operations which he does according to providence; because everything that he does in time, he disposed in eternal providence. The proper operation of God is when he works through his very self without a mediating creature, from which, doubtless, the first participation comes; because they receive him without a medium who become the first effects of his operation. For the first operation of God is when he moves himself from himself; the first participation, when he presents himself through himself. And that indeed is the most lofty creature, unto which the first operation is, [1128D] and in which is the first participation through it, then descending to the things that follow. In this manner the subject angel attributed what he did to the superior seraphim, from whom he received the power to be able to do this. « For this, » that he received from another the power to do it, he says, « teaching me to manifest these things, » or to signify « the mission of the seraphim itself. » He, he says, who, furnishing an apology for all these aforesaid things, taught me, himself says that the mission of the seraphim signifies this, namely that what he did he had assuredly received to do from him from whom he came. Therefore fittingly the angel himself can say to the prophet: « I am not the principle of the purgation which I work in thee. But the exalted and first principle before me is » God, who is Creator and cause of all. Then, moreover, that « adornment [1129A] of the excelling essences, » that is, the order of the highest spirits, which is « after God the priest, and leader » bestowing divine things, and leading back to God: « from which » order « I also was instructed to purge deiformly, » even as now I do in thee. « It, » namely the order, « is that which purges thee through me, » on this account, because it taught me to purge: « through which, » namely order, the very cause and worker of the whole purgation (understand: the divinity) « brings forth its provident actions from the hidden even into us » lowest ones, namely the angels. If the angel working the purgation should say these and words like these to the prophet, it would not be unfitting: whence it is plain that when the seraphim is said to fly to the prophet, and is said to purge the prophet, it can be understood not unfittingly that this operation [1129B] was indeed administered through the subject angel, but attributed to the superior one, from whom it was.

There follows: « These things indeed he taught me: » they are the words of the author returning to his subject. He, he says, who furnished me the apology, taught me all these aforesaid things. « But I, » O Timothy! « hand on to thee » all things either said by me, or approved, but holding to either side. « But to thy intellectual and discretive knowledge, » that is, to thy discretion: « I would grant that the one part of the said causes be released, » namely by thee, « from doubt, » that is, to be held as ratified and undoubted: « and that the same be honored before the other as having what is consequent, and reasonable, and equally true. » This I grant both to thy will, and to thy discretion, and to thy knowledge, that thou choose the one part of these opinions, doubt being removed, [1130A] and honor it more than the other as true and reasonable. « Or, » if this pleases not, I grant thee « to find from thyself what is truly nearer to the true. Or, » if that still pleases not, I grant thee « to learn from another, God, namely, giving it, » whether thou recognize it in this or in any other way: « yet the angels first » receiving the very knowledge of truth « from God, » and afterward, through the angels, revealing it to us holy men, the friends of the angels — that is, receiving it for this, that they may reveal it to us. Or thus. And I grant thee to reveal to us « through its more lovable contemplation, » the angels, and the friends of the angels, first receiving, namely, the very knowledge of truth. A wonderful word. The angels receive from God, and men from the angels; and men themselves in turn, [1130B] taught by the angels, teach other men; and the doctrine of truth comes through some into others, and many seem to be masters and teachers of truth; and yet no one is taught, because he is not illuminated from the contemplation of it. Other masters apply the ministry; there is one master, who alone furnishes the understanding. He can teach without the others; the others cannot without him. Thou givest me the word. But what is a word without intelligence? Through the word indeed intelligence comes. But he places intelligence in the heart who illuminates within, not he who sounds without. His unction teaches us concerning all things. Thou speakest without; but within thou dost not anoint. Therefore thy erudition is indeed lovable to me. But more lovable is the contemplation of him; because even this, which in thy erudition is lovable, is nothing except from the contemplation of him.
