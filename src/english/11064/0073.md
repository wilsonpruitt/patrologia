## THE TEXT. (cont.)

*Come, on then, let us say what remains, sending back, if it seems good, our intellectual eye, intent about the sublime contemplations by an angelic vigor, to the divided and much-parted breadth of the multiform variety of the angelic specifications, descending again to them, as being inconsequent, analytically reflected back into the simplicity of the celestial minds. But let one thing be foreknown by thee: how the sacred discretions of the formed images sometimes signify the same dispositions of the celestial essences as ordaining, and again as ordained, and the last as ordaining, and the first as ordained, and the same, as has been said, as having first, and middle, and last virtues, no inordinate reasoning being introduced, according to the manner of such unlockings. [1131D] For if we should say that certain ones are ordained by the prior ones, then that the prior ones themselves ordain the same, and again ordain of the last ones, and are ordained by those very ones that were ordained, truly the interposed matter would be commingled with much inordination and confusion. But if we say that the same both ordain and are ordained, yet not that they ordain and are ordained by the same, but that they severally are indeed ordained by the prior ones, and ordain the last: not inconsequently perhaps would someone say that the sacred-made forms in the oracles can sometimes be beautifully and truly encompassed about by first, and middle, and last virtues. And that they can therefore be extended upward convertibly, and be firmly enwrapped about themselves, being keepers of their own virtues, [1132C] and, by a sociable procession toward the things that come, be providently in participation of virtue — this one will not falsely conjoin to all the celestial essences, although to some superplacedly and universally, as has often been said, but to others particularly and subjectly. But one must begin with reason, and inquire in the first discretion of the forms, for what cause theology is found honoring the fiery sacred description almost beyond all. Thou wilt find it therefore not only conforming fiery wheels, but also fiery animals, and men flashing as it were with fire, and setting about those celestial essences heaps of coals of fire, and rivers blazing with fire with an immeasurable sound [n: (Ezech. I; Dan. VII; IV Reg. II)]*. *But it says also that thrones are fiery, and that the most exalted seraphim themselves signify by their surname that they are burning after a heavenly manner: and [1132D] it distributes to them the property and operation of fire, and altogether, upward and downward, it selectively honors the fiery making of the forms. Therefore I judge the fiery to signify the most deiform thing of the celestial minds. For the holy theologians themselves [n: (Hebr. X)] often describe the superessential and formless essence in fire, as having (if it is lawful to say) many images of the divine property, as far as is possible in things invisible. For fire perceptible — thus indeed must it be said — comes clearly in all things and through all things, and is removed from all; luminous at once, and as it were hidden; itself unknown through itself, no matter lying by in which it manifests its own action, and immeasurable and invisible, through itself [1133A] at once potent over all, and, whatsoever things are done among them, movable to its own action, delivering itself to all that in any way draw near, renovative, a guardianship of nature, illuminative by veiled-about splendors, incomprehensible, clear, discrete, leaping back, bearing upward, acutely passing, exalted, not to receive the contumely of diminution, ever-moved with a motion through itself, moving another, comprehending, uncomprehended, not needing another, latently growing of itself, and, to the materials it has taken up, manifesting its own magnitude, active, potent, present to all at once invisibly, when neglected it is thought not to be, but it is rubbed into being, as by a certain vengeance, connaturally and properly it suddenly shines back forth, and again incomprehensibly impalpable, not lessened, in all its most rich [1133B] deliveries of itself. And perhaps someone will find many other beautiful properties of fire, as images, though insensible, of the divine operation. Knowing this therefore, the theosophers conform the celestial essences out of fire, signifying their deiform and, as far as possible, God-imitable character. But they also describe them as human-formed, on account of the intellectual nature, and by having upward the intuitive virtues, and the straightness and brightness of the figure, and, according to nature, the principal and regal quality, and, according to sense, the least indeed as compared with the remaining virtues of the irrational animals. Yet potent over all, by virtue according to the magnitude of intellect, and by continuity according to rational knowledge, and, according to the nature of the soul, free and most potent. It is possible also, as I judge, through the several members of our corporeal manifold [1133C] partition to find the celestial virtues, saying that the sight-virtues indeed signify their most clear regard toward the divine luminaries, and again the tender and liquid and not-repercussed, but acutely movable, and pure, and full reception, impassibly, of the divine illuminations. But the discretive virtues of the smells signify that reception, as far as possible, of the above-intellect distribution of the sweetly-smelling thing, and the discernment through knowledge of those things which are not so, and the utterly-fleeing from them. But the virtues of the ears, that partaking and gnostic reception of the divine inspiration. And the tasting virtues, that reception of the fullness of the invisible foods, and of the divine and nourishing advancements. But the touching virtues, the discernment through knowledge of what is fitting or hurtful. Then the eyelids [1133D] and eyebrows, the keeping of the divine visions and of intelligence. And youthful and adult age, that property of the ever-renewing vital virtue. But the teeth, the dividing of the imparted nourishing perfection. For each intellectual essence divides by provident virtue the uniform intelligence bestowed on it by a more divine one, and multiplies it unto the leading analogy of the lower. But the shoulders, and arms, and likewise hands, the making, and operative, and active power. But the heart to be a symbol of deiform life, sowing its own vital virtue deiformly into those things which were pre-understood. The breasts again to signify the hard and keeping power, as of the life-giving distribution from the heart placed beneath; but the back, the continuous holding at once of all the fertile virtues. But the feet, [1134A] the movable, and swift, and running power of the motion ever going into the divine; on which account theology figured also the feet of the holy intellects as winged [n: (Ezech. I)]*. *For the winged signifies anagogic velocity, and the celestial active power of the upward journey, and the removed-from-all-that-is-lowly by bearing upward. But the very lightness of the wings signifies nothing earthly, but the whole that is cleanly and without weight ascending on high. The naked also, and unshod [n: (Gen. XVIII, XIX)]*, *and the loosed, and the let-down, and the immeasurable, and the pure, is assimilative, by the setting-away of those things which are outside, and to the divine simplicity as far as possible. But since again the simple and much-varied wisdom both clothes the naked, and gives them certain vessels to carry about, come, let us open up, as far as is possible [1134B] for us, the sacred vestments and the instruments of the celestial minds. For the bright and fiery garment [n: (Apoc. I)]*, *I judge to signify the deiform, according to the image of fire and its brightness, on account of the reposes in heaven, where the light must be said to be altogether invisible, or intellectually illuminating, or intellectually illuminated. But the priestly vestment signifies the leading toward the divine and mystical contemplations, and the vow of the whole life. The girdles also signify the keeping of their fecund virtues, and the habit that gathers them to be turned unitedly into itself, and to be carried round about itself circularly with ease, lacking a fall, from the likeness of nature. The rods also signify the regal and principal power, and that which defines all things straight. But the darts, and axes, the separative power of dissimilitudes, and the acumen of the discerning virtues, both efficacious and full of action. The geometric and tectonic vessels [1134C] signify the founding, and building, and perfecting power, and whatsoever else belongs to the providence of the seconds that lead back and convert. But there is a time when the instruments too, those things which have been done by the holy angels, are symbols unto us of the divine judgments; some declaring the correcting discipline, or the punishing justice; but others, liberty from anguish, or the end of discipline, or the resumption of the prior beneficence, or the addition of other goods, small or great, sensible or invisible [n: (Ezech. X; Judic. VI; II Machab. V; Ezech. XXXI; Apoc. III; Zach. VIII)]*. *And altogether perhaps a perspicacious mind would not doubt to unite beautifully the visible things to the invisible. That they are named winds also, signifies their velocity, and their effect arriving almost without delay upon all, [1134D] and the transporting motion from above into the things that are below, and upward into the things that are below, and the wind as raising the seconds to a higher loftiness, and moving the first to a communicative and provident procession of the lesser [n: (Gen. XXVIII; Joan. I)]*. *But perhaps someone will say that the windy surname of the aerial spirit signifies also the deiform character of the celestial ones: for this too has an image and form of the divine operation, as in the symbolic theology it is demonstrated through many things by a four-line adjudication, according to the moving, and generating, and swift, and potent capacity of nature, and the unknown to us, and invisible hiding-place of the moving principles and consummations. « For thou knowest not, he says, whence it comes, and whither it goes (Joan. III [1135A] Isai. V)*. » *But theology forms also about them the appearance of a cloud, signifying through this that the sacred intellects, supermundanely over-filled indeed with hidden light, receive the first manifestation pompously, and distribute the same copiously, lucidly and proportionally, into the things that are second: and that there subsists in them a genital, and vivific, and active, and perfective power, according to the intellectual conception of showers, receiving the bosom that calls forth into vital births by moist rains. Theology also sets about the celestial essences the appearance of brass, and of electrum, and of many-colored stones [n: (Isai. XXXV; Apoc. I; Dan. II)]*. *The electrum indeed signifies as it were the gold-formed, and at once the silvery: an incorruptible, as in gold, and ample, and not lessened, and uncontaminated splendor; and an open, as in silver, [1135B] and light-formed and celestial clarity. But in brass, according to the traditions handed down, either the fiery or the gold-formed is to be attributed. But the many-colored kinds of stones must be thought to signify: either, as it were, the white ones, the light-formed; or, as it were, the ruddy ones, the gold-formed; or, as it were, the pallid ones, the youthful and new; and through the several kinds thou wilt find an anagogic adjudication of the typic images. But since indeed I judge these things to have been sufficiently said by us according to our virtue, we must pass over to the sacred unlocking of the sacred-figured bestial formation of the celestial minds. For the lion must be thought to signify the principal, and robust, and untamed power, and the hidden power of the ineffable divinity, as a virtue, assimilative of the intellectual traces by a veiling-about and, mystically perhaps, by a pompous garment, its journey being restored into itself according to the divine [1135C] illumination. But the form of the ox signifies the firm and new, and that which recalls [renovans] the intellectual furrows into the reception of the celestial and generating showers, and the keeping and most strong power. Thence the form of the eagle signifies the regal, and high-bearing, and swift-flying, and, unto potent nourishment, the sharp, and sober, and agile, and well-contrived power, and, unto the copious and much-lucent ray of the divine sun, the power contemplative by desire, immediately, rightly and inflexibly, in the sound gazes of the speculative virtues. But that form of the horses signifies the obedient, and bridle-able; and the white indeed signifies the truly lucid, and, as it were, most greatly most-akin to the divine light. But of those which are black, the hidden; the red indeed the fiery, and active; and of those commingled of white and black, the power conjunctive of the extremes [1135D] with a perfective virtue, and connecting the first to the seconds, and the seconds to the first, convertibly and providently. But if we should not be limiting the commensuration of the discourse, we should perhaps not incongruously have united, part by part, the properties of the said animals, and all their corporeal conformations, to the celestial virtues according to unlike likenesses. The furious indeed to their intellectual fortitude, of which fury is the lowest image; but concupiscence again to the divine love: and, to be said summarily, referring at once all the senses, and the manifold parts of the irrational animals, unto the immaterial intelligences and uniform virtues of the celestial essence. But for the wise there suffice not only these very things, but the adjudication of one significative [1136A] image, unto the declaration, in like manner, of the neighboring ones. And this too must be inspected, that rivers were said to be, and wheels, and chariots connected with the celestial essences [n: (Dan. VII; Ezech. I, X)]*. *For the rivers signify the fiery divine advancements, giving to them a copious and not-failing affluence, and nourishing them with a vivific fecundity. But the chariots, the conjunctive society of likenesses [n: (IV Reg. I; Ezech. I)]*, *But the wheels, since indeed they are winged, going unconvertibly into the things that are before the sight, by a straight and just way signify the virtue of their outgoing operation into the same way without bending, and rightly cut, all of them at once by an intellectual rotation supermundanely directed. But it is possible also, by another anagogy, to adjudge the imaginary description of the intellectual wheels. For there was cried out [1136B] to them, as the theologian says, gel, gel, gel [n: (Ezech. X)]*. *But this signifies, according to the Hebrew word, revolutions and revelations. For the fiery and deiform wheels have revolutions indeed about the very same best thing by an ever-movable motion; but revelations, by the manifestation of secrets, and the circular-leading-back of the things subjected, and by the deductive perfection of high illuminations into the things which are subjected. But there remains to us the discourse in explanation concerning the joy of the celestial dispositions. For they are altogether not receivers of that passible delight which is according to us. But they are said to rejoice together with God [n: (Luc. XVI)] at the finding of the lost, according to the deiform banqueting, and at the deiformity of those returning into God in providence and in salvation, and the gladness of incorruption, and that ineffable beneficence, [1136C] in the participation of which holy men too have often been made partakers through the deific advents, from above, of the divine illuminations. So many things have been said by me concerning the holy formations, deficient indeed as regards a diligent manifestation of them; but perfect, as I judge, for our not remaining lowlily in figurative phantasies. But if thou shouldest say this also, why then we have not made mention of all the angelic virtues, or operations, or images in the oracles, we answer the truth: that of those whose supermundane knowledge we have been ignorant, we desire in them another light-leader to teach us [n: (Job XII)]*. *But certain things, as being equally potent with the things said, we have passed over, providing for the commensuration of the discourse, and honoring by silence the secret that is above us.*
