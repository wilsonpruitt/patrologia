## EXPOSITION.

[1136D]

The title of the fifteenth and last chapter is: What are the formative images of the angelic virtues, and what next, that is, and the rest? For thus far he has treated of the invisible properties of the angels: which are, namely, those gifts of the virtues, the invisible operations in them. Now consequently he begins to treat of the visible formations: which the sacred word attributes to them by a mystical signification according to the images and likenesses of corporeal things. "Come, on, speak, ho!" is the voice of one exhorting either himself, or also Timothy, to whom he speaks: « Ho, let us say what remains. » What is it that remains? This, namely, that we, « if it so seems, » send back to thee, or relax, [1137A] « our intellectual eye, » hitherto « intent about the sublime contemplations » held by beholding « concerning the angelic vigor; » and, the eye being turned back, that we descend « to the divided and much-parted breadth of the multiform variety of the angelic specifications, » that is, to the multiform variety, or the variety of many forms, which is in the angelic specifications, or formations: in which variety, because there is an ample multitude, or a manifold amplitude, in place of simplicity the divided is found, and in place of unity the much-parted. Thus therefore let us say what remains, sending back our eye from the invisible things, and descending to the visible. « Again in them, » namely descending, and fixing our consideration, and yet « he reflected » again, or those images being led back [1137B] « analytically, » that is, resolutively, « into the simplicity of the celestial minds: as » of themselves « in things consequent, » that is, in things unfitting. And the sense is: we, descending through contemplation from the invisible things to the visible, and fixing our consideration namely in the visible things themselves, let us again reflect them back to the simplicity of the invisible things, by resolving and expounding, or showing how, through that which is discerned as visible, that which cannot be seen is signified. Otherwise the visible formation would be inconsequent and unfitting, if in that simple nature this were believed to be by the truth of essence, which is attributed to it as manifold in signification according to the various species of corporeal things. As therefore we descend [1137C] by representing the simple natures through visible things, so we ascend, and, as it were resolutively, reflect ourselves back to the divine consideration, when, by expounding the same forms spiritually, we declare them to be understood mystically. We descend, when we fit the multiplicity of corporeal formations to the simplicity of the celestial natures through signification: we ascend, when we resolve the same multiplicity of visible figurations, by expounding it, unto the understanding of the simple truth.

There follows: « But one thing, etc. » O Timothy, before we treat of the visible formations of the angels, let one thing be foreknown by thee, that thou first know this. What is that? this, namely, that thou know it. « How the sacred discretions of the formed [1137D] images, » that is, how the Holy Scriptures, distinguished in the images which they form concerning them, the angels, « signify and represent the same dispositions, » that is, the orders « of the celestial essences. » Sometimes the lower ones as « ordaining, and again » the same as « ordained » by the higher: wherein they signify in a certain way that these are middle, and that they have lower ones whom they may ordain, and higher ones by whom they may be ordained. « And again sometimes they signify even the last dispositions as ordaining, and the first as ordained: » which nevertheless seems a wonder, since neither do those lower ones have any whom they may ordain, nor do these higher ones have any by whom they may be ordained. Wherein nevertheless it is given to be understood that the supreme ones, although they have no other angels above themselves by whom they may be ordained, yet have God, by whom they are ordained [1138A] and sanctified. And the last ones again, although they have no angels beneath themselves whom they may ordain, yet have men, who are ordained, and disposed, and sanctified by them. Or certainly in this, that the supreme ones are introduced as ordained, and the last as ordaining, it is signified that among them some differ from others: and there are among the higher ones certain lower ones, who are nevertheless ordained unto something by others established in the same order; and among the lower ones certain higher ones, who ordain unto something others placed in the same order. And this is what follows: That the very words signify the same dispositions, namely, as having first, and middle, and last virtues. As has already been said above, that each disposition has distinction in the persons, and each person in [1138B] the properties, so, he says, the discretions of the sacred formations represent the first, and middle, and last spirits in the same order, and the first, and middle, and last virtues in the same person; « yet no » « inordinate reasoning being introduced in this, according to the manner of such unlockings, » that is, expositions. For if it is understood in this manner, as we have expounded above, there is nothing unfitting in this representation. And I well say that in this representation, by which the same ones are introduced both as ordaining and as ordained, there is no unfittingness, because there is no confusion, or transposition, of the right disposition there, since they are introduced as ordained by some and as ordaining others. But if they were said to be ordaining and [1138C] ordained in respect of the same ones, it would be unfitting, this is what he says: « If indeed we should say that certain ones, » understand: dispositions, namely the middle ones, « are ordained by the prior ones; » and then that those « prior ones ordain, » or are ordainers, « of the same, » namely of the middle ones, « and again, » through those middle ones, « ordain, » or are ordainers, « of the last ones: » if, he says, we should say that those prior ordainers of the middle and last ones, in a reversed order, « are ordained by those very ones that were ordained: truly the interposed matter would be commingled with much inordination and confusion; » because it would not be orderly that some should be ordained by those whom they ordain. « But if we say that the same ones, » in a different respect, « ordain and are ordained; yet not that they are ordainers of the same ones by whom they are ordained, « or that they are ordained by the same ones » whom [1138D] they ordain; « but that they severally, » that is, remaining distinct without confusion of reciprocation, « are indeed ordained by the prior ones, but ordain the last ones, » there is nothing unfitting. And according to this perhaps « not inconsequently, » that is, not unfittingly, « someone would say that the sacred-made forms which are in the words can sometimes both beautifully, and truly, » that is, fittingly and truthfully, « be encompassed about, » that is, be fitted, « the same forms, to first, and middle, and last virtues. »

There follows: « And upward therefore, etc. » As if he should say: Since someone can truthfully say that the same forms are fittingly fitted to first, and middle, and last virtues. Therefore that very one who shall have said this will not falsely conjoin, that is, will commonly [1139A] attribute to all the celestial essences: both to be extended upward through the love of God, and to be firmed in themselves through the keeping of themselves, and to advance below themselves through the love of the neighbor — this is what he says: « To be extended upward convertibly [ *convertibiliter* ], » namely through the love of God, « and to be firmly enwrapped about themselves, » that is, to be constrained and encompassed: constrained, lest the good flow out; encompassed, lest the evil flow in. Wherein, namely, because they are enwrapped about themselves, « they exist as keepers of their own virtues, » that is, they keep their own virtues. This therefore, that is, to be extended upward, and to be enwrapped in themselves, « and that they are in participation of virtue, » understand: prepared, « providently, » that is, intently or devotedly, « toward the things that come, by an associable procession. » These three things therefore someone will commonly and truly [1139B] attribute to all « the celestial essences, » namely that they are extended upward through the love of God, and that they are enwrapped about themselves through the keeping of themselves, and that they are prepared providently, and intently, and devotedly to impart their virtue toward the things that come to them, proceeding by a social love, and freely offering themselves unto social love. All these things therefore can be commonly attributed to all: « although, » that is, though, « to some indeed superplacedly and universally, » that is, they belong excellently and fully, as « has already often been said; but to others particularly and subjectly. »

There follows: « But one must begin with reason, » etc. As if he should say: Since we must treat of the angelic formations, therefore we must in the first place begin [1139C] with reason, that is, rationally, « and inquire in the first discretion » of the very « forms, » why « theology is found honoring the fiery sacred description, » that is, formation, in which the species of fire is set forth for signification, « almost beyond all the other descriptions. » For rarely is any visible species found (if indeed it is found) having a more excellent demonstration toward the invisible things. « Thou wilt find it therefore, » namely theology, « not only forming fiery wheels » for the signification of those invisible things, « but also fiery animals, and men flashing as it were with fire; » and thou wilt find that theology also « sets heaps of coals of fire about those celestial essences, » and also describes « rivers blazing with fire with an immeasurable [1139D] sound. » And not only this: « But it says also that thrones are fiery, » namely theology itself, that they are in heaven; and it says also « that the most exalted seraphim themselves signify by their surname that they are burning after a heavenly manner, and it distributes » also « to them, » the seraphim, « the property and operation of fire. And, » to say it briefly, « altogether, upward, and downward, everywhere it selectively honors, » that is, specially, the fiery making of the forms.

There follows: « Therefore the fiery, » etc. As if he should say: Since theology so greatly venerates the fiery formation: « Therefore, » I judge « the fiery to signify the most deiform thing of the celestial minds, » that is, that virtue which is most deiform in them. And rightly, because « the theologians themselves often describe in [1140A] fire, » that is, in the species and figure of fire, even the very « superessential and formless essence » of the divinity: which is both superessential by majesty, and formless by incomprehensibility. They describe, I say, in fire « as having many images of the divine property, as far as, in » « visible » things, an image of the divine property can be had. Yet I say thus, « if it is lawful to say it, » that the divine property has some likeness, the property which is very incomprehensible and invisible.
