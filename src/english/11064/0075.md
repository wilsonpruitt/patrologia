## EXPOSITION. (cont.)

There follows: « For fire, » etc. Now he enters upon describing the properties of fire, in which he shows its nature, and quality, and wonderful power, and demonstrates its excellent signification. « For fire, » he says, « is sensible. So indeed [1140B] it must be said, » namely that it is sensible. He adds this for this reason: because to some, perhaps, it could seem — on the ground that the nature of fire is perceived by sense only in subjacent matter — that fire is not a body at all. He says therefore that fire is truly sensible, and a body, though far more excellent and subtle than all other corporeal things, and nearest to the spiritual nature, and by this demonstrating its effect far more unlike all others. « For fire in all things, and through all things comes clearly, and » yet « is removed from all things. » It is near by presence; it is far by excellence. It is in all things because it makes itself visible through illumination, and it is far from all things because it does not offer itself perceptible through a distinct perception of its substance. [1140C] This is what follows: And « it is at once bright, and as it were hidden. » For it is bright, because it is perceived in subjacent matter; hidden, because in its own essence it is not seen corporeally. « It is unknown » itself « through itself, matter not lying by; » because without subjacent matter, as has been said, it cannot be perceived: « in which » matter, namely, « it manifests its proper action. » From which in a certain way it seems to be more incorporeal; because apart from subjacent matter it does not come to sense. Whence fire seems in a certain way to be midway between the invisible and the visible? inasmuch as it approaches those, incorporeal, and is corporeal inasmuch as it draws near to these. It is also immeasurable, because it grows out [1140D] into the infinite, and as far as the subjacent matter suffices, it itself does not fail; and it is invisible, because in its pure substance, outside subjacent matter, it is not seen. It is also « through itself potent over all things at once, » namely things, « and potent » also « over all things, whatsoever in them, » namely in things, « come to pass unto proper action. For in fire indeed is the first corporeal motion, which moves all other things, and is itself not moved by another. Therefore whatever is done in all things is not proper to those in which it comes to pass, but rather to that itself through which it comes to pass.

There follows: « Movable, giving itself to all things drawing near in whatsoever manner. » For because it is moved of itself by its own motion, it diffuses itself into all things, moving each according as it draws near to it, [1141A] that is, according as it is found more or less movable. It is also « renovative » of all things, because it renews things growing old, lest they wholly fail and go into nothing. For we see through the several years how fire renews nature, when those things which had grown old with the winter's cold, warmed by the spring's heat, revive. In this therefore fire itself is « the guardian » of « nature; » because it guards nature, lest it wholly cease to be, if it always suffered defect and were never repaired. It is also « illuminative, » because it illuminates all things, « with splendors veiled about. » For its splendors flash forth without unto illumination, but are veiled about when, drawn back again to the secret of their nature, they hide themselves. Therefore, then, it is « incomprehensible, » because outwardly indeed [1141B] poured forth it is perceived by sense, but inwardly withdrawn it is not comprehended. It is also « clear, » manifestly appearing, and « distinct, » distinguishing diverse things. And it is « resilient, » because it is fed downward, and is moved upward — laying hold of lower things by its ardor, but from its own lightness rebounding to the heights.

Therefore there follows: « Bearing upward, » because, lacking weight, it seeks the highest things, « and going acutely, » because by the motion of its proper power it penetrates all things, « and lofty, » because it stands eminent above the universe; because whatever is corporeal besides itself is by nature beneath itself: « Not about to receive the affront of diminution; » because, just as when it is poured forth it is not increased, so when it is received it is not diminished, neither, in that [1141C] it seems to receive increase, taking glory, nor, in that it is thought to be diminished, having suffered affront, because what it is in itself it is always whole. It is also « always moved, » that is, having motion, and it is « moved through itself, » because the motion which it has is not from another; and in that it moves itself, it moves all other things having motion; and therefore it is « moving another, » and is itself « comprehending » though « incomprehended. » For changing all matter by its own power, by using and consuming it it draws it into itself, and is held by no body, since, that which it burns up being consumed, it returns into itself. It is therefore « not needing another, » because through the subjacent matter, in which it burns, it is not so much held that it may be, as held that it may be there; and if it seem to grow from [1141D] it, it does not receive from it the substance of increase, although from it it takes in it the cause of growing, because it becomes not greater in itself, but rather in it. Therefore he says that it is « latently growing from itself, » because, as to the manifest, it seems to grow from the subjacent matter itself; but in the truth of the thing it grows from itself, not that it be greater in itself, but that it be more in the matter itself: therefore it receives increase indeed from itself, when it seems to grow into the subjacent things, « manifesting only its own magnitude unto those matters, » in which it is; that is, it so grows in the matter which it takes up, that is, lays hold of, that in it indeed it appears greater, but nevertheless in itself it is not greater. For it does not there [1142A] receive magnitude, but shows it, manifesting there sometimes what in itself it has always. It is also « active, » and « potent, » because it acts powerfully, and is moved, destroying all things standing in the way; and it is « at once present to all things invisibly. » For in certain things it is only seen, but it is held present to all things invisibly: which appears from this, that sometimes by the striking and collision of bodies without attrition it is struck out even thence where it seemed not to be. « Neglected » indeed, and not moved, or remaining quiet, « it is thought not to be; but by attrition » or rubbing, provoked and set in motion, « as though by a certain vengeance it suddenly shines forth » leaping out, and raging upon those things which it has seized: which nevertheless it does « connaturally and properly, » that is, out of its own power and potency. [1142B] « And again incomprehensibly impalpable, » when it slides back, it remains incomprehensible to sense: « not lessened » by extinction, just as neither increased by kindling. All of which it does « in all » things, « by its most rich deliveries; » because whatever from itself it delivers to the subjacent things unto some power, it has not the less in itself, ever persisting full. These properties of fire, then, I have enumerated; and perhaps « someone will find many other properties of fire, beautiful » — namely, likenesses — « of the divine operation: as, » that is, just as « in sensible images, » namely, the likeness of it can be. And therefore « the theosophists, knowing this, » that is, so great an excellence of fire, « conform, » or figure, « the celestial essences out of fire: signifying » by this « their deiform, [1142C] and God-imitable, » quality which is in them, « as far as is possible » to be had. And not only do they conform them out of fire, « but they also describe them humaniform, » that is, in human form, « on account of the intellectual, » or rational, in them — that is, their rationality — since man alone among all visible things is found rational. Likewise in human form they describe them, on account of « the intuitive, » that is, contemplative, « virtues » of them, which they have by holding themselves upward, just as man alone among all living things has his countenance erect toward things above: « and » they also describe them in human form on account of the « rectitude » of the human « figure » itself, that is, the uprightness, because the human stature is erect, not prone like that of other [1142D] living things; « and » on account of « the luculent, » that is, the clear and evident, or the distinguishing mark, of the human figure itself; because from its very erectness it affords a more evident and more comely aspect; « and » on account of « the principal, and regal, » which is given to man himself « according to nature, » that he may have the first place and rule over the other creatures; and on account of « the least according to sense as regards the remaining virtues of the irrational animals, » that is, because man is less strong in corporeal sense than the other living things, which in the power of perceiving received a greater strength than he. On account of the least indeed in sense, and on account of the Omnipotent in power, or on account of « potent » with every power « according to the greatness of the intellect and according to rational knowledge remaining in continuity, » [1143A] « and on account of the free, and most potent, » which is in him « according to the nature of the soul. » For on account of this the angel is described in human form; because man, by how much he is less strong in sense, where he is surpassed by the beasts, by so much is he more potent in reason, and intelligence, and the freedom of natural choice, where he is likened to the angels.
