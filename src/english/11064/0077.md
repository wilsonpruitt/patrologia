## EXPOSITION. (cont.)

There follows: « But there is, etc. » As if he should say: Those [1146C] things which are attributed to the angels according to the form of visible things are not only signs of their invisible virtue, but signify also sometimes those things which are done in us and about us. This is what he says: « There is, » that is, it happens, « when those organs, » that is, instruments, « which are enacted, » that is, exhibited or presented, « by the holy angels, are symbols, » that is, signs and demonstrations « of the divine judgments, » which are exercised upon us or toward us. « Some, » namely organs, by signification « declaring the corrective discipline, » namely of God, as the rod, which seems to strike, « or » declaring « the punishing justice, » as the axes and swords. « But others, » organs declaring « liberty, » or liberation from distress, « or the end of discipline, » that is, [1146D] of correction, and punishment, « or resumption, » that is, recuperation « of the prior beneficence, or the apposition of other gifts » after the prior ones given, « small or great, sensible, » as in corporeal goods, « or invisible, » as in spiritual. « And, that » I may say it universally, « perchance a perspicacious mind would not doubt at all, that is, in all things, beautifully to unite, » that is, to adapt by signification, « the visible to the invisible, » so that, namely, it would leave nothing of all visible things which it would not demonstrate to have some signification unto the invisible. And rightly: because even « that » — namely, the angels — « are named winds, » that is, that even this, that the angels themselves are named winds, « signifies their [1147A] swiftness, and their effect reaching into almost all things without delay. » And it signifies also their transvective motion « from above into those things which are below, and » again from those « which are below into those which are above. » But what that motion may convey hither and thither he consequently adds. « A motion, » I say, « erecting the second things, » that is, the lower unto the higher loftiness, « and moving the first things, » that is, the higher, « to a communicative and provident procession of the lesser, » that is, that they proceed to the lesser, that is, the lower, to provide for them, and to communicate to them their virtue.

There follows: « But perhaps someone will say, » etc. He had said above that the angels are named winds on account of their swiftness; now he subjoins that perhaps [1147B] someone will be able to say that they are named winds on account of the divine likeness; because the wind too, according to something, has the form and image of the divine operation, as in another theology, concerning the quaternary dijudication, that is, concerning the signification of the four elements by several examples, or arguments, he testifies that he has demonstrated: which work is not had among us. « Perhaps someone will say, he says, that is, some one, that the windy denomination of the aerial spirit signifies » not only swiftness, but also « the deiform, » that is, the deiformity of the celestial spirits. « For it has even this » (understand: element) in itself « both the image, and the form, of the divine operation, » as, that is, just as by several examples, namely, it is demonstrated « in the symbolic theology, » that is, in the theology [1147C] which is demonstrated concerning symbols, that is, concerning figures and visible likenesses: I say « by the tetrastic, » that is, quaternary « dijudication, » where by the four elements severally it is shown how the visible are both likenesses and signs of the invisible. According to that dijudication, then, it was demonstrated how this element, that is, the wind, has both the image and the form of the divine operation. But wherein it has this image and form, he subjoins: « According to the motive capacity of its nature, and generative, and swift, and potent, » because the moved air subjects motion and increase to things, and running swiftly from without impels potently, or violently, « and according to the unknown to us, [1147D] and invisible lurking-place of the moving principles and consummations. » For the wind moved comes forth to sense; but the beginning and end of its motion sense does not apprehend. Whence it is written: « Who produces the winds out of his treasuries [n: (Psal. CXXXIV)]. » For to produce the winds out of treasuries is to bring forth to sense, by manifest agitation, the breathings of the winds out of hidden and latent causes. According to this likeness also the Creator Spirit, hiddenly presiding over all things and remaining invisible, moves and promotes all things by his virtue; and the things which are moved are seen, but he who moves is not seen; potent in doing, swift in perfecting; whose works indeed are discerned without, but the unknown and invisible lurking-place of the first causes and [1148A] the principles moving those very works unto effect is not penetrated. Likewise also the unknown lurking-place of the consummations is not penetrated; because, when anything is moved unto effect, we discern indeed that which comes to pass; but whence it so comes to pass, or unto what it comes to pass, we do not see. This is that, in the vision of the seraphim, they cover the head and the feet of him sitting on the throne, because we discern the middle things of the divine operation, which are done in time; but the things which were first before the times, and which shall be last after the times, we cannot investigate, because the lurking-place of the moving principles, and of the consummations, is invisible to us and unknown. Likewise also when the Spirit comes to the heart, it is felt indeed in that it is perceived. « But whence » it « comes, or whither it goes, » is not investigated. Whence [1148B] it comes, is the beginning; whither it goes, is the consummation and end. « But thou knowest not, » he says, « whence it comes, or whither it goes, » because the lurking-place both of the beginning and of the end is at once unknown and invisible. Therefore thou knowest not whence it comes, because thou findest no merit in thee for which thou mightest say thyself to be the grace. Thou knowest not whither it goes, because from the gift received thou dost not understand unto what faith thou mayest attain. According to these things, therefore, every wind has the image and form of the divine operation.

There follows: « But also of the cloud, » etc. Not only, he says, does theology name the angels winds, « but it also forms about the appearance of a cloud, or adapts it to them: by calling them clouds, signifying by this the sacred intellects super-filled supermundanely, » that is, spiritually, « with the occult light, » that is, invisible charity: those [1148C] I say « receiving pompously, » that is, excellently, « the first manifestation » of that light in its first appearing, because it is first shown to them, and excellently perceived by them: therefore they themselves are « receiving the first manifestation of it pompously, » and gloriously; and they are also distributing that same, namely the manifestation, « into those which are second after them; distributing it to them lucidly, » that is, clearly, « and proportionally, » that is, according to the measure of each one. So also the clouds first receive the light of the sun, then diffuse it, resplendent from themselves, into the things below. And therefore also the angels are called clouds, because « in them subsists the general, and vivific, and active, and perfective, according to the intellectual conception [1148D] of » spiritual « showers. » As the clouds, which by the effusion of showers, making the earth fruitful, give to the things born of it the force of generating, and of vivification, and of the increase of perfection. So then they are called clouds, on account of such a conception of showers — I say « evoking, » or provoking, or eliciting, « unto vital births » the receiving bosom, that is, the earth, which receives the showers in its bosom: « evoking, I say, by moist rains, » that is, through moist rains. « For through the rains it provokes the earth by nourishing to bring forth vital births.
