## HOMILY THE FIRST. *On the title of the work, on Solomon's names, and books, and on the beginnings of this work, on the explanation of the vanities.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *[n: (ECCLE. I.)] The words of Ecclesiastes, the son of David, king of Jerusalem.* ⟦s2⟧ This is the title of the book: in which both the quality of the work that follows is briefly expressed, and equally the person of the author is commended. ⟦s3⟧ For in that it is said, *words*, a manifold disputation is signified, and one drawn out into diverse sentiments. ⟦s4⟧ For because in this book the manners, the pursuits, and the works of many are described, [0115D] therefore it is necessary that the speaker take up the voices of many, and express in his own discourse the opinions of many, so that he may be able to present in his own person the persons of many (though he himself who speaks be but one). ⟦s5⟧ For near the end of the book he testifies that he has spoken to many, and that many were in himself, saying: Let us all alike hear the end of speaking. ⟦s6⟧ *Fear God, and keep his commandments: this is every man.* ⟦s7⟧ This also is why he willed to be named Ecclesiastes in this work; because, namely, his discourse here is directed not to some one man in particular, but to the whole Church, that is, to the assembly, or the multitude of the people, and his oration in this book serves at once for the expressing and the forming of the manners of many. ⟦s8⟧ By three [0116A] words indeed we read that Solomon was called: Idida, which is interpreted *beloved*; and Coeleth, which in Greek is called *Ecclesiastes*, in Latin *concionator*, an assembly-speaker; and Solomon, which sounds *peaceable*. ⟦s9⟧ Further, he may be called Ecclesiastes, or assembly-speaker, who instructs the Church or the assembly, that is, the multitude of the people, as he himself is plainly shown to have done in this book. ⟦s10⟧ And that Solomon was beloved by the Lord, and enjoyed great peace in his kingdom until the last times of his life, when he transgressed the covenant and the law of the Lord, the history plainly discloses the kingdom. ⟦s11⟧ And so, according to the three names, he composed three volumes. ⟦s12⟧ The first, whose title is Parables, or the Proverbs of Solomon. ⟦s13⟧ The second, which we now have in hand, which is called Ecclesiastes. ⟦s14⟧ The third, which is called [0116B] the Song of songs. ⟦s15⟧ In the first, as out of a father's affection, he addresses his beloved son, and by frequent admonition exhorts him to turn aside from vices and to pursue the virtues. ⟦s16⟧ In the second, he admonishes a man grown and of ripe age not to think anything among the things of the world to be perpetual. ⟦s17⟧ But at the last, when he is now perfected and, having trodden the world underfoot, is unencumbered, in the Song of songs he joins him to the embraces of the Bridegroom. ⟦s18⟧ Not far indeed from this kind of treatment even the philosophers of the Gentiles were accustomed to form their hearers, setting before those to be instructed first ethics, then physics, and last of all theology.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ These things having been set down first for the opening up of the understanding of what is to be said, let us now come to the very sequence of the book. ⟦s2⟧ Solomon's matter in this work is all things subject [0116C] to vanity, that is, things falling and transitory. ⟦s3⟧ His intention is to persuade contempt of the world. ⟦s4⟧ His manner of treatment is such. ⟦s5⟧ He shows, according to a threefold vanity, that all things are subject to vanity, that is, are falling and transitory — namely both the things which were made for the sake of men, and the things which were made by men, and the things which were made in men. ⟦s6⟧ In those things which were made for the sake of men, the vanity is of mutability. ⟦s7⟧ In those which were made by men, the vanity is of curiosity. ⟦s8⟧ In those which were made in men, the vanity is of mortality, and all is vanity. ⟦s9⟧ And of these indeed we shall discourse more amply hereafter, if we first treat of the things that must be said concerning the very kind of contemplation. ⟦s10⟧ In which the mind of the beholder, lifted up, was able either to see or to recount such new and such wondrous things concerning [0116D] the condition of man's estate. ⟦s11⟧ For let us see what he says:

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *Vanity of vanities, said Ecclesiastes,* etc. ⟦s2⟧ But where do you suppose the mind of this man was when he said these things? ⟦s3⟧ He was a man, but he was above man. ⟦s4⟧ For unless he had gone beyond man, he would not have seen that every man is a liar. ⟦s5⟧ Therefore concerning the consideration of this man a consideration must first be had by us, and the kinds of spiritual beholding must be distinguished. ⟦s6⟧ There are three visions of the rational soul: cogitation, meditation, contemplation. ⟦s7⟧ Cogitation is when the mind is touched in passing by the notion of things, when the thing itself is suddenly presented to the soul by its own image, either entering through sense or rising up from memory. ⟦s8⟧ Meditation is the assiduous and shrewd turning over of cogitation, striving either to unfold [0117A] something involved, or, searching, to penetrate something hidden. ⟦s9⟧ Contemplation is the clear-sighted and free gaze of the soul, poured out on every side upon the things to be beheld. ⟦s10⟧ Between meditation and contemplation this difference appears to lie. ⟦s11⟧ That meditation is always about things hidden from our understanding. ⟦s12⟧ But contemplation is about things manifest, either according to their own nature or according to our capacity. ⟦s13⟧ And that meditation is always occupied about the probing of some one thing; contemplation is poured out to the grasping of many things, or even of all. ⟦s14⟧ Meditation therefore is a certain curious force of the mind, and a shrewd one, striving to investigate obscure things and to unravel perplexed ones. ⟦s15⟧ Contemplation is that liveliness of understanding which, having all things in the open, grasps them by a manifest vision. ⟦s16⟧ And thus [0117B] in a certain manner what meditation seeks, contemplation possesses. ⟦s17⟧ But of contemplation there are two kinds: one which is both prior and belongs to beginners, in the consideration of creatures; the other which is later, and belongs to the perfect, in the contemplation of the Creator. ⟦s18⟧ In the Proverbs, Solomon went forward as though by meditating. ⟦s19⟧ In Ecclesiastes he ascended to the first step of contemplation. ⟦s20⟧ In the Song of songs he carried himself over to the highest. ⟦s21⟧ In meditation there is as it were a certain wrestling of ignorance with knowledge, and the light of truth in a certain manner flashes out in the midst of the darkness of error, just as fire in green wood at first indeed takes hold with difficulty; but when it has been roused by a more vehement blast, and has begun to blaze more sharply into the material set under it, then we see certain great globes of smoky gloom [0117C] rise up, and wrap round that flame, still of a slight sparkling, shining through but rarely, until at length, the burning gradually increasing, when all the vapor is drawn off and the gloom scattered, a serene splendor appears. ⟦s22⟧ Then the victorious flame, running through the whole heap of the crackling pyre, freely lords it, and flying about the material set under it, and grazing it with a soft touch, by licking burns it up and penetrates it; nor does it rest before, by penetrating, it has stolen into the inmost parts and drawn wholly into itself, in a certain manner, whatever it found outside itself. ⟦s23⟧ But after that which is to be burned up has, by the burning, been consumed and has passed over wholly, as it were out of its own nature, into the likeness and the property of fire, then all the crash falls away, and the noise [0117D] is lulled, and those darts of flame are lifted and taken out of the midst, and that savage and devouring fire, all things being subjected to it and incorporated with it by a certain friendly likeness, composes itself in deep peace and silence; because now it finds nothing either diverse beside itself, or adverse against itself. ⟦s24⟧ First therefore fire was seen with flame and smoke, then fire with flame or smoke, last of all pure fire without flame and smoke. ⟦s25⟧ Just so, without doubt, the carnal heart, like green wood not yet dried out from the moisture of carnal concupiscence, if at any time it has conceived some spark of divine fear or love, first indeed, while depraved desires struggle against it, a smoke of passions and disturbances arises; then, when the mind has been strengthened, and it has begun with the flame of love to burn more strongly and to shine more brightly, [0118A] presently all the gloom of the disturbances vanishes: and now with a pure mind the soul pours itself out to the contemplation of truth. ⟦s26⟧ But last of all, after the heart has been penetrated by assiduous contemplation of the truth, and has entered to the very fountain of the highest truth, to the marrow, with the whole affection of the soul, then, being as it were wholly set on fire into that very sweetness and turned into the fire of love, it rests most at peace from all noise and disturbance. ⟦s27⟧ First therefore, because among the perils of temptations counsel is sought, it is as though in meditation there were smoke with flame. ⟦s28⟧ Secondly, because with a pure mind the heart is poured out to the contemplation of truth, it is as though at the beginning of contemplation there were flame without smoke. ⟦s29⟧ Thirdly, because now, truth having been found and charity perfected, nothing further is sought beyond that [0118B] which alone is; in the fire of love alone, in the highest tranquillity and felicity, he is sweetly beaten back. ⟦s30⟧ Then, when the whole heart is turned into the fire of love, God is truly felt to be all in all, since he is received with so inward a love that, besides him, nothing even of itself is left to the heart. ⟦s31+32⟧ That we may therefore distinguish these three by their proper words: the first is meditation; the second, speculation; the third, contemplation. ⟦s33⟧ In meditation, the disturbance of carnal passions, rising up importunately, beclouds the mind kindled with pious devotion. ⟦s34⟧ In speculation, the newness of an unwonted vision lifts it up into wonder. ⟦s35⟧ In contemplation, the taste of a wondrous sweetness changes the whole into joy and delight. ⟦s36⟧ Therefore in meditation there is solicitude; in speculation, wonder; in contemplation, sweetness. [0118C] Yet even spiritual speculation itself is wont to refresh the soul with great delight, when after the wrestling of temptations and the gloom of error it suddenly settles it in unhoped-for peace, and floods it with an unwonted brightness. ⟦s37⟧ Into this, therefore, this man, caught up in mind above all things falling and transitory, saw that there is nothing of all things that abides, and, terrified as it were by the stupor of this new and unwonted vision, cried out: Vanity of vanities, and all is vanity. ⟦s38⟧ For he was gazing upon the whole universe, and upon it wholly subject to vanity, and called its vanity the vanity of all vanities, that is, the vanity containing in itself every vanity, as it were the genus of all genera (in that it contains in itself all the genera of things). ⟦s39⟧ For all is vanity, and out of all things [0118D] is the universe, and the universe is vanity. ⟦s40+41⟧ But now, since we have already said, as much as seemed fitting for the present, concerning that very kind of contemplation by which this human mind was able to look through into these things concerning man, or concerning human affairs, we shall return to our proposed subject, and shall next distinguish the kinds of all the vanities which, illumined by that same contemplation, he detected to be present in things that fall; because in that we strive first to explain lucidly the very matter of the whole work, we also open its text more manifestly to those who read it. ⟦s42⟧ For his whole narrative is woven out of the vanity of temporal things.
