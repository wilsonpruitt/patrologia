## HOMILY THE FIRST. *On the title of the work, on Solomon's names, and books, and on the beginnings of this work, on the explanation of the vanities.* (cont.)

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ There are therefore three kinds of vanities, which this book especially pursues, in which it embraces every vanity: and it testifies that all things which are done under the sun [0119A] lie subject to these. ⟦s2⟧ The first is the vanity of mutability, which is present in all falling things by their condition. ⟦s3⟧ The second is the vanity of curiosity, or of cupidity, which is present in the minds of men through the disordered love of passing and vain things. ⟦s4⟧ The third is the vanity of mortality, which is present in human bodies through penalty. ⟦s5⟧ The first vanity therefore is natural, and fit or congruous. ⟦s6⟧ The second vanity is culpable, because it is perverse. ⟦s7⟧ The third vanity is penal and wretched. ⟦s8⟧ And we call the natural vanity, that is, mutability, fit for this reason: because out of the ordered alternation of passing things the greater beauty of the universe stands firm, and its instability is dispensed to the greater advantage; because in it both weariness is taken away from human minds by the variety of things, and the comeliness [0119B] of the universe, as has been said, is increased. ⟦s9⟧ For corporeal nature, whose beauty is perfected according to diverse species and forms, is adorned with an ampler comeliness out of its very mutability, while through the intervals of times, and the alteration of things passing away in time and things succeeding in time, it takes what it could not take all at once; because its species come on by succession, which could not be present in it all together. ⟦s10⟧ Further, that vanity which rules over depraved minds stands forth so much the more deformed than the rest, inasmuch as mutability was alien to spiritual substance not only through grace but even through nature. ⟦s11⟧ Therefore this alone is reproved among all the rest, and before all the rest this alone is shown to be iniquitous and perverse. ⟦s12⟧ But the penal vanity, which is in human bodies through mortality, is for this reason [0119C] called wretched, because man now has it from the penalty of sin, that he lies subject to the condition of things temporally passing away — he who before, in his own creation, had received to stand above the condition of all visible things. ⟦s13⟧ For the condition of passing things is that all things which have risen should set, and things increased should grow old. ⟦s14⟧ But the nature of man, taken indeed out of corruptible matter, yet through grace raised above corruption and confirmed against corruption, had received this as a gift: that it should indeed have a rising with the rest, but should not have a setting; and that among things to be born, but not to die, it should receive increase by the growths of age, and should not suffer defect. ⟦s15⟧ This dignity man lost through the sin of transgression, when, stripped of the robe of immortality, and fallen back to the condition of his own origin, [0119D] he heard from the sentence of the Creator: *Earth thou art, and unto earth thou shalt go [n: (Gen. III)]*. ⟦s16⟧ To this vanity therefore (which was born to lie subject to the rest) to man alone is it reckoned for a penalty; because man alone had received to stand above it through grace. ⟦s17⟧ Since therefore we have distinguished three vanities: the first indeed, that is, the vanity of mutability, is the cause of sin; the second, that is, the vanity of cupidity, is sin; but the third, that is, the vanity of mortality, is the penalty of sin. ⟦s18⟧ Therefore the vanity of cupidity is reproved in the vanity of mutability. ⟦s19⟧ In the vanity of mortality it is punished. ⟦s20⟧ For as the Psalmist says: *Man when he was in honour did not understand; but was compared to senseless beasts, and was made like unto them [n: (Psal. XLVIII)]*. ⟦s21⟧ For unless he had first, through disordered [0120A] concupiscence of the flesh, inclined himself to the things that belong to beasts, he would in no wise have been made like to beasts through the mortality of the flesh. ⟦s22⟧ But now, because through the desire of his mind he began to lean upon mutable things, he too could not be stable in that which he had been. ⟦s23⟧ We can still distinguish these vanities by another division, so that the same thing, the more manifoldly it is set forth, may so much the more evidently be known. ⟦s24⟧ One vanity is in created things. ⟦s25⟧ Another in human works, another in bodies, another in minds. ⟦s26⟧ The first is of mutability; the second of curiosity; the third of mortality; the fourth of iniquity. ⟦s27⟧ The vanity of mutability is considered in things in two ways: either because they are empty, or because they are transitory. ⟦s28⟧ In the one they are vain, because they show what they do not have; in the other they are vain, because they do not remain [0120B] in what they have. ⟦s29⟧ There they are vain, because they have form alone, and do not have essence. ⟦s30⟧ Here they are vain, because although they have some essence, yet they have no substance. ⟦s31⟧ There they are vain, because without truth they set an appearance before us. ⟦s32⟧ Here they are vain, because they hold out a standing and pass away. ⟦s33⟧ So are all things falling and transitory, false and deceiving: they who love vain things, and trust in vanity, and for that reason are made not only vain, but vanity itself out of vanity, fail away.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ The vanity of cupidity consists in three things: namely in the concupiscence of the eyes, in the concupiscence of the flesh, and in the pride of life. ⟦s2⟧ For in all these there is vanity. ⟦s3⟧ For all things which pertain to the flesh, whether they seem good or evil, are neither true evils nor true goods; nor can they always be, whether they be good or evil, [0120C] that which they are. ⟦s4⟧ Therefore it is as vain to place hope in those things which seem good as to dread those which are thought evil. ⟦s5⟧ For what does the concupiscence of the eyes do? ⟦s6⟧ See what it does, and you will find how great a vanity lies hidden, cloaked under this veil. ⟦s7⟧ It catches at the deceitful paints of things and at slippery sights. ⟦s8⟧ Forgetful of itself, it is poured out abroad, and giving itself wholly to curiosity it goes round all things, ranges over the universe, in case anything new, anything unwonted, anything wondrous should occur — what this or that may mean, what appearance each single thing may have, or what signification they hold out. ⟦s9⟧ To all the motions of things it is always changeable, always headlong, rash, forward, unstable, wanton, impatient and slippery; often exulting in vain hope, often trembling with empty fear, having no [0120D] root within, but always suspicious, hanging upon the outward motions of things. ⟦s10⟧ Before peril it wavers, before danger it trembles; everything which can befall on either side, whether good or evil, fearing and desiring both, it turns aside from vainly, by suspicion alone. ⟦s11⟧ It burns after the manner of one impatient, awaiting to see what is to come; at things present it is dissolved, at things that stand firm it is dulled, and at every vicissitude of things, whether they be sad or glad, it is varied by an inconstant fluctuation of the mind. ⟦s12⟧ But consider how vain this is. ⟦s13⟧ Assuredly every creature is such to a man as he himself is to it, so that by those things which are without, neither can a good mind be harmed, nor an evil one helped. ⟦s14⟧ Therefore all these things can be neither for evil to a good man, nor for good to an evil one, [0121A] except so far as the soul itself either profits by spurning them, or fails by loving them perversely, or by fearing them vainly. ⟦s15⟧ How great a vanity then it is, to look upon these things with so great solicitude and curiosity, as though they were of some moment either to give or to take away salvation, and either not to attend to, or to dissemble, those inward goods in which true salvation consists? ⟦s16⟧ What shall I say of the concupiscence of the flesh? ⟦s17⟧ How vain it is, since the delight of the flesh itself, however great it be, can neither help when past, nor remain when present? ⟦s18⟧ What does it profit to defend with so great effort against corruption a flesh that is to die, by the excess of delights and the affluence of pleasures, when there is no one who can guard it from corruption? ⟦s19⟧ For as to the pride of life, whether it will glory in riches or in dignities, what can be vainer? [0121B] For who would not vainly glory that he is more heavily laden than the rest? ⟦s20⟧ This indeed is all that riches, all that dignities confer upon their possessors: that whereas they seem to receive in them more than others, they are thereby found more heavily laden than others; whom certainly, for the acquiring or the keeping of those same things, they always either wear down with labor or afflict with care. ⟦s21⟧ But in all these things there is great vanity, and superfluous care. ⟦s22⟧ The vanity of mortality, which consists in two things: namely in the penalty which wears down, and in the penalty which dissolves. ⟦s23⟧ The penalty which wears down first makes us grow old through the failing of life. ⟦s24⟧ The penalty which dissolves afterwards compels us to go into rottenness, and to return, through the corruption of the flesh, into the dust from which we were taken according to the flesh. ⟦s25⟧ Hence therefore let man weigh in what vanity every man lives, who unwilling [0121C] suffers the corruption of the flesh, because willing he works the corruption of the mind. ⟦s26⟧ Wholly then is he subject to vanity who both flows away in flesh through mortality, and in mind through iniquity. ⟦s27⟧ But the one of these is his own from himself, the other his own against himself, and the whole is in himself. ⟦s28⟧ These three kinds of vanities the author distinguishes at once in the beginning of his book, which afterwards, pursuing them one by one through the whole sequence of his work, he extends by copious [sic: *disputat one*], everywhere reproving in the three that fourth kind of vanity which we said is present in depraved minds, and showing this alone to be liable to fault and owing a penalty. ⟦s29⟧ The vanity which is present in created things through mutability he sets forth first. ⟦s30⟧ Vanity of vanities, and all is vanity. ⟦s31⟧ Then in the second place [0121D] he adds the vanity which is in human works. ⟦s32⟧ What hath a man more of all his labour that he laboureth under the sun? ⟦s33⟧ In the third place he subjoins that vanity which is present in human bodies through mortality, saying: *One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh.* ⟦s34⟧ And according to these three distinctions he also divides the whole sequence of the following work into three parts, assigning to each its own portion. ⟦s35⟧ In the first part, whose beginning is: *The sun riseth, and goeth down,* he disputes concerning the vanity of mutability. ⟦s36⟧ In the second part, which begins thus: *I Ecclesiastes was king of Israel in Jerusalem.* ⟦s37⟧ He pursues most widely the vanity of human cupidity. ⟦s38⟧ In the third part (which is placed as it were for a close in the last position in the disputation, whose beginning is: *Remember thy Creator in the days of thy youth* ) he calls to mind the vanity [0122A] of mortality, so that the human soul may as it were hear at the end that which it is the more needful to perceive with intent consideration, and to recall to memory the more often. ⟦s39⟧ These things we have prepared, according to the grasp of our understanding, for the opening of Ecclesiastes, which is the second of Solomon's books, for those entering upon the reading of it. ⟦s40⟧ But we confess that to explain worthily its whole breadth is above our powers: in matters of this kind, covered over with deep profundity, seeking rather to hear a teacher than to snatch a teacher's place. ⟦s41⟧ For it is better, as Plato says, modestly to learn what is another's than shamelessly to thrust forward one's own. ⟦s42⟧ But if we have been able to say anything fitting upon this, let it be beyond hope and expectation. ⟦s43⟧ If anything usefully, not beyond our intention. ⟦s44⟧ First, as it were [0122B] a certain proem, he sets before his book: in which he distinguishes that threefold matter of the following work which we have called to mind, concerning which he afterwards enters more amply into disputation. ⟦s45⟧ The proem therefore is: *Vanity of vanities, vanity of vanities, and all is vanity,* as far as: *The sun riseth, and goeth down.* ⟦s46⟧ From there the first part of the book is begun, and the rest as we have said. ⟦s47⟧ Now let us consider the very words of the letter, whether we may be able to grasp in them anything of the things which have been said above.
