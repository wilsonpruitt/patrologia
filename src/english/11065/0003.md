## HOMILY THE FIRST. *On the title of the work, on Solomon's names, and books, and on the beginnings of this work, on the explanation of the vanities.* (cont.)

*Vanity of vanities (said Ecclesiastes), vanity of vanities, and all is vanity.* With weight is that to be pronounced which he speaks of himself as though of another: said Ecclesiastes. For because for authority's sake he set down his own person, he fittingly spoke of himself not as of himself, but as of another, so that, since he had to speak of himself as some great one, as the matter [0122C] demanded, he might more becomingly and more maturely say this of himself in another than in himself. So Balaam, speaking of himself, says: *He hath said, the hearer of the words of God, who hath beheld the visions of the Almighty, who falleth and hath his eyes opened [n: (Num. XXIV)]*. And John in his Gospel. He speaks of himself as though of another, saying: *This is that disciple whom Jesus loved, who giveth testimony of these things, and we know that his testimony is true [n: (Joan. XXI)]*. And Paul speaks of himself as though of another to the Corinthians, saying: *I know a man in Christ, whether in the body, or out of the body, I know not, God knoweth, such a one caught up even to the third heaven, and such a one caught up into paradise, and that he heard secret words, which it is not granted to man to utter [n: (II Cor. XII)]*. But this kind [0122D] of speech, as often as it is taken up in Scripture, is done either for the sake of humility, or of admiration — namely, that when we would say great things, we should rather ascribe them as it were to others, lest it seem either proud or excessive, if we should attribute them to ourselves. For we ought both to remove great things from ourselves for humility's sake, and to show wondrous things as it were from afar, for admiration's sake. For those things are less wondrous which come nearer to knowledge. Therefore, that the minds of all may be raised up to the utterance that is to come, and may awake as at some miracle of newness, it is rightly said: Said Ecclesiastes. As if it were said: What so great and such a one has said could not be vain, even though he said it concerning vanity. The common teacher and the instructor of all, Ecclesiastes himself, has said it. What did he say? Hear what he said, and attend. For they are no small things [0123A] that are said: Vanity of vanities, vanity of vanities, and all is vanity. If all is vanity, then he also is vanity who said this. And how will it stand firm that what vanity said concerning vanity is not vain? But if it is true that what he said is vain, it is not to be heard, but spat out. What then shall we say? Either not all is vanity, as he said, and he said what is false; or all is vanity, as he said, and he himself is vanity, and said a vain thing. But assuredly, as he himself said: All is vanity, and he himself, who said it, is vanity; and not only vanity, but even universal vanity: and yet what he said is not vanity, but truth; because in that which he said he himself was not vanity, since he said against vanity what he said concerning vanity; and it is agreed that he could not be contrary to vanity in that in which he was vanity [0123B]. Something therefore was in him which was not vanity, and that could speak not vainly against vanity. But what was this, or where was it? Assuredly, if we shall say that this body was it, or something in the body, which spoke without vanity concerning vanity: then could the body, which is corrupted, subject to vanity and born for vanity, at some time be without vanity — a body which wholly lives in vanity? We remember the saying of the Psalmist: *Universal vanity, every man living [n: (Psal. XXXVIII)]*. For that which dies is the very thing which lives: which is born that it may live, and lives that it may die. But what is it that dies, save that which is deprived of life? That therefore dies in man which is extinguished from life. And what is this, save the body and the bodily sense? For it is not itself after the living creature, [0123C] from which it is a living creature, and when it ceases to be capable of sensation, the sense ceases to be. Therefore that which dies in man is the very thing which lives in man; which is for that reason subject to vanity, because it is liable to mortality. Therefore the bodily sense could not reprove vanity, in which nothing can be found free and pure from vanity. What then must be said this was? Was it perhaps the soul? Have we not shown above that it too is subject to vanity? For in it we placed the vanity of iniquity: which stands forth so much the more deformed than all other things, in that from it alone the others have either that they are harmful, or that they are at all. For without this vanity, neither would there be the vanity of mortality, nor would the vanity of mutability be harmful [0123D]. How then can the soul, which is subject to so great a vanity, truly reprove vanity? Or is something surviving found in it free from vanity, and was it said without cause: Universal vanity, every man living? For man is universal vanity, because in body and in mind he is subject to vanity. What man? *Every man living.* Not every man life, but every man living. For man is both life and man is living. Where the life, and where the living. Life in that which quickens; living in that which is quickened. And the flesh lives from the soul, and the soul from the flesh: and both live, but the flesh has wholly from the soul that it lives? the soul has not wholly in the flesh that it lives. For it lives also in God, and would that it did live? What lives in the flesh is vain. What lives in God [0124A] is not vain, but is true, because it is from truth. In that part therefore in which it lives from truth, it truly reproves vanity, and it is found how vanity reproves vanity, not through vanity but through truth. Rightly therefore Ecclesiastes, who in mind was lifted above all vanity and had come through to truth itself, saw sublimely in that same truth what he truly said concerning the reproving of vanity: Vanity of vanities, vanity of vanities, and all is vanity. The driving home of the words with the affection of the speaker signifies both the greatness of the matter and the newness of the wonder. And it is a sentiment uttered with heightening. For there are certain degrees, and as it were certain progressions, for one going both to the better and to the worse. For thus is it said the Song of songs, and the age of ages [0124B], just as here it is now said: Vanity of vanities — so that by a certain like distance and a similar difference the Song of songs may be understood to excel above the song only so far as a song excels a word, and next a word silence. And an age of ages to excel an age only so far as an age alone excels a moment, or again a moment nothing. And with a like progression made here by the contrary: that vanity of vanities is worse than simple vanity by so much as simple vanity is worse than a solid and abiding essence. Thus by driving home the sentiment may be read: Vanity of vanities, vanity of vanities, and all is vanity — so that in all things vanity of every kind may be expressed, and out of all things a certain sum may be understood to be made up, which contains every vanity, so that as it were each single [0124C] thing is by itself vanity, and the universe gathered out of them all is vanity of vanities. So in the creation of things, when God was completing his works, each single work is by itself called *good [n: (Gen. I)]*, and last of all, all things being perfected, the universe is named *very good*. For if that which in part is good is found better in the whole, that also which in part is evil must needs be worse in the whole. Therefore vanity is of vanities, and the vanity of all vanities is universal vanity, and the universality of vanity is all vanity. Or else that vanity of human minds is here reproved by comparison with the other vanities: which, as risen out of the other vanities, and having surpassed all vanities by comparison of vanity, is deservedly called vanity [0124D] of vanities. It can also not incongruously be that this threefold repetition of vanity is referred to the three kinds of vanities recalled above, so that as it were one of the vanities is the work of man, another mortal nature, the third this whole world with its universe of mutable things. For this man considered the works of mortals, and saw of each single one that with labor and grief they come to their effect. When made they subsist for a brief time; but passing away they leave no fruit behind them. He saw with how great affliction and misery human life is worn down daily without ceasing, even in those things which it works for its own consolation, and how it almost always suffers more loss in seeking a remedy than it receives of consolation in obtaining a solace. And in all these things, at how vainly every man living is troubled, wondering and amazed [0125A] he says: Vanity of vanities. Then, attending to the very condition of mortals, and finding in it a greater and more miserable vanity, he saw that if it is reckoned a great vanity when that which man makes passes away, then that vanity is altogether to be grieved over and pitied when that which man is cannot stand. Upon this therefore, redoubling again, he cried out and said: Vanity of vanities. Last of all, beholding the [sic: *inconstantinam*] and the fluctuation of all mutable things hastening to their setting, concluding as it were in a sum he added and said: All is vanity.
