## HOMILY THE FIRST. *On the title of the work, on Solomon's names, and books, and on the beginnings of this work, on the explanation of the vanities.* (cont.)

It is to be noted, however, that he does not say: What more is the labor of man? But: What hath a man more, he says, of all his labour that he laboureth under the sun? Whence it stands firm that here the matter treated is not so much the labor of man as the fruit and profit of human labor; [0126D] because it is not asked what or of what sort the labor of man is, but what fruit man obtains of all his labor by which he labors under the sun. And all this fruit is declared to be nothing other than vanity, when it is said: What hath he more? As if it were said: Man hath nothing more than vanity of his labor. Therefore vanity is the whole fruit of man's labor, and every man labors in vain, being about to receive nothing but vanity of all his labor. Was the labor then of the saints also vain, who faithfully served God, and for love of him suffered so many torments and kinds of tortures? Who would presume to say this? For how was the labor of those men vain who, vexed indeed for a little while, came through transitory griefs to abiding joys, and through a temporal death obtained everlasting life? [0127A] Or is that labor to be called vain for this reason, because what they suffered has passed away: and yet according to something not vain, because what they received abides? For that even the labor of the just is vain — so far, that is, as regards the transitory penalty and this as it were imaginary appearance of misery — the Psalmist seems to hint, when he says: *Thou who framest labour in commandment [n: (Psal. XCIII)]*. For God as it were frames labor for his elect, when outwardly, as though angry, he inflicts upon them by judgment a temporal penalty, for whom inwardly, by the providence of his mercy, he keeps the rewards of eternal blessedness. Since therefore the Psalmist names the labor of the just, and this imaginary grief, because it has the appearance of misery but does not have true misery, a *framed* thing, he assuredly shows [0127B] that this same labor can also, according to something, be not unfittingly called vain. Behold therefore the labor of the work passes away. What was vain passes away; what was transitory has passed away: the labor has passed away, the grief has passed away. Has the fruit of the work passed away, or the wage of the labor? Therefore it is permitted: behold, according to a certain manner of speaking, the labor itself and the temporal grief is not unfittingly called vain. Yet will the wage of that same labor ever rightly be called vanity? God forbid! How then shall that stand which was said: What hath a man more of all his labour? For it was said of the reward of labor and not of the labor itself, of the profit, not of the work: What hath a man more of all his labour? Behold, man hath more, and much more hath he, [0127C] and thou sayest: What more hath he? Therefore he added and said: That he laboureth under the sun. Behold, we have the difficulty of this question resolved. For here he openly shows what labors he reproves of vanity. Those which are done, he says, under the sun. What is it, to be done under the sun? Perhaps what he says, under the sun, is to be taken simply; because men, dwelling in this lower part of the world, receive from above the light of the sun, and are lighted so that they may see for the doing of the works necessary to human uses. For on this account the times of human life have been so distributed by divine providence in alternating turns, that the very vicissitude of the alternating times might repair sick bodies wearied by too great a stretching out of their actions. Therefore night and day unceasingly succeed one another [0127D] with mutual evenness, so that by day the sun, passing over from its rising to its setting in its course above, may pour out its light on every side upon the things beneath it, and, illuminating human eyes by its presence, may rouse both those eyes and those of the other living things of the earth to the exercise of acting, and at the same time afford the means of working. But by night, withdrawing itself from human sight, and, as with certain swaddling-bands of infancy, spreading over them the veil of darkness and covering the eyes of mortals, it may foster their weary limbs to rest and slumber, that it may restore them again, repaired and more eager, to labor and to the day's exercise. This wondrous order of the divine dispensation the Psalmist beheld, when, commending God's works, he calls this to mind among the rest as something excellent and worthy of no small praise, saying: *He hath made [0128A] the moon for seasons; the sun knoweth his going down. Thou hast appointed darkness, and it is night: in it shall all the beasts of the woods go about. The young lions roaring after their prey, and seeking their meat from God. The sun ariseth, and they are gathered together, and they shall lie down in their dens. Man shall go forth [sic: od] his work, and to his labour until the evening [n: (Psal. CIII)]*. Hence also is that which God promises to Noah as he goes out of the ark after the flood, as for some great sign of his loving-kindness and gift of his bounty, saying: *All the days of seedtime and harvest, cold and heat, summer and winter, night and day shall not cease [n: (Gen. VIII)]*. Beautifully therefore he said that men, distended down below in this ark of the world through the various runnings-about of the occupations of mortal life, labor under the sun, so that this too belongs to the vanity [0128B] of human work: that a light passing over from above, and to be ended in darkness, gives light to man for his working. For he considered how abject and wretched is the condition of mortals, who upon the surface of the earth, like certain worms creeping in vain curiosity, are roused by a light suddenly poured in from above, as though to be mocked, to snatch at what they vainly desire, and, the same being withdrawn after a little, are wrapped about again in a sudden blindness, and can in no wise go forward further into effect. Therefore the very light by which they are lighted is shown to them from afar and at a distance from above, and unapproachable to man, so that it may be proved to stand in no way in man's power; so far that man receives from it only so much as the graces of God, who bestows and orders all things, shall have permitted, not so much as [0128C] man's perverse will, serving cupidity, desires. A great vanity therefore it is to labor under the sun, and small confidence to labor under the sun: whose light, however pleasant to look upon and delightful to the sight it be, yet cannot always be present to human eyes nor profit them. But if it please us to push our consideration yet higher and to look into the matter more subtly, we can take time to be signified by the name of the sun, so that what he said, under the sun, may be the same as if he had said, under time. For because chiefly by the sun both the course of the times is distinguished and the nature of temporal things is varied, time itself is fittingly expressed by the sun, when it is said under the sun it is the same as if he had said, under time. But has he perhaps in this too willed to [sic: *in nuere*] something to us, in that he did not say in time or with time, [0128D] but under time: and must a discernment be had by us, that we may understand that some things are those which were as it were made with time; but others those which were either made in time, or are done under time? Let us discern therefore, if we can, whether perchance we may be able to find the reason why he said rather that the works of men are vain as made under time than either with time or in time. All things which were made were either made with time, or in time. For those things were made with time whose rising time did not precede: such as the angelic nature, and that formless matter of visible things, which in the beginning God created. Those things were made in time whose rising time preceded, such as those works of the six days in which God perfected and completed [0129A] the fabric of this sensible world; but those things also which afterwards were made formally, or essentially, were all made in time. Again, of those things which are done in time, some are done in time and under time, others are done in time only, not under time. Those are done in time and not under time which indeed begin in time, but are not ended in time. Those are done in time and under time which both begin in time and are ended in time. Likewise, of those things which are done under time, some are done for the sake of time, others are done for the sake of eternity. Those therefore which are done for the sake of time are vain both in act and in fruit. But those which are done for the sake of eternity are indeed vain in act; but in fruit they are not vain, because, even if they pass away in the work, yet they abide in the recompense. For a good work [0129B] is not this alone which appears outwardly transitory; because it consists both of a visible action and of an invisible devotion. For, as man consists of a living thing and of life, and the living thing indeed dies, but the life does not die; so without doubt the good has both a visible action in itself, and an invisible devotion. The visible action is as it were the body; the inward devotion is as it were the spirit. The action lives through devotion, as the body through the soul. But devotion quickens the action, as the soul the body. For every action which is without devotion is dead. Therefore the visible action itself as it were lives, and the invisible devotion itself is life. But that which lives, this dies; but that which is life, this does not die. Because a transitory action, just as it has its beginning from time, so indeed it is ended in time. That therefore which dies, [0129C] which is ended, which passes away, which is given over to mutability, is itself liable to vanity. But that which is life, which abides, which does not admit of an end or of corruption, does not itself admit vanity. Let it be said therefore: What hath a man more of all his labour that he laboureth under the sun? [nt: it is understood: apart from vanity.] Nothing more, because all things which are done under the sun are vain. And what would he have of vain things but vanity? What then? Will good works which are done under the sun be able to confer nothing more upon those who work them than vanity? And how did we say above that this question was resolved by that which he says, under the sun? Behold, the same question rises up for us again, no less difficult than the one above, because we cannot deny that good works too are done under the sun, that is, under time, according to what [0129D] has been expounded above; and yet we do not dare to say that nothing but vanity follows from them. And behold, the book plainly says: What hath a man more of all his labour that he laboureth under the sun? But if we are willing to consider the very words more diligently, it is plainly shown what those works are which are reproved as vain. For he did not say: What hath a man more of all his labour which is done, or which was done, under the sun? But rather he says, which man himself laboureth under the sun. He does not therefore reprove the labor so much as the laborer as being under the sun. So that because man himself, set under the sun, labors, therefore what he labors at is vain. But who is he who labors under the sun? He who, pressed down by love of temporal and transitory [0130A] things, labors for the obtaining of the same, if he does not have them, or for the keeping of them, if he has them. This man, set under the sun, labors, and this man labors in vain. But he who exercises good works for the hope and desire of eternal things, this man does not labor under the sun, even though the labor in which he labors is under the sun; because, although the work which he carries through is temporal, yet he himself transcends all temporal and falling things in mind and in devotion. This man therefore does not labor in vain, even though that in which he labors is vain; because that for which he labors is not vain. And when that which is vain, in which he labors, has passed away, he has ready that which is not vanity, for which he labors. Such a man therefore does not belong to that sentiment in which it was said: What hath a man more of all his labour that he laboureth under the sun? Because he does not [0130B] labor under the sun, but above the sun, who does not set the wage of his labor in things rolling and passing away with time.
