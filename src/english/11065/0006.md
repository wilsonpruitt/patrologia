## HOMILY THE FIRST. *On the title of the work, on Solomon's names, and books, and on the beginnings of this work, on the explanation of the vanities.* (cont.)

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh.* ⟦s2⟧ Behold vanity. ⟦s3⟧ One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh. ⟦s4⟧ This human nature conceived, corrupted in the first root of its stock, and, sown together with this same corruption, it sprouted out into the whole offspring of posterity. ⟦s5⟧ For the first parent of the human race, as soon as he heard: Earth thou art and unto earth thou shalt go, from that time made mortal, because he fell from the state of incorruption, began as it were to pass over from life to death through mortality. ⟦s6⟧ This way therefore all men after him pass through, who descend from him by the birth of the flesh. ⟦s7⟧ This is that vanity of which the Psalmist spoke, saying: *Nevertheless [0130C] universal vanity, every man living. ⟦s8⟧ Nevertheless man passeth as an image [n: (Psal. XXXVIII)]*. ⟦s9⟧ This vanity therefore, as greater than the rest and more horrible to human minds because of the dread necessity of death, he added in the last place, so that man might hear this at the end, in which he may more evidently recognize the misery of his own condition. ⟦s10⟧ For the first vanity is not even in the name; nor is it referred to man. ⟦s11⟧ The second vanity is indeed not in man; but yet it is referred to man. ⟦s12⟧ The third vanity both is in man, and is referred to man. ⟦s13⟧ Accordingly, as though advancing by certain steps always to higher things, he set first that vanity which is neither the penalty of man nor his fault. ⟦s14⟧ Then he added that vanity which is not the penalty of man, but the fault. ⟦s15⟧ Last of all, that which is not the fault, but the penalty: [0130D] although yet neither can such a fault ever be without penalty, nor is such a penalty — at least in those to whom the sacrament of salvation does not come to aid — altogether free from fault. ⟦s16⟧ But I have called that the fault of man, not the penalty, and this the penalty, not the fault; because in the one man's desert is discerned, and in the other judgment is found. ⟦s17⟧ This penalty therefore, as heavier and more monstrous than the rest, he set last: which, though it be not more wicked than the fault, is yet more grievous to the flesh, and more effectual for the rousing of carnal minds. ⟦s18⟧ Let him say therefore: One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh. ⟦s19⟧ As if he were saying: If you think it is to be neglected that everything you do is vain, is even this to be neglected, that you yourselves are vanity? ⟦s20⟧ One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh. ⟦s21⟧ If the dying [0131A] depart and those who are to die follow after, what joy is there? ⟦s22⟧ The grief of the living seems indeed to receive consolation in those who are born; but from that whence it is thought to be ended so that it should not be, from thence it receives that it should always be. ⟦s23⟧ For that the dying depart is grief. ⟦s24⟧ What consolation follows in that those who are to die come on? ⟦s25⟧ A consolation which is bounded by mourning. ⟦s26⟧ And what can be vainer in either? ⟦s27⟧ One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh. ⟦s28⟧ If some lamentable chance were to involve only a few, or at least not all, it would seem tolerable; but as it is, a savage plague drags all with it to destruction. ⟦s29⟧ One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh. ⟦s30⟧ If it passeth away, whither does it go? ⟦s31⟧ If it cometh, whence does it come? ⟦s32⟧ Alas, dread lot! ⟦s33⟧ How canst thou be secure, O man, wrapped in such great darkness? ⟦s34⟧ Behold, thou knowest that by living thou tendest to death. ⟦s35⟧ But canst thou know [0131B] of what sort, or where, thou art to be after death? ⟦s36⟧ Consider therefore in how great a vanity thou livest, thou who daily seest that which thou art pass away; nor yet canst thou by any reasoning know of what sort is that which thou art to be. ⟦s37⟧ One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1+s2+s3+s4+s5+s6+s7+s8+s9+s10+s11+s12+s13+s14+s15+s16+s17+s18+s19+s20+s21+s22+s23+s24+s25+s26+s27+s28+s29+s30+s31+s32+s33+s34+s35+s36+s37+s38+s39+s40⟧ *But the earth standeth for ever.* Hearken, O man, and blush. The earth standeth for ever. Why does the earth stand, and thou canst not stand, for whose sake the earth was made? Perhaps thou wilt say, because it stands for thy consolation. It stands that it may send forth those who come, bear those who pass over, and receive those who depart. For those who are born come from the earth by the origin of the flesh, and the living pass over upon the earth by the mutability of the flesh, and the dying return into the earth by the corruption of the flesh. Therefore it stands for thy consolation; nay rather it stands for thy confusion [0131C]. How? See of what sort it stands, of what sort it bears, and whom it bears, and out of what sort it bears. Of what sort therefore does it bear? Hear of what sort: *Cursed is the earth in thy work; thorns and thistles shall it bring forth to thee [n: (Gen. III)]*. And whom does it bear? Hear again: *Cursed art thou upon the earth [n: (Gen. IV)]*. And out of what sort does it bear: *In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat thy bread; and thou shalt eat the herbs of the earth? [n: (Gen. III.)]* Therefore the cursed bears the cursed. And yet it bears them. But from that whence it bears, it has something for thy consolation and something for thy penalty. What is for thy consolation is with thy labor; what is for thy penalty is through thy labor. It has bread, and it has thorns also. Bread with thy labor, for thy consolation; thorns through thy labor, for thy penalty. In the sweat of thy face [0131D] shalt thou eat thy bread: this is for thy consolation, with thy labor. When thou hast worked, the earth will not give thee its fruit, but thorns and thistles shall it bring forth to thee. This is for thy penalty, through thy labor. Therefore it stands not only for thy consolation, but much more it stands for thy confusion. It stands not only for the easing of thy misery, but it stands for the testimony and the penalty of thy fault. The earth standeth, and thou standest upon the earth. Stable is she who bears, and thou fallest in ruin who art borne. Why is this? Art thou too not earth? What then was said to thee: Earth thou art, and unto earth thou shalt go? Therefore thou art earth, and thou art upon the earth, and thou art of earth. Why then dost thou not stand for ever? Hast thou been made weaker than that earth from which thou wast taken? What then did the hand of the craftsman work, if it did not advance [0132A] its work to something better? Was there then no knowledge with him, or was he found weak, so that in his hands that good matter should be made worse? God forbid! Nay rather, he made thee much better than that had been out of which he made thee; because he had made the earth stable, but thee he made out of earth immortal. Therefore he made thee better than that had been out of which he made thee. Not therefore from the craftsman did thy corruption proceed, who made thee with so great power and with such goodness that nothing could be less than perfect, nothing would he have worse than the best. And so the best craftsman made a good thing formed, whose eternal incorruption and immortal goodness neither begets a fault nor receives a fault.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But perhaps the matter is to be blamed, which was such that nothing else could be made of it. ⟦s2⟧ For the matter was earth, and there came forth an earthen vessel. ⟦s3⟧ What? ⟦s4⟧ This is a wonder. [0132B] Thou therefore, in saying this, oughtest to have looked to this: that, if thou art an earthen vessel, then thou wast hardened out of soft mud into a potsherd, and thy clod, if it had not the nature of firmness, yet had the nature of confirmation. ⟦s5⟧ If it had not that this should be by nature, yet it had by nature that this could be by grace. ⟦s6⟧ Therefore thy matter did thee no harm, which both keeps whole what nature conferred, and does not itself diminish what grace gave. ⟦s7⟧ For the earth standeth for ever. ⟦s8⟧ What is *standeth*? ⟦s9⟧ It perseveres in that which it was made; it keeps its own nature; it does not desert its own condition; what it received, this it retains uncorrupted. ⟦s10⟧ Earth it was made, and earth it remains; never is it other than what it is. ⟦s11⟧ And when it is other, it is other not by the failing of corruption, but by the advance of exaltation. ⟦s12⟧ Just as in thee [0132C] it has been made other, and is to be other, than it was born; but by exaltation, not by corruption. ⟦s13⟧ And when in certain earthly things it seems to carry itself over from one thing into another, yet it is never other than it is; because it is earth, and always it is earth, and that which passes over is earth and that into which it passes over is earth; and when this passes over into that, or into that other, yet that which is earth does not pass over, which in this and in that is the same. ⟦s14⟧ Therefore the earth has to be moved within the bosom of its own nature, and has to work out of that which it is, that which it is: it has not to pass over beyond the bounds of its own nature out of that which it is into that which it is not: and for that reason, the earth standeth for ever in that which it is not. ⟦s15⟧ Why therefore hast not thou too stood in that in which thou wast made upon the earth, just as [0132D] the earth stands in that in which it was made? ⟦s16⟧ Why hast thou degenerated, so as not even to imitate thy matter, which so far keeps what it is, that even when thou hast been made together with the earth, thou canst not be other than the earth is? ⟦s17⟧ As far as that thou flowest away by thy own fault; beyond it thou art not permitted to go forward by its nature. ⟦s18⟧ Behold how much it guards what it is, so that even when it has received thee, flowing and unstable, it does not suffer thee to pass over further into another thing which itself is not. ⟦s19⟧ Whence then is it for thee to flow away? ⟦s20⟧ Search diligently, and think. ⟦s21⟧ Neither thy craftsman nor thy matter did this for thee; because she, both by standing for ever, keeps in thee also what she herself is, and he, by making thee for eternity, gave also more than she is. ⟦s22⟧ Whence therefore is this for thee, save from thy own fault, out of which it is now a penalty for thee that thou returnest into her, to whom this had been given of grace, that thou shouldest stand always [0133A] above her? ⟦s23⟧ Therefore thou passest away, and the earth standeth in testimony against thee, so that, reproving thee as mortal and receiving thee as dying, it may convict thee before it punishes thee. ⟦s24⟧ Therefore the earth standeth for ever. ⟦s25⟧ One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh; but the earth standeth for ever.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ These things we have said, according to the littleness or the scantiness of our understanding, upon the opening of the book of Solomon which is called Ecclesiastes, in no way presuming that we have said what most of all had to be said, since it is so great a thing for us only not to have said what had not to be said. ⟦s2⟧ But for the rest it must be known that this book requires a certain new kind of exposition; because, since it wholly aims at moving the affections of the human heart, one must more often form one's discourse in it as though by conversing than by expounding. ⟦s3⟧ Whence [0133B] it is needful in these matters sometimes even to dwell longer upon words in those things which seem plain and open, so that the very driving home of the utterance may touch more strongly and penetrate more effectually the heart of the hearer. ⟦s4⟧ He who shall wish to handle this Scripture otherwise, even if he serves conveniently the understanding of his hearers, yet, not retaining its force and property, profits perhaps less unto edification.
