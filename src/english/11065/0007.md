## HOMILY II. *On the proof of the vanity of all things under heaven: through the corruption of the elements, through the generation of things, the succession, and the forgetting of those things which have been.*

The words of Ecclesiastes, which testify that all things under the sun are vain, have — I know not by what compact — just now, when they were read, been made sweet in our ears. And behold, we have begun willingly to hear our own evils, and what we do not love [0133C] we nevertheless love to hear. For we do not love our own evils, and we love to hear our own evils; because by hearing the evils which we do not love, we remember the goods which we do love. And this very remembrance of goods (even amid evils) is sweet to the soul, and assuredly so much the sweeter the more bitter are those evils themselves, which, whether by hearing or by feeling, we recognize to be far from those goods for which, at least by remembrance, we sigh. This was the whole reason why we wonder that this recital alone has just now been savory to us, in which the hardship of our exile is described to us, and it is declared of what sort and how great our misery and calamity is. For our mind, in the hearing of its own evils, as though awaking out of a certain sleep of former time, suddenly [0133D] recognized where it was, and, amazed and wondering at so great an abyss of ruin, began at the same time also to consider from how great a height it had fallen into this very depth of misery, and, because it could not yet do so in effect, it sighed after that height with a certain wondrous desire of inward ardor. This therefore was what in those words which recited our misery drew our affection. This made it that it should ever be pleasing to us thus to hear our own evils, and that whatever that be which is perceived along with that which is so sweet cannot be bitter. This daily makes their very laments sweet to the wretched, and amid sighs and groans feeds the afflicted delightfully with tears; because the remembrance of the things that are loved is sweeter amid adversities, and the soul itself burns the more with desire of things absent, is touched the more sweetly by the memory of things past, [0134A] is the more strongly inflamed by the deferring of things to come. This our Ecclesiastes knew excellently well, who in his whole narrative so diligently pursues the vanity of passing things, that out of the consideration of it he may admonish the human heart to meditate upon heavenly things and to awake in desire of things eternal. For he knew that the more evidently the vanity of those things which pass away in time is recognized, the more sublimely within is the eye of the mind opened to the state of eternity. Beginning therefore, in this first part of his work, he shows that all things are vain which are under the sun, that is, that whatsoever things are visible are subject to corruption, or liable to mutability. For he shows that neither in heaven, nor under heaven, nor upon the earth, nor in the earth is there anything perpetual which does not suffer vicissitude nor [0134B] pass over into alteration. And therefore that nothing truly is out of all those things which are always without ceasing drawn into that which they are not, and cannot keep stable or uncorrupted what they are.

*The sun riseth, and goeth down, and returneth to his place: and there rising again, he goeth about by the south, and turneth again to the north.* This is to be distinguished by itself. Then follows:

*Surveying all things round about, the spirit goeth forward, and returneth in his circuits.* And this is to be set down by itself. Afterwards follows:

*All the rivers run into the sea, yet the sea doth not overflow: unto the place from whence the rivers come, they return, to flow again.* And this is to be joined together.

Three things therefore he seems to set forth as an argument [0134C] for the mutability of all things: the sun, the spirit, the rivers, that is, fire, air, moisture — so that by the sun fire, by the spirit air, by the rivers moisture may be understood to be signified, as the whole from a part. For above he set the earth as it were an immovable foundation in the middle place. Now he disposes the other three elements, that is, fire, air, water, fluctuating about it with unstable motion, so that in the procreating of mutable things he may show that to these three elements the motion of acting and, in a certain manner, the office of the craftsman has been assigned. But that the earth lies subject, immovable, as a certain matter of all things to be procreated; powerless indeed to act, but only fit that out of it, while the rest work, there may be formed those things which are to be created [0134D]. Through these three elements therefore nature exercises its power in that fourth, which elements, running about that fourth and in it by a wondrous law, and tempering one another, bring forth out of it so many innumerable forms and species of things as are shown by the yearly, the monthly, the daily vicissitudes of the seasons, alternating continually without ceasing in their approach and their withdrawal. Since therefore all things being born which rise out of the earth and receive from the earth their substance and nourishment obtain the nature of vital motion from these three elements, rightly did he place in them both the origin of mutability, and did he express the nature and property of all mutable things, so that in these we may equally see both whence it is that whatever rises under time cannot stand, and by what way that which is not comes forth to be present, or that which [0135A] is returns to not being. The sun riseth, and goeth down, and returneth to his place, and there rising again he goeth about by the south, and turneth again to the north. In few words he has embraced a great matter: concerning which we must say some things according to the time, yet not detain the hearer's mind longer upon such matters, lest we seem, against our own purpose, to be rolled away from the sacred exposition into describing the courses of the stars. All these things indeed have been said for this alone, that the human mind may be the more conveniently roused by the recital of those things which it knows, and that through these it may awake to think upon those things which it does not know: and therefore it ought not to be a weariness to us, nor seem unworthy, to attend diligently to those things which the divine discourse has taken care to recount diligently to us. He shows therefore by what way [0135B] this great luminary, which illuminates all things by its presence, is carried round in this globe of the sensible world with unceasing agitation, and by its approach and withdrawal works the various changes of things. The sun riseth, and goeth down, and returneth to his place. First he shows that it has a circular motion, and always returns to that from which it goes forward, so that, because in a circle no end can be found, its course may be shown to be perpetual. The sun riseth, and goeth down, and returneth to his place. Then he describes the windings of its progression, saying: He goeth about by the south, and turneth again to the north — so that this very thing may belong to the great changing of things, that it does not always in the same manner nor in a straight line direct its course as it goes. The sun riseth, and goeth down. It riseth daily, when emerging [0135C] it is presented to human sight. It goeth down, when descending to the lower parts according to the convexity of its [sic: *circum actionis*] it withdraws itself from our eyes. It returneth to his place; because, its rolling driving it on, it is called back beneath again to its rising. But after its rising, tending to its setting, it is first turned toward the south, because by an oblique course, especially when it tarries in the summer signs, it climbs from the rising to the meridian line. But then, descending to the setting, it is bent again by an oblique progression toward the north. Still another exposition, fitting enough, can be applied to these words: namely, that we take all these things to be said not of the long-continued but of the yearly course of the sun. For the sun riseth to us when, [0135D] entering through the vernal equinox, it rises toward our pole. But it goeth down, when going out through the autumnal equinox it descends to the lower parts. It goeth about by the south, when it tarries in the winter signs. It turneth again to the north, when it is carried round in the summer signs, which are nearer to the northern pole. The sun riseth therefore, and at its rising all things equally rise which revive from its heat. It goeth down, and at once, the vital fire being withdrawn, all things fall which flourished from its presence. It goeth about by the south, that the winter's cold may temper the summer's burnings. It turneth again to the north, that those things which the winter's frost and icy torpor had bound fast may, warmed again by the summer's heats, be quickened. Thou seest therefore how great changes of things one body draws along with it.
