## HOMILY II. *On the proof of the vanity of all things under heaven: through the corruption of the elements, through the generation of things, the succession, and the forgetting of those things which have been.* (cont.)

[0136A]Surveying all things round about, the spirit goeth forward, and returneth in his circuits. If by the spirit we have taken the air, then in fitting order, after he had described the instability of the heavenly element, he shows that the lower things also suffer the same law, saying: Surveying all things round about, the spirit goeth forward. As if he were saying: If the force of nature does not suffer those things which are in heaven to stand, it is necessary that the same condition of mutability govern those things also which are under heaven. Indeed, just as to the sun? so also to the air he attributes an orbicular motion, so that its agitation may be shown to be perpetual. But that he subjoins in the plural: He returneth in his circuits — this can signify that the motion of the air is not always and everywhere the same, but that, since in one place it is stirred more vehemently, in another more moderately, its very fluctuation, [0136B] poured back into itself by a reversed turn, is moved unequally; and does not uniformly come to rest, so that we may not be forced to extend a motion once begun as far as the uttermost, but that the several motions and impulses, made as it were upon themselves; and gradually, the motion languishing over the space, come to rest. But that also which he says, Surveying, is aptly attributed to this element, in that by its mobility it cleanses and scatters all the filth sprinkled or gathered anywhere. Or because, penetrating all things by its subtlety, it pours itself out everywhere. Or else this word is coupled to the preceding sentiment thus: The sun riseth, and goeth down, and returneth to his place; and there rising again he goeth about by the south, and turneth again to the north, surveying all things round about. And here, a distinction being made, then the following sentiment is brought in thus: [0136C] The spirit goeth forward, etc. For the sun by its course surveys all things round about; because it touches all the parts of the world, rising or setting through the diverse signs. For in Cancer, rising and setting, it touches the north. In Capricorn, the south. In Libra and in Aries, it passes through the middle by the rising and the setting. There follows: The spirit goeth forward, and returneth in his circuits. According to the preceding exposition, we can now not incongruously [sic: *incovenienter*] take the spirit to be that fiery power which, proceeding from the sun itself, pours itself out through all things, and, invisibly penetrating the universe, quickens and moves it. Whence the ancients also said that nature is. A craftsman fire, going forward into sensible things to be procreated. For of vital motion, and of sensible quickening, in all things that are born [0136D] the fiery power is the origin: which supplies increase to all things, and, feeding and cherishing them with an invisible nourishment, brings them at length to an invisible substance. And this hidden power of nature, moving and feeding all things, the poet also named a spirit: and his words are well known: *First, the heaven and the lands, and the liquid plains,*
*And the shining globe of the moon, and the Titanian stars,*
*A spirit within doth nourish: and, poured through the limbs,*
*Mind stirs the whole mass, and mingles itself with the great body.*
[n: (Aeneid. VI, 688.)] Although in these words he seems rather to approve that error which asserts that this sensible world is as it were a soul consisting of soul and body, and that its very soul is the spirit moving all things. But [0137A] in whatever way the opinion of those who err be expounded, we with a sound understanding can take the spirit going forward into all things, and returning in his circuits, to be the hidden power of nature, which invisibly nourishes and quickens the universe, and leads each single thing to a certain end and lawful term. This spirit therefore, that is, the hidden power of nature, surveys all things; because, pouring itself out invisibly through all the parts of things, it distributes to each its proper quality according to the capacity of its nature. It goeth forward, and returneth, because it first advances by increase all things which rise in time, and then leads them back, as they grow old, to their origin. For it goeth forward, because it goes out in things being born, and in things growing it proceeds as far as a certain measure. It returneth, because in things growing old, and slipping backward through the failing [0137B] of their nature, it is called back as far as the beginning of its own origin. Thus therefore we rightly take the courses and progressions of individual things to be as it were certain circles and orbs running back into themselves; because all things that have risen in time return at length by their setting thither whence they went out by the beginning of their birth. And each single thing indeed is proved to have, according to its own measure and the time in which it subsists, a course differing from another, that is, greater or less. But all things are known to have, as one origin, so also one end; because all things return by their setting to the earth, which at the beginning of their rising went out of the earth. Just as therefore the sun riseth and goeth down, and returneth to his place, so the spirit goeth forward, and returneth in his circuits. Because [0137C] just as in the body of the sun the fire, going forward locally, is led back to the beginning of its rising, so also naturally in all things that are born, the motion which it raised up through the increase of things, it leads back to its own origin through their failing and setting.

There follows a third clause in which is treated the element of water, which in the order of things is placed third after fire, in procreation is associated as second. For as much as pertains to the disposition and the order of things, fire possesses the first and highest place; air the second, next to fire. Afterwards water claims the third place; in the last and lowest place the earth settles. Among these four, fire and water, by nature indeed most opposed, yet friendly by an even confederation [0137D], are the origin of all things that are born. And therefore if we attend to place or to nature, we discern these two to be separated from one another. If we look to their effect, we find them both nearest to one another and friendly. Therefore concerning the element of water, which affords to things being born the other vital fostering along with fire, as though third after fire and air, or second along with fire, silence was not to be kept among the natures of mutable things. And for that reason he describes this element's circuit also, and its running out into the procreating of sensible things, saying: All the rivers run into the sea, and the sea doth not overflow. And at once he adds the reason why the sea does not overflow when all the rivers have been received into it: namely, because again they return to the place from whence the rivers come, to flow again. For the channels of the waters, through the whole [0138A] body of the earth, draw moisture inwardly and from without after the manner of veins, so that the watering may be evenly poured out into every part. But that this infusion may be able to be continual and perpetual, therefore the course of the waters has been disposed in an orb, and in that perennial circling, so that what is poured in from above may never fail; as much as flows downward in the one part, so much in the other part is called back to its rising through hidden passages. So also the food of the body is carried by a certain circuit, and first descending from the palate into the belly, and then passing over into the privy, and in that very passage of it, as by a certain instilling of nature, nourishment is supplied to what is failing; and when this has been evaporated and emptied out, it is necessary again that recourse be had to the help of eating for the repairing of the weakening limbs. As it were therefore a circle [0138B] of ever-renewed need, running back into its own origin, is drawn; because, while without ceasing what has been taken passes away, it is always necessary that it be taken again, so that it may be able to furnish substance for the supplying of the defect. Such nourishment therefore as the substance of food supplies by assiduous repetition to the body it feeds, altogether such nourishment does the reflowing of the elements pour into the body of the earth for the procreating of its offspring and the quickening of its seeds. This fire, this water, this air cease not to do by continual approach and withdrawal without intermission, so that they may always replenish the things that befall and empty out the things that recede, so that, because the vigor of the past infusion cannot subsist, of necessity the watering for the repairing of fullness may be repeated anew. What confidence, therefore, of perpetual subsistence will the nourishments of things be able to afford, [0138C] when in all things thus always without ceasing both the things that repair pass away, and the things repaired fail? For although a repeated infusion seems to afford some sort of consolation to this defect, yet it is a great misery always to receive for need, and never to be able to lack the need of receiving. Whence it is manifest that all transitory and falling things are vain, which continually grow empty of the solidity of their own state, either by passing away or by failing. A great spectacle therefore has here been prepared for holy minds, which know how to draw from visible things the knowledge of invisible ones. Which know how to say to their Creator: *Thou hast made all things in wisdom, O Lord [n: (Psal. CIII)]*. *Thou hast delighted me, O Lord, in thy making, and in the works of thy hands [0138D] I shall rejoice. How magnified are thy works, O Lord, exceeding deep are thy thoughts become [n: (Psal. XCI)]*.
