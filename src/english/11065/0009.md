## HOMILY II. *On the proof of the vanity of all things under heaven: through the corruption of the elements, through the generation of things, the succession, and the forgetting of those things which have been.* (cont.)

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ For behold how all things transitory and subject to vanity are borne about in a circuit. ⟦s2⟧ And we know that a circle has no end. ⟦s3⟧ The things therefore which run in a circuit do indeed run, but they never arrive at an end. ⟦s4⟧ What rest, then, can be hoped for, where there can be no standing still? ⟦s5⟧ For where there is perpetual running, there is no standing. ⟦s6⟧ But where the way is a circuit, there is no place where the course could surely have an end. ⟦s7⟧ The things therefore which run in a circuit always run and never come to a standing. ⟦s8⟧ They always pass away, and never subsist. ⟦s9⟧ They are always being ended, and cannot find an end. ⟦s10⟧ When they have gone by, they are yet to come; when they have come upon us, [0139A] they do not subsist. ⟦s11⟧ This is the way of all mutable things, and the way of all who love mutable things and who follow mutable things. ⟦s12⟧ For of the wicked also it is said: *The wicked walk in a circuit [n: (Psal. XI)]*. ⟦s13⟧ And again: *The head of their circuit [n: (Psal. CXXXIX)]*, *and make them as a wheel, O Lord [n: (Psal. LXXXII)]*. ⟦s14⟧ The head too of all the wicked bears witness of himself, saying: *I have gone round about the earth, and walked through it [n: (Job I)]*. ⟦s15⟧ And of him the Apostle says: *He goeth about as a roaring lion, seeking whom he may devour [n: (I Petr. V)]*. ⟦s16⟧ You see therefore how the perverse always love the circuit, and do not withdraw from the circuit. ⟦s17⟧ Therefore the things which are perversely loved by the perverse, these themselves also all run in a circuit, for a testimony to them that they too follow a circuit. ⟦s18⟧ On the contrary the lover of eternal things says: *My foot hath stood in the straight way* [0139B] [n: (Psal. XXVII)]. ⟦s19⟧ And again: *Direct me in the path of thy commandments, for the same have I desired [n: (Psal. CXVIII)]*. ⟦s20⟧ And again: *The path of the just is straight [n: (Isai. XXVI)]*. ⟦s21⟧ And elsewhere once more: *They shall go,* he says, *straight, the things sent forth; and the Lord our God shall lead them to a sure place [n: (Sap. V)]*. ⟦s22⟧ O straight way, O circuit, whither dost thou lead. ⟦s23⟧ And thou, whither dost thou lead? ⟦s24⟧ Thou leadest, and leadest through. ⟦s25⟧ But thou leadest, and leadest astray; for those whom thou leadest, thou destroyest, thou dost not lead through. ⟦s26⟧ O circuit, how dost thou enwrap, how dost thou embrace thy followers? ⟦s27⟧ To those who run through thee thou grantest neither a going out from thee, nor an arrival within thee. ⟦s28⟧ Therefore all things transitory and falling are rolled through circuits while they pass away, so that we may not seek in them the good of eternity — things which we always behold both so to arrive that they pass away, and so to pass away that they do not subsist. ⟦s29⟧ Let us see [0139C] therefore yet further these circuits of vanity. ⟦s30⟧ For the wisdom of God, as has already been said, has wonderfully provided this: that the motion of all things should be driven round in a circle, so that bodily nature — which by flowing away might at some time have felt failure — always by running back into its own origin, while without interruption it receives what it has poured out, might pour out without failure what it receives, and that there might be a failing without failure; nor might that ever be wanting which is able to fail, so that it may always be failing. ⟦s31⟧ Whence in one and the same thing, both that which it is, is wretched, and that which has been made is wonderful, because in the same work both the matter is found frail, and the craftsman's design is to be admired. ⟦s32⟧ And corruptible nature indeed persuades to contempt of the world, but the mind sinks down in wonder at the contemplation of the design. [0139D] To the beholding therefore of this design, after the course of mutable things has been unfolded, our Ecclesiastes turns himself, that in it he may exercise the mind's gaze through contemplation; because knowledge is not able sufficiently to comprehend it, he is eager to venerate it with worthy wonder. ⟦s33⟧ For, since he has unfolded the mutability of all things in three kinds of things — fire, air and water — according to the motion that moves, he shows thereafter how inexplicable that same mutability is according to the motion which is moved, through each several thing that rises and sets in the universe.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *All things are hard: man cannot explain them by speech: the eye is not sated with seeing, nor is the ear filled with hearing.* ⟦s2⟧ When any thing whatever is discerned on its surface, not yet is either its hidden cause or [0140A] its nature penetrated. ⟦s3⟧ For that which is, is seen; but of what sort that is which is seen, or what quality it keeps in the hidden place (whither bodily sense cannot come); why also it is so as it is seen, or whence it is so, or to what end it is — what man (I will not say in all things, but not even in any one thing whatever) is able to comprehend to the full? ⟦s4⟧ In how great darkness therefore is man enwrapped; by how great a blindness of ignorance is he hemmed in, whose sense can scarcely reach even so much as the surface of a few things? ⟦s5⟧ And even if he should discern all things that are according to their outward appearance, he would still not penetrate the hidden power of things and their invisible nature. ⟦s6⟧ Therefore the totality of things is in both ways incomprehensible to man: namely, both according to outward appearance, and according to inward [0140B] quality. ⟦s7⟧ But some individual things come outwardly in part by their appearance to the sense, others are hidden altogether — either because they are remote in place, or because, though present in place, they exceed by their own subtlety the slowness of human sense. ⟦s8⟧ Scarcely therefore does human sense suffice to attain some few things among things; but of the whole it comprehends nothing perfectly as it is. ⟦s9⟧ Therefore all things are hard: hard according to the totality, namely, in that they cannot be attained, and hard according to each several thing, because they can be fully comprehended by no sense nor understanding. ⟦s10⟧ And if they cannot be perfectly searched out by reason, much less can they be explained by speech. ⟦s11⟧ For this very thing is more unspeakable than all things in things: that in so great a confusion of mutability nowhere is an evident and manifest [0140C] design lacking to one who considers well; that even that which in things is not good is so well disposed, that in a certain manner the corruption of individual things is the beauty of the whole. ⟦s12⟧ Therefore human sense, sinking down in the consideration of these things, is sweetly refreshed out of its own failure; because in that which displeases according to corruption, in the most ordered beauty of the whole, even corruption itself is beautiful and pleases. ⟦s13⟧ Thus in a wondrous and unspeakable manner the Founder, in his creature, both punishes malice through the corruption of things and at once delights nature through beauty, so that man may recognize in his punishment what he has deserved through fault, and in his delight what he has lost. ⟦s14⟧ For how great an affluence of sweetness do we believe to be in the Creator of things, if [0140D] we find so wondrous a pleasantness in the beauty of creatures? ⟦s15⟧ Therefore, when he had spoken of the failure of human understanding in so manifold and perplexed and various a depth of things, he at once adds the delightfulness of nature in the beauty of those same things.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ The eye is not sated with seeing, nor is the ear filled with hearing. ⟦s2⟧ Since all the bodily senses find in created things their own delights, and each of them as it were certain proper dainties prepared for it — sight, namely, appearance; hearing, the sweetness of melody; smell, odor; taste, savor; and touch, smoothness. ⟦s3⟧ All the rest indeed serve bodily necessity, or even pleasure. ⟦s4⟧ But these two senses, that is sight and hearing, draw their nourishment rather toward spiritual delightfulness. ⟦s5⟧ Whence their refreshment also has no draining off of dregs, [0141A] because together with the perceiving of sweetness they draw no mass of corruption. ⟦s6⟧ For which reason their refreshment does not arrive at a satiety of sweetness; because no difficulty running through delays their appetite from perceiving sweetness, nor does any ensuing trouble restrain it. ⟦s7⟧ This nourishment therefore, pure and unmixed, without all bodily bulk, passes wholly into the refreshment of the soul; because although it flows forth from bodily appearance and passes through the sense of the body, it cannot nevertheless be retained by the body, since it has nothing bodily, nor does it bring in anything corpulent. ⟦s8⟧ For this reason therefore sight and hearing, as being more excellent than the rest and having nothing of depressing weight, have obtained a loftier seat in the body, so that they may freely and without obstacle flow out to their delights, or [0141B] admit into themselves without difficulty those things that flow in. ⟦s9⟧ There are, to be sure, two things which enter to the mind from the appearance of things through sight. ⟦s10⟧ And two likewise which enter in through hearing from the perceiving of voices. ⟦s11⟧ For through sight there comes from things to the mind's knowledge substance and form. ⟦s12⟧ From voices through hearing, signification and melody. ⟦s13⟧ And substance from things works the same in the mind through sight as signification from voices does through hearing. ⟦s14⟧ And likewise form from things does the same in the mind through sight as melody from voices does through hearing. ⟦s15⟧ For there are two things by which the whole nature of the rational soul is ordered, namely knowledge and affection, that is, wisdom and love. ⟦s16⟧ And if the soul perfectly obtains these two and lawfully orders them, it is blessed. ⟦s17⟧ But if either it falls short in obtaining them so far as nature requires, [0141C] or perverts them, once obtained, against nature, then in that part, without doubt, in which it suffers either the failure or the confusion of them, it must needs be made wretched. ⟦s18⟧ The whole substance therefore of the rational soul is governed by these two, that is by knowledge and affection, so that through wisdom it may find truth, and through love may embrace virtue. ⟦s19⟧ Therefore, that outward things also might serve the rational soul for the sake of blessedness, these two instruments of the senses were placed in the human body, so that through them the notions of visible things might enter in to the soul, and might produce in it wisdom or virtue, if it were not there at all, or increase it, if it were there in less measure. ⟦s20⟧ Therefore the essence of things entering in to the soul through sight, and the signification of voices through hearing, beget knowledge. ⟦s21⟧ But the form of things entering in through sight, [0141D] and the melody of voices through hearing, kindle the mind to delightfulness. ⟦s22⟧ For as often as outwardly either sight is affected by the appearance of things, or hearing is soothed by the sweetness of voices, the mind awakes within with wondrous affections, answering to that by which it feels itself touched outwardly. ⟦s23⟧ And it comes to pass sometimes that, through the sweetness which it draws by bodily sense, it returns to the remembrance of invisible goods and, admonished in a certain manner from the likeness, begins to long with unspeakable desire for that whose shadow and image, as it were, it feels itself to have perceived in bodily affection.
