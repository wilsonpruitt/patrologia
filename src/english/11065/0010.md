## HOMILY II. *On the proof of the vanity of all things under heaven: through the corruption of the elements, through the generation of things, the succession, and the forgetting of those things which have been.* (cont.)

Let Ecclesiastes therefore say: The eye is not sated with seeing, nor is the ear filled with hearing. Because through these senses the beauty of visible things, while it affects the mind with delight in [0142A] the contemplation of itself, kindles in it an unspeakable longing for invisible goods. Which longing, namely, thirsting after an ineffable sweetness, can rather be provoked than filled out by the bodily sight or hearing by which it is aroused, however great a delightfulness they may bring; and by human speech, however profuse or eloquent it may have been — though it is scarcely told even thinly — it is never perfectly explained. For from the appearance of visible things the rational mind conceives only, as it were, certain seeds of joys; but as desire soon grows out into immensity, it travails and does not contain itself in so great a delight. Let no one therefore think the sight of visible things harmful to chaste minds; because, if to see the works of God were altogether harmful, the use of seeing would by no means [0142B] have been created by him. For the work of God is as it were his word, through which he speaks to us, and the eyes themselves are as it were instruments by which the words of God are perceived through contemplation. Just as therefore the ear is an instrument for perceiving the word of man, [d: so the eye is an instrument for perceiving the word of man,] so the eye is an instrument for perceiving the word of the Creator. Therefore fittingly in the human body both the eyes are set in front and the ears placed at the side, as though by this it were signified to us that our intention ought to be directed secondarily to our neighbor, principally to God. Rightly therefore, that it might be shown how from the consideration of visible things the desire of eternal things is born in the mind, the eyes and ears alone are mentioned. Because by these alone, well nigh, does the way of wisdom [0142C] and of virtue lie open to the mind, since, while beyond the other senses they perceive the beauty of visible things unmixed, they alone more purely kindle within the love of invisible goods. All things are hard: man cannot explain them by speech.

The eye is not sated with seeing, nor is the ear filled with hearing. It is a marvel. The human heart does not suffice for the whole world, and the whole world does not suffice for the human heart. Why does the human heart not suffice for the whole world? Because all things are hard, and man cannot explain them by speech. Why does the whole world not suffice for the human heart? Because the eye is not sated with seeing, nor is the ear filled with hearing. Therefore knowledge sinks down, affection transcends. Knowledge stretches itself as far as it can, and cannot take in the whole; but love drinks up the whole, [0142D] and still cannot be satisfied. Why is this? Because charity rises high above knowledge, nor does the human heart say: It is enough — until it has come to him, and has found him by whom it was made that it might be, and for whom it was made that in him it might be blessed. Every delightfulness, every pleasantness, every beauty of created things can affect the human heart; satisfy it they cannot, save that sweetness alone for which it was made. For the appearances of visible things are only, as it were, certain veins through which the invisible beauty, manifesting itself, flows out even to us. And therefore, when these naturally provoke our sense and affection toward themselves, they do not indeed fill up desire, but goad it to seek out the appearance of the Founder and to long for his beauty. [0143A] Therefore all these things which are seen outwardly were made for provoking human affection, not for satisfying it, so that, aroused by them, it may rise up and, increased, may transcend them. Wherefore the eye is not sated with seeing, nor is the ear filled with hearing. For that which we behold made beautiful does indeed delight, but it does not suffice, unless we find him who made it.

Or what he says can be understood thus: The eye is not sated with seeing, nor is the ear filled with hearing. For because he had set down before that all things are hard, and that man cannot explain them by speech — lest perhaps it should be thought that they could be comprehended even by knowledge, he added: The eye is not sated with seeing, nor is the ear filled with hearing. As if he were to say: It is no marvel if man cannot explain by speech the difficulty [0143B] [sic: *cultatem*] of things, who cannot even by sense take in or search out their depth. For whether he seek through himself the things to be found, or hear from another the things already found, everything that he can take in is beneath the satiety of fullness. For if he begins to contemplate through himself, the eye is not sated with seeing. If he seeks to be taught by another, the ear is not filled with hearing. For the narrownesses of the bodily instruments, if they pour themselves outward, do not suffice for the depth or the immensity of things to be unfolded. But if they pour themselves inward, they cannot fill the capacity of the human heart. For because the heart of man, blinded by the darkness of sin, has not an eye within by which it may fully see the light of truth, nor an ear by which it may perceive the word of God sounding inwardly, it is compelled to open bodily eyes and ears for the watering of the dryness [0143C] of its ignorance, that outwardly it may draw at least some drop of truth in the appearance of things, whereby its thirst, though it cannot be altogether quenched, may at least a little be tempered. For this is what the Lord reproaches to his people through the prophet, saying: *My people have done two sins. They have forsaken me, the fountain of living water, and have digged for themselves cisterns broken apart, that cannot hold waters [n: (Jer. II)]*. For we know that a cistern is dug for this reason, that water gathered from without may flow down into it, and be drawn from it again to pass over into the uses of men. But because this has no living vein, however great it may seem and overflowing with a gathering of waters, it can at some time be drained out [0143D] and dried up; because, when that which is poured in from elsewhere has been taken away and consumed, nothing of its own is left over to it whence it might be repaired. But the fountain which has a living vein, even if it is small, yet cannot altogether fail, nor does it ever feel a failure of its own outpouring, since to it an increase from its own comes always without failure. The bodily eyes and ears therefore, although they are the chief instruments by which the way is opened for the disciplines to the mind, yet, because the notions of visible things do not come from within from the fountain of truth, they lead them down to the heart of man through the narrow conduits of their own capacity and as it were like waters gathered here and there into a cistern. And because in the very things which they perceive they are able neither to comprehend the totality nor to penetrate the innermost parts, rightly is it now said: The eye is not satisfied [0144A] with seeing, nor is the ear filled with hearing. Because the human mind, whether by living or by hearing, even if it perceives something, however little, of the truth, is nevertheless driven far from the fullness thereof by the condition of its own infirmity. It is no marvel therefore if the dryness of human speech does not suffice to explain the difficulty of things, since human sense also sinks down in the consideration of it. And therefore all things are hard, and man cannot explain them by speech: the eye is not sated with seeing, nor is the ear filled with hearing.

*What is it that hath been? The very thing that is to be. What is it that hath been done? The very thing that is to be done. Nothing new under the sun; nor is any man able to say: Behold, this is new. For it hath already gone before in the ages which were before us.* Perhaps someone may reckon that that error is [0144B] here confirmed by which the philosophers of the Gentiles, concerning the revolution of the ages and the return of all things into the selfsame, attempted with marvelous madness to establish the eternity of times. For they said that the series of mutable things had run from eternity in the same order in which it is now beheld, and would run without end, by the same law, the world in its totality having neither beginning nor end, through certain ages succeeding one another. And that the great age consists of fifteen thousand years (which they call the year); and that when it is completed, all the heavenly bodies being called back to their own place whence each several one had gone forth from the beginning of the age, and again beginning their motion into the opening of another age by a like plan and manner, and likewise moving the nature subject to them by a like plan — that again absolutely the same [0144C] things all rise up anew, in the order in which they had gone before in the preceding ages, in the same order nonetheless, with the same essence, the same form, in altogether the same places and times, and accomplish their own course and a fate like to the former and the very same; that they suffer also the same law and undergo the same fortune, so that it befalls that the same men are born, that the same men likewise beget the same sons, live by the same fortune, die by the same lot: the same outcome of things, absolutely the same quality, and the same state and course of all things which the past times of the earlier age had held. And that such a revolution and repetition of all things into the selfsame always has been and always shall be without end, so that, just as by [0144D] the return of one star through each several year all these things are then renewed to their former state, so by the return of all the stars and of the whole of nature to their rising, through each several age and the great years, all things are restored to their first condition. But how great this error is, manifest reason will easily prove. For that times cannot be eternal is most evidently proved by this: that all time had a beginning; and it stands without contradiction that everything which had a beginning at some time was not. Further, all past time was at some time present. But all time which was at some time present, before it was present, was not yet. Otherwise, if it was always present, it was no longer time, but eternity. If therefore all past time at some time was [0145A] not, there was [a time] when there was no time. And so times could not be eternal; but before times there was an eternity without time, which times neither increased when they began, nor will they consume when they shall be ended. It is clear therefore that the assertion of those is false who have proclaimed the eternity of the ages, and have testified that the beginning of mutability was without beginning. But lest perhaps in this place an error of this kind should seem to be confirmed, when it is said: Nothing new under the sun; nor is any man able to say: Behold, this is new.
