## HOMILY II. *On the proof of the vanity of all things under heaven: through the corruption of the elements, through the generation of things, the succession, and the forgetting of those things which have been.* (cont.)

*There is no remembrance of former things; nor indeed shall there be any recollection of those which are to be hereafter, with them that [0145B] shall be in the last time.* For if the former things and the last were absolutely the same as the present, he would by no means call men now present other than the former and other than those to come in the last time; nor would he say, with them that are to be after us in the last time, but he would say, with us who are to be in the last time. Unless perhaps someone should say that the first and the last are to be referred to the beginning and the end of the same age; otherwise those are neither first nor last who have been without beginning and are to be without end. But that the first and the last should be referred to the beginning and the end of the same age is forbidden by what he says: It hath already gone before in the ages which were before us. Because, while he speaks of some ages of those going before and others of those coming after, he shows that he has by no means meant the first and the last of the same age. [0145C] Therefore, since that sentiment shrinks back not only from Catholic truth but from reason as well, let us refer this saying to the likeness of things, which have been so established in their kinds from the beginning that each several one extends its offspring according to the likeness and the first form of its own origin, and there is nothing which exceeds the limit of the first disposition in its own kind. Thus in the very elements of the world, thus in those things which have been or are procreated out of them, nature keeps in all things the first disposition, so that nothing can be found under the sun other than the first, that is, diverse or unlike. As, for example, from the beginning heaven above, and earth below. From the beginning the lights in heaven — sun, moon and stars; birds in the air, fishes in the water, beasts on the earth. From the beginning flight for birds, swimming [0145D] for fishes, walking for men, and for certain beasts crawling, and dragging for serpents. And all these things, as they were, so they are, so they remain. And if at any time perchance, by some miracle happening beside the accustomed course of nature, they become other than they always are, yet they do not become so in the whole, nor do they always become so, so that that first establishment, although it may sometimes seem to be interrupted, may never nevertheless seem to be dissolved. Therefore the wisdom of God rules this confusion also. And what is confused to us is not so to him. And therefore nothing is new under the sun; because that which passes away in time has been ordained from eternity by him who is above the sun. And therefore each several thing, because they cannot stand in themselves by immutability, yet keep their standing in their own kind [0146A] through likeness, so that although individual things cannot always be what they are, yet the whole are never found to be other than the individuals. Therefore nothing new under the sun. How much more above the sun? For therefore also nothing is new under the sun, because that which is above the sun is eternal. Under the sun and moon there is nothing new; nor yet is there anything eternal. For nothing under the sun is eternal, because everything that is under the sun passes away in itself; yet it is not new, because it subsists in the likeness of its own kind. Therefore under the sun nothing is new, and nothing eternal. For because it hath gone before in the ages which were before us, therefore it is not new; and because there is no remembrance of things past, therefore it is not eternal. And therefore, because everything that comes passes away and does not subsist, nor does anything remain [0146B] under the sun, the eye is not sated with seeing, nor is the ear filled with hearing; because, while it enters it slips away, and everything flees while it is thought to be held; and the sentiment is fulfilled: Vanity of vanities, and all is vanity. What then, since under the sun the eye is not sated with seeing, nor the ear filled with hearing? Let it go above the sun, where not only is there nothing new, but nothing transitory either; where there is both the remembrance of former things and the presence of things to come — nay rather, where all things past and to come are present; because nothing there is either past or future, where everything that is, is present, and everything that is, is there. There therefore both the eye can be sated with seeing and the ear filled with hearing: where a consummate and full perfection is perceived, and, the fullness having been perceived, eternity is not emptied out. So therefore this whole sentiment, by which it is said that under the sun there is neither anything new [0146C] nor anything eternal, seems to look to this: that man may recognize what he ought either to flee or to follow. For in this, that nothing under the sun is new, the eternal disposition is shown. But in this, that nothing under the sun can be abiding, it is demonstrated that in those things which are under the sun eternity is indeed signified, yet is not. In those very things therefore which are under the sun we recognize at once both: in that nothing is eternal, that what they are must be fled; and in that nothing is new, that what they are not must be followed. Wherefore let him run back to the highest sentiment, that, because in those things which are under the sun the eyes cannot be sated nor the ears filled by knowledge — since they do not comprehend the whole that is — nor can they be by desire, because, even if they should comprehend, the whole that is does not suffice, [0146D] nor does that which is present remain as long as they are able. They know that everything which is under the sun is vanity, and that man can have nothing more out of all his labor wherewith he labors under the sun.

Let him say then: All things are hard, so as to be comprehended by human sense — things which can neither be numbered in their multitude, nor comprehended in their quantity, nor searched out in their depth. And therefore it is no marvel if speech fails where understanding sinks down; and on this account man cannot explain them by speech, so as to render an account of each several thing, because he does not know all things: For the eye is not sated with seeing, when it considers by itself; nor is the ear filled with hearing, when it is taught by another — so that either the intention going out to the search of things might comprehend the whole, as much as is without, [0147A] or knowledge entering in might fill up the whole, as much as is within. Therefore the eye and the ear suffice neither for the whole world, so as to take in the whole, nor for the whole mind, so as to fill the whole. And yet through those things which are without they are admonished to go forward thither where life is, where they may find the whole and take in the whole, because all things *in him were life, and the life was the light of men [n: (Joan. I)]*. Thither therefore let them go forward, where at once they may find the whole and take in the whole and possess the whole, where in the eternal disposition those things have already been made which are to be in time. For what is it that hath been? For the very same, and not another, is to be in time than that which was before times, in the eternal disposition of God. Nor is any other mutable thing unfolded thereafter in the order of things than that which was ever fixed and abiding [0147B] in that ratified and unvarying divine disposition. And that it may manifestly appear that the subsequent effect of things accords with providence, therefore also in the very series of things the future does not disaccord with the past. For what is it that hath been done? The very thing that is to be done. Therefore what has always been in providence is at some time to be in fact; and what has already been done in fact is still to be done through likeness. But it seems to me that this ought not to be passed over, that he does not say. What is it that is to be, that he might then subjoin. The very thing that hath been — since past things are more certain than things to come, and because, even if they are not, yet they were at some time and were seen. But things to come never were.

And therefore that which is less certain would seem [0147C] rather to be put in the question; but in the answer, that which is agreed to be more known. But it is to be noted that the effect of things, according to cause, is later than providence; in our knowledge, earlier. Because until we behold a thing in its own act, we are utterly ignorant of what has been disposed concerning it in the eternal providence. And therefore rightly, when he asked, saying: What is it that hath been? (because providence in itself is hidden) he at once sends his hearers to the act of things for the understanding of it, saying: The very thing that is to be; because we cannot know what goes before in foreknowledge, until we have seen what follows after in the effect of things. But in the things themselves also we shall gather the quality of past things which we have not seen from the comparison of things to come [0147D] when they shall have come upon us. Let him say then: What is it that hath been? The very thing that is to be. Because, as has been said, that which goes before in providence — this same and not another follows after in the effect of things. And thereafter: What is it that hath been done? The very thing that is to be done. Because the same nature of things in each several kind has exhibited in act, in things past, the same likeness which it will bring forth they shall have come upon in things to come. And therefore there is nothing new in the kinds of things under the sun whose likeness has not gone before. Nor is any man able to say: This is new — as far as concerns the likeness of its own kind. For it hath already gone before in the ages which were before us, in its own kind, of which it is. And yet all things flow, nor does anything remain under the sun. And the things which are fixed in the disposition of him who orders [0148A] them, passing away in themselves, withdraw at once both from our presence and from our memory. For first they are withdrawn from our presence, so that they do not subsist by appearance; then they are also blotted out from memory by forgetfulness, so that they do not subsist even by recollection. And lest perchance the passing of things past should not suffice to draw the hard hearts of men to contempt of visible things, if, when those had been taken away, they who are present could have had confidence about their own standing. Therefore, after he said in recalling their passing: There is no remembrance of former things, he at once demonstrates the destruction of these also, saying: But neither of those who are to be after us. For if they are to be after us, they are surely others: it is plain that we who now subsist, when those shall have come who are to be after us, and we too taken away by the same [0148B] lot, shall then by no means come even into the recollection of the living. What then does it profit us that we have succeeded into this life those who were taken away by death, since we too, going down when our own time shall have come, shall leave other successors behind us. If therefore we wish to know of what sort we shall be with those who are to be after us, let us consider of what sort they now are with us who, long since taken away from this life, have gone before us. And that every hope of present things may be taken away from wicked minds, those also who are to be after us are said not to be about to remain, nor to be about to come into recollection with their own posterity — lest even this should come as a consolation to depraved minds, if he who cannot stand in himself might at least subsist in his heir or successor. Therefore [0148C] he shows one and the same destruction of all things past, present, and to come. There is no remembrance of former things; nor shall there be any recollection of those who are to be after us, with them that shall be in the last time — that he may subject all things to vanity, and turn the human mind from the love of those things which are seen under the sun to the desire of eternal things.
