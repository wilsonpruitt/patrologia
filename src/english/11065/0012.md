## HOMILY II. *On the proof of the vanity of all things under heaven: through the corruption of the elements, through the generation of things, the succession, and the forgetting of those things which have been.* (cont.)

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But if anyone should not be willing to be content with the simple exposition, he has something else which he may understand in these words that have been expounded above, fittingly enough and perhaps more manifestly. ⟦s2⟧ For because he had said that neither is the eye sated with seeing nor the ear filled with hearing, he immediately adds the cause why neither can sight fill the eye nor hearing the ear: namely, because [0148D] the appearances of transitory things, coming indeed through the sense, arouse longing, but, swiftly fleeing away, cheat desire by their passing. ⟦s3⟧ For concerning this passing he subjoins the sentiment, saying: What is it that hath been? ⟦s4⟧ It already is not: for it has passed away, and now is not. ⟦s5⟧ The very same shall that also be which is to be: for it too, when it shall have come, will pass through, and when it shall have passed through, it will be no more. ⟦s6⟧ What is it that hath been done? ⟦s7⟧ That too has already passed through, and is not itself. ⟦s8⟧ Therefore the same shall that also be which is to be done; because it too, when it shall have been done, will pass through, and will be no more. ⟦s9⟧ But it can be asked why he mentioned past things only and things to come, when he rather intends to persuade to contempt of present things? ⟦s10⟧ But this very thing pertains to a great contempt of those things which [0149A] are seen: that he willed to mention in their number only those things which alone the sense of the flesh comprehends as present, as though he saw that those things alone are not, which alone seem to be — so that he cannot even say what it is that is. ⟦s11⟧ That which is, while it is, is well nigh nothing: for the moments of present things flee away with so hasty a course and so swift a slipping by that they cannot even wait for the words of those who speak about them. ⟦s12⟧ For even if it can sometimes be rightly said by the questioner, according to something, "it is," yet it is always more truly said by the answerer, "it was, and is not." ⟦s13⟧ Thus the question itself seeks an answer which as yet is not. ⟦s14⟧ But the answer, when it comes, does not find the question, which now is not. ⟦s15⟧ In a true consideration therefore he saw that this alone, as it were, is not, which alone seems to be. ⟦s16⟧ For since it at once both [0149B] begins to be out of that which is not yet, and ceases to be into that which now is not, that which is, is well nigh nothing. ⟦s17⟧ And therefore by no means ask: what is it that is? ⟦s17⟧ Because that very thing of the questioner, to the answerer however near at hand, has already been, and is not. ⟦s18⟧ Rightly therefore is it said: What is it that hath been? ⟦s19⟧ The very thing that is to be. ⟦s20⟧ What is it that hath been done? ⟦s21⟧ The very thing that is to be done. ⟦s22⟧ As if he were to say: From the consideration of things past weigh what you ought to expect concerning the things that come after; for both are the same. ⟦s23⟧ Both that which hath been, and that which is to be. ⟦s24⟧ That which hath been done, and that which is to be done. ⟦s25⟧ It is the same, not by essence, but by a like nature. ⟦s26⟧ For that which hath been (as far as concerns things) and that which hath been done (as far as concerns actions), both have passed through. ⟦s27⟧ And there is not now even that which hath been [0149C] done. ⟦s28⟧ Likewise that which is to be among things, and that which is to be done among actions, the whole will pass through, and there will be nothing perpetual under the sun. ⟦s29⟧ Whence is this? ⟦s30⟧ Because the condition of things past, and of those which are to be, is the same, and the nature alike. ⟦s31⟧ For nothing is new under the sun, nor is any man able to say: Behold, this is new. ⟦s32⟧ For it hath already gone before in the ages which were before us. ⟦s33⟧ And because the condition is alike, therefore as things past, so also things to come. ⟦s34⟧ And of what sort, thinkest thou, are those things past themselves? ⟦s35⟧ Hear of what sort: There is, he says, no remembrance of former things. ⟦s36⟧ Therefore not only has their presence been taken away by destruction, but their memory too has been blotted out by forgetfulness. ⟦s37⟧ A great ruin. ⟦s38⟧ Of such sort are also those things which are to be. ⟦s39⟧ For neither of those who are to be [0149D] after us shall there be any recollection with them that shall be in the last time. ⟦s40⟧ And the sentiment is true: Vanity of vanities, vanity of vanities, and all is vanity.
