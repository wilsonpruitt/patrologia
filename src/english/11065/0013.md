## HOMILY III. *How Ecclesiastes proves through his own works that all the works of men are vain, with an epilogue of the things aforesaid.*

*I Ecclesiastes was king of Israel in Jerusalem: and I proposed in my mind to seek and to search out wisely concerning all things that are done under the sun.* To search out the words of the wise and their riddles, and to draw forth to the taste that sweetness of wisdom which is hidden within, and to bring its beauty forth into the light — who is able? A scribe learned in the kingdom of heaven, *who bringeth forth out of his treasure new things and old [n: (Matth. XIII)]*, if he should come to us — behold, he who God gave wisdom [0150A] and understanding to lay it open and to make it manifest, that men may know that in it is life. But what are we? Darkness before light, that we should think to comprehend what God has hidden from the eyes of men, to reveal the secret of his wisdom to his friends. What do we do? Therefore we shall not meditate on his commandments, nor shall we search out her paths, and our heart will be able to rest until it find her. Only let us pursue her with these very words; and if perhaps the finding be deferred, let the search for her meanwhile feed us above all things; if we cannot [teach] her as found, yet let us teach her as sought. Behold, our Ecclesiastes, disputing thus far concerning the vanity of mutable things, has embraced in a brief discourse so many things that in those words of his this appears wonderful to us: in what manner [0150B] the whole, and the whole concerning the whole, has been so said that for brevity indeed nothing less, but for evidence nothing more, seems needful to be said. But wisdom did this, which is whole in the whole and whole in each several thing. Nor is she, whole in individual things, contracted; nor is she, whole in the whole, expanded; but she is as great in individual things as in the whole, and in the whole such as she is whole in individual things, because neither can unity be less than itself, nor immensity greater. That same wisdom therefore has expressed in this brief body of discourse the whole image of the universe, that we, being small, may see the great in the small, since in itself we cannot [see] the whole. And we have seen there the whole world, and have recognized that truly there is nothing of all things that abides, but that all things which are under the sun flow and pass through, and that the sentiment stands true, that all is vanity. Now therefore, [0150C] after he has demonstrated to us of what sort those things are which were made along with us, he passes on to teach us what we ought to think or to expect concerning those things which are done by us. Because if that also which God made is subject to vanity, it cannot be doubted that what man, who is vanity, makes is altogether vain, and much more vain. This is what he now enters upon to demonstrate: namely, that all the works of men which are done under the sun are vain, and that man has nothing more out of all his labor wherewith he labors under the sun. But that he may win credence for his assertion, he sets himself forth as an example of all the things he is about to say, asserting that he has learned by trial all that he speaks and has proved it true. And therefore he sets forth his own person as of such a kind [0150D] that it may not seem incredible that he could have had trial of all these things. Therefore he demonstrates his power and dignity: king of Israel in Jerusalem. He shows his wisdom also: And I went before in wisdom all who were before me in Jerusalem. He enumerates his riches: houses, vineyards, gardens, orchards, fishponds, menservants, maidservants, herds, flocks, gold, silver. Lastly, lest anything should be thought to have been lacking, he adds luxury also and pleasure: singing men, singing women, and the delights of the sons of men; and all things, he says, which my eyes desired I denied them not, nor did I keep back my heart from enjoying every pleasure of its own in those things which I had prepared. And he testifies that in all these he ran through every trial of things, and found nothing beyond vanity and [0151A] affliction of spirit and superfluous care in all things that are done under the sun. But what those things are which are done under the sun has already been said above. But that the discourse may pass over more suitably to those things which are to be said, we shall now briefly and summarily repeat what has been said.

For we have shown that by the sun time is signified, for this reason: because chiefly by the sun is all the mutability and vicissitude of times distinguished, so that the same is understood by that which is said, under the sun, as if it had been said, under time. Again, that it might appear more evidently what those things were which, done under time, the author reproves of vanity, we distinguished that some things were made with time, others made in time. Likewise of those which were made in time, [0151B] some were made in time, not under time; others in time and under time. And those indeed which were made under time are more subject to time and liable to mutability, because they began in time and will likewise be ended in time; and therefore, lest they should extend themselves in any degree to eternity, they are in a certain manner shut in by time, since their time both went before their beginning and follows after their end. Yet, lest vanity should altogether rule in these, and truth should not find under the sun where to lay its head [n: (Matth. VIII)], we have excepted certain of those things which are done under time; and if we do not altogether take vanity away from them, yet we forbid them to serve vanity. For of those things which are done under time, some are done for time, others are done for eternity. And those which are done under time for time [0151C] are vain in act and in fruit. But those which are done under time for eternity are in a certain manner vain in act, but in fruit are not; because, even if they pass away in the work, yet they remain in the recompense. Wherefore such things, even if they have not fully escaped vanity, yet serve not vanity but truth; nor is that which they are altogether vain in them, because that for which they are is not vanity. But those which are done under time and for time are altogether subject to vanity; because both that which is in them is vain, and that which is from them is likewise vanity. And these are the things of which we treat at present. In which, under the sun, nothing beyond vanity and affliction of spirit — either when they are being done, or when they have been done — is found by those who love transitory things.
