## HOMILY IV. *On the vanity of things, and the worst occupation of men.*

[0151D]

And yet in these things vanity itself rises up to increase as though by certain steps, and corruption, growing out as it were from a little thing, increased by degrees, always proceeds to the worse. For in them is found the first origin of vanity, curiosity; then cupidity; lastly pleasure. Among which the first indeed pertains to the concupiscence of the eyes, the second to the ambition of the world, the third to the concupiscence of the flesh. Through which vanities, namely, the human mind, flowing down from the highest truth, is cast headlong so far that, pressed at last by carnal desires and wholly bound fast [0152A] by the enticements of pleasures, it not only cannot of itself remember its own origin, but cannot even when admonished will either that what evilly is should not be, or that what evilly is not should be. For what is, is evilly; and what ought to be, evilly is not. And that it wills either that what is should not be, or that what is not should be: this is worse than both. This therefore our Ecclesiastes here begins to demonstrate by the example of himself: namely, in what manner the human mind falls down through these steps of errors from the highest truth into vanity, and, its own vice driving it on, always goes to the worse. For to cleave to truth, and to love truth itself for its own sake, ought to have been its true good. When therefore it forsakes truth and turns away from her the mind's attention, then first, repulsed from her, it suffers the punishment of its own error: that [0152B] the very truth which it would not love in itself and for its own sake it now begins to seek for the sake of vanity; and because it has ceased to contemplate that one thing in which it might see all things, now, aiming not so much at truth as at novelty, it longs to know many things. For because it has lost that inward contemplation in which is the true refreshment of the mind, it seeks hungrily, as it were the food of understanding, in the knowledge of those things which are without. But because in all those things which appear outwardly a perfect knowledge of the truth can by no means be found, the wretch, languishing in the misery of his own failing, begins, as with a certain loathing, to run about always from some things to the desiring of others; and, casting away the former as empty and barren, he hopes that he can obtain the effect of his desire in those that remain — so foolish and blind that, though [0152C] he sees himself always frustrated in them, and never reaching the end of his desire, yet these things by no means ever cease to be courted as vain and unfruitful. But a just punishment nevertheless follows upon this so shameless desire and foolish curiosity, by which it stretches itself out superfluously in the knowledge of visible things, and either neglects the knowledge of its Creator — in which alone true blessedness consists — when it is had, or dissembles to seek it when it is not had; since in the very place where it perversely seeks delight, it finds nothing beyond grief and affliction of spirit. For in so great an occupation, finding a difficulty greater than its strength, it is oppressed as by a certain weight of its own anxiety; because, while desire drives it to seek wisdom in things of this kind, where it cannot be found, it is at once distracted by their multitude [0152D] and beaten back by their depth. This then is the labor and affliction of spirit by which the foolish and shameless mind is deservedly worn away for its own rashness, while, not only forgetting its Creator but also neglecting itself, it is occupied in searching out the lowest things, which do not pertain to salvation. And yet sometimes knowledge, sought with such vanity and found with so great labor in however small a part, puffs up the mind through pride, and casts it headlong from curiosity to elation, as from vanity to vanity. Which elation, namely, bringing in the ambition of the world, while it makes the mind greedy of praise, kindles it to the love of pomp and the glory of riches, so that, while it slips down from the glory of the inward good to the appetite of the outward, it is always [0153A] cast headlong into a worse vanity. But neither can corruption, once begun, stand still here. For as the goods of the flesh, while they are not yet had, flatter the greedy man by their appearance, so that they must needs be sought, so afterward, while they are possessed, they entice to pleasure one nervelessly broken and softened by use. And so the wretched mind, after the love of vain praise, after the ardor of a more vain cupidity, is lastly inflamed to the appetite of pleasure, so that now it begins even to sow vanity in the flesh, that afterward, made flesh out of flesh, it may reap the fruit of vanity in corruption. For this is the fruit of vanity: that the wretched mind, overwhelmed at last by carnal desires and lulled asleep by the pleasure of carnal desires, sleeps so deep that now it seeks wisdom not only not in truth, but not even in appearance. [0153B] And it pours itself out wholly to the trying of the evilly sweet pleasure of the flesh the more greedily, by how much the more, wearied within and repulsed from the intention of its own search, it despairs of finding the truth. And because, stretching above itself through search, it considers that it has sunk down under labor and excessive difficulty, it lets itself loose with all greediness to another consolation, and one that can be had without labor; but there too, finding vanity, it is not suffered to rest, because in the pleasure of the flesh it can neither keep hold of the fleeing delight when it is present, nor call it back when it has passed away. And thus, wearied and scattered on every side, it is shaken by the storm of its own vanity, so that through that very thing wherein it went away evilly delighted, it may at last, wholesomely afflicted, be compelled to return. And often there is a certain grievous struggle in the mind, so that, while [0153C] it considers itself at once delighted and afflicted in the lowest things, with great desire, for love of liberty, it longs to forsake even the things it has loved, if it could be done. This struggle therefore and tumult of the human mind through the various pursuits of mortal life Ecclesiastes will express in the words that follow, taking upon himself in a sentiment the case of all; and he will show in what manner it falls down from the vanity of curiosity to the vanity of pomp and of ambition, and thereafter to the vanity of pleasure, as is demonstrated in the sequence and order of the book. But since we have completed those things which were to be said in the preface, let us now return to expounding the words of his narrative.
