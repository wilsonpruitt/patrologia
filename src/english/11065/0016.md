## HOMILY V. *On the literal and moral exposition of the words of Ecclesiastes already spoken.* (cont.)

*For God hath given this worst occupation to the sons of men, that [they may be distended] they might be occupied therein.* Rightly and justly: that because they would not stand in truth, [0155D] they should, divided through multiplicity, be distended and occupied in vanity. Therefore, he says, I saw all things which are done under the sun, and behold, all is vanity and affliction of spirit. Thanks be to God. Now for the first time thou hast begun to be wise. Now I see that thou hast the wisdom by which thou canst seek and search out wisely concerning all things which are done under the sun. For wisely hast thou found, and wisely hast thou understood, that all is vanity and affliction of spirit. Yet I should like to know whence comes to thee this so sudden repentance. We expected something great, and that grand purpose had already made all men not attentive and expectant. For who would think that anything scanty could come forth out of so great a promise? To seek and to search out wisely concerning all things which are done under the sun. And how afterward: God hath given this worst occupation [0156A] to the sons of men, that they may be distended therein. What occupation dost thou mean? This, thou sayest: that they propose in their mind to seek and to search out wisely concerning all things which are done under the sun. Why then dost thou call this the worst occupation: To seek and to search out wisely concerning all things which are done under the sun? Take away, he says, "wisely." For it would not be the worst occupation, wisely to seek and to search out concerning all things which are done under the sun. But who can do this? It is above the sense of man to search out the causes of all things and to comprehend the reason of all that is done under the sun. Therefore it can be proposed, but it cannot be done, to seek and to search out wisely concerning all things which are done under the sun. For many things lie hid, and very many are hidden away, and all are reserved for hereafter; nor ought that to be searched out [0156B] which is not lawful to be done. Therefore what cannot wisely be done cannot wisely be proposed, even if it is proposed to be done wisely. I therefore foolishly proposed what I could not wisely do: to seek and to search out concerning all things which are done under the sun. Foolish on both sides — both when I proposed what I could not, and when I did what I ought not — with a third folly added: that I knew neither of the two. Yet I sought and searched out what I proposed to seek and to search out; I sought, and I did not find what I sought. Yet I found something: namely, that I could not do what I had believed; and there I recognized that God hath given this worst occupation to the sons of men, that they may be distended therein. Therefore it profited to have been foolish; but yet folly was not wisdom. Yet folly was given, that it might train [0156C] the sons of men unto wisdom. They have been left to themselves, that they may know themselves, and knowing may condemn and flee what they have become of themselves. This worst occupation therefore God hath given to the sons of men, that they may be distended therein. For because the mind of man would not stand in that one good in which it could happily rest, and without distraction or occupation possess the fullness of the highest truth, it is cast forth outside itself and scattered into the multiplicity of visible things; and the truth which, blinded within, it cannot draw from the fountain, it attempts at least to flee, with parched inward parts, as it were through certain rivulets of visible things. But the more it pours itself outward by intention toward visible things, so much the more does it grow dark within from the knowledge of invisible things; [0156D] because, while it is scattered more widely through outward things, it is the more blinded, being turned away from inward things. Whence fittingly Cain, when he beheld his own crime uncovered, and, reprobate by a greater crime, despaired of pardon for his crime, is read to have said to the Lord. *Behold, thou dost cast me out this day from the face of the earth! and from thy face I shall be hid: and I shall be a vagabond and a fugitive upon the earth [n: (Gen. IV)]*. For the reprobate mind, cast out from the face of the Lord, is hidden, while, scattering itself through outward things, it is so much the more covered from the sight of the inward light, by how much the more it is held always occupied in thinking upon those things which are without. Whence fittingly it is there said, a vagabond and a fugitive. A vagabond, namely, through disordered concupiscence, and a fugitive through a sinful conscience: while he divides himself in concupiscence through thought, and in conscience through despair [0157A] turns himself away, the sinner himself hides from himself the light of truth, that he may not be able to return. Therefore God hath given this worst occupation to the sons of men, that they may be distended therein. Why did he give it, that they may be distended therein? That they may stretch and distend themselves, may labor and fail, and may return, and he may heal them. For if they were not distended in their occupation, nor labored, that occupation itself would become exceedingly sweet, and they would not be willing to forsake it, nor would they long any more to return where truth is. Therefore God gave them the worst occupation, that they may be distended therein. Let us give thanks to his mercy, because he has made us labor in our evil, in our turning away; because he has hedged our way with thorns, that we may return and be converted to him, the husband and spouse and friend of our soul, because it was well with us then [0157B] more than now. But this we did not understand, and we thought it was well with us when it was ill. And therefore he himself sprinkled bitterness and wormwood, and gave gall into the cup, upon our evilly sweet turning-away, which flatters unto death, and upon the worst occupation. And he gave this worst occupation to the sons of men, that they may be distended therein. For occupation is the distraction and the binding of minds, which turns aside and scatters and ensnares souls, that they may not be strong enough to think upon those things which are of salvation — and therefore, occupation. Why the worst? Because there is another evil occupation also, but not the worst; and it is that which is busied about the necessities of mortal life, and is anxious about very many things; and that occupation is evil; and, to say something further, it is malice itself, so that of it that word is rightly understood: *Sufficient for the day is its own malice. [0157C] [n: (Matth. VI)]*. This occupation therefore is necessary to the misery of mortal life, to be swallowed up afterward by the glory of immortal life; and therefore it is evil unto misery, but not evil unto fault; since, if they have a punishment alien from blessedness, it has not a fault contrary to justice and truth. But the occupation of curiosity, by which the mind stretches itself out to unlawful things, and presses on to search and to investigate what it either does not befit it to know, or is harmful to it — that is the worst occupation; because, while it spreads itself beyond measure, either it takes hold of what it has set its affection upon, and puffs up the mind through empty knowledge, or, if it is not able to lay hold of it, it wearies the mind through delay and despair.
