## HOMILY VII. *That the perverse are corrected with difficulty,* etc. (cont.)

*And I knew that in these also there was vanity and affliction of spirit: in that in much wisdom there is much indignation; and he who adds knowledge, adds also labor.* But if anyone wishes to distinguish the sentiment, and to divide off what we have brought forward last from the things said above, [0163A] he can draw it from what precedes in this way: that all the things which have been said above he spoke, as it were, in his heart — namely, that he was made great, that he surpassed in wisdom all who were before him in Jerusalem, that his mind contemplated many things wisely and learned them; and when he had said all these things in his heart, he then testifies that things higher still are to be investigated. As if he were saying: All these things which have been said above I spoke in my heart, and when I had spoken all these things together in my heart, then at last, taking confidence from the preceding contemplation, I gave my heart, that I might seek things higher still and investigate things deeper; namely, that I might know prudence and doctrine, and errors and folly. But that he says he gave his heart is to be understood thus: that he made it free [0163B] and unencumbered, so that it should not be divided among this thing or that, but should stretch itself wholly toward the consideration set before it. In this word he also expresses the type of pride and the swelling of elation: that he says he gave his heart to the contemplation of wisdom, as though the possibility of finding the truth lay ready at hand for him, if only the will to seek were not lacking. But to what end he gave his heart he sets forth, saying: That I might know prudence and doctrine, and errors and folly. Above he testified that he had contemplated and learned many things wisely; but now, after all these things, he disposes himself to investigate, that he may know prudence and doctrine, and errors and folly. Whence it is plain that the things above are to be referred to the nature of things, but these which he subjoins to the morals of men — using indeed a preposterous order, [0163C] since first his own life ought to have been corrected by circumspection of morals, and then study applied to the other things which lay outside his own nature, so that first the inner eyes of the heart might be cleansed from vices, and afterward opened in the contemplation of truth. But elation, which wished to appear great without, sought first the outward things, so that first, through outward knowledge, it might open a way for boasting, and then, by searching its own inward things as well, might cast them forth without for display. Whence rightly, when he had said that he had sought prudence, he added doctrine equally; because a mind greedy of glory and display wished by no means to have the prudence of circumspection for living well, but for teaching; and therefore it reckoned that the knowledge of prudence, without the knowledge of doctrine, would be useless to it and unfruitful. [0163D] But as for his confessing that he wished to know errors and folly also: by no means did he wish to know evils for this, that once known he might avoid them; but that he might both exalt himself over the knowledge of evils, and condemn others for their work. Now error is when evil is committed through ignorance, but folly is when evil that is known is not avoided. Therefore he wished to know both goods and evils alike, and along with the knowledge of both he coveted doctrine at the same time, so that all that the mind might comprehend through knowledge, it might be able to manifest for display through doctrine. But the perverse soul, finding everywhere the punishment of its own wickedness, fled back, on account of the labor and affliction of spirit, from all that it had inordinately desired for the sake of elation. [0164A] For because curiosity was driving him to inquiry and pride to display alike, it was fitting that labor should press him as swollen, and that occupation should scatter him as curious, so that at least in punishment he might recognize his own vice, and that, if not the love of virtue, then the pain of affliction might hold him within measure. Therefore above, being curious, he found occupation; but here, being elated, labor and affliction; since, while he seeks to know evils and goods not for edification but for elation, that very knowledge becomes for him a witness of his own depravity, so that the knowledge which in his elation he was seeking for display reproves the wicked man through his conscience. For he is compelled to despise himself from the very thing by which he strives to become great in the eyes of others; because while he seeks to know more of the truth that he may insinuate it to others, [0164B] he sees the more in himself, of his depravity, what he must condemn. Hence, therefore, the soul is indignant with its very self, and takes up a certain quarrel and wrestling against itself, because the foulness of the vices which pride despises, depravity defends. In this conflict of such a kind, therefore, great labor and indignation of pride is born; because, while it disdains to lie subject to the vices and strives to drive them from itself, it is overcome by its own depraved desires, so that it may not be able to accomplish what it wills. Thence, therefore, it now begins to abominate even the very knowledge of truth, so that, since on account of its infirmity it cannot take away what it hates in itself, it may at least be able, through ignorance, not to see it.
