## HOMILY VIII. *That man flees away from the truth: as Adam in paradise fled, and hid himself.*

*[n: (ECCLES. II.)] I said in my heart: I will go and abound in delights and enjoy good things.* Behold how the despairing mind casts itself wholly into pleasure, and now calls the blandishments of the flesh alone good things, because in them it finds no labor and affliction, reckoning as evil, as it were, [0164D] the inquiry into truth, in which it had earlier labored, distending itself through curiosity more than was right. But this estimation, in its turn, because it displays manifest folly, is the more quickly caught out; because the pleasure of the flesh, which seemed sweet to one desiring it, is at once rejected by one who has tried it. Whence he immediately adds, saying:

*And I saw that this also was vanity. Laughter I counted an error, and to joy I said: Why art thou deceived in vain?* But how great a vanity there is in the delights of the flesh, I remember that I said above. Whence the remaining things which follow are to be considered by us, because these too, although they seem plain in themselves, yet have perhaps something which diligent investigation may add. He says, therefore: Laughter I counted an error, and to joy I said: In vain art thou deceived? [0165A] It is clear to all that through laughter the gladness conceived within is in a certain fashion drawn outward, and that what lay hidden within of joy is published abroad — the bars of modesty being, as it were, broken — by a certain levity and incontinence of a slippery mind. And therefore laughter is rightly called an error; because when the mind pours its joy outward through unfitting gladness, going as it were to the left, it forsakes the straight way by which it ought to have gone, rejoicing, into God. But it is to be known that spiritual joy by no means dissolves the soul into laughter; but only that gladness which is conceived from the blandishment of the flesh is easily opened out also through a motion of the flesh. For because inordinate joy, in first entering the soul, broke the bars of continence, afterward, when it has begun to grow within, it easily bursts out without [0165B] modesty by the same way by which it slipped in. For a slippery mind pours out the perverse seed which it first conceives without modesty, bringing it to birth afterward, as it were, without chastity; nor can it hold itself back from casting out the gladness growing within, the bars of whose integrity were broken when it was conceiving it. First, therefore, it is deceived, when it takes in the allurements of the flattering world to its joy within; afterward it errs, when it pours out the joy conceived within, through laughter, into the wantonness of the world. It is to be noted that joy is only reproved, but laughter is altogether rejected; because laughter is in every way evil, while joy is not always evil, save when it is of evil; and therefore he rejects the one and chastises the other, so that both what is utterly evil may be guarded against, and what can be good may be corrected. [0165C] Therefore, he says: Laughter I counted an error, and to joy I said: Why art thou deceived in vain? It is deceived, therefore, when it takes delight in harmful things, and thinks it is well with it when it is ill. It is deceived also when it is wrapped up in the occupation of present delights, and is hindered, so that it may not be able to consider the evils to come.

But why in vain? It is deceived in vain, because the delights of the world can indeed themselves turn the heart away from the consideration of evils to come; but they cannot free it from the pressure of those that come upon it. It is deceived in vain also, because the joys of a deceptive good, in which it exults, are mingled with such great bitternesses that they ought rightly to displease, even if they were bound to remain. In vain, therefore, is it deceived on both sides; namely, both when it does not see the evil which it suffers, and when it does not foresee the evil [0165D] which it shall suffer; since both the reason is manifest which can judge the one for him who considers it well, and the experience is present which shows the other to him who suffers it.
