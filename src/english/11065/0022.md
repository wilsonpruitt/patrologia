## HOMILY IX. *On the various vain endeavors of Ecclesiastes.*

*I made my works great. What works? I built myself houses, I planted vineyards, I made gardens and orchards, and I planted them with trees of every kind, and I built myself pools of water, that I might water the wood of sprouting trees.* This is what I said. Thou seekest without [0170A] what thou hast lost within. Thou makest thy works great, because in thine own self thou hast been made less. Thou buildest houses, because thou hast been cast forth from the dwelling of thine own conscience. Thou plantest vineyards and makest gardens, because the shoots of wisdom in thy heart have withered. Thou buildest pools, thou who hast not the fountain of life leaping up within. Thou waterest the wood of sprouting trees, because thou seekest shade, the light of truth being lost. What hast thou to do with these delights, unhappy one, forgetful of thine own goods? Where is now what a little before thou didst propose — to withdraw thy flesh from wine — thou who now makest thy soul drunk with every wantonness and vanity? Is wisdom sought thus? Thou didst seem to have gone out thither; how hast thou returned so quickly? How? Save because thou hast walked in a circuit, and thy path was tangled, and thrift led thee back [0170B] where gluttony had laid thee low. Behold, again thou hast fallen into pleasure, and hast been made the captive of vanity. How hast thou come hither, save because thou didst hear his voice and didst hide thyself? For this is the shadow of vanity under which thou liest hid, that thou mayest not see the truth. How, therefore, hast thou come hither? Oughtest thou not to have sought wisdom? But thy mind, exiled far from the truth and ignorant of the way of truth — from the very point at which it restrained itself from the one vice of pleasure by continence of the gullet, from that same point it poured itself out the more licentiously into every wantonness and superstition through the concupiscence of the eyes; and it is now the more wickedly corrupted in the whole, the more it seemed corrected in some part, however small. For before, thou wast corrupted and wast humbled, since thou didst see and understand what thou wast suffering; now, under the appearance of virtues, thou hast been made [0170C] the prey of vices. Within, avarice under the cover of thrift; and while it persuades the mind not to desert temperance in the things it possesses, it makes it keep things that will bring no profit. Thence, riches being multiplied, pride follows, while what has been sought through ambition is possessed for elation. Thence all these portents of vanity have arisen: that the foolish mind reckons the true good is to be found outside, in the trials of things — not as though the true good were to be sought within by means of the things which are without, but as though it consisted in them. Therefore without modesty it boldly diffuses itself through all things; and that mind which had found affliction in the abstinence of the flesh, another approach having been found through the concupiscence of the eyes, feeds carnal affection the more manifoldly. For it does not suffice the wanton man [0170D] to take delight in the things which were made for human service and for enjoyment, unless also, by a pride transgressing the equality of condition, he lord it over men. For this is what he adds after the rest and among the rest of his superstitions, saying.

*I possessed menservants and maidservants, and had a great household.* He was made lord of men, who was the servant of vices. How much better would a Lord be for thee, and here a companion? For there, to be lord would be the uprightness of one who reigns. But here, to be lord is not the uprightness of one who resists, but the iniquity of one who oppresses and the profit of one who endures. But the creature of glory attends to the appearance outwardly; the disease within it does not attend to. It thinks that what is puffed up is great, and therefore rejoices to be seen great outwardly. [0171A] But it does not fear to be empty and void within. I possessed, he says, menservants and maidservants, and had a great household. What could a great household have done without great possession, and much money, and an abundance of means? Therefore he added and said:

*Herds also, and great flocks of sheep, beyond all who were before me in Jerusalem.* Behold abundance. Where is glory? *I heaped up for myself silver and gold, and the substance of kings and of provinces.* How great the glory. And what follows, save that a mind forward to boasting quickly grows soft through wantonness and is unnerved, and says:

*I made myself singers, men and women, and the delights of the sons of men, cups and small pitchers [urceos] for the service [0171B] of pouring wines: and I surpassed in riches all who were before me in Jerusalem. Wisdom also persevered with me.* Great confidence. Therefore thou hast sinned securely. Therefore thou hast confidently poured out thy heart into the delights of pleasure, and upon the allurements of the beauties and enjoyments of vanity. Why? Because wisdom had persevered with thee. For why shouldest thou seek her, set within, if she herself did not leave thee wandering without? Yet what is this wisdom which so loves runnings-about and disdains the secret place? See lest perchance the likeness deceive thee, and what seems to be wisdom be not wisdom. Beware lest thou be deceived in the shadow of the sons, where thy father lay hid, who could not see the truth. For a shadow makes darkness; and if it seems to have rest, it has not light. Beware therefore lest thou too, while [0171C] under the shadow, thou seekest the rest of leaves, begin to suffer gloom. And lest thou be able, set in the shadow, to discern clearly; because the image which appears is shadow alone, not truth. Hold this shadow of leaves, therefore, in suspicion, lest thou be deceived. What are the leaves? The appearances of visible things are the leaves, which now indeed appear beautiful and green, but will fall suddenly when the whirlwind has gone forth. What are the leaves? Houses, vineyards, gardens, pools, the wood of sprouting trees, households, possessions, gold, silver, the substance of kings and of provinces; lyres, citharas, pipes, organs, cups and pitchers, and precious vessels riches and pomps, and glory: all these are leaves. Why leaves? Because vain, because fleeting, because transitory: therefore leaves. They are green indeed [0171D] for a little time, but quickly wither and fall. But yet, while they stand, they make shade and have their own refreshment; but it is a dark shadow, and hostile to light. To the flesh indeed it seems to afford refreshment for a time, but it makes the eyes grow dim. Therefore we ought to hold the shadow in suspicion, and not easily to believe the things which are seen in the shadow. For images deceive the eyes, because the shadow itself too is an image, not truth. Therefore I said that thou shouldest hold the shadow in suspicion, thou who confessest thyself set under the leaves. Under the leaves thou art, in the shadow thou art, and thou seest wisdom beside thee. See diligently lest perchance it be not wisdom, but something else lying hid under her appearance. For what wisdom is there in the shadow of leaves? For the shadow of the leaves is delight, and enjoyment in the appearance and beauty of transitory things. And [0172A] this has its own wisdom. For thus men call wisdom that by which this rest, and this tranquility of the flesh, is cunningly and craftily sought and prudently preserved. And this is the wisdom by which *the children of this world* and the children of darkness *are more prudent than the children of light in their generation [n: (Luc. XVI)]* — which perseveres with those who lie in the shadow of the leaves; and they cannot see the light of true wisdom, in whose sight this wisdom is folly. For because they look to the advantages of the flesh alone, they do not attend to the losses of the soul; and because they wish to be cautious where there is nothing to be feared, they become foolish and imprudent to thee, where the danger is grave and the loss intolerable. For what wisdom is it to cherish the flesh and to neglect the soul? What wisdom is it to flee with the whole intention that which is troublesome [0172B] to the flesh for a little time, and not to guard against that which is agreed to be always deadly to the soul? This is the wisdom in which this man glories, lying under the shadow of the leaves. O leaves, and shadow, of what sort is your fruit? See, Adam, of what sort are thy leaves, and of what sort is thy fruit? Thou hast hidden thyself, and thy leaves afforded a covert, that, covered, thou mightest not see the truth. Thou hast eaten, and thy fruit furnished food, that, corrupted by the eating, thou mightest lose immortality. Recognize, wretched one; recognize thy evil; recognize thy misery; recognize where thou art and what thou hast been made. Turn thyself from the shadow to the brightness of light, that thou mayest not only hear the words but also see the face of the truth.
