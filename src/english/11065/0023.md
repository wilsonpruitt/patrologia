## HOMILY X. *On the remaining vanities, as far as that place: « The fool walketh in darkness. »*

[0172C]

*And when I had turned myself to all the works which my hands had made, and to the labors in which I had sweated in vain, I saw in all things vanity and affliction of mind, and that nothing remains under the sun.* We have heard above with how great zeal this man toiled at gathering riches and increasing his goods; and then, on his own testimony, we have learned how he poured out his soul upon wantonness and upon the trying of pleasure, in those affluences of things which he had prepared; but now let us hear what he subjoins after all these things. When, he says, I had turned myself to all the works which my hands had made, and to the labors in which I had sweated in vain, I saw in [0172D] all things vanity, and affliction of mind, and that nothing remains under the sun. And so at the outset this presses a question upon us: how does this man now for the first time say that he has turned himself to seeing his own works, when hitherto he seems to have done nothing else than to have applied his intention and his zeal to these things? When he made his works great, built houses, planted vineyards, and made gardens and orchards, and built pools, had menservants and maidservants, herds and flocks, silver and gold, the substance of kings and of provinces, and the delights of the sons of men. When, in short, not only in deed but also in mind he had so plunged himself into the love of his own things and into pleasure that he reckoned this his own portion, and in a manner believed himself to have been made for nothing else than that [0173A] he might delight himself in all these things which he had prepared: did he not see what he was doing? But he saw the appearance; he did not foresee the corruption. He saw what those things seemed to be which stood, but he did not attend to what those had become which had perished. For the love of present things, while it turned the whole intention of the mind upon itself, and, having entered through the gaze of the flesh into the deceptive appearance of the things that were seen, occupied the eyes of the heart as well, at one and the same time both took away the memory of things past and hid the foresight of things to come. Whence it came about that, while he looked to those things alone which were seen, he could not recognize the truth in them either; and at the same time, in his own works, being turned toward them, he saw the appearance which was coveted, and being turned away, he did not recognize the vanity which was to be guarded against. But it sometimes happens that [0173B] temporal goods are the more quickly detected to be vain when they have begun to abound the more — goods which often, when they are not had, are reckoned to have been able to profit, had they been at hand. But when, once had, they cannot, even in their own affluence, drive out the poverty of the mind, then for the first time they make manifest to him who tries them how scant a fruit they confer for those who labor. Whence fittingly this man, after so great an affluence of all things and the use of pleasures, says that he turned himself, that he might understand his own vanity. Because when he began to learn by experience that the things he possessed could not make him happy, then he recognized that the things sought with empty labor had made him far more wretched. When, he says, I had turned myself to all the works which my hands had made — namely, looking back to what sort of fruit could have followed such great labors — then, he says, I found [0173C] that the fruit of my labors was not repaid me in an equal balance, and therefore I judged that I had labored in vain, looking back on the labors in which I had sweated in vain. For in all things there is vanity and affliction of mind. First affliction, afterward vanity; but also after vanity, affliction; and before vanity, affliction; and in vanity, affliction; and the whole is vanity, and the whole is affliction. For all things are vain which, even while they are, do not show forth to their lovers what they promise; and when they are thought to be held fast, they pass away. But it could perhaps have seemed tolerable, if there were vanity alone and there were not affliction. But as it is, there is both affliction before vanity, and affliction in vanity, and affliction after vanity, since the things that were possessed with love can neither be acquired without labor, nor kept without anxiety, [0173D] nor lost without grief. And therefore both the whole is vanity and the whole is affliction, because both in affliction there is vanity and in vanity affliction. And on this account, being turned, he rightly says: I saw in all things vanity and affliction of mind: and that nothing remains under the sun. For, instructed in his own affairs, he began to judge the affairs of others also; and what he had come to know in part, he truly pronounced of the whole, saying: Nothing remains under the sun. And from this it can be gathered how much, in other matters, whether toward good or toward evil, it avails a man to know what sort of man he himself has been to himself. For in some measure all things begin to be to you that which you yourself have been to yourself. If you do not see yourself, you see nothing well. If you are not deceived in the judgment of yourself, you are easily directed in the judgment of another. As here too, [0174A] being turned and directed, when he had subjoined concerning his own works: He saw in all things vanity and affliction of mind — at once, as one instructed in his own affairs, he begins to judge the affairs of others, and from the judgment of his own works he forms a sentiment about all things, saying: And that nothing remains under the sun.
