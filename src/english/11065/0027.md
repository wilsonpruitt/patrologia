## HOMILY XI. *What the ends of the earth are in which the eyes of fools are: and how wisdom reacheth from end to end mightily,* etc.

[0183A]

Yet where is the darkness, and where is the light? And which is the end? And the end where wisdom reaches. And which are the ends of the earth, where the eyes of fools are, who walk in darkness and do not see. And what is the head of the wise man, where his eyes are, who does not walk in darkness like the fool, but has enlightened eyes? Lest, therefore, we too perchance remain in darkness, let us seek out the light of wisdom, that we may understand where wisdom herself abides, and the division of light and darkness. Where light and darkness are bounded, and the limit of each. How far wisdom herself reaches, and what she is, and what is [0183B] outside her, and what is without her. First, because we have heard of two ends — the one from which and the other to which Wisdom extends herself, *reaching from end to end mightily, and ordering all things sweetly [n: (Sap. VIII)]*. And we have not yet come to know which these two ends are, and where they are, and whether these ends have an end, and whether beyond them there is anything else where wisdom does not reach. And if there is, how are they ends which do not arrive at an end? But if there is not, how are the ends of the earth, where the darkness is, contained within these ends, and how can there be darkness in the light, since Wisdom reaches from end to end mightily and orders all things sweetly. First, therefore, there are two ends of all things. The one is an end above which is nothing, and the other one below which is nothing. The end above which is nothing is the highest [0183C] of all. The end below which is nothing, the lowest. And so there are two ends of all things, the highest and the lowest. And whatever is in the universe besides the highest and the lowest is a middle between the highest and the lowest. And each single thing, the more it departs from the one, so much the more does it approach the other, whether from the highest to the lowest or from the lowest to the highest. Do you wish to know whether Scripture perchance somewhere mentions these ends more manifestly? *If I shall ascend,* says the Psalmist, *into heaven, thou art there; if I shall descend into hell, thou art present [n: (Psal. CXXXVIII)]*. Therefore he has established heaven and hell as the two ends of his own course. But nothing is higher than heaven, and nothing deeper than hell. And so heaven is the highest of all, hell the lowest — above [0183D] which is nothing and below which is nothing — and Wisdom reaches from end to end, and fills the whole and penetrates it. If I ascend into heaven, he says, thou art there; if I shall descend into hell, thou art present. He did not say, In heaven thou art present. Nor, In hell thou art there. But, if I shall ascend into heaven, thou art there; if I shall descend into hell, thou art present. Therefore in heaven he is, in hell he is present; abiding in heaven, coming into hell; in heaven he awaits those who come, in hell he follows those who flee. Those who ascend into heaven find him there; those who descend into hell do not escape him there. If I shall ascend into heaven, he says, thou art there; if I shall descend into hell, thou art present. And yet in both places thou art — both where thou art and where thou art present — reaching from end to end. From the end where thou art, to the end where thou art present, mightily, and ordering all things sweetly. Behold, [0184A] we have now distinguished the two ends to which wisdom reaches, the highest and the lowest, of which the one is as it were the place of wisdom where she abides, but the other is like a certain running-out and, so to speak, the goal of the stretching by which she extends herself. That end from which wisdom goes forth is the head, before which is nothing. But the end into which she runs out is the end beyond which is nothing. That end is both head and end. This end is not head, but only end. That end is head, because from it, and not above it; it is end, because up to it, and not beyond. But the other end is not head, because from it is nothing; yet it is an end, because whatever there is, is up to it, not beyond it. That end, therefore, which is above, is as it were a beginning and origin, above which is nothing. But the end which is below is as it were a goal and limit beyond which is nothing. For the origin and beginning [0184B] of all things is from the highest; but the limit and end is in the lowest. Wherefore the end below is an end, and only an end. But the end above is not only an end, but both head and end. If, therefore, heaven is above and hell below, and nothing higher than heaven and nothing deeper than hell can be found: heaven is the beginning, and hell the end. O how good a beginning, and how evil an end.

Therefore perhaps the eyes of the wise man are in his head, because, set in the middle, he looks back to his own beginning, that he may make his end there where he received his origin. For it is not good to be ended in the other end, where the end is one of consuming, not of consummation. Therefore man, who was created for eternity, alone among all animals has an upright countenance, that he may contemplate heaven [0184C] and may tend toward the end of consummation; and that he may reckon that not only is this visible heaven to be looked up to with bodily eyes, but that also with the mind and with the vision of the heart, lifting the inner eyes with a clearer and more piercing gaze above heaven even to the invisible things of heaven and the spiritual things of the virtues, he may climb up by contemplation, in the light of eternity, even to the end which has no end. That is the true heaven above every heaven, which is not only the heaven of heaven, but the heaven of heavens. Not second to the first, but third to the first and to the second. On this account Paul glories that he was caught up even to the third heaven [n: (II Cor. XII)], who had come in mind to the highest of all; and he had passed over not only the visible heaven, which human thought too can surpass, [0184D] but, having passed over also all those invisible things which were created above all this visible whole, he had been lifted up to behold the first beginning and the head of eternity. This is the third heaven. For the first heaven is the visible heaven. The second heaven is the invisible heaven of the visible heaven. The third heaven is the invisible heaven of the visible heaven and of the invisible heaven. The first heaven is only heaven, and the heaven of the earth. The second heaven, the heaven of heaven. The third heaven, the heaven of heavens. And this heaven has no heaven above itself. The first heaven is the supreme state of creation. The second heaven is the advancement of virtue above creation. The third heaven is the sublimity of contemplation. The first hell is the lowest state of creation. The second hell is [0185A] the failing of iniquity below creation. The third hell is the depth of damnation. The first heaven and the first hell, end and end. The second heaven and the second hell, end and end. The third heaven and the third hell, end and end. And everywhere Wisdom: She reaches from end to end mightily, and orders all things sweetly. From the first heaven to the first hell is the first course of wisdom. For beginning from the highest downward, and descending from the topmost even to the bottom of things, she accompanies and cherishes all things which she has wrought, so that the things which have been made and created by her may not subsist without her. But from the topmost even to the lowest, among all the things that subsist, each single thing, the more it degenerates from the perfect beauty and consummate comeliness of the highest things, [0185B] so much the more, as if fleeing, does it in a certain manner go far off from wisdom. But Wisdom herself follows the flight of things from end to end; because, deserting nothing wholly among all the things which she made, and tempering each single thing from the highest to the lowest with a comeliness proper and fitting to its own kind, she testifies that she is present to these also. For she herself, the very same who disposed the most excellent of creatures in a highest and supereminent beauty, fitting the lower things also in due sequence and following them up with her disposition, leaves nothing disordered even to the bottom of things. And so Wisdom reaches to the end, even to those things which in all things are lowest; because in these too something of her is found, which through the infirmity of their creation and the defect of their nature have withdrawn far from the highest. But yet [0185C] Wisdom herself abides in the highest things as it were by remaining, but reaches the lowest as it were by running out; because those things, subsisting unchangeably in their own perfect comeliness, nowhere flow away from her likeness. But these, fleeing through defect, yet by that very thing which they retain of beauty in their own kind, show that wisdom is present to them also. Wisdom therefore reaches everywhere, because in everything that is there is something beautiful, and everything that is beautiful is the work of wisdom. But what, in that which is, is not beautiful, is not the work of Wisdom; because what is beautiful in everything that is, she herself made; but what is not beautiful, she did not make, but permitted in that which she made beautiful. Why then did she not make the whole beautiful in that which she made; and why did she not bring it about that what is not beautiful [0185D] should nowhere be in that which she made? Perhaps she had been wearied in making those former things, in which she had wrought a certain highest and perfect beauty. And therefore she pursued the rest afterward as she could, bringing it about indeed that the whole should be beautiful which she made in them, but not bringing it about that what is not beautiful should not be in them — which she did not make, but permitted; because she could not bring it about that it should not be at all. And so thereafter, descending to one thing after another and making one thing after another, she conferred always so much the less of beauty and comeliness upon the later of her works, as she the more, in making the former, consumed the strength of her might, by which she could do more, and the force and ingenuity of her prudence, by which she might know more. Therefore the order [0186A] of things degenerated downward, descending to the lowest. And unless wisdom had looked out for herself, so as at last to set in these an end of making, there was perhaps nothing further of beauty which she could have conferred on the things to be made.
