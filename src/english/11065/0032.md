## HOMILY XII. *On that passage, according to another translation: The eyes of fools are in the ends of the earth; where our copy has: The fool walketh in darkness, and on the remainder of the second chapter.* (cont.)

[0196B]But lest perchance anyone think it contrary, that here the fool is said to walk in darkness, and that there, as has been recalled, the eyes of fools are declared to be in the ends of the earth. For both seem to be affirmed, that they see and that they do not see, although both may nevertheless be true at once, that they see and that they do not see. For he who said: The fool walketh in darkness, seems to have said outright that the fool does not see. And again he who said: The eyes of fools are in the ends of the earth, seems to have said that they see something, namely those things which are in the ends of the earth. How then do they walk in darkness, if they see? And how do they not see, if they see those things which are in the ends of the earth? But it must be known that fools are said to walk in darkness not because they see nothing: for they see [0196C] the things which are in the ends of the earth, but because, whether in the things they see or in the things they do not see, they do not see the truth. For they do see visible things by intention and desire, and they do not see invisible things. And in the things they see, they think that to be good which is not; and in the things they do not see, they do not know that to be good which is. And so nowhere do they see the truth, and therefore they walk in darkness even in the things which they see. For the delight of the flesh and the [sic: *illececebra*] of pleasures has blinded their hearts, so that they do not see the truth. Because the mind, made drunk with a false sweetness, while it thinks of this alone which seems sweet and pleasant in the present, can neither remember how great is the sweetness of those goods from which it fell by its own turning away, nor foresee how great is the bitterness of the evils that are coming upon it, toward which [0196D] the ruin of its own headlong fall always hastens. And note how the sentiment of truth follows upon itself. For above, this man, having considered the vanity of worldly things, despising all, betook himself to the contemplation of wisdom, saying: I passed over to the contemplation of wisdom, and errors and folly. Then considering that the true wisdom of man is nothing other than to seek true goods, and to love the things that are above, where our King is: who therefore went before us, ascending thither, that he might show whither we should follow. Noting also how difficult it is to raise up a mind accustomed to earthly delights toward the desires of eternal things, he added and said: What is man, that he can follow the King his maker? Yet you see [0197A] that hearts which plunge themselves into carnal delights grow dark away from the light of truth — he gave, as it were, a definitive sentiment concerning good and evil, saying: I saw that wisdom went before folly as much as light differs from darkness. Then, commending the pursuit of both, he added at the last and said: The eyes of a wise man are in his head, but the fool walketh in darkness. Wherefore let the sentiment run back to its sum, and say: It is indeed difficult for mortal man to raise up to the contemplation of the wisdom above a heart pressed down with earthly desires; but nevertheless, because wisdom is light, and all who fail from the contemplation of it are in darkness, it is necessary that even mortal man, so far as his capacity supplies, apply his zeal to the seeking and searching out of Wisdom. [0197B] For this is the wisdom of man in this life: to seek and to search out Wisdom. For to find wisdom and perfectly to lay hold of the truth is not granted to this life, but laid up for the life to come. Yet this very inquiry after Wisdom has now its own light, by which it distinguishes those who love and seek Wisdom from those who love folly and walk in darkness. But the carnal mind, loving the pleasantness of visible light, counts vain the labor for the inner light to be sought; because it either doubts or is wholly ignorant that its brightness, after this light has been taken away, remains inextinguishable in holy minds. For because it discerns that the wise and fools, by an equal lot and like condition, suffer to be withdrawn from this visible light after the death of the flesh, a doubt arises for it also concerning the light [0197C] of Wisdom, whether it can be preserved inextinguishable in holy minds after the death of the flesh. Therefore, although it considers the pursuit of Wisdom itself to be good and praiseworthy, yet it doubts whether it is to be procured with so great a labor, since as to whether its fruit can be perpetual, no undoubted reason as yet makes anything manifest to it. This wavering of the human heart, then, this man, both seeing it in himself through the contemplation of truth and feeling it through the affection of the common infirmity, takes up also in a sentiment, showing how, wisdom having been sought and seen, that very consideration of mortal life was calling him back from the pursuit of wisdom, and that he therefore judged it wholly vain that others should advance in wisdom, because in paying the debt of death [0197D] he recognized himself to be equal with the rest. For this is what, after the praise of Wisdom and the commendation of the pursuit of it, he now suddenly brings in, turned to the consideration of the common infirmity, saying:

*I learned that the destruction of both was one.* For because he saw that both, that is, both the wise man and the fool, are withdrawn from this life by one destruction of death, he began to doubt this also: whether after this life diverse rewards of merits ought to be found according to this same diverse manner of life. And if it stands firm that this is true, why do they go out from this life by one way, who after this life do not attain to one reward? But if, just as the passage is one for all, so also there comes to be one arrival for all: why should they run before death by laboring unlike one another, [0198A] who, going out by a like death, would not come after death to an unlike reward? For this is what follows: *And I said in my heart: If the fall of the fool and my own shall be one, what does it profit me that I have given greater labor to wisdom?* For by those who believe this life alone to be, the pursuit of Wisdom is judged wholly vain: for this reason, that its labor belongs to the present life, but the reward and fruit of the labor to the life to come. Therefore, because they see the labor, they quickly grow weary of the labor itself, and easily turn to those things which, even though wisdom teaches that they are by no means to be sought after, yet, because their fruit is present, they judge more convenient. But because Wisdom does not allow her labor to be wholly fruitless even in this life, and cherishes those who follow after her with a great inward consolation, [0198B] and through the illumination of truth persuades them to have confidence, in this misery of mortality, concerning the reward of eternity, this man, immediately after he had begun, because of the failing of the carnal life, to doubt concerning the fruit of the perpetual life, accuses himself out of his inmost consideration for this same doubt of his, saying:

*And speaking with my own mind, I perceived that this also was vanity.* And at once, adding the reason why he testifies that the aforesaid doubt is a vain thing, he brings in also the vanity itself in respect of which the truth could have come into doubt for him, saying:

*For there shall not be remembrance of the wise man in like manner as of the fool for ever.* For Wisdom, which does not take away mortality from the present life, grants [0198C] eternity to the life to come: and although the condition of the wise man and of the fool is alike at the going out of this life, yet in the life to come the remembrance will not be alike. For as here an unlike manner of living divides them, so there too, when they shall have come into remembrance so that he may be brought to judgment, an unlike requital according to their own merits shall separate them. For future times shall not cover all things with oblivion, but all things that have been done shall come into recollection in their own time, so that every work, whether it be good or evil, may be called into judgment. Therefore the memory of the wise man shall not for ever not be in like manner as that of the fool, and future times shall not cover all things with oblivion, because, when the time shall have come, the one shall receive glory for the merit of his virtue, and the other shall receive punishment for the excess of his folly. [0198D] This, then, is that for which it is not vain to shun folly, and to give greater labor to wisdom; because, when the reward of requital is received, it is made manifest how great is the fruit of just labor. Yet this very consideration of our mortality has a certain just weariness in it; because it is fitting that a man should despise what he sees to be transitory even before it comes to an end? and that he should not afflict himself with empty labor for the attaining of that which cannot long stand. On this account he again redoubles the sentiment concerning the same equal condition of mortality, saying:

*The learned dieth in like manner as the unlearned: and therefore my life became wearisome to me.* And deservedly indeed does such a life come into weariness, which, being wholly liable to confusion, neither in its goods while it seems to stand, nor in its evils while [0199A] it comes to an end, separates the good from the evil or the worthy from the unworthy. For its goods come without discernment to the unworthy equally as to the worthy, and its evils afflict the good and the evil together by an equal lot and with the same outcome. Whence the goods also of this life rightly come into contempt with the just: goods which both afflict with various sorrows those who labor for them, and often, sought with much labor, pass over without profit to those who possess them, from the good to the wicked, and from their own to strangers. Considering which, this man, setting all things in one part — not only those which seem the evils of this life, but those also which put forward the appearance of good, while in real truth they are evils — and adjudging them by a like estimation, gives one sentiment concerning them all, saying:

*Seeing that all things under the sun are evil, and all vanity, [0199B] and affliction of spirit, again I detested all my industry.* And lest perchance you should think that he here detests that industry by which he gave greater labor to wisdom, and not rather that by which he was distended by the occupation of those things which are vain under the sun: presently he sets forth what industry he judges is to be detested, saying: And what [qua] under the sun I most studiously labored at. "I detested" is understood. And immediately he opens the reason why he detests both these things and equally the industry spent on them, when he subjoins:

*Since I am to have an heir after me, whom I know not whether he will be wise or foolish: and he shall have dominion over my labors, in which I have toiled and been anxious.* And lastly, commending his judgment concerning this, he brings in:

[0199C] *And is there anything so vain?* As if he were to say: I judge nothing so vain as when one man, greedily gathering, is worn away with fruitless labor, and another, living both idly and luxuriously, has dominion over the labors of another. For manifold is the vanity where one man is afflicted without cause, and another is unlawfully delighted. For the first vanity is to seek after things that will not profit; the second, to gather things that pass away; the third, to leave the things gotten by our own labor to be possessed by others, and above all by those of whom we do not know what sort they will be. For if all these things were vain even when sought and kept for our own uses, what will they be when handed over to the pleasure of fools? Whence he says:

*I ceased, and my heart renounced to labor any more under the sun.* Redoubling also the reason why he ceased from labor, [0199D] he subjoins, saying:
