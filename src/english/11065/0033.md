## HOMILY XII. *On that passage, according to another translation: The eyes of fools are in the ends of the earth; where our copy has: The fool walketh in darkness, and on the remainder of the second chapter.* (cont.)

*For when one man labors in wisdom, and doctrine, and carefulness, he leaves what he has gotten to a man that is idle.* This befell Solomon himself, who, laboring in wisdom and doctrine and carefulness, gathered riches and heaped up wealth, and at last left all to be possessed by a foolish heir. For he left the folly of his own stock, his son Rehoboam, who, succeeding to his father's wealth, scattered all, and left the kingdom of his father — which he had received ample enough and magnificent — torn and troubled by a diminishing of sense and a surfeit of heart. But many also labor in wisdom and doctrine and carefulness, searching out with great labor and zeal the secrets of nature and the hidden things of truth, and handing on what they have found to the memory [0200A] of posterity, by word alike and by writing, for the commending of their own studies; and often they leave what they have gotten to idle men, because sometimes the negligence of those who come after refuses to take up for use this thing prepared, which the keen diligence of the earlier men acquired not without great labor and care.

*This then is vanity, a great evil.* It is therefore vain to labor in the knowledge of truth in order that we may come to the notice of those who neither wish to receive truth for its own sake, nor know how to approve it in others. Fruit, then, does not follow upon labor of this kind, because it neither gathers things that will profit itself, nor are those to whom it leaves what has been gathered stirred by any emulation of virtue to the pursuit of the good. For if there is labor for the one who seeks, and there is no profit for the one who receives, what does such labor profit? [0200B] But it is also a great evil, that from what one man is worn away with labor, another should abuse idly and unlawfully. For it is a great evil, because both the one gathers to his own harm and the other receives to his own harm. Therefore this is vanity and a great evil.

*For what does it profit a man of all his labor, and affliction of spirit, wherewith he has been tormented under the sun?* If it does not profit toward anything else, it profits nothing; nay rather, if it does not profit toward anything else, it does much harm, because the searching after it has great punishment, but the finding has no fruit. For what a punishment it is never to be without punishment?

*All his days are full of sorrows and hardships: nor does he rest in mind by night.* Very wretched is he who cannot have quiet even in that time which is naturally given [0200C] to quiet. By day he is worn away with labor, by night he is tormented with the care and anxiety of labor. For him whom, waking, the distension of labor and its restlessness harasses, it wearies also when he sleeps, being heaped upon his soul through the images and phantasies of dreams. No time, then, is allowed to pass for rest; the whole is taken up with sorrow and labor, and nothing is gained for profit and salvation.

*And is not this vanity?* Truly it is vanity, and utterly a great vanity. But what then? If this is vanity, what then is to be done?

*Is it not better,* he says, *to eat and drink, and to shew his soul good things of his labors?* If those labors are vain which confer no profit on those who labor, [0200D] then it is better to labor and to take the fruit of one's own labors. The reasoning seems plain, but it has many an exception. For it would have been much better if it had been said thus, and it would have stood true everywhere: If those labors are vain from which no fruit comes to those who labor, then labors of this sort are altogether to be set aside, and one must rather labor in those things wherein a fruit both certain and true may come to those who labor. But now by this reasoning vanity has been changed, not dismissed, and one vanity has succeeded another, not every vanity has departed. [sic: *Vena*] carefulness is rebuked, so that a mind ill at ease may be loosed through torpor and idleness and incontinence into baseness. For if that man who distends himself with superfluous labor is truly seen to be blameworthy, why on that account is he who dissolves himself into pleasure and incontinence [0201A] thought to please? But the carnal soul finds the middle of truth with difficulty; and therefore it sometimes willingly consents to forsake one vice, since through it it sees a way opened for itself to another which pleases it more. And it pours itself out into the contrary with its whole intention the more boldly, the more certain it holds it that what it forsakes is an evil. For now, in its own evil, it not only judges itself worthy of no rebuke, but even supposes itself praiseworthy; and although it manifestly commits evil, it does not wish to be seen to be doing evil, but rather to be shunning evil. For it does not consider that by turning aside from a vice it has fallen into a vice, and therefore it thinks that by no means is the evil which it does to be marked with ill repute; because in it, as it were, it labors not so much to do evil as to turn aside from it. Whence here too, [0201B] when he had seen that those men are wretched and worthy of rebuke who greedily gather with labor and carefulness riches that will not profit them, and neither use the things they possess, but leave them to others to be possessed and used after them, in order to shun their folly he freely casts himself into the contrary sentiment, saying: Is it not better to eat and drink, and to shew his soul good things of his labors? There is one sentiment of all who are prone to vice. They always rebuke the opposite, that they may commend their own. Let the greedy perish, says the glutton and the spendthrift — and he devours, and gets drunk, and distends himself and fills himself to the point of choking. And his proverb is always: Let the greedy perish. And he thinks himself commended because he is not such a man, lest he be blameworthily such a man as he is. Nay, even for this reason he wishes to be seen to be such [0201C] as he is, lest perchance he be such as he accuses others of being, which he does not wish to be — as though indeed there were no middle ground where both might come to pass; both that he be not such as he accuses others of being, and yet that he be not such as with shameless boasting he exults that he is. It is better to eat and drink, and to shew his soul good things of his labors. How fair are the words, to eat and to drink! Who is there that shudders when he hears "to eat and to drink," or thinks that anything has been said here against modesty or chastity? But a shameless baseness lies hidden, covered with the veil of chastity. Let him therefore say openly: It is better to devour, and to get drunk, and to serve the desires of one's concupiscence with unbridled liberty. For if he had spoken thus, what sort of sentiment would it be? And indeed [0201D] he did speak altogether thus; because he wished to signify this in that which he said: It is better to eat and drink, and to shew his soul good things of his labors.

*And this is from the hand of God.* Men say this, and there is a kind of men who are wont to say this: Let us devour, and let us get drunk, and let us do well by ourselves out of those things which God has given us, because this is from the hand of God; because these things too were given us for this, that we might do well by ourselves. For why were all these things made, save for the uses of men, that men might use them, and that it might be well with them in those things which God gave them? And by saying these things they are blinded, and are carried away without measure and without chastity into their own pleasures, so as to applaud themselves, since God made all these things for use; but they neither know nor consider that although God made all good things for use, [0202A] yet he made nothing for crooked and disordered uses.

*Who shall so devour, and abound with delights, as I?* An evil boast is this. And yet there are those who glory in such things: and these too wisdom ought not to have passed over in the number of the vanities. Such men the prophet rebukes in another place, saying: *Woe to you that are mighty to drink wine, and stout men at mingling drunkenness [n: (Isa. V)]*. Who shall so devour, and abound with delights, as I? What manner of lover of wisdom is this? How far turned backward, nay, how far turned away and perverted from him who a little before saw the light of wisdom, and divided it from the darkness of folly with so subtle a consideration, saying: I saw that wisdom went before folly as much as light differs from darkness. Whence, do you think, was this sense so quickly made worst [0202B] [pessundatus]? Did he not say for his own sake what he said of himself, that he was a lover of wisdom: and this was not what he said; but he wished to signify certain men of that sort, for whose sake he said this which he said of himself? Or was he himself also, in some respect, all this truly, and yet did not on that account cease to be a lover of wisdom; because at once in one man both the sense of the flesh, according to the inborn corruption and the sin that dwells in it, urged wickedness, and yet the spirit, according to the affection of virtue and the judgment of uprightness, loved wisdom? See, nevertheless, what man is, whether in him who said this and was such a man, or in those for whose sake he said what he said of himself: whom he wished to signify to be such. See indeed what man is. Who shall so devour, and abound with delights, [0202C] as I? *Why dost thou glory in malice, thou that art mighty in iniquity? [n: (Psal. LI.)]* If no one can do what thou canst do, it is a great thing if he is able for good. But if thou art able for more in evil, why dost thou glory? For to be able for evil is not power, but weakness. Who shall so devour, and abound with delights, as I? For who is there, he says, to gainsay it, when God commands this? For God created gladness, and willed that those who please him should take joy in it.
