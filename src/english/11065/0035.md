## HOMILY XIII. *How all things have their own time.*

Many are the words of man, because the heart of man is not one. For unless the mind first scattered itself inwardly, away from the fount of truth, through many desires, in no wise would speech derive itself outwardly through so many assertions. But now, because the ruling soul is torn away from [0205A] the love of virtue, through various desires, into the concupiscence of vanity, therefore in the approving of that same vanity the tongue serves it outwardly by word with a various and inconstant judgment. For this reason, then, Solomon, disputing about vanity, so often changes his sentiment in his discourse, namely that he may show his understanding to have been changed in his thinking through the love of vanity. For the human heart, which is not fixed in the desire of eternity, is never able to be stable; since it is shaken from its own stability by the seething of carnal desires as often as it is moved, from those things to which it had clung by love, to the coveting of others. For because it seeks out the fruit of eternity in the use of things that pass away, and thinks that it can find the joy of happiness [0205B] in those things which were made only for the consolation of a time: therefore always, when in the things it has, with experience teaching, it has found either a false pleasantness or a true misery, it straightway turns itself to the seeking of other things, as though better, by which it may either lighten its own sorrow or fill up its pleasantness. Hence, therefore, it always seeks things to come and loathes things present: and it comes about in a certain wondrous way that he who is always deceived in the desire of what is to come is scarcely ever wholly deceived in the experience of things present. For man is his own witness that these very things which he loves are not the true goods; because while he always gapes in mind after things to come, and never acknowledges himself truly happy in the things he enjoys, in this he plainly proves even himself that all these things, although they can be sought after by fools when they are not had, as though for the highest happiness, yet can never, when they are had, be [0205C] possessed unto the highest happiness. And yet the carnal mind, blinded by the darkness of its own concupiscence, cannot plainly see what it also cannot be wholly ignorant of: that although it always sees vanity in the experience of things present, yet it never restrains itself from the appetite of things to come. It disapproves what it has experienced, it praises what is yet to be experienced; and the more immoderately it pours itself out through concupiscence in the desire of the things it does not yet have, the more grievously is it vexed with sorrow in the failing of the things it already has. This wavering of the human mind, then, Ecclesiastes has thus far set forth to us in himself, when in the approving of things, whether from loathing of things present or from appetite for things to come, with a changed mind he so often varied his judgment. For he thought [0205D] to find in those things which are rolled along under time something stable in which his soul might rest: and therefore, approving one thing after another as though by experiment, and disapproving all things once experienced, he acknowledged at last that there is nothing in them all which can either abide perpetually for those who enjoy it, or exhibit, while it is present, the full fruit of pleasantness. Therefore now, reproving his own ignorance, in that he believed himself to possess an abiding gladness in changeable things, he shows that just as all things, not only prosperous but adverse also, are good in their own time to him who uses them well, so to those who with a perverse love love temporal things instead of eternal, and hope that they can become happy in them, not even prosperous things can confer true happiness. He did ill, then, when he chose certain of them, as though better than all, [0206A] for his own pleasantness; because a strong soul, which through temporal things hastens to the state of eternity, not only seeks prosperous things of this world meanwhile for consolation, but also willingly takes adverse things into use, when the time demands, for the exercising of its own virtue. And therefore, all things being proved by experiment, he at last acknowledges the condition of changeable things, and confesses that nothing out of all the things that pass away can stand perpetually, saying:

*[n: (ECCLE. V.)] All things have their time, and all things pass in their own spaces under heaven.* Why then should you either fear the evils of this world or covet its goods, since all things pass under heaven? Since rather this accords with truth: that in all these things you hold not anything, whether in good or in evil, for the highest; because all things [0206B] pass away. Nor should you think anything is to be cast away in its own time, because all things have been justly ordered. For all things have their time, and all things pass in their own spaces under heaven. All things have their time, so that nothing is found perpetual and always abiding: but everything that is either follows something else, so that it does not come from the beginning, or runs before something else, so that it does not extend itself even to the end. All things have also a certain and determined time, when they begin and when they end; and likewise how long each subsists, and when each subsists, so that nothing is beside reason; and let the prudent soul study so to fit itself both to times and to temporal things that it neither set its confidence in the things that pass away as though they abided, nor presume to murmur, in the things that have been well ordered, against the disposition [0206C] of the Creator. And in every event, conforming itself to true and abiding goods, let it so despise the variety of changeable things that, although it use each thing for a time while it is present, it never allow its soul, at the passing of that thing, to turn aside from its own state. For that man is most prudent who so knows how to turn passing things to use that nevertheless he does not know how to bend his mind from its own stability at their failing. For all things in their own time are good to him who uses them well, and yet all things whatsoever that lie subject to changeableness, although in misery they afford some sort of consolation, cannot nevertheless confer ease. Nothing therefore is to be cast away in its own time, and nothing is to be chosen not in its own time; but let the soul be so [0206D] prepared for the use of a time that it nevertheless be not changed to the changeableness of the time. All things have their time, and all things pass in their own spaces under heaven. Why then should any one thing be chosen for every time, since all things have their time? For if some one thing out of them all had every time, all things would not have their time; since, of two things contrary and at odds with each other, if there were a time for the one always to be, there would be a time for the other never to be. And thus this one would have no time, since that one, with which it could not have the same time, would have every time. Now therefore, since all things have their time, no one thing out of them all has every time; but each thing has its own time, when that it should be is good [sic: *es*], and a time when it is good that it should not be. [0207A] When, therefore, it has its time, it is good that it should be, even if that is not good which it is good should be; and when it has not a time to be, it is good that it should not be, even if that is good which it is good should not be. Job knew this, and received good things, praising God in his benefits, and the enemy thought that a holy conscience lay subject to time. He took away riches and goods; he slew the pledges of his house; he smote the flesh, he assailed him with reproaches and injuries, that he might break by adverse things the man whom he could not soften by prosperous ones. But Job, knowing that all things have their time, just as in good things he restrained himself by continence, so in evil things also bravely bore adversities by patience. But his wife, because she was foolish, and did not understand that all things have their time, thought that the rewards of merits were to be weighed by the moments [0207B] of times: and therefore, seeing him smitten and believing him forsaken, she rebuked him for having worshipped God in vain, and mocked him, as though he had deserved the scourges in return for his blessing, saying: *Now bless God, and die [n: (Job II)]*. To whom he answered: *Thou hast spoken like one of the foolish women. If we have received good things from the hand of the Lord, why should we not receive evil? [n: (Ibid.)]* For all things have their time, and all things pass in their own spaces under heaven. If there is a time for the weak to be cherished with prosperous things, there is a time also for the perfect to be exercised with adverse things; if there is a time for good things to the good and evil things to the evil for the sake of truth, there is a time also for evil things to the good and good things to the evil for the sake of testing. All things have their time, and all things pass in their own spaces under heaven.
