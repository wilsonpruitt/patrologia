## HOMILY XIII. *How all things have their own time.* (cont.)

[0207C]Why do you plead, all of you under time, about the laws of times? It belongs to time to lie subject to changeableness and to suffer vicissitude. For if all these things do not come to pass in time, when are they to come to pass? For beyond times there will be eternity, and there will be no changeableness: and there will not then be there, "all things have their time"; but everything that will be will so be that it cannot be now one thing and now another according to a time, but what will be will always be. Therefore, so long as you live under time, suffer the laws of time. Suffer them willingly, lest perchance, even if you were unwilling, you should nevertheless suffer them. Suffer the laws of time, that you may pass through time to the state of eternity. For one cannot find eternity in time, because eternity comes only after times: and those find eternity after times who, in the flowing of alternating times, fixed in mind [0207D] upon the desire of eternity, stand unchangeable. All things have their time, and all things pass in their own spaces under heaven. For heaven itself also measures the spaces of all times, and all things that are under heaven pass away in time, and heaven itself does not pass away, from which time is. For time is, as it were, from heaven itself, and in heaven itself there is no time: and although all things that are under heaven receive their time from heaven, yet in heaven itself eternity stands firm. A great spectacle of wisdom is the visible image, that from the contemplation of visible things we may be roused to the emulation of invisible ones. Look upon heaven. It seems, as it were, to be moved, and it does not seem to be changed. But the things which are under heaven are changed, and thence is the origin of all motion, where [0208A] there is no changeableness. Therefore all things pass away under heaven, because in heaven all things stand firm. And the very things which pass away measure their own being from those things which subsist; because there would be no certain subsistence of things passing away if the state of the things that subsist were not perpetual. For those things which truly are, always are; and always are what they are; and there are other things which truly are not, and those which truly are: in a certain way they emulate them, that they may be something in that which they are; and when they have begun to be what they are, they pass over into that which they are not; and they are not truly this which they are, because they cease to be this, and now are not; nor are they truly this which they begin to be, because they only begin to be this, and are not yet. So these things are wanderers in true being, and in the very passage in which they run they seem to partake of a being [0208B] which they neither take in nor comprehend, although they seem to touch it, lest they be nothing at all. For they appear there, and when they are sought they are not, because they have gone away into the nothing whence they had come, and they were not out of that in which they were. For that alone in which they were truly was; and the whole of what it was, it itself was, nor had it received from elsewhere what it was; and in it all these things were admitted, so that out of that which itself truly was, these too might be something. As much, therefore, as they partake of true being, so much are all these things which do not have true being in themselves: to which this is the whole of being, that they are that very thing which has true being. And although in itself it is one, yet in them it seems to be another according to the capacity of those partaking, since they themselves are others in that which is not another. Each thing, then, has, according to its partaking of that [0208C] which always is, its own spaces of subsisting, some greater and some lesser; according as they are in that which is neither greater nor lesser, but is always the same itself. Therefore from it there is divided and measured out to each its own space and its own time, to one short, to another long; and there are days and months, and years, and lustra, and ages, and short hours, and moments, and instants; and all these are time in the things themselves which pass away and truly are not; and in that from which they are what they are, they are one, and there is found there not time, but eternity. All things have their time, and all things pass in their own spaces under heaven. For out of all the things that are under heaven nothing is eternal, but all lie subject to time and to changeableness. For all things at some time had a beginning, and at some time will have an end: and between the beginning [0208D] and the end is the space itself of time, which is time to that which has a beginning and an end; and that same space is eternity to that which has neither beginning nor end. For that very thing which began, before it began, was not; and when it ceased to be, it then was no longer; and then to it there was nothing, because that was nothing; and yet even then there was that which always was; and that same thing afterward was when this was; and this which began, began in that; and what was, was in that, so long as it was; and when this was, which was not always, that also was which always was, because this was in that; and what to the one was time, because it was not always, was to the other eternity, because it always was. So out of eternity all things have their time, and all things pass in their own spaces under [0209A] heaven. They have a time when they begin, and they have a time when they end. They have spaces for as long as they are to subsist: for this space is the delay of subsisting between the beginning and the end; and these spaces are like for many, unlike for many; yet all are weighed out by an equal judgment. And when the delay comes to those who live in gladness and joy, and lead their days among good things, it seems a great happiness; and when for those who are in hardships and are wearied by adversities the long spaces run on, it is judged a great misery and a great unhappiness; and yet man does not know his own lot, nor can he search out the judgment of God upon himself: for the long times themselves also have an end; and when they have received their end, those things are now no more which seemed good; and in like manner the things also which were thought evil are now [0209B] no more. And often upon passing goods there follow evils that will abide, and upon passing evils, after their end, goods which have no end: and then past goods can profit nothing in the multitude of evils, and past evils can hurt nothing, in the abundance of goods, to those happily brought to consummation.

Let not a man, therefore, glory on account of his own time, before the time, nor let him who is in tribulation be broken by the adversities of times; because all things pass away, until the time comes when there shall be measured back in eternity whatever was drawn out in the spaces of times, whether greater or lesser. Then the long spaces of times will not please the impious, the happiness of times being spent; and their faults abiding which they contracted through the times, when they shall have seen eternal punishments for temporal [0209C] deeds. But to the just their own long times will then seem even scant, when they shall have seen their temporal merits recompensed with eternal rewards. Before this time, whatever is done in time is hidden, whether it be long or short, so that man may not dare to search out the judgment of God, but may be prepared for every time, whether it be good or evil, so that, being proved through good things and evil, he may pass over to good things, to be glorified. And if meanwhile the spaces of time be long, let him not presume in good things, let him not fall in evil ones; because all things pass in their own spaces under heaven, so that, when the time under heaven passes away, there may succeed the eternity which is in heaven.

*A time to be born, and a time to die.* Now [0209D] he pursues, with examples following one upon another, what he had set forth generally, saying: All things have their time. And here he enumerates both goods and evils: and after goods, evils, and again after evils, goods; nowhere evils alone, or goods alone. For at this time of this life it is not possible to have goods without evils, nor evils without goods; because all things are mingled, so long as it is the time that all things have their time.
