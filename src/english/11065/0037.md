## HOMILY XIII. *How all things have their own time.* (cont.)

*A time to be born and a time to die; a time to plant and a time to pluck up; a time to kill and a time to heal; a time to destroy and a time to build.* First good things and evils: a time to be born and a time to die. Afterwards evils and good things: a time to kill and a time to heal. Not good things alone, nor evils alone; but both good things and evils. Nor only after [0210A] good things evils, but also after evils good things: yet first after good things evils, and then after evils good things follow. For there are two lives: one according to the flesh, the other according to the spirit. And we know, on the Apostle's testimony, that *not first that which is spiritual, but that which* is carnal, is first [n: (I Cor. XV)]. There are therefore two lives, the first carnal, the second spiritual. And each life has its own good things and its own evils; because *all the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth [n: (Psal. XXIV)]*; *for he himself saves men and beasts [n: (Psal. XXXV)]*; *he opens his hand, and fills every living thing with blessing [n: (Psal. CXLIV)]*. For he has set before every man both good things and evils. Good things indeed, by which he may cherish him through mercy; evils, by which through justice, examining his faults, he may condemn him. And every life has [0210B] its own good things and its own evils, so that all things may have their time, and all things under heaven pass away in their own spans; both are given, and both are set forth; the one out of justice, the other out of mercy. All the ways of the Lord are mercy, truth. *Mercy and truth have met one another: justice and peace have kissed [n: (Psal. LXXXIV)]*. If evils alone, there would be no mercy. If good things alone, there would be no justice. God therefore sets forth both good things and evils, and leaves to man the power of choosing which of the two he shall receive first. For it behooves him to [sic: *gustaro*] both, but which of them first lies in himself. But the carnal man, hastening as it were to his inheritance and turning aside from judgment, so that he may lack the blessing at the last, chooses good things first; and God gives him temporal goods that have an end, so that evils that shall abide without [0210C] end may follow upon them. But the spiritual man is first tried by adversities that he may be brought through to prosperity, and he too has his own evils and his own good things; but the evils in time, the good things in eternity. Thus every life is led to its consummation by an alternating lot, the one from good things to evils, the other from evils to good things. And all things have their time that they may be, and pass away that they may not be, running forward in their own spans to their end so long as they are. Good things pass away amid evils, and evils amid good things; and while they pass they sometimes make delay, and they have certain spans, each according as they are ordained, until they receive their end. Therefore he enumerates certain of the things that alternate in the world, that he may show the fluctuation of all mutable things. And first he sets down good things and evils, as though describing the state of the carnal life; [0210D] then evils and good things, expressing the condition of the spiritual life. Afterwards he alternates, and changes good things and evils, and evils and good things, that he may show a certain further confusion: because neither to the evil are there good things unto the end, nor to the good evils unto the end. But even before the end, now good things and evils, now evils and good things follow one upon another. Yet this whole variation of the distribution has its beginning from good into evil, when it is said: A time to be born, and a time to die; and its end from evil into good, when it is said, a time of war and a time of peace. Because the first life has good first, and evil last. The second life has evil first, and good last; in the middle of each, with evils and goods fluctuating by an uncertain lot. But among all these things which are enumerated for an example of all things, [0211A] certain are taken from human life itself, certain from those things that belong to human life, so that the whole of what is man's may be shown mutable — man who perhaps would not go wholly into corruption, if he could have stood at least in some measure either in himself or in his own. A time to be born and a time to die, this is [sic: *devita humana*]; a time to plant and a time to pluck up what has been planted, this is from those things that belong to human life. For the work of man will perish together with man; because just as in man what is born is born that it may die, so what is planted by man is planted that it may be plucked up. For the time of every thing under heaven is not in the will of man, but in the judgment of the Maker. Since for every thing under heaven there is a time when God wills it, even [0211B] if man, when the time is, does not will what God wills. Therefore the things that God does, he always does when the time is; but the things that man does, he does not always do when the time is, since either by ignorance he does not know what is right, or by a depraved will he desires what is disordered. Therefore the time of every thing lies under the will of the Creator, when it is time that what is to be done in time be done, and everything that is done in time; if it does not accord with his will, there was a time when it was being done, but it was not the time when it ought to have been done. Therefore the works of men alone do not keep their own times, even when they are done in time; because in that part man alone does not follow the appointed time, where, abusing the liberty of choice, he does not govern himself under the will of the Maker.

[0211C]There is therefore one time which is allotted to each thing by the disposition of the Maker; another which is usurped by human presumption against the disposition of the Maker. In that time man, with the rest, submits to the law; but here he alone, beyond the rest, transgresses the law. And therefore there it is rightly done with him, even in that which he himself does not will; but here he often acts against what is right, doing nothing but what he himself wills. A time to be born, and a time to die. No one is born when he wills, even if someone sometimes dies when he wills. And yet many, when they die, do not die by will, when the time is; and many who are born by necessity are born when the time is. Even those who are born before the time are born when the time is; and [0211D] those who are born after the time are born when the time is: because necessity confers the time of being born, while a depraved will sometimes chooses the time of dying. Therefore no one offends in the time when he is born; and many offend in the time when they die. For even if nature sometimes passes by, yet the will does not offend, because that is not laid to the fault of the sufferer which not his own will concerning himself, but a just disposition from above, governs. Then therefore is there a time for every thing, when it is just that what is to be done be done, whatever that shall have been. Either by God without man, or by man, with God. For that anything should be done by man without God — there is no time for it, just as nothing just can be done by man without God. A time to be born and a time to die. A time to plant and a time [0212A] to pluck up. Here we may attend to a certain summary and universal comprehension of the mutability of all things, so that, as we distinguished at the beginning concerning the whole course of the work, so here too he may first express that mutability to which all things are subject, and then, pursuing more at large that one in which human actions fluctuate, may show it more monstrous than all and more to be feared. For these first four things which are set down seem to look especially to that mutability which is in all things that have a rising and a setting, and all the rest to belong properly to that which is engaged in human actions. For that all things that rise set, and that things increased grow old, was said on account of those things that live and feel: a time to be born and a time to die; on account of those things that live and do not [0212B] feel: a time to plant, and a time to pluck up; or else for those things that have a natural rising he set first a time to be born, and a time to die; and then for those that are fitted for quickening by care and industry he subjoined: a time to plant, and a time to pluck up. This therefore is that great vanity, vanity of vanities, for which it is too little that all things be mutable, unless they be further snatched into their contraries while all things pass away. For how great a thing it is to go far off and to sojourn away from one's own being, and to go and pass over into a contrary being, where you cannot at all be what you begin to be, unless you have utterly ceased to be what you are? There, then, the passage is hard, where by no reasoning can what follows subsist, unless first the whole of what precedes be destroyed. And what do you think, when there is a going from good things to [0212C] evils, when good things are changed into their contrary evils — what a change that is, most of all there where there is no running back, nor can any return be hoped for? For evils too are sometimes changed into good things, and again good things into evils; and often it happens that the two succeed one another by turns. Nor is that altogether grievous which is mutually tempered, so that neither are the evils great upon which good things follow, nor the good things best which are ended by evils following after; and all this can be borne, and whatever it may be, when it has come to pass, has its comparison, that it be not reckoned too much: there is one thing which, when it has befallen, who will be able to bear?

*A time to be silent and a time to speak. A time to gather and a time to lose. A time to mourn and a time [0212D] to laugh.* All these things are bearable: and when some one of them has befallen a man, that one passes and another comes; and this too, when it has stood its own time, passes, and that returns which had passed; and this happens everywhere again and again as long as one lives; and never does any one thing remain so as to be alone always.
