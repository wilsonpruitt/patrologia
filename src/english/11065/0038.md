## HOMILY XIII. *How all things have their own time.* (cont.)

*A time to be born and a time to die.* These are not like those, nor have they any running back once it has come to pass. For there is one death and one birth: and just as after one birth there is one death, so before one death there is not more than one birth. And no one dies who has not first been born; nor, when someone has once died, can he thereafter be born. Birth is the beginning of life, death the end, and there is a going from birth to death; but after death the way back to birth [0213A] does not lie open. For from birth the time of man begins, and by death it is ended. And therefore these have their time unlike all other things: the one, that in it the time is first, and it itself is no more; the other, that in it there is no more time, and it is always. This therefore is heavier than all, that it so has its time that it is itself without time, because after its time there will be no time in which what its time has taken away and consumed could be restored anew. Therefore the time to be born seems better than the time to die, because in the time of being born life is given, but in the time of dying life is taken away. And yet those who are born die, and those who die are born; since those who are born unto death begin to die from the moment they begin to live under the failing of mortality; [0213B] and those who die unto life begin truly to live from the moment when, after death, they have now ceased to be mortal. Wherefore a good death is more to be wished for than an evil life, because by a good death one proceeds to a good life, and by an evil life one arrives at an evil death. Therefore all things have their time, and nothing is to be cast away in its own time: since there is both a time to be born, that the first life may begin, which is exercised through justice in time; and a time to die, that the second life may follow, which enjoys the reward of justice in eternity. Afterwards he adds what follows:

*A time to plant, and a time to pluck up what has been planted.* This second thing therefore is of the work of the craftsman, together with nature. For the first work was of nature alone, [0213C] and that too was transitory. But now the work of the craftsman together with nature is subjoined; and that too is shown to be transitory, so that nothing may remain under the sun, the gladness which springs up from birth or from planting be tempered by the memory of death and of uprooting. Then there follow the works of the craftsman alone without the work of nature, and these too are enumerated in great number, and all are shown to be transitory, so that vanity may increase by its degrees; and yet all these things, just as by their mutability they are vain, so in their time they become fitting, so that in all these things man, even if he cannot hold the state of eternity, may yet study to keep the order of reason.

*A time to kill and a time to heal. A time to destroy and a time to build. A time to weep and a time to laugh. A time to mourn and a time to dance.* [0213D]And many other things which are enumerated, and many others which cannot be enumerated. And in all these human life suffers vicissitude and lies subject to mutability. For all these things pass over it, and run in by turns, one after another; and those that had gone before follow after, and again those that had followed go before; and without intermission this is done in the waves of great fluctuations. After weeping comes laughter, and after laughter weeping succeeds; and when joy has succeeded grief, again joys are changed into grief. And all these things have their time, when the will of man conforms itself through justice, according to the time, to the divine will, either willingly for the doing of them, or submits itself for the suffering of them. For all things have their time when man either does them according to God or suffers them for God, because for the man who walks according to [0214A] God all things in their own time, if prosperous things befall, cherish him; if adverse things happen, exercise him. Therefore all things have their time which man can justly do or justly suffer. But what cannot be done without injustice never has a time that it be done, because man has received no time for doing evil — man, to whom his time has been given for this alone, that he may either correct the evils he has done, or supply the good things he has not yet done. Therefore sins have no time; and all things have their time, because in their own time all things are to be well done; and things are good when all are well done, and out of all things sins are not among those that are to be well done, nor are sins good, although it is good that sins themselves should be. Therefore sins have no time, and sins are permitted to be done in time; [0214B] and they are not justly done when they are done, and they are justly permitted to be done when they are done; and when they have been done, they are justly ordered; and there comes a time that they be permitted to be done, and there comes no time that they be done, because they are not well done, and they are well permitted, that they may be justly ordered. A time to plant, a time to mourn, a time to dance, *a time to acquire.* Where is a time to be wanton and a time to get drunk? But those things can be well done in their own time: therefore they have their time. These have no time, because they are never to be well done. But perhaps it may seem to be the contrary that there has been set down a time to kill, a time of war, a time of hatred. But all these can be well done; and therefore they have their time, since they are to be justly done. For there is also a sword of justice, [0214C] with which kings and princes punish criminals out of zeal for equity, and put to death men condemned for their crimes, serving God in this very thing by a power received according to the precepts of the divine law. And these wage wars for the defense of the fatherland and the peace of the Churches, and fight down the enemies of peace, lest they oppress the innocent. There is also a holy hatred by which crimes are hated, not men; and this sometimes, for a wholesome terror, inflicts a strict severity outwardly for the correcting of vices, and inwardly preserves inviolate the sweetness of love, by which it thirsts for the salvation of men. Therefore there is a time of hatred, and a time of war: and all things have their time, when they are to be justly done. It is permitted me to confess what I confessed also at the beginning: that for these high and lofty matters I am too small in understanding to tell what is fitting, because [0214D] as for what is not fitting and does not agree, it is enough to have turned aside from this, if it be granted. For many are the secrets hidden in the deep of wisdom, and man is not able to search out her mysteries; and the words of wisdom, surpassing all understanding, scatter some light concerning the things that lie hidden, and that light is small in comparison with the whole. Therefore man labors when he sees it, and thinks to enter in that he may contemplate; and he is not able, unless wisdom be with him, and show him within the secrets of her ways in that part in which he has been made acceptable to her. And when he has drunk much, this much is much to him, and it is itself scant in comparison with her overflowing fullness. Therefore we have said many things about the waves of things and about the contrary agitation of those things that are under heaven; and all this is still little in comparison with the whole; and there will perhaps be those who will reckon us too lavish [0215A] in speaking, because they do not attend to the multitude of things to be said, and to the profundity of the things that have been said. We have spoken this briefly, that we may bring back again a fresh utterance to the beginning of the narrative to come, and may have our minds more eager.
