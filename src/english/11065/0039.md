## HOMILY XIV. *A declaration of the remaining things that have their own time, and a repetition of what has been said.*

*All things have their time, and all things under heaven pass away in their own spans.* Ecclesiastes had sought above some one thing in the world in which he might have rested in mind; and he did not find it among all things, whether because each one was imperfect, or because all were transitory. Therefore he began to loathe [0215B] and to reject all things; and yet he did not cease always to seek in them a full joy, and he could not find it. At length therefore, coming at last to know the condition of mutable things, he saw that he had erred in both: whether in that he charged those things which in their own kind were good with being evil, or in that he supposed that those things which were transitory could stand forever. Now therefore, correcting himself for his past estimate, he professes that all things under heaven are at once both passing away with time and good in their own time. And that the mutability and fluctuation of all these may be the better understood, how great it is, he shows that all these things not only pass away with time, but also with time go over into their contraries, saying: All things have their time, and all things under heaven pass away in their own spans. And [0215C] he then enumerates them all, distinguishing them in their own kind: *A time to be born and a time to die.* First he describes the course of mutable things according to its beginning and its end, that afterwards he may pursue more carefully the mutability and vicissitude of the things that turn in the middle. Now there are four works, by which all temporal things and all things passing away with time are unfolded. The first is the work of God; the second is the work of nature; the third is the work of the craftsman together with nature; the fourth is the work of the craftsman alone, without nature. The work of God is to create the essence of things out of nothing, to dispose the matter of things into form, and to temper the motion of things under a fixed order. Therefore these three belong to the work of God, [0215D] that is, the essence of things, their form, and their order. And these three, as the author himself afterwards testifies concerning the works of God, are stable, and do not admit the mutability of time; because the essences of things never cease to be what they are, and the forms of things, according to the first institution of their Maker, keep in their kinds a perpetual identity, and the motions of things never overstep the law of the first order. For neither could the essences of things be nothing, nor the forms of things be otherwise, nor the order and disposition of things from the beginning be otherwise than they are; but all things keep the norm of the first law and the covenant of the ancient institution; never running otherwise, they all still guard them. Therefore then let the works of God abide, because that which God made is stable in [0216A] all things, so that it may remain always. The work of God, therefore, is to create, to form, to dispose all things. The work of nature is to bring forth the seeds of things out of the hidden bosom by increase, and again, when the same wither and fall through failure, to call them back to the hidden bosom whence they had come forth. The work of the craftsman together with nature is to help by care and industry the things that rise from the earth. The work of the craftsman alone is to work upon the underlying matter of things, either by composing what is disjoined or by separating what is joined; so that it can neither create with God, that there be what was not, nor with nature bestow increase upon the offspring of things, so that greater be that which was less [sic: *crat*]

There are therefore three workers in this world: God, nature, and the craftsman who imitates nature. But these three [0216B] bring about what they produce to effect with very unequal power. For God in his work needs neither the work of nature nor that of the craftsman who imitates nature. For God sometimes works without nature, sometimes in nature, sometimes above nature. For without nature he first made nature itself. For when he made nature, he had no nature working with him. For that which was not yet could not itself work together with him who alone was. Thus therefore he first made nature without nature, so that afterwards, in nature, he might make some things with nature, others above nature. For with nature he makes those things which he brings forth from nature according to nature; above nature he makes, when in nature, beyond nature's accustomed course and first power, by a greater power added, [0216C] he brings something forth to effect. When therefore God works nature, and when he works above nature, nature does not work together with God; because there nature only receives that it should be what it was not, but here it receives that it should be able to do what it could not do out of what it had received. God therefore can work without nature; nature cannot work without God, because what nature does, it does out of that which God does, and together with that which God does. For God made the first power of nature; and that it may come forth to effect, he again himself does it together with that very nature which he makes. Everything therefore that nature does, God does; but not everything that God does, nature also does: he who without nature makes nature. In the third place there follow the works of the craftsman [0216D] who imitates nature; and he indeed sometimes works with God without nature, sometimes with nature without God, sometimes with God and with nature together, sometimes without God and equally without nature. With God he works when he works the works of justice, because God works together with a right will, one ordered according to justice. Without God he works when he works the works of iniquity, since sins are not from God, nor does that which is done against his will come to be through his cooperation. With nature the craftsman works when he applies outwardly his industry and care to the propagating of the seeds and offspring of things, to which nature supplies the increase. Without nature he works when, besides the things that look to the propagation of seeds, and to the tending of things being born [0217A] and of those things that have the power of growth and of sense, he unfolds his effort upon the underlying matter, so as to advance something, whatever it be, to effect; in which nature only suffers, and does not work, because it furnishes the matter to the worker, and does not exercise the effecting of the work. Such are all the works of men that are done upon the earth, of which many the necessity of mortal life compels, many desire urges, many vanity works. And in all these, because nothing stable is found, and all things pass away that are done under the sun — those which either nature brings forth, or the craftsman imitating [sic: *imitant*] nature fashions — the contemplator of wisdom subjects all things to vanity, proving that the works of God alone abide; since neither is a created essence ever consumed, nor is the reason of the disposition [0217B] or order of things changed. Therefore he shows first the works of nature made subject to vanity, whether in those things which nature alone works, because things that rise set; or in those things which the craftsman does together with nature, because things increased grow old. Afterwards he enumerates many and diverse works of the craftsman who imitates nature: some of necessity, some of desire, some of vanity; and he subjects all to vanity, because he shows them liable to mutability. The work of nature alone is told when it is said: A time to be born and a time to die.

Then the work of the craftsman working together with nature is brought in, when it is said: *A time to plant and a time to pluck up.* Last of all the work of man without the work of nature is added, when it is brought in: *A time to kill and a time to heal; a time to destroy and [0217C] a time to build.* And this is then set forth more manifoldly, with more things numbered up, that it may be plainly shown in how many things the vanity of the human heart is entangled and how widely it is scattered. For this pertained most of all to the matter: that the vanity of human minds, which follows the uncertainty of mortal life — because he had undertaken to reprove it — he should both set forth more diligently in the narrative, and set before the rest in the comparison; so that all the others might seem to have been recalled for this alone, that by comparison with them it might be recognized how great this one is. A time to be born and a time to die. It is a time to be born, when man is to be led into this life. It is a time to die, when he is to be called forth from this life. But meanwhile, after [0217D] birth and before death, while he lives in this mortal life, which needs sustenance and is nourished with food, that it may subsist. It is a time to plant and a time to pluck up, that man may till the earth from which he was taken, and sow, and reap, and plant, and gather. Now these two following things can also be compared with the two preceding by a distinction, in this way: A time to be born and a time to plant; a time to die and a time to pluck up. For when a man is born it is a time to plant, that earthly life may be fed with earthly fruits. But when a man dies, it is a time to pluck up; because when mortal life is destroyed, those things also which had been established for the sake of mortal life are taken away. And because to human infirmity, after the fruits of the earth, the use of flesh [0218A] is also granted, therefore after the time to plant and the time to pluck up there is fittingly brought in also a time to kill. And because in this eating a measure is necessary, lest the flesh take for an incitement to vices what it has received for the sustaining of its infirmity, immediately after the time to kill he adds a time to heal: namely, that in all other times we may study with careful concern to nourish and preserve the life of those very animals which we have received to be killed at the opportune time — that is, when necessity demands — for the sustaining of our infirmity. For this is, as it were, to heal the ailing, by coming to their aid in those things wherein they are not sufficient for themselves, and to repair what is failing. Whence perhaps, fittingly, when he had said, A time to kill, he did not add, a time to heal [0218B] what has been killed. As before, after he had said a time to plant, he immediately added, *and a time to pluck up what has been planted.* Namely because things planted can be plucked up, but things killed cannot be healed. Thus perhaps, if we follow the sense of the letter, we prejudge nothing of the spiritual understanding (which is to be traced out afterwards).

*A time to destroy, and a time to build.* When man ought to destroy and when to build, someone may reckon is by no means to be asked; since he may think that here this alone is shown: that man, if by chance he is subjected to various things by the events of the times, suffers these things unequally, even unwilling. But we, applying to an easier understanding these things which have been [0218C] said, can say that it is a time to kill when God, willing to punish the sins of men, brings the sword of slaughter for chastisement; but a time to heal when, cherishing again the afflicted and those wounded by the strokes of tribulations, he restores them with his wonted compassion. And plainly this may seem more consonant with truth, that here we ought to take the times of things not as those which serve the human will, but as those which obey the divine disposition. For neither is it the time of anything when man wills and God does not will. Because man often wills when it is not the time, and when God wills it is always the time. Therefore all times are referred to the divine ordinance; and it is said to be then the time of every thing, when it is justly disposed by God, [0218D] whether for the chastisement and correction of a crime, or for the consolation of infirmity. A time to kill and a time to heal. A time to destroy and a time to build.

*A time to weep and a time to laugh. A time to mourn and a time to dance.* Everywhere evils are enumerated first, afterwards good things are added; because first we are justly afflicted for our sins, afterwards we are kindly cherished again by God's compassion. And it is manifest in what way human life daily suffers the vicissitude of all these things, as often as by a hidden dispensation God either instructs us with scourges or consoles us with gifts.
