## HOMILY XV. *On time and on the things subject to time, according to another interpretation.*

Let it not therefore irk us to attempt once again the hardness of these parables, until perhaps, the bark being worn away by the use of speech, the inward parts of its word may lie open. For we are in times, and our discourse rolls along with us, until we be made stable in that which is not changed by time; and this is, that we should contemplate above all human actions, because in them is the whole of vanity, over which we groan.

*A time to kill and a time to heal.* What were the times of killing and the times of healing? A pitiable beginning of human actions, from slaying and [0220A] slaughter. So must the work of a life given over to death have begun, so as to follow the sentiment of its author: *Accursed, raging unto its own slaughter.* Let us run over in mind the times past, and see the wars and slaughters made from the beginning until now, of nation upon nation and of innumerable peoples. And God bore with the human race raging against its own bowels; and in all these things he wrought his judgments, twisting the furies of men to the vindication of his justice, so that some justly suffered what others unjustly willed. And others rose up, and many were slain and were damned; and many were slain and were saved; and some were freed from the death of slaughter and were kept for damnation; and others were freed lest they be damned if they were slain. In all these he fulfilled his just judgment [0220B] upon them, so as to chastise and to spare according to the governance of that equity by which God disposes all things. Therefore there is a time to kill and a time to heal; and some do what he willed, others suffer what they do not will, and the just Arbiter sits above, doing what he wills and disposing all things that he may be feared. A time to kill and a time to heal: *A time to destroy and a time to build.* How great things from the beginning have the lovers of this world built, and have called their own name upon their own land, that they might have a glorious fame among those who come after. For they labored with heavy labor in their works, that the monuments of their boasting might stand after them; and they prevailed to a certain point, leaving to posterity great signs of their industry; and there still appear certain traces of ruins among them, of ages [0220C] gone by; and we marvel at those very men whom we do not see, that they could do such things, and we groan the more over them, because, though they themselves were men created for eternity, they loved vain things and vanished away, because they were made vain in themselves. For they themselves perished, and after them their works fell, and others came in their place; and these too did great works, and after these went into nothing; and all that they had made was destroyed together with them. And again others rose up and built, and did not understand where the former generation was, and its works, so as to see the vanity which is under the sun. And God permitted them to labor in vain, and to build things that would perish, that man might understand his own vanity; that what has been [0220D] built be destroyed, and again that what has been destroyed be built; and that the work of man upon the earth be not stable. Therefore *all things have their time,* that man may be instructed concerning his own vanity, and concerning the uncertainty of the mortal life through which he walks.

*A time to weep and a time to laugh: a time to mourn and a time to dance.* Weeping and laughter pertain to the mind. But mourning — or, to speak more properly, wailing — and dancing pertain to the body. By weeping, then, and by laughter, the grief and gladness of the mind are expressed; but wailing and dancing signify these same things, only when they are shown forth by the motion of the body. A time to weep, and a time to laugh: [sic: *templus*] to wail, and a time to dance. All these things have their time among men, while this mortal life runs on; and God permits, by a varying lot and by unequal outcome, [0221A] according to the height of his judgments and the depth of his counsel by which he disposes us, that these things should run and be varied over us, alternating prosperity with adversity, and changing adversity into prosperity in due time. And glad times come, and men laugh and dance and think it is well with them; and they often forget their Creator, and do not consider that they are to die. For they look upon present things alone, and rejoice in them as though they were to abide always; nor do they attend to their own condition and to the doubtfulness of mortal life, nor do they think of the perils that are to come upon them. And therefore they sin securely, and pour themselves out into harmful pleasures, and walk after the desires of their heart in the gaze of their eyes, so as to do what is not fitting, and to multiply wrath and indignation. And God sees this, and sows over them [0221B] griefs and bitternesses upon the wantonness of ill-sweet pleasures; and he calls back and restrains them as they flow forth, that they may not go into utter estrangement. Then comes a time to weep, and a time to wail; and men grieve, and often lay the blame upon God when it is dealt well with them. But he dissembles their depravity, and after a time mingles prosperity again with adversity, lest they fail; and again, when it is time, adversity with prosperity, lest they presume; and this he often does, nourishing and exercising, chastising and cherishing man, that he may learn to be fearful, and may know that with the Lord is the power of life and of death. Therefore there is a time to weep, and a time to laugh: a time to wail, and a time to dance. And upon these there follows a time to scatter stones, and a time to gather. This time is enumerated after those, but [0221C] is ordered among them: because in time there are often together causes that lead to one reckoning of time. *A time to scatter stones, and a time to gather.* The whole world is an exile to those for whom heaven ought to have been the fatherland: yet the minds of mortals grow together with it by use, and men begin to love their own places, and their own lands in which they were born or reared; and a great forgetfulness of things eternal succeeds, for the sake of affection for things temporal; and unless God wiped away these ingrown sweetnesses of the passions, there was no way by which desire for him might enter in to us. Therefore he gives his judgments, and there come commotions and shakings of nations and migrations of peoples; and some rise up to snatch what is not their own, and others are plundered and scattered; and again the stronger, to whom the power has been given, when they too have come, [0221D] cast out those who had violently snatched what belonged to others, and possess what is not their own; and the world fluctuates within itself, so that man may see that there is here no stable dwelling, and may accustom himself little by little to draw back his mind and loose it from the bonds of earthly delights. Therefore there comes a time to scatter stones, that there be no stable dwelling upon the earth, and that man may be forced to sigh in his exile for the fatherland, where joys remain unshaken, and the security of peace is troubled by no infestation coming upon it. This, then, is what the scattering of stones does in its own time. But because human infirmity quickly collapses, and cannot long stand unharmed amid adversities, after the time of scattering there comes a time of gathering: that by the quiet of the season it may grow strong, being admonished in this very thing [0222A] to be wise, and to learn by experience how great are the joys of the rest to come; that it may hasten to that place where there is no fear of scattering, nor is the hardship of exile dreaded, to whom eternity of life in the fatherland has been given. A time to scatter stones, and a time to gather. Then again another time follows after that time; and others are named which themselves also have their time — often one time together with those times that have been spoken of, often, apart from those times, another separately in its own time.

*A time to embrace, and a time to be far from embraces.* A time has been given to chaste embraces, that flesh may be sown for propagation, and that the harvest of the human race, which daily throughout the whole world is reaped by death, may be repaired by the care of generation. For the covenant of the first institution, by which flesh was to be taken from flesh [0222B] without the contagion of the flesh, is after the sin of the first transgression confirmed in the flesh of sin by indulgence, not only for the fulfilling of a duty, but also for the conferring of a remedy: namely, lest the motion of the flesh, now boiling up too immoderately, should flow out more shamefully into every transgression, if it could nowhere be lawfully relaxed. A boundary is therefore prescribed, up to which the affection of the flesh may extend itself without loss of chastity: so that what is done in the chastity of matrimony by concession, even if it belong to infirmity, may yet not be imputed to shamefulness. But lest again the blandishments of pleasure turn the mind aside into forgetfulness of the Maker, by a wholesome dispensation man is sometimes suspended even from what is conceded: lest perhaps, if he gape too immoderately after these things that seem sweet in the present, he should not hasten to those things that are eternal [0222C]. Therefore a time to embrace has been given, and a time to be far from embraces. For a time to embrace has been given, that mortality may be repaired and the infirmity of mortals sustained; a time to be far from embraces has been given, that while the flesh is withdrawn from the delight of the flesh, the mind may be nourished unto spiritual delight. A time to embrace and a time to be far from embraces. For when the stones are gathered, it is a time to embrace; but when they are scattered, a time to be far from embraces: since for this reason the times of [sic: *augustiae*] have been given, that the afflicted flesh may temper itself from its own delights, and that afterwards, in the time of joy and consolation, it may chastise itself with fear and reverence from excess. There are also the embraces of dear ones and the fellowships [0222D] of friendly society, in which, apart from the experience of the flesh, a sweet companionship is bound together by a most welcome consent — and these too are loosed in their own time, lest the heart of man rest in man, until there come that covenant of eternal charity which is not loosed. On account of all these things there is a time to embrace, and a time to be far from embraces: so that in time man may not seek what is eternal, and that he who loves the society of a living man may always think of the separation of one who is to die.

*A time to acquire, and a time to lose.* What are the good things that are first acquired and afterwards lost? For certain good things had been lost and were afterwards acquired, so that thenceforth they could not be lost. And there were again other good things unlike the former, [0223A] which nevertheless seemed good: and there was truly something in them toward good, but not as the first good things which were true. And these good things were not the good things of man toward the true good: and man came, after he had lost the true, and acquired false good things, reckoning himself happy in them. For he willed to console himself and to have in time what eternity confers; and he gathered things that would perish, which he could not hold; and God granted this, that he might recognize the distance of the good, and be instructed by his own vanity, so as to turn himself to those things that are true. Therefore, then, God let go his heart, that he might gather together those things that cannot stand; and these very things, in their own time, he now grants to be acquired, now takes away when acquired: testing man in all his works, and showing that all things under heaven are vanity. For this a time is given [0223B] to acquire, and a time to lose: that while man has obtained what he had coveted, he may learn by experience what these joys can confer upon those who greatly esteem things that perish; but when he loses what he had, he may recognize that all things which could be taken from him against his will had been foreign to him even while they were possessed.

*A time to keep, and a time to cast away.* Temporal goods are to be kept in their own time, as much as the necessity of temporal life demands; but because in the death of the flesh everything that belongs to the pleasantness of carnal life is of necessity lost, it is worthy that even before death it be cast away by the will from the love of the heart.
