## HOMILY XV. *On time and on the things subject to time, according to another interpretation.* (cont.)

*A time to rend, and a time to sew.* He who rends divides what is united; but he who sews joins together what is divided and [0223C] separated. All these things this man had foreseen to be done without ceasing in the life of men, so long as it lies subject to mutability: in which life the fellowship of human converse is now to be sought after, because it seems to confer something of usefulness; and now to be fled, because often the same brings loss. Social consent, therefore, has its own sewing in its own time; the zeal of a just contradiction has also its own rending in its own time: so that both with those who wish well and with those who do good we may be united in assent and in work; but from those who love depraved things and work iniquities we may be divided in mind and equally in working. But lest perhaps this sentiment seem the same as that in which it was said above, a time to embrace and a time to be far from embraces — since in that place likewise we asserted that the conjunction and separation [0223D] of social love was treated of — the things that were said there can not unfittingly be taken as signifying only love and affection, while the things that are recalled here are referred to the figuring of any fellowship whatever, or of any compact in which there is the same consent and the same pleasure of concord. Thus do the likenesses of things offer themselves for the understanding of wisdom: and wisdom herself uses her own works that she may manifest herself in us. There are many things that are said in parables and worthy of deep searching; but they do not all wish to come out, that they may always be sought within.

*A time to be silent, and a time to speak.* First a time to be silent, afterwards a time to speak. For wisdom goes out from [0224A] the heart, nor has the tongue from anywhere else what it may rightly bring forth, unless understanding first form the word. Therefore first there is a time to be silent, after that a time to speak: so that first, in silence, shrewd thought may gather wisdom into itself, before the tongue afterwards sends it forth by word to the hearing of others. For this reason too there is a time to be silent and a time to speak, since the hearers of the word sometimes receive it with desire and love, but sometimes without reverence despise it by casting it away. Wherefore it is a time to be silent, when we see hearers in every way impatient of admonition; but a time to speak, when we see that they either desire the word or at least are willing to bear it. For this reason also there is a time to be silent, because in those things which we cannot discuss we ought not [0224B] to presume. And likewise a time to speak: because as for other things that we can comprehend, it is necessary, for the instruction of our neighbors, not to keep silent. Whence not only the works of men, but their words also, follow the uncertainty of fleeting life, so that vanity occupies the whole and nothing stable or constant is left: since we can neither always say what we know, nor always retain what we have learned; and now it may be harmful to have kept silent the things that ought to have been said, but now it may be a fault to have said the things that ought to have been kept silent. Likewise we often cannot when we will, and we do not will to be wise when we can; but more often we ought not to do either, when we do it. In this there is great uncertainty, and the gloom of a deep ignorance: so that man is tossed from time to time, and does not understand his own time in all his work [0224C].

*A time of love, and a time of hatred.* Many are the things that are loved in this world, and likewise very many that are hated. And in all the things that are loved, few love truth; and in those that are hated, few hate iniquity. For this reason men love, and often think that they love well, and love ill: because in the things they love they do not love truth, nor do they love them on account of truth. Likewise very many practice hatreds, and boast of their zeal for justice and equity; and within there lurks strife in the heart, and a savage fury feeding itself on unjust hatred. And therefore love and hatred cannot at the first discern their own time: because they do not follow the judgment of justice and truth, but are borne by their own inclination into depraved appetites, so that they do what they will. [0224D] They love and they hate, they cherish and they are zealous, often for things they ought not, often when they ought not; and there comes a great confusion of love and hatred, and the heart is scattered into universal vanity, fleeing the things that are right and coveting the things that are depraved. Therefore a time of love and of hatred has been set before man: that he may understand and discern when, out of those things which according to the time can be good and evil, he ought either to seek after something or to turn aside from it. For outside the times there are certain things which are not changed by time so as to be able to be otherwise: and among these there are good things and evil. And the things that are good are eternal, so that they are always good; and the things that are evil are likewise eternal, so that they are always evil. It is not of those that it is said to thee: A time of love, and a time of hatred. For to those belongs eternal love, and eternal [0225A] hatred: since the things that are to be loved are there always to be loved, and the things that are to be hated are always to be hated. But meanwhile, now in time, while all things are uncertain, the things that meet us as we pass by change themselves according to the time, so that what had stood as fortunate for one gives way into evil for another; and what this man has grieved over as adverse, that man recognizes as having turned out happily for himself. Therefore in all these things that pass away with time there is a time of love and a time of hatred: so that man may not fix his heart upon them either by love or by hatred; things which, when held in hatred, can again be justly loved, and, when loved, can again be justly held in hatred.

*A time of war, and a time of peace.* On account of the time to kill, a time of war is ordained; on account of the time to heal, [0225B] a time of peace follows. And that was the first, but this was recalled last of all: so that the beginning and the end may be enclosed with all the labors and perils that lie between; so that the life of man, which passes through only to cease, may never be safe. For man went out that he might withdraw and not stand with God; and he made a covenant of love with the pleasure of this world, that he might rest in it. And God would not endure that covenant, that man should abide in estrangement; and he raised up against him the things he had perversely loved, that they might oppose and afflict him; and there came about for man a war with all things that are in the world, that he might find grief and affliction in them until he return to peace with God. Then all things will compose themselves to peace with him, so that the things that had been adverse to the exalted may be subject [0225C] to the humble. For at the first all things had been at peace, just as at the last all things will be pacified: so that in the middle the things that pass may run their course by an uncertain lot; until they come to this, that either, for one falling under judgment, all things be adverse forever, or, happily, consummation, all things be made subject. For where hatred will be eternal, there will also be perpetual war; and where there will be eternal love, there everlasting peace will stand fast. Nor will it be said there, a time of peace and a time of war, just as it will not be said, a time of love and a time of hatred: but there will be war, and peace; war without end, and without end peace. Let not man therefore reckon that war greatly to be feared to which peace can succeed; nor that peace true which a war following after can disturb: because all things that pass away are to be reckoned vain in this very thing, that in [0225D] good, and likewise in evil, they cannot always remain.

*What more has man from his labor?* Therefore the labor of man runs in uncertainty: because he cannot have more from his labor, lest man be content with those things that consist in his own labor; because all things are vanity, and pass away with time, to be ended with time.
