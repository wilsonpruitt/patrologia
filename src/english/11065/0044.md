## HOMILY XVI. *On the spiritual understanding of those things which have been said concerning time.* (cont.)

*A time to mourn, or to wail, and a time to dance.* First the flesh ought in penitence to be afflicted with torments for its depraved delight, afterwards to be exercised in good works in the pursuit of virtues. Thus assuredly the life of the just is instructed by griefs, and is prepared by punishments for joys, that [0229D] it may come clean to its own fruit; and its evils always go before that they may pass away, and its good things are deferred to the end that they may remain without end. And we can consider in this narrative a certain order of the correcting and amending of a soul that is to be justified. For the first of all is that in him who is corrected the depraved will be slain, and then the good, either infirm, be [sic: *sanctur*], or dead, be made alive. For what lives ill is well killed; and what is ill infirm is well healed. After the will has been changed, the ill-built mass of depraved works is destroyed, and the pursuits of good works, scattered by long negligence, are rebuilt. Then, for past fault, weeping follows, which, when it has cleansed the conscience with a fountain of tears, [0230A] is at last changed into the laughter of consolation and into joy. There follow also in penitence wailings, or mournings, when the grief of the mind is carried on even to the affliction of the flesh, so that afterwards, with the exultation of the mind, the flesh also, though worn down, may be gladdened by its own consolation

*A time to scatter stones and a time to gather.* If by stones we understand the strong works of the virtues, what else ought we to understand in the scattering of stones but the multiplication of good works? For to scatter stones is to offer far and wide, to the knowledge of many, the examples of strong works. But to gather stones is, after the pursuit of labor, to receive the fruit of good works. It is therefore a time to scatter stones, and again a time to gather stones; because a man ought first to exercise himself in good works in the pursuits of the active life [0230B], that he may be able afterwards to receive the fruit of his own work in the taste of contemplation. And because that taste is most pleasant to those who enjoy it and full of the highest delight, so that it seems to excel all the things that can be loved, there is fittingly subjoined at once:

*A time to embrace.* For there are certain embraces of wisdom and delightful bonds of inward love: when the mind, admitted within, lays hold of the joy of its own love, and, binding it fast with certain arms of its desires, makes a covenant and demands a compact of perpetual abiding. But because mortal life is quickly cut off and driven back from the sight of the inward light by the gloom of its own darkness, and by the mutability of its own condition is separated from the fellowship of eternal stability, [0230C] there is fittingly subjoined:

*A time to be far from embraces.* For after the time to embrace there follows a time to be far from embraces; because the human mind, although sometimes, lifted up by grace, it is admitted to the touch of inward [sic: *dulcedidinis*], yet, soon pressed by the weight of its own infirmity, slips back again to earthly things and to its accustomed thoughts. Although this sentiment may seem to belong particularly to the evil, in that it recalls as it were prosperous and good things at its beginning, but adverse things at its end, yet let it suffice to have observed this distinction in the preceding six which we have expounded, so that we may apply the rest that follow rather to a general signification. For one thing is noted by the disposition, and another is expressed by the signification; because the disposition [0230D] of the order suggests as it were a distinction of persons, whereas the signification shows by the sentiment of things only the truth. Therefore what is told of the evil only, or of the good, with the order of speaking changed, is not unfittingly taken without distinction of the good and the evil alike, so that sometimes this even belongs rather to the good according to the signification, which, so far as pertains to the order of the narrative, shows forth the life of the reprobate. Yet we can also take this very thing as not unfittingly said of any of the reprobate: who, even if they sometimes seem to draw near, as it were, to the embrace of wisdom, by the love of the good according to a certain affection having the appearance of virtue, yet because they do not divide their mind from carnal [0231A] desires and earthly cupidities, quickly slipping back to their accustomed ways, in that very thing wherein they seemed to have drawn near, they are made far off. As therefore the elect are for a time held back from the embrace of the highest truth, that by the very deferring of it they may burn the more with desire of it, and at last may more fully grasp what they have desired, so the reprobate too are for a time in a certain manner permitted — that they may see whereby they are confounded, and that malice may not be excused — to approach to the touching of it by some knowledge. But this is the difference: that the just and the elect, after they have tasted the sweetness of the inward good, are for this reason for a time driven back, that they may burn the more with desire; whereas the reprobate, after they have recognized the truth, are cast away, that from that very knowledge they may take against themselves the testimony of their own damnation. The former, then, are admitted [0231B] and driven back that they may be provoked; the latter are admitted and cast away that they may be confounded. Thus the philosophers of the gentiles were admitted in very great part; but they did not touch wisdom from near at hand, nor did they draw her to themselves that they might fully enjoy her sweetness. For they saw and knew, and thought that they loved; but it was a foreign love from far off, commending her appearance, but not drinking in her sweetness. From afar, as with arms stretched out and hands spread wide, they wished to make an embrace; and the beloved was not between the breasts, that he might be strongly held fast, and make delay, and abide; and therefore quickly and swiftly he fled from them, nor could he be held back by a love that was a stranger. The beloved fled, then. But the beloved does not flee that he may escape; rather he only hides himself, and conceals himself for the provoking of love, [0231C] that he may be sought in desire and not found, until he be most ardently loved. And often he returns again as though about to abide, and offers himself to be experienced; and after a little he slips away again, reserving the fruit for hereafter. And in all these things there is a time to embrace, and a time to be far from embraces.

*A time to acquire, and a time to lose.* When is it a time to acquire, save when it is a time to embrace? And again, when is a time to lose, save when it is a time to be far from embraces? For when the beloved is present, the increase of all the virtues is multiplied, and the inner house is filled with abundance and richness. Then barren things bear [0231D] fruit, and the things that had withered are fattened with fruitfulness, and the soul itself marvels at the riches it possesses. Holy affections blaze, and chaste desires, while adverse motions are lulled to sleep; and whatever had been wont to fight back, or to stand in the way importunately, is made far off, so that all things are at peace and quiet. The power of doing well is supplied according to one's wish, and the abundance of a rich will overflows even to the multiplication of holy works. This therefore is a time to acquire, when holy merits are heaped up, and the wages of good works are multiplied in the treasuries of eternal riches. But lest perhaps continual increase should in some measure cast down the human heart through elation, there follows a time to lose: that while by a wholesome dispensation the increase of the virtues is taken from him for a time, he may be the better [0232A] made solid in virtue through humility. And thus the holy mind, resting within in the embrace of wisdom, is increased with the riches of the virtues; but outwardly, scattered through occupation, it is pinched with want and worn thin by poverty: just as, on the contrary, carnal men gather by their labor riches that will perish, and when they have begun to be at leisure for the embraces of pleasures, they soon consume the same by living idly and luxuriously. We can also fittingly take the times of acquiring and the times of losing in another understanding. For there are two lives, namely the active and the contemplative: of which the one, that is the active, in the pursuit of good works gathers by laborious exercise the gains of holy merits; but the other, that is the contemplative, in the knowledge of truth [sic: *peccata*] rests by love. Which lives indeed we rightly understand to have been signified by [0232B] the two wives of Jacob, namely Leah and Rachel: of whom the one, that is Leah, is said to have been of bleared eyes indeed, but fruitful; whereas the other, that is Rachel, is reported to have been of beautiful eyes, but barren. For the active life is fruitful in the offspring of good work, but the contemplative is clear-sighted in the knowledge of truth. And because in each life the virtue of the saints is exercised by turns, and is turned about by a kind of alternating change — namely, that now its patience is proved in the labor of work, now its desire is refreshed in the sweetness of contemplation — rightly is it now said: A time to acquire, and a time to lose. For it is a time to acquire when the devout mind multiplies in the pursuit of good work the gains of merits, and then as it were a time to lose follows, [0232C] when for love of inmost quiet it willingly bears the losses of working. For as it were willingly it sets aside the riches of good work, that it may be able to rest, even with little, in the sweetness of contemplation. Yet from this nothing of its merits is diminished, which it earns more by the very desire and love of the highest good than by the pursuit of work. A time to acquire, and a time to lose. Concerning any of the perverse it is a settled thing: that if at any time they acquire something of good by acting rightly, they quickly lose it, because they have no perseverance in the good begun. To whom assuredly the time of acquiring could profit nothing, because for them there followed the time of losing the things acquired. And these a greater loss often follows in this very thing, because for the things they have acquired they swell up, but for the things they lose they are not humbled.

[0232D] *A time to keep and a time to cast away.* There is a time that God should keep his elect from temptation, because perhaps they might have been broken by adversities; and again there is a time to cast away, that when they are to be exercised with troubles he may, as though angry, wholesomely expose them for a time to temptation. But God also often keeps the evil safe and quiet in this life from the troubles that press upon others, that afterwards in [sic: *judicie*] he may cast them away the more heavily, the more patiently he bears them here also, while he protects his adversaries. But man himself too ought to keep many things for a time, which afterwards in their own time he should cast away: things which, kept in their own time, can bring aid to him who keeps them; and again, unless in their own [0233A] time they be cast away, can make a hindrance. For earthly substance is rightly to be kept for a time and for the sustaining of the flesh; and again in its own time, if peril to the soul is known to stand in it, it is without doubt to be cast away. There are also many things in spiritual discipline and in the pursuit of virtues which for a time one must now keep, now cast away: lest perhaps, if a work of virtue be done without discretion, that very thing be reckoned to fault which might have been counted for merit. For all things are changed with time, and there is not anything that always remains the same; and therefore he who lives under time ought to weigh the causes and outcomes of all things against their times.
