## HOMILY XVI. *On the spiritual understanding of those things which have been said concerning time.* (cont.)

*A time of rending, and a time of sewing.* Everyone who lives in the flesh according to the flesh is, through the affection of the flesh, [0233B] as it were one with those things which the flesh covets. It is necessary therefore that the man who is turned from carnal things to spiritual should first be torn away in mind from those things to which he had clung by affection. For then indeed he freely glues himself to spiritual things by love, when nothing is left over of earthly affections to turn his mind aside into alien desires. And so it is a time of rending, that first a man may tear his mind away from earthly desires; afterward a time of sewing, that in mind, through spiritual love, he may begin to cleave to eternal and abiding goods. Nor does this seem to be passed over, that the things which are rent are substantially one; whereas the things which are sewn are indeed essentially diverse, but by the mediating bond of the thread become as it were accidentally one. For so every [0233C] man is united to corruptible and perishable things by nature, but is made a partaker of eternal things by grace. For thus human things and divine are sewn into one, so that what had been two by nature become one by grace. Compunction indeed is the needle itself, and love the thread; and the needle makes the way, and pierces both, that the thread may hold both together. For the sewing could not have been made otherwise, unless both were bored through and both held together, since the one would depart from the other, and would easily divide itself from unity, if the bond did not hold both. For this reason the needle of compunction pierces both, and the thread of love binds both together, that they may stand together and not depart from one another. For the needle of compunction pierces human things [0233D] when it pursues fault; it pierces divine things when it searches out the hidden things of eternal things. For thus compunction stands twofold, and there is an upper watering and a lower watering *(Josue, XV)*. Through the upper watering divine things are pierced, and through the lower watering human things are pierced. For through the compunction which rises from fault the sinful conscience is transfixed; and through the compunction which rises from the desire of eternal things the hidden things are penetrated. The one subtly penetrates the conscience, that it may drive out harmful things; the other by deep consideration searches out invisible things, that it may lay hold of the things it loves. So both are bored through, and both are transfixed: the one by grief, by which guilt is rooted out, the other by desire, by which the things coveted are sought out. After these follows the thread of love: [0234A] by which on both sides the bond of unity is effected, so that things thenceforth joined may not be separated from one another. For love rises on both sides, and the bond of love runs together into one, because the human mind, looking back, sees there how great things have been entrusted to it through mercy; but considering divine things, it contemplates in them how great things have been promised it unto glory. And it loves both, and the knot of charity is made in both, because mercy is sweet unto glory, and glory pleasant unto mercy. And the love of these cannot be separated, because the sewing has been made, and the bonds of eternal charity stand firm. The time of rending therefore was then, but now is the time of sewing.

*A time of keeping silence, and a time of speaking. He that heareth,* saith the Scripture, *let him say: Come [n: (Apoc. XXII)]*. For he who [0234B] first opens the ear of the heart by obedience to the word of God, he afterward rightly looses his tongue to speak to his neighbor. For he who has not first learned by obedience to keep silence before another in the word of edification is convicted of lying under blame, if he himself presumes to teach others. Therefore first is the time of keeping silence, that a man may learn what he is to teach; and then the time of speaking, that he who has now known the truth may not blush to speak it in its own time. For each is a fault: namely, both when a man rashly presumes what he ought not, and when through negligence he is sluggish about that which he ought. And therefore we must first beware lest we commit unlawful things, and afterward take pains lest we neglect what is due. A time of keeping silence, and a time of speaking. Times succeed one another, and a time comes after times.

[0234C] *A time of love, and a time of hatred.* If anyone shall say that in the time of love there is a time of speaking, and that in the time of hatred there is a time of keeping silence, so that what is holy be not given to dogs, nor pearls cast before swine [n: (Matth. VII)], but that the just man set his finger upon his mouth when the days are evil, and be dumb when the sinner stands against him: has not justice therefore come upon the earth, that sinners may be reproved, and that the wicked may be converted, lest they perish? For this reason times are ambiguous, since it belongs to them to run their course into uncertainty. Yet nothing is without a time, whether it be love or hatred. For love has its time, and hatred its time. For to him who is commanded to love his neighbor as himself, to the same it is said that he should hate himself. [0234D] Whence it stands that he who loves God as himself, when in God he loves his neighbor: so, against God, he does not know how to love even himself. There is therefore a time of love, and a time of hatred, so that we who for God's sake strive to love our enemies, when God's cause is injured, may not presume to love even our friends. There is yet another love, and another hatred: that each man both love his own flesh, and that in those things which are necessary for sustaining its weakness, and hate it in those things which demand depraved desires. And so there is a time of love, and a time of hatred, since it behooves us, so long as we live under the defect of this mortality and carry the contradiction of sin in our flesh, to weigh with great discretion applied, when we ought by compassion to cherish our failing nature, lest it fall away, and when again [0235A] to press down by afflicting it the motion of vices rising up in the flesh, lest it grow strong. For it is written: *No man ever hated his own flesh [n: (Ephes. V)]*. And again it is said: *Make not provision for the flesh in its desires [n: (Rom. XIII)]*. Whence it is plainly shown that the flesh is both to be loved, so far as pertains to compassion for nature, and again, so far as regards the chastisement of fault, to be hated. And because in this there is need of a great governance of discretion, a time of love and of hatred is prescribed, so that walking along the middle boundary we may neither smother nature while we pursue fault, nor nourish fault when we cherish nature. But because this cannot be done without great toil and heavy wrestling, there is subjoined: A time of war, and a time of peace, that a man may at once acknowledge both that no one without a fight [0235B] comes through to victory, and that he who lawfully strives rests in peace after victory is obtained. For the time of peace is set after the time of war for this reason, that he who could be broken by the weight of the struggle may be consoled by the gift of peace. A time of war, and a time of peace. There are three who stir up war against us: namely the devil, and the world, and our flesh. For Paul knew that he had all these as enemies, who asserted that he had undertaken a fight against them all. For he testified that the fight against demons was continual for him, equally with all the faithful, when he said: *For our wrestling is not against flesh and blood: but against the powers and principalities; against the rulers of these darknesses, against the spiritual things of wickedness in the heavenly places [n: (Eph. VI)]*. Again against the world, that [0235C] is, perverse men, lovers of the world, he asserted that there had been a fight for him, who related that he had fought against beasts at Ephesus [n: (I Cor. XV)]. Likewise he testifies that against his own flesh there is for him a ceaseless fight, saying: *So I fight, not as one beating the air; but I chastise my body, and bring it into servitude: lest perhaps, when I have preached to others, I myself become a reprobate [n: (I Cor. IX)]*. There are therefore three tyrants who lead forth their armies into battle against us: and each has his own battle lines drawn up for making war upon the soul. The devil indeed draws up columns of suggestions against the faithful soul; the world brings forth prosperity and adversity to overcome us; but the flesh, stirring up crowds of carnal desires, rises up into battle against us. But against all these [0235D] the faithful soul must contend manfully, and, fortified by God's protection, must ply the javelins of the virtues to war down the forces of the adverse power. For we conquer the devil, if with perseverance we refuse to receive his suggestions. We conquer the world, if we both steadfastly despise its prosperity and patiently endure its adversities. And we conquer the flesh, if we do not consent to its depraved desires. But enemies once conquered do not at once cease, so that there might be peace continually; but they attempt again, and again they contrive, and try how they can: and often, as if daring, though they are timid, they themselves make an assault with terror, that they may test what spirit is in us; often they lay ambushes, and advance secretly to explore, [0236A] and hide themselves, and wish to be hidden, until they come upon us unforeseen. And in all these things many perils arise, and daily circumspection, and fear on every side: and there comes to be a great war, and a long-lasting struggle, until our side grows strong in every way, and they, despairing utterly, cease and depart from their hope; so that they may either be frightened away from us, or be subjected to our dominion. For this also will come to pass without doubt, if we have not failed from our hope and from good patience, that at length a full and entire peace may be restored after the labor of the struggle is completed. For then the devil shall be put to flight from us by divine power, so that he dare not presume against us; but terrified he shall flee, and depart into confusion. The world too itself, which was wont to rage in its commotions, [0236B] shall be pacified, and the desires of the flesh, the spirit being strengthened, shall be lulled asleep. Then in the time of our peace we shall remember the ancient times, when there was war, and we shall not then repent: but we shall be glad, and shall exult for the days in which we saw evils; and there shall be sweet peace in thanksgiving for past labors. For this reason there is a time of war, and a time of peace

*What hath a man more of his labor?* What has he more than war and peace, love and hatred, and all the other things which the wheel of its own turning brings forth as the outcome of times? He has therefore no more of his labor than war and peace: since by this labor of his he cannot bring it about that he have peace [0236C] alone. Therefore neither by his labor does he have peace, just as by his will he does not have war. For all things follow the nod of the supernal disposition: nor are the moments of times subject to human choice, that this or that should come to pass; but man suffers the law in all things, whether willing, unto merit, or unwilling, unto torment. Man ought not therefore to trust in his labor, nor to place hope in his own deeds, as though he could himself either effect what has not been ordained from above, or hinder what has been disposed. For the labor of man can add nothing to the sum of things, that there should be anything more: because the just moderation of the Artificer has so disposed all things in equity, that everything which strives without his co-operation, deserting the order of the universe, is moved rather toward not-being.
