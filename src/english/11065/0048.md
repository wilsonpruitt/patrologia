## HOMILY XVII. *On the confusion of minds arising from the passing of times.* (cont.)

*I have known that there was nothing better than to be glad and to do well in one's life. For every man that eateth and drinketh, and seeth good of his labor, this is the gift of God.* For every man who contends and disputes concerning the works of God, and lays blame on the judgments of God, and accuses his dispositions in the world: this is not the gift of God, nor is it from God, but against God; and he does better who lawfully uses in this world the gifts of God acquired by just labor, [0242B] than he who quarrels against God over those things which in this world come to pass contrary to his own will or estimation. For man cannot by quarreling and murmuring against God change his disposition: and therefore it is better to receive his judgments with fear, than to murmur, in wrong done to him, over our own hurt. For to this end also God hides his judgments from us, that, while we can neither penetrate them by our own understanding nor change them at our own choice, we may fear the more. Therefore he goes on and says:

*I have learned that all the works which God hath made continue for ever. We cannot add anything to them nor take away the things which God hath made, that he may be feared.* Not only of the works of God by which he created the essences of things, [0242C] and imposed upon the things created, through their several genera and species, form and measure, is this true, that the works of God continue for ever: in that his disposition in them is not changed, and each thing keeps to being what he appointed it to be: and if they pass away, this is not from him, since what they are they are from him; but that they cease to be, they have from themselves: just as this also, that before they were, they were nothing, they had from themselves. And so not only is this true of the works of God by which he disposes the creation of the world, that they remain for ever without change and confusion of order; but also of the disposition and predestination of his judgments, by which he examines the deeds of men, this is true, that they [0242D] remain for ever, and he himself does not change the law of his own providence, and the decree of his own thought. And just as to his works, to the genera of things, we can neither add by creating, so that there should be what was not, nor take away by destroying and demolishing, so that what was something should be nothing at all: so neither can we change his judgments, either by adding, so that what he himself has not disposed should come to pass, or by taking away, so that what he himself has ordained to be done should not come to pass. For that he alone might be feared, and that at all times the regard of the human conscience might be toward him in mercy, therefore he has reserved to himself alone the power of his works and of his judgments, that no one may change what he himself has fixed by eternal laws, to be fulfilled. For this reason let not man dispute in all the things which come upon him, but let him receive the judgments of God with [0243A] reverence and fear: and when indeed God bestows prosperity and grants pleasant things, let him be glad with chaste exultation in his benefits; but when adverse things come forth, and troublesome things meet him, confessing his own evil deserts, let him commend God's justice and truth. But if at any time, when troubles press upon him, he has seen his own patience giving way, let him set against the vice of murmuring and impatience the gifts of his Creator's munificence: so that the mind which is pressed by the weight of temptation may, lest it break out in any degree into blasphemy, be softened by the remembrance of God's benefits. For then he will begin to do well, and to be glad in his life, and to see good things of his labor: not only when with thanksgiving he enjoys the goods of this world acquired by his own industry; but then also when, set [0243B] in adversities, he is glad, for the labor of his patience, in the hope of consolation to come. For he shall eat and shall be glad in the gifts of God, when in prosperity he shall exult over the gift, and in adversity shall be gladdened over the recompense. Thus at all times he will compose himself unto peace with God, so that he may see and understand that he makes all things good in their time, so that he may not justly be accused by man in all the things that shall have befallen. For his judgments are established: and of his laws, standing for ever, nothing great or small can be changed. For just as in all the genera of things which seem to be diminished or to perish, in those things which pass away, they are restored in the things that come upon them and succeed, so that the disposition of the universe may keep its own order established and immovable; and the ordinance of the first disposition may remain always [0243C] inviolable, so that the sure moderation of the works of God neither passes beyond nor remains short: so also his judgments run according to a sure providence, so that nothing can be added to them or diminished from them. And if at any time he himself seems to do otherwise, and to judge otherwise, there is a hidden dispensation by which the counsel is not changed, but the work is varied and the judgment exercised. For this very thing also is a judgment: that judgment is sometimes deferred, that in its own time it may be restored, so that nothing may perish of all the things which have been made, but that they may remain always. For this is always so, that fault has its punishment, and justice obtains its reward. But there is a hidden punishment, and a manifest punishment. Likewise there is a hidden reward, and a manifest reward. And often God tolerates the evil, and defers their manifest punishment: [0243D] and yet all malice has its hidden punishment. And God judges, and seems to defer judgment, or not to attend at all to the deeds of men, in the eyes of those who see nothing but the things which are without. Likewise God sometimes proves the just, exercises them with adversities and tribulations, and justice seems not to have its reward with him: and yet it never lacks its own hidden recompense: and the manifest one is sometimes deferred, that the consciences of men might be questioned concerning their perseverance.
