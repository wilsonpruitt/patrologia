## HOMILY XVIII. *On the perverse morals of men: and what Ecclesiastes judged concerning them.*

[0244A]

*I saw under the sun in the place of judgment impiety, and in the place of justice iniquity. And I said in my heart: The just and the impious shall the Lord judge, and then shall be the time of every thing.* But because in human minds a grievous disputation has arisen concerning the judgments of God and concerning the contrary outcome of all things, and because the reasons must now be set forth why the hearts of men are moved against him, and rise up into murmuring, we shall briefly repeat what went before, so that of the things which are to be said we may speak the sentiment out of those very things as though from the beginning. Above, Ecclesiastes had shown that nothing in this world can stand perpetually in the same state; but that the changes [0244B] of times are hurried along without ceasing by contraries going their way: which mutability of things, although it be rightly ordered by the Creator of all things, nevertheless the human heart is through impatience shaken into murmuring and blasphemy against him. And therefore, after the times of things had been enumerated, he subjoined and said: *I saw the affliction which God gave to the sons of men, that they should be distended in it. He hath made all things good in their time, and hath delivered the world to their disputation.* This therefore is the affliction of men, their disputation against God; because, in that they will not be humbly subjected to the judgments of God, and contumaciously reprehend his disposition, which is good in all things, when anything is done contrary to their depraved will, not only does the punishment of adversity and the trouble of tribulation [0244C] wear them down outwardly, but much more does fury within, and the impatience of murmuring, afflict them. Which affliction God is said to have given them, not by corrupting their mind with depravity, but by withdrawing his judgments justly from their knowledge, that they themselves may be proved to be evil; so that, when they cannot see by what justice that is done which is done concerning them, they may the more, through the vice of their own impatience, be blinded away from the knowledge of truth. Therefore he gave them affliction when he gave disputation by concealing his judgments, that man might not find every work which God works from the beginning unto the end. For sometimes certain manifest causes go before the judgments of God in this world, when God, by a recompense that follows in the present life, either rewards the good deeds of men or punishes the evil. And in these alone [0244D] the judgments of God avail to be discerned in some degree, so that man may have some judgment of the hidden truth, in so far as he may venerate even those things which he cannot penetrate.

But when the preceding causes are hidden, or those which follow lie concealed, very many disputations and contradictions arise, and the depraved minds of men afflict themselves by quarreling and murmuring against God, since they cannot penetrate his judgments, nor find the work which God hath wrought from the beginning unto the end. For they cannot penetrate, in so great a darkness, to what end anything is done, even if certain causes seem to stand forth at the beginning, but all things are hidden because of the end. Therefore man does not find every work of God from the beginning unto the end; since, [0245A] even if he seems to see certain things, he does not penetrate the whole, and the whole becomes doubtful, because the whole is not made manifest; therefore, lest man quarrel against God in doubtful things, and murmur when set in adversities, by the gift of God many prosperous things are granted, and very many pleasant ones, that man may lawfully use goods acquired by just labor, and rejoice, and be glad, and soften his own conscience toward God. And let him think well of him, and not dispute nor contend against him, as though God were adverse who has bestowed good things. For this is what is meant by that which is subjoined, saying: *And I have known that there was nothing better than to be glad, and to do good things in one's life.* For he who does good to himself in his life out of those things which by the gift of God have been granted to men to enjoy, that he may be glad and rejoice in his God, does better than he who [0245B] disputes and contends against God. *For every man that eateth, and drinketh, and seeth good of his labor, this is the gift of God.* Therefore, because this is the gift of God, and lawfully granted by God, it is better than to dispute, and contend, and murmur: which is not the gift of God, nor granted to men by God; because it is evil and a great affliction. For he is much afflicted who can neither love nor [sic: *approhare*] what he endures; nor change what he hates. Therefore he is much afflicted who disputes and quarrels against God, blaspheming his judgments and detesting his works, since man can by no means [sic: *mullatenus*] change the things which God works.

Therefore that which follows, and he says: *I have learned that all the works which God hath made continue for ever.* [0245C] For just as the works of creation in their own genera do not pass beyond the order of the divine institution, so also the works of his judgments, by which he disposes the deeds of men and the whole world, do not confound the sentence of his predestination and providence fixed for ever. *For neither can we add anything to them,* that there should be more than has been provided; *nor take away,* that the things which have been made should be less, that God may be feared. For for this reason the disposition of visible things does not confound its order in the course of things passing away and in the vicissitude of things succeeding, so that man may know and understand that providence is eternal, which from the beginning disposed all things, by the established and constant examination of its own precept, in what manner their outcomes should follow [0245D] in their own times. And that he may also learn to fear in his own deeds him whose omnipotence he discerns in all his works, and in his judgments a constancy that remains unchangeable. For the established disposition of the work, and the sure moderation by which God brings all things to effect in their own times, manifestly demonstrates that, just as his wisdom is not deceived in discerning, so also his counsel is not changed in judging. *What is done, the same remaineth.* Just as in this whole body of created things what is done remains, because the order of the disposition is not confounded, even if mutable nature be varied, so also in his judgments what is done remains, because even in that which, according to his ineffable dispensation, he ordains dissimilarly for the variation of times, [0246A] he does not change the sentence of his own providence. Therefore what is done remains, both in the universe of things, so far as pertains to the order of the disposition, and in the exhibition of his judgment, so far as regards the sentence of predestination. The things which are to come have already been. For in both, the things which are to come have already been; because both in the genera of all things, what those things were which now are not, this same thing, according to the likeness of nature and the identity of the course, are the several things in their own kind to be, which are not yet. And in the divine judgments, the truth which we have heard was kept in things past, the same we expect to be exhibited in things that come after. For God everywhere restores what has gone away; because just as he repairs the failing of things that pass away by the multiplication of those that succeed in their own genera, [0246B] so, by judging daily the deeds of men, he demonstrates that he has held entire the ancient truth of his judgments, which he exercised from the beginning, although it seemed to have been interrupted unto the temple. For with him malice always has its punishment, and virtue its reward. But because of punishments one is hidden, another manifest: while God through patience tolerates the impious and sinners, and does not straightway inflict upon them a manifest punishment, human folly thinks that he either does not know or does not care about the deeds of men, or even, what is worse, that he approves the depravity of the malignant. Thence therefore the heart of man is shaken against God, whence God is shown to be the more to be loved and praised by men. Man complains that an evil man is tolerated by God, [0246C] when it is manifest that no man would have been good, if no evil man had ever been tolerated. And yet the hearts of the weak are scandalized, while they see in this world the impious prospering and the innocent oppressed; because they look only to those things which are without in the open, and do not attend to those which either lie hidden within, or are kept to be exhibited manifest in the future. From what cause their complaint arises is opened when it is added:

*I saw under the sun in the place of judgment impiety and in the place of justice iniquity.* He saw indeed that in this world God permits the impious to be judges, and to obtain power so that they lord it over and oppress the innocent, and that the deeds of men are iniquitous which ought to have been just, [0246D] and that in all these things there is great confusion under the sun. For because God grants power in this world to the perverse, therefore there is impiety in the place of judgment, and in the place of justice iniquity: so that there is impiety where judgment ought to be, and there iniquity where justice ought to be. For, because those set over are impious, therefore the subjects are iniquitous, because unless those oppressed innocence through impiety, these would not assail justice through iniquity. For inferiors are bold to perpetrate iniquity for this reason, that superiors are not dutiful to protect innocence; because if those did judgment for them that suffer wrong, these would not have been so ready to inflict wrong. But in this too under the sun there is impiety in the place of judgment, and in the place of justice iniquity: that every [0247A] man pursues without mercy the evils of his neighbor, but each one strives, even against justice, as far as he can, to defend his own evils. For in his neighbor's cause every judge is impious, and in his own cause against his neighbor iniquitous, because when his neighbor's evils are to be judged, he does not remember mercy; but when his own acts against his neighbor are to be examined, he does not keep justice. And in this manner all judgments under the sun are perverse, and the minds of men totter, and they wonder where is the judgment of God who endures these things.
