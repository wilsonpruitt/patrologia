## HOMILY XVIII. *On the perverse morals of men: and what Ecclesiastes judged concerning them.* (cont.)

We can still, with another and perhaps more convenient understanding, consider impiety under the sun in the place of judgment, and iniquity in the place of justice. For there is under the sun impiety in the place of judgment, and iniquity in the place of justice, [0247B] when in this world both the just man endures the punishment of the iniquitous, and the iniquitous receives the reward of the just. For the good are judged in this life only, that here they may receive the scourges of God for their own excesses, and may pass more purified to the reward to come. But the evil, because they are kept for the judgment to come, often in this world not only endure no adverse things, but are also permitted to fulfill their own desires, so that all things fall out for them according to their wish, unto the pleasantness and happiness of the present life. And recompenses seem to run dissimilarly, so that the good receive evils and the evil goods, and the judgment of the just seems to be impious, so that the innocent are oppressed and endure the punishment of the iniquitous, and so that the iniquitous take away the reward of the just: and all things confused and mingled, because God does not straightway exercise judgment so as [0247C] to rescue the innocent, and the just from the oppression of the impious, and to punish those who work iniquitous things. Thence arises a grievous shaking of minds, and the pusillanimous who see nothing but what is without alone are kindled with zeal, and think as though God did not care for human things, and had committed all things to fortune, and that nothing came to pass by judgment. And, what is more cruel, often men go into such perversity that God is blasphemed, and it is said as though he approved iniquity, and as though impiety were with him, and he did not know true judgment. And all this arises from this, that under the sun impiety is in the place of judgment, and in the place of justice iniquity, and that, as it were in the vanity which is under the sun, all things are confused, and all come forth dissimilarly. And yet, when man considers the works [0247D] of God, and sees in what manner he disposes all things with established and constant moderation, he understands that God is the inspector and moderator of all: and that his judgments cannot be made void, and that the deeds of men which he does not judge in this life he keeps to be examined hereafter. For if, as has been proved from the disposition of his works, God is a just judge, it remains that what is not done in the present in judging the deeds of men be without doubt believed to be brought to completion in the future. Therefore, in order to set forth the complaint of the weak in that God, in the fickleness and passing of this life, leaves all things as it were undiscussed, and disposes the deeds of men with a recompense unlike their merits, he said: I saw under the sun impiety in the place of judgment and in the place of justice iniquity. But straightway, [0248A] considering that the just judgments of God could not at all be made void, he understood that judgment was only deferred, not taken away, and he said:

*I said in my heart: The just and the impious shall the Lord judge, and then shall be the time of every thing under heaven.*

Then, when the Lord shall judge the just and the impious, there shall be the time of every thing under heaven: because everything which is done in life, whether it be good or evil, shall then be brought to judgment. And even now indeed in this life the Lord judges the just and the impious, but those recompenses are hidden, and those which shall be manifest are deferred unto the future. And therefore the small-hearted murmur, and complain, and think that the judgment is not just; because they do not see the recompenses. Yet even now just judgment is done: and each one [0248B] is judged according to his own merits. But all this is within, whither the eye of the flesh does not reach. And the things which are manifest are deferred until their judgment comes; and then all hidden things also shall be made manifest, and all things shall be judged according to just judgment. And then the Lord shall judge the just and the impious; and then shall be the time of every thing under heaven. But meanwhile all things are obscure, and run mingled toward their end. And the just are wrapped up in one lot with the impious, so long as they run together on the way, so that, when they shall have gone forth together, they may be distinguished and set in order unlike. For up to that point there is no distinction in all the things which appear without under the sun. But just as all things arise together, so they live together, and all things pass away together. And all this comes to pass, that the hearts of men may be proved: [0248C] whether there live in them anything of the knowledge of truth, and of the affection for the good which is hidden, if perchance out of that they may begin to take an argument of faith that they are something more than that alone which is seen. For outside there is nothing whence this can be known, and all arguments are taken away without, that what is within may begin to work, and be proved how great it is. For, if a man shall have known himself out of that, it is a great thing, and it stands to his merit that he seeks God through faith, whom he does not see through sight. For he first withdrew, and turned himself away when he was contemplating him present: and it was a great guilt and a grievous fault to have hated the light that was present; and it has been appointed for man as a remedy of propitiation, if he shall seek him who is absent and shall desire him who is hidden; [0248D] and that he may know that to be which is not seen, that it may be made manifest in its own time. Therefore now all things are withdrawn, and the things which are invisible are hidden, and man is left outside alone with things not his own, so that he may see nothing of his own, that it may be proved whether perchance any remembrance remains in him for turning to the seeking of him. Therefore now this man, considering more deeply the profundity of the judgments of God, sees that it is no wonder if in the cloud of this life no distinction is made between the just and the impious, since our mortality is wrapped in so great a darkness that in it man may even seem like to the beasts. Therefore he infers and says:

*I said in my heart concerning the sons of men, that God might prove them, and show them to be like the beasts. Therefore there is one destruction of man and of the beasts, and equally [0249A] the condition of both.* For man was made mortal for this reason, and therefore the sons of men die in like manner as beasts, that they may be proved and shown to be like the beasts. For two things had been made in man, one to the likeness of God, the other to the likeness of a beast. And that indeed which was created to the likeness of God was made immortal by nature, as God was immortal, to whose likeness it was made. But that which had been made to the likeness of a beast was corruptible by nature, as were those things to whose likeness it had been made. For both were taken from earth, and earth was the matter of both; and that earth was corruptible by nature, as were the things which were made of earth. So then two things were made in man, one earthly, the other heavenly; one by nature [0249B] corruptible, the other immortal; one the likeness of a beast, the other of God. And the body indeed was earthly, by nature corruptible, made to the likeness of a beast; but the soul was heavenly, by nature immortal, founded to the image of God. And body and soul were joined in man, two into one: and to the body was given the benefit of the fellowship, that it might share of the immortality of the soul unto incorruption: and all this was done for the glory of the soul, because it was pleasing to God, standing in justice and truth, so that its garment might not be worn away if its obedience persevered. And that which was the likeness of a beast in man was covered with the glory of incorruption, so that it might as it were be dissembled; nor might it see it unto the confusion of love, but might behold wholly the form of that which it loved, [0249C] and might not go far off anywhere. Afterward the soul turned itself away into the estrangement of love, and forgot that which was better, its own, and gazed without upon an alien beauty. And it bent itself upon the dye of its own cloak, that it might delight itself there; and it began to commit fornication with bodily enticements, and spiritual love was withdrawn from its eyes. And God was angry, and this turning away did not please him, and he willed to call man back within to that which was true, so that the gaze of the deceiver might not always cleave upon painted images. And he drew away the glory of its garment, that it might go into corruption, and commanded the soul to turn back, lest it should quicken it and bear it to eternity; and he cut down its garment, that he might show man that his glory was not in that wherein he should see himself like to the beasts. And man fell, and that slipped away [0249D] which was earthly and corruptible, and it began to flow so as to return whence it had come. And it came to pass that man was proved, whether he remembered his own good, and whether he knew how to seek it while he is compelled to leave the vain appearance, and to go out from that to which he had perversely clung.

*Therefore, that God might prove the sons of men, and show them to be like the beasts. Therefore there is one destruction of man and of the beasts, and the condition of both equal; as man dieth, so also they die.* In death therefore the condition of man and of the beast is one; because as man dies, so also they die. But nevertheless the condition of mortality is for man from judgment, for the beast from nature. And what they have alike they do not have from a like cause; because man had it from [0250A] fault that he should die, but the beast received it from nature that it should die. Nevertheless man is proved, and is shown to be like the beasts; because there is one destruction of man and of the beasts, and the condition of both alike: and so man dies as they die.

*And all things breathe alike.* That is, both the one and the other have a like life, and a like breath of quickening. And in all these things:

*Man hath nothing more than the beast.* Because common to both is both their rising, since they were alike made of earth; and their course, since together:

*All things are subject to vanity and to mutability.* And all things pass away, and the consummation of the end the same, they die similar, and they return to the earth of which they were first taken. So also formerly man had with the beasts one origin in the body, which was taken from earth, and one [0250B] condition of living with the beasts, so that the earthly body should likewise be nourished from the earth; but man did not have one end with the beasts, because man had been made that he should not die, nor return into the earth of which he was made. So then through the incorruption of the body his lowly origin was covered, and it was dissembled through immortality, lest it should come into reproach that man was like the beasts; nor did God will to show man whence his ignobility was, so as to expose him wholly in his own condition. But he clothed him with the beauty of immortality, and set him apart outside his own kind into another lot. But when man had sinned against God, he deprived him of his glory, and sent him back to his origin, so that by that whither he was going [0250C] he might know whence he had come. Therefore there is one destruction of man and of the beasts, and the condition of both equal; because as man dies, so also they die: and all things breathe alike, and in all these things man has nothing more than the beast, but all alike lie subject to vanity.
