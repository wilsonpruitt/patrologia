## HOMILY XIX. *On the oppression and abandonment of the innocent: and on the various and foolish judgment of the impious concerning this life.*

*I turned me to other things. and I saw the calumnies (Eccles, IV)*, *which are wrought under the sun, and the tears of the innocent, and [0253A] no comforter; nor that they could withstand their violence, being destitute of the help of all.* Therefore do not think that here is the fatherland. But consider and acknowledge that you are under the sun, where all things are rolled about and all are confused, since this was done for this reason, that you might acknowledge your exile, and seek another fatherland. Therefore calumnies happen here, and unjust oppressions, so that some assail others without cause, and the stronger oppress the weak without mercy; and God does not bring consolation here to the innocent, because he reserves his consolations for them elsewhere, and now meanwhile leaves them destitute of the help of all, lest they be consoled in another's help, which they do not have as their own, and less groan, and sigh, and desire his consolation, which they do not yet have. But the mind of the perverse, impatient of correction on either [0253B] side, is neither roused by prosperity, nor chastened by adversity. For when they look upon the sweet things of this life, they cleave to them in mind, and say: This is enough, and there is no other good of man besides this to be hereafter. But when they attend to adverse things, they are blunted, and fall down in mind, and fall down in distrust, and despair of themselves, because they have no other hope. And they choose rather not to be, than to be in evil; because it is evil, that which they are, and they have not known a good which they might choose, so as to transfer themselves to it; since in the time of their pleasure they were unwilling to learn it, that in the evil time they might rest in it. And therefore they do that which alone they have known: when they are in evil, they wish to be nothing, because they have not known another way by which to escape being in evil, unless they pass over [0253C] to not-being. Therefore, the evils which are under the sun having been seen, straightway in their voice it is subjoined, when it is said:

*And I praised the dead rather than the living; and I judged him happier than both, who is not yet born; nor hath seen the evils which are done under the sun.* This indeed is the voice of those who have not known the true good, nor think that there is anything for man unto good or unto evil, except what is present in its entirety. Therefore in good things they exult beyond measure, in evil things they despair; and where their hope is, there their despair stands. For this reason, the evils of this world having been seen, they say that the dead are happier than the living, and that than both, those not yet born are happier. For because they reckon that this alone is, which is seen, [0253D] the misery which is in it having been seen, they will not now seek happiness in another being, because they have not known a being other than this; but they set it in not-being alone; since in this whole which alone they think to be, they have found unhappiness. Therefore they praise rather those who have been, and are not, than those who still are, and they proclaim more blessed than both those who are not yet. For if, as it seems to them, it is evil to be at all, it is good not to be, and better not to have been. For if it is evil to be in evil, it is good not to be in evil, and better not to have been. For that which is further from evil in good, is itself the greater good. Just as that which is further from good in evil, is itself the greater evil. If therefore it is evil to be, it is assuredly good also not to be, and much better than both, never to have been. And so such [0254A] perversities do the minds of men bring forth of themselves under the sun, that this also is added to the confusion of all, that man himself grows dark as to his own living. For if man saw what man is and why man was made, he would assuredly recognize his own good, and would no longer say that those who are nothing are happier than those who are something. For the mind bound by the desire of so great a good, although, afflicted by temporal evils, it might loathe the present life, yet in the hope of the consolation to come it would altogether love to be rather than not to be. But now a great perversity grows up in the minds of men who are ignorant of their own good, and a vanity at its consummation, than which none can be greater. For man, flowing away from true essence by the vanity of his own mutability, without ceasing at all times ceases to be that [0254B] which he is; and passes always into that which is not; and so, in a manner accustomed to his own evil, at last casts himself headlong into such perversity that he now covets to be nothing at all. But many, desiring to avoid this madness as though set forth in the open, and such as cannot be hidden, turn themselves about, that they may guard their own being, and they apply great carefulness and industry of their labors, that they may make their life secure from the evils which are under the sun; and gathering wealth, and multiplying riches, and all the other things which can be for the solace of life, they seek out with much foresight and carefulness. And they do many things, and work very many things worthy of memory; and not seldom by their own industry and labor [0254C] they escape another's violence, but another's envy they cannot evade. Wherefore concerning these also, after the impatience of those who despair, he subjoins a sentiment, saying:

*Again I contemplated all the labors of men, and I observed that their industries lie open to the envy of a neighbor.* For when he considered the labors of men, he saw that the industries of the good lay open to the envy of neighbors; since the perverse also, just as they are bound by sloth so that they do not exercise in themselves the works of the virtues, so are they goaded by envy that they may carp at them in their neighbors

*And in this therefore is vanity, and superfluous care.* Either this is vanity, that a man, by envying another's good works, brings care upon his own mind, and the anguish of malice, [0254D] since by envying he harms not him whom he envies, but himself; or else this too is vanity, that a man labors too much for these temporal goods, and is anxious, and afflicts himself with superfluous care, since the acquiring of them sooner stirs a neighbor to envy than draws him to love. For while the industry by which a man is exercised is good, the superfluous care and solicitude by which he is afflicted is evil. Likewise also vain confidence, and the hope by which true security is expected in the multitude of riches; whereas the acquiring of them rather takes away security, when it inflames to envy of us those neighbors who in poverty might perhaps have been able to love us, by reason of their own rivalry. Rightly therefore is industry approved, and vain solicitude reproved. But there comes again another kind of men from [0255A] the flock of vanity, seeking an occasion for their own torpor. And these reprehend those who work, and seem as it were to reprove them as anxious, that they themselves may be dissolute. For thus vanity runs, that it never finds the middle boundary of virtue.

*And therefore the fool foldeth his hands together, and eateth his own flesh, saying: Better is a handful with rest, than both hands full with labor, and affliction of mind.* The fool folds his hands together. For he is a fool who does not know how to provide for himself hereafter. He is a fool who thinks that hands folded in idleness are filled with riches. He folds his hands together: he folds the one to the other, lest they be stretched out to work. He folds his hands together, as though making a covenant with idleness. And he eats his own flesh. He thinks he spares his own body, [0255B] because he does not afflict it with labors, but thence his flesh is consumed by want, whence it is nourished by idleness. He eats his own flesh. For he feeds with his own folly, and his [sic: *otiocitas*] fattens him. But nevertheless his flesh, as much as it is fed in profitable leisure, so much is it wasted by the hunger that follows. Therefore he eats his own flesh, saying: Better is a handful with rest, than both hands full with labor, and affliction of mind. It is true that a handful is better, if it knew not how to fail, and if the blessing of the widow of Sarepta had been given to all [n: (III Reg. XVII)]. But Elias was not sent to all. Why therefore do you foolishly say that a handful with rest is better than both hands full with labor? Perhaps you consider when you have a handful, and have rest; but you do not attend to when you shall not have even that [0255C] handful, what you will then do, and what rest you will then be going to have. Therefore now you eat your handful in rest, and your idleness pleases you; nor do you care meanwhile now to seek with labor another handful, which you may eat when this one has failed. Therefore you eat your own flesh now, because it will be beaten after this importunate idleness, and afterward by its own wasting will pay back what ill-advised rest has spent. And perhaps you may think that now all the kinds of vanities [0256A] have been told, and that there is no other kind of vanity which the watcher may find under the sun. Therefore there follows:

*Considering, I found another vanity under the sun. There is one, and he hath not a second, not a son, not a brother, and yet he ceaseth not to labor; neither are his eyes satisfied with riches, neither doth he reflect, saying: For whom do I labor, and defraud my soul of good things?* For he who is one, and alone has no other successor, neither a son whom he has begotten, nor a brother with whom he himself was begotten: for of his father he himself is the successor. He therefore who has not a second, whom he may leave after him as successor in his own goods: and yet does not cease to gather, nor enjoys the things which he possesses; but keeps avariciously what he has greedily gathered: what can be more vain than this? For he who labors and enjoys his own labor, [0256B] takes some fruit; and he who labors, and does not use the things which he acquires by his labor, and yet leaves them to be possessed by those whom he loves, takes some profit of his labor, the desire and the wish of his own love. But he who labors, and labors neither for himself, nor for another whom he loves: for what does he labor, unless for his own vice alone, which he serves? For this man serves his vice alone, and there is no other affection which might excuse his vain solicitude, to which pardon could be given, save that one alone which vice has begotten. Many are the labors of men, which are left to strangers, and those who do them take no fruit from them. And many also labor in wisdom, and give effort, and study to know many things, and to say very many, and commit their thoughts to writing, that they may be handed on to posterity; nor do they take fruit from [0256C] all these things, so that it should be better for them, but they remain empty of truth and void of the sweetness of wisdom. And all their labor is vanity, even if it seem to draw near to truth. For men of this kind do not know for whom they labor, and defraud their own soul of good things; because, while they intend only upon the greatness of the work, taking no fruit from it, they see also that no usefulness comes to them from it, and they do not know whether these very things will profit others after them.
