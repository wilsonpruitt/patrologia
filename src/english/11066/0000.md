## I. ON THE EPISTLE TO THE ROMANS.

[0431C] *Paul* is a common noun among the Hebrews, the Greeks, and the Latins, though not with the same ending [n: (Rom. I)]. Among the Hebrews it means *wonderful* or *chosen*; among the Greeks *quiet*; among the Latins *small*. This name was aptly imposed on Paul as a mark of a twofold, or a threefold, virtue, which the aforesaid interpretations suggest; or Paul was called an Apostle from Paulus Sergius the proconsul, whom he converted in Cyprus; or he bore two names.

QUESTION I. It is asked how Paul calls himself a servant, since elsewhere he says: *For you have not received the spirit of bondage again in fear [n: (Rom. VIII)]*. And elsewhere: *Now he is no longer a servant, but a son [n: (Galat. IV)]*. And the Lord in the Gospel: *I will now no longer call you servants, but friends [n: (Joan. XV)]*. Was Paul then not a friend? Solution. [0431D] Two kinds of fear make two kinds of servants: namely servile and filial. Paul, then, was a servant by filial fear, and not by servile: which servitude does not take away liberty or friendship, but rather establishes it.

QUESTION II. Why does Paul say that Christ was made from [0432C] the seed of David, when we confess him born of the Virgin? Solution. That he might note that Christ was conceived not after the manner of other men, but by the sole working of the Holy Spirit, from the Virgin, without male seed: whence other men can beget other men, but not make them.

QUESTION III. How was the incarnation itself brought about? Solution. I say that the very Word of God was made flesh, that is, man: yet not changed or converted into man or flesh, but clothed with flesh (that he might appear to mortals); for that union was so brought about that neither was the divine nature changed into the human, nor the human into the divine: nor was a new nature or a new person made out of the two natures, but ineffably the two natures are united in Christ: so that the one assuming should confer upon the assumed by grace all that it had by nature, and all [0432D] that was the assumed's by nature should become the assuming one's by condescension; whence the whole is called God, the whole man, and in turn man is God, and God man, which does not happen in the substances of man.

[0433A] QUESTION IV. How is it here said that the whole is God and the whole man, since those two natures are not parts of that person? Solution. This is said on account of a likeness, because the two natures are so united in Christ as parts in a whole, but differently. For not as a whole has its being from the conjunction of its parts, does the person of Christ have its being from the union of the humanity and the divinity, for neither nature has any new being. But that those natures are not parts of the person is clear from this, that either of them is predicated of the person, as when it is said: Christ is the divine nature. Likewise the names of the natures are predicated of one another, as when it is said: God is man and man is God: which does not happen in an integral whole and its parts.

QUESTION V. How is the image referred to the essence [0433B] only, when it is said of the Son only in a relative sense? Solution. Image sometimes signifies the divine essence, and then it is predicated in common of the three persons: it also signifies a relation, and then it is said of the Son alone.

QUESTION VI. What is there in the substance of man that is not in the substances of Christ? Solution. Because neither is the flesh the soul, nor the soul the flesh; nor is man the flesh or the soul, as God is man, and man is God, and Christ is both. And this comes about because the union between God and man is greater than that between flesh and soul. For the soul united to the flesh does not confer upon it all that it has by nature, since the flesh is not capable of the many things that are the soul's, as God united to man wholly infuses himself [0433C] into him.

QUESTION VII. What is it that is said, when it is said: Man is God and God is man, or Christ is God, Christ is man? Solution. In answering this question the modern doctors disagree among themselves. For some say that the same is predicated of itself, namely: this person, Christ; which is astonishing, since God means one thing, man another; and the son of man one thing, the Son of God another, as authority says. Which also seems to me to be false, because this name God means the same, when it is said: man is God, and: Christ is God, and: the Father is God. If it be said, it does not follow that the same is God, man, and Christ, and God the Father. Likewise man and God are common [0433D] nouns; how then do they properly signify this person, or when, or by whom was such an imposition made? Likewise Christ is man, and the Virgin [the Word] is man, is not the same thing predicated of both? If it be said no: then they are not of the same nature, since the name of the nature is not common to both according to the same signification. Others say that when it is said: man is God, that which is signified by this name God is not predicated, but rather to be personally united to God; and when it is said: God is man, to have man united in person is predicated. But according to this neither is man truly God, nor is God truly man. Others say that when it is said: man is God, the person of the divine nature is predicated of the person of the human nature; and when it is said: [0434A] God is man, the person of the human nature is predicated of the person of the divine nature: yet the person of the divine and of the human nature is the same, namely Christ; yet another is understood when one says: man, and when one says: God. These plainly concede that Christ is two things, of which one is simple and the other composite; one eternal, the other temporal; and that Christ is something composite according to his humanity, from flesh and soul, and something simple according to his divinity, and that the divinity is not a part of this person, but is the very person, while the flesh and soul are only parts.

QUESTION VIII. Whether Christ was twice begotten. Response. He was twice born and twice begotten: once from eternity out of the substance of the Father, again in time of the Virgin. [0434B] He has therefore two nativities, two generations, two filiations, an eternal and a temporal, yet he is not two sons or two born, but one and the same Son of God and son of man, though not by the same filiation.

QUESTION IX. Whether a person took up a person, or a nature a person, or a nature a nature. Solution. A person is not assumed, therefore neither did a person assume a person, nor a nature a person. But that a person assumed a nature, all concede; whether a nature assumed a nature is doubted by some: but, beyond doubt, the divine nature assumed the human, not into a unity of nature, but of person, that is, the union was so brought about that the one assuming and the assumed should be one person, not one nature, which, although authority manifestly [0434C] often says this, many deny, saying: if that which is assumed is a person, how is a person not assumed? But we say: although a person is assumed, yet God did not assume the person of man, but the nature, because he was not made of another person than he had been before, as he was made of another nature than he was, retaining his own; for in assuming the humanity, he did not lose the divinity. Therefore it is conceded that a nature, not a person, is assumed, not only by a person, but by a nature. Those authorities which say that the Word alone was incarnate exclude only the other persons, not the divine nature.

QUESTION X. What is predestination? Response. [0434D] The preparation of grace. Sometimes also the very applying of grace is called predestination.

QUESTION XI. Likewise it is asked, of what predestination was made: whether of the person, or of the nature? Solution. Of the person, not according to the divine nature, but according to the human. It can also be said that the nature was predestined, or the man assumed, so as to be exalted such that there was nothing higher to which he might be lifted.

QUESTION XII. Whether it ought simply to be conceded that Christ is a creature, or made. Solution. It is not to be conceded without such a qualification, according to the flesh: lest we seem to consent to the heretics who say that Christ was made according to either nature, and also on account of the implicit negation [0435A] contained in this name, creature: because that is called a creature which began to be and did not always exist. For it does not follow, if according to his humanity Christ did not always exist: therefore he did not always exist; as it rightly follows, if according to his Divinity he always existed, therefore he always existed.

QUESTION XIII. Whether Christ according to his humanity is the Son of God, or God? Which they wish to prove thus: according as he is man, he is predestined to be the Son of God: but that is, that he might be, he was predestined according as he is man, therefore according as he is man, he is the Son of God. Solution. It is not true that if according to man he is predestined to be the Son of God: therefore according to man he is the Son of God, unless "according to" be expressive of the person. But if [0435B] it notes a cause or a condition, it is not true, as appears in this like case: This man, according to the magnitude of his sin, repents that he may be saved, therefore according to the magnitude of his sin he is made saved.

QUESTION XIV. Whether according as he is man he is a person. Solution. Some say: If "according to" signifies a cause or a condition, according as he is man, he is not a person; saying: If according as he is man, he is a person, therefore a third in the Trinity, or another; but there is no other, therefore a third in the Trinity, and thus according as he is man he is God. Again, according as he is man, he is not distinguished by a personal property from the Father or the Holy Spirit, but by filiation alone, which he had from eternity. Others say that, inasmuch as he is man, he is a person. Whence it rightly follows, [0435C] if he is man, he is a person, and that consequence: if he is a person, he is a third person in the Trinity or another, they call false. For the whole posited does not posit the part, as the removed removes it, but the part posited posits the whole. But the part removed neither posits nor removes the whole. But that that which is assumed is a person is clear, since Augustine says that that which he took up and that which is assumed are one person.

QUESTION XV. Whether the soul of Christ is God? Solution. It is not to be conceded simply, lest we seem to consent to those who said that the Word assumed flesh only, and that the Word itself quickened the flesh in place of a soul. For this reason also the soul is not called God, because the name of soul redounds more upon the nature [0435D] than upon the person.

QUESTION XVI. Whether it ought to be conceded, That man existed from eternity? Solution. If by the pronoun "that" a person is indicated, it is true that that man existed from eternity; but if the human nature, it is not true, That man existed from eternity.

QUESTION XVII. Whether the soul of Christ is a person? Some say that it is not, while it is joined to the flesh: but separated it is a person. Others say that Christ's soul is the same person with the Word.
