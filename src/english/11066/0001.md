## I. ON THE EPISTLE TO THE ROMANS. (cont.)

QUESTION XVIII. Whether that which is signified by this name Christ is a universal? Which seems to be so: because it is predicated of several, as when it is said, man is Christ, and God is Christ. Solution. It need not [0436A] be conceded on that account, since in each case the predication is not conformable, although too there are two things which are predicated of several according to some, yet it is not that which is predicated of several. Likewise they wish to prove that it is that which is predicated of several, because the person, which is said of the one assuming, is also said of the assumed. Solution. Person is as it were a comprehensive name, for two, or several, are one person. Yet there are some who concede that what is signified by this name, Christ, is common to several: and signifies as much as this term, the person of the divine and human nature, yet Christ is not a universal, but two singulars according to these men.

QUESTION XIX. Whether the man assumed is God. Solution. [0436B] Many say that he is not, whom authority plainly contradicts. For the Apostle says that in him dwells the fullness of the divinity [n: (Col. II)]. And Ambrose: Whatever the Son of God has by nature, the son of man has by grace. Likewise the Lord speaking of himself: *All power is given to me in heaven,* etc. [n: (Matth. XXVIII)]. If he has omnipotence, he is omnipotent; if he is omnipotent, he is God. Likewise John the apostle says of him, that *he received the Spirit not by measure [n: (Joan. III)]*; but according to those who deny that the man assumed is God, the Spirit was given to him by measure, since he does not have all that the Word has, to which he is personally united.

QUESTION XX. Whether that man can forgive sins, since he is omnipotent? Solution. That man [0436C] can forgive sins, not because he is man, but because he is God, just as he can reduce the world to nothing, if he willed.

QUESTION XXI. Whether the creature is made equal to the Creator, since the soul of Christ, or the man assumed, has wholly by grace what God has by nature? Solution. The creature is not equal to God, because it is one thing to be wisdom, another to be wise by wisdom, another to have something by nature, another by grace.

Likewise the objection is made thus: It has been said that whatever the Word has by nature, the man has by grace: but the Word has eternity by nature, therefore the man has the same by grace, and if this is so, the man himself is eternal. Solution. This term, eternal, notes [0436D] a negation. For that is properly called eternal which always was, and did not begin to be, whence, because "eternal" does not simply predicate the person of the divine nature, but also designates such a negation, the man is not to be called eternal. Or it can be put thus: that man is the eternal God, he is not an eternal man.

QUESTION XXII. Whether the man assumed is an adoptive son, or a natural son. Some wish to prove that he is an adoptive son thus: by grace alone he was predestined, so as to be the Son of God, therefore he is a son of grace, and thus of adoption, therefore adoptive. Solution. To be the Son by grace alone he has not from grace but from nature, that is, the man assumed is not an adoptive son, [0437A] but a natural son, not by nature but by grace, through which he has whatever he possesses.

QUESTION XXIII. How does the Apostle show his affection toward the Romans, as the expositor says, by giving thanks to God, since for all the good things that God gives to good and bad alike, thanks are to be given to God? Solution. He gives thanks not only in respect of the gifts, but rather in respect of the Romans, namely because the gifts were conferred on them. But when someone gives thanks for evil gifts conferred, he does this only in respect of the gifts, not of those on whom they are conferred.

QUESTION XXIV. The expositor says that the Apostle in the Romans does not praise the faith, but the readiness of faith, which seems not to be praiseworthy, because he who [0437B] easily believes, easily disbelieves. Solution. There is a twofold readiness; the one, which comes from lightness of mind, and this is unworthy of praise; the other, which comes from vigor of mind, and much consideration of reason, and this is worthy of praise.

QUESTION XXV. The Lord forbids in the Gospel to swear by heaven or by earth [n: (Matth. V)]; the Apostle swears not by a creature but by the Creator, which is more, when he says: *God is my witness [n: (Rom. I)]*. Whence he seems to be a transgressor of the precept, and thus to sin unto death. Solution. The Lord forbids what is evil, namely to swear falsely, or truly without necessity; he counseled to speak the truth; he permitted an oath when it is necessary, in which three things ought to be present: judgment, as to the usefulness of discretion; [0437C] justice, as to whether it is to be done; truth, as to knowledge. If one of these be lacking, the guilt of perjury is incurred.

QUESTION XXVI. What it is to swear by God. Solution. The sense is: So may God profit me, or not, or as the truth is in God, so in this man; and if he empties it out as far as in him lies, he annihilates the truth of God, setting God up as a guarantor to those men: but if it is not true, he in a manner imprisons God.

QUESTION XXVII. Likewise what it is to swear by a creature? Solution. By God, who made it. Whence Augustine says that he who swears falsely by a stone is a perjurer.

QUESTION XXVIII. Whether an oath is good, [0437D] or evil, or indifferent: for if it is good, it is not to be forbidden; if evil, it is by no means to be done; if it is indifferent, how is it always from evil? Solution. It is not itself evil, but nevertheless an occasion of evil; whence the Lord himself counsels to avoid it, lest by its constant use we incur perjury.

QUESTION XXIX. How does the Apostle show his affection toward the Romans by saying: *I desire to see you that I may have some fruit among you [n: (ibid.)]*, since he seems to attend to his own advantage rather than to theirs? Solution. He does not seek his own advantage so much as theirs, while he desires them to bear fruit in good works, so that he too may obtain some advantage from their progress.

[0438A] QUESTION XXX. If it is true, as many hold, that the will with the deed is not worth more than the will without the deed, how does the Apostle say: I desire to see you that I may have some fruit among you; since he would not have more merit from the deed than he had from the will alone? Solution. To us, however, it seems that the deed with the will is a greater good than the will alone without the deed. But those men say: He desired to come for this reason, that with the deed the will might grow, and the merit become greater. What will they say about the passion of Christ? Did he not merit more by suffering than before by desire alone? For they cannot say that his will in the passion was augmented, so that thus the merit might grow.

[0438B] QUESTION XXXI. How does the Apostle say that he is a debtor to the Greeks and to the barbarians: did he not preach to them freely? therefore not out of debt. Solution. He does not say that he owes them anything from their merit, but from the office enjoined he owes to evangelize to them; and out of grace alone, as regards them, he preached, so this debt therefore does not take away grace, as he says elsewhere: *Necessity lies upon me to evangelize [n: (I Cor. IX)]*. Which necessity does not take away the will, as this debt does not remove grace.

Likewise it seems that there was in them that whence he owed this to them; from the law of nature, by which he owed them that which he would wish to be done to himself. Solution. We concede that by natural law he was bound to illumine them with the light of truth, yet it is not simply to be conceded that he [0438C] owed this to them.

QUESTION XXXII. Likewise when it is said: *The just man lives by faith [n: (Hebr. X)]*, it is asked why the efficacy of justice is attributed to faith, and not to charity; since faith is somewhere where there is no justice; charity nowhere without justice. To this some respond, saying: Charity and justice are the same, and therefore neither is the cause of the other. Or otherwise: although charity is the cause of justice, yet fittingly faith, which is the cause of charity, is said to be the cause of justice; because whatever is the cause of a cause, is the cause also of the effect. And it is to be known that it is one thing to believe that God exists, which is the faith of knowledge; another to believe God, which is called the faith of consent; another to believe in God, which is called the faith of trust; another to believe unto God, which is by faith [0438D] and love to tend toward God. Likewise it is one thing which is believed; another that by which it is believed; each is often designated by the name of faith. Likewise that by which it is believed is sometimes informed by charity, and then only, according to some, is it called a virtue; but when it is without charity, it is an unformed quality; it is neither a virtue nor does it justify. To others it seems that wherever there is faith, even of knowledge, as far as in itself, it always justifies: yet its effect is sometimes hindered by the abundance of evil. Faith is a virtue by which are believed the things that are not seen: or a certitude of invisible things pertaining to religion, above opinion and within knowledge. Charity justifies, and faith, and grace, and God: therefore four justify. [0439A] Solution. It is not therefore true, that four, because this would be separately: for in faith and through faith charity justifies, and in charity, grace, and through grace, God.

QUESTION XXXIII. How does he say in the plural, the invisible things of God, since God is simple and one, nor is there anything in God that is not God, nor is anything here called the invisible of God which is not God? Solution. That which is one and simple in nature, comes into our knowledge not as one and simple, but as many: and this is rarely grasped, why, namely, it comes thus, because the inner eye is not yet able to attain to that simplicity and ineffable unity which is God, so as to understand him as he is. Whence, when it understands God as good, wise, omnipotent, [0439B] and the like, as it were several things come into the mind, which is one in nature. From which a higher question arises: whether those things which are understood by man's reason as several, are in themselves, or in God, distinguished by any difference. They do not differ among themselves substantially or personally: as the eternal reasons of things, which were in the mind of God, it is clear that they differ in some way, yet in what way is not certain.
