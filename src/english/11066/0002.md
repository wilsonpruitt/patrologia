## I. ON THE EPISTLE TO THE ROMANS. (cont.)

QUESTION XXXIV. Likewise, since it is said: His everlasting power also, and divinity, it may be asked: These two, the power of God and the divinity, since they are invisible, how are they distinguished from the invisible things? For it cannot fittingly be said, animals live, and men, and horses, since men and horses are animals. Solution. [0439C] The names that are said of God, some signify what God is not, as immortal and immeasurable, eternal and infinite. Some note what is in God, as wise, good: some by their predication suggest what God is, as goodness and wisdom. Lest anyone then should suppose that only those are beheld by the creature through the understanding of God which indicate what God is not, as immeasurable, he added, his everlasting power also, etc. Otherwise according to others: by the invisible things is understood the Father; by the power, the Son; by the divinity, the Holy Spirit. According to this it seems that the philosophers had knowledge of the supreme Trinity through the things that are made. But Augustine on Exodus says that the philosophers did not attain to the knowledge of the third person, [0439D] but only περὶ τοῦ αγάθου, that is, concerning the Father, and περὶ νόον, that is, concerning the Son, did they philosophize. To this some say that that distinction of the supreme Trinity which the catholic faith confesses they did not have, nor could have, except by revelation. For God is known in four ways: in two ways inwardly, namely by natural reason, which the Apostle notes according to some, saying: *What is known of God is manifest in them [n: (Rom. I)]*, and by divine inspiration, which the Apostle there notes: *For God has manifested it to them [n: (ibid.)]*. In two ways outwardly, by the created work, as the Apostle suggests, saying: *The invisible things of God [n: (ibid.)]*, and by Scripture, which way is sufficiently plain. God willed therefore to lie hidden in some things, that faith might have [0440A] its merit, and to appear in some, that unbelief might have no excuse. Note, in the magnitude of the universe the divine power is noted, in its beauty the wisdom, in its usefulness the goodness, whence it is clear that not only in all things together, but in each single thing, there shines a certain image and vestige of the Trinity. Nevertheless it is to be observed carefully, when likenesses are brought in, whether it is to demonstrate the identity of essence, or to suggest the personal distinction, or to show that the incarnation pertains to the Son alone, although it is itself the work of the Trinity.

QUESTION XXXV. Why is the Father understood through the invisible things rather than the Son, or the Holy Spirit, since they too are invisible? Solution. Because the Father is nowhere read to have appeared in a visible form, as the Son in the man [0440B] assumed, and the Holy Spirit in the form of a dove, and in tongues of fire.

QUESTION XXXVI. Why the Son through the power? Solution. Because he himself is the operative power of the Father, through whom all things were made.

QUESTION XXXVII. Why is the Holy Spirit signified through the divinity? Solution. That it may be shown to proceed in common from the Father and the Son, he obtains a common name as though proper. Or by the invisible things the Holy Spirit is understood, whence "invisible things" is said in the plural on account of the diversity of gifts. By the power the Father, to whose property power is wont to be referred. By the divinity, or Deity, is understood the Son, for thus different men hold different views.

QUESTION XXXVIII. It is asked concerning that which the Apostle says [0440C] of the philosophers, that they were inexcusable: *Because when they had known God, they did not glorify him as God [n: (ibid.)]*. For they seem to have had an excuse: for not so great a grace was conferred on them, out of which they could glorify God. For could they glorify God without charity? Could anyone have charity without faith? Could they, from that degree of knowledge which they had, love? Solution. They were therefore inexcusable, because they did not do as much as they could.

QUESTION XXXIX. Likewise it is asked, if they had done as much as they could, whether they would be worthy of salvation, if they had departed from this life in such a state. For who is worthy of salvation without faith, or who can perish with charity? [0440D] Master Peter Abelard says that they were worthy of salvation, that is, that there should be given to them the means whereby they might be saved; because if they had done as much as they could, God would never permit them to pass on without faith. Others say that they were therefore inexcusable, because from that degree of knowledge they could love: and at once from this state faith would be given to them. But according to this, faith is from charity, not charity from faith. But a third group hold that by no degree of knowledge could they love; yet if they had done what they could, at once faith would be given to them, out of which they would love God, and thus glorify him. To whom it is objected: Could they merit faith? Perhaps they will say: That, although they could not merit faith, yet from that which they had, they could be made fit and apt to receive faith. But how do they know that [0441A] from this they would be made fit; or if fit, how do they know that faith would be given to them? Were not the Tyrians and Sidonians fit? yet the preaching was not made to them. Solution. But we believe that they could glorify God in part, even if not yet perfectly, for if they attributed to God what they had received, and sought his glory and not their own, they would glorify God according to something: in which they would love God, even if they could not perfectly.

It is objected. From that knowledge which they had, they could at least in part love God: therefore from charity they could do this, which they had, or which they did not have; but not from that which they did not have: therefore from that which they had, and thus they had the love of God, and thus were worthy of salvation. Solution. It is to be conceded [0441B] that they could love God from the charity which they had; yet it is not simply to be said that they had the love of God: just as this sick and weak man carries a stone from the strength which he has; yet it is not simply to be said that this man is strong, or has strength. For we do not concede that those who are in mortal sin can do no good work, as some hold, but they do many good things, although insufficient for salvation on account of the greater evil which they have.

QUESTION XL. Whether natural reason can do anything of itself without the aid of grace? Solution. Some say that natural reason can do much of itself, as appears in the philosophers, who, relying on reason alone, knew much not only in the comprehension of truth concerning creatures, [0441C] but even concerning the Creator, namely that God is, and is one, and that he is triune. But to this thought they seem not to have attained without the aid of grace. Whence the Apostle: What is known of God is manifest in them; and at once he adds: For God has manifested it to them.

QUESTION XLI. How then without grace, if God manifested it to them, is not this grace? To this some respond thus: God is said to have manifested, because he made such creatures that from them the Creator himself could be known. Whence he adds: For the invisible things of God, etc.

QUESTION XLII. Likewise if natural reason avails so much, as to suffice for this degree of knowledge, [0441D] it is asked in what it was more efficacious before sin than now; or how it is now weaker than then? For as then it knew that God is, and is one, and triune; so also now. And as now it cannot comprehend the mystery of the incarnation without the aid of grace, so neither then that mystery which is hidden in God, who made all things, as the Apostle says, as though nothing served the creatures whereby this could be known. Solution. Reason before sin comprehended more easily and more perfectly what now with great difficulty, and less perfectly, and from afar it descries: it would also then have known many things which now it does not know. It is objected against those who say that natural reason can do something of itself: Can not the outer eye see nothing without the illumination of the light coming upon [0442A] it, or of the ray of the sun, or of another? So the inner eye can do nothing of itself without the illumination of the light which illumines every man coming into this world. What then can natural reason do without grace, since such light is from grace? To which some respond thus: In the first creation there was set out and set before the inner eye the illumination of the supreme light: as far as and to what end, from such a setting out and setting before, without any grace supervening, it might of itself avail to attain: which illumination was not of the substance of reason, nor of its nature, but of its gratuitous gift. Yet it can be said that it sees naturally; because, with the nature was given the aptitude and fitness for seeing, that light being set out, of which the discourse before proceeded. And so without every kind of grace the eye of the mind can see [0442B] nothing: yet it can well see without a supervening grace other than that which was conferred with the nature, which supervening grace is most especially wont to be called grace.
