## I. ON THE EPISTLE TO THE ROMANS. (cont.)

QUESTION XLV. Whether the penalty itself, which is also [0444D] fault, is from God. Which seems to be so, because all that is just is from God, and every such penalty is just. Therefore it seems that such a penalty is from God. Solution. It is not necessary to say this: since from the quality of itself it does not have that it is just, but from the law of God, as has already been said: for it is written: *God did not make death, nor does he rejoice in the destruction of the living [n: (Sap. I)]*. Or, even if it be conceded that the penalty is from God, yet not the fault, although penalty and fault are the same. For it does not have from God that it is fault, even if in some way it has from God that it is penalty. For since a penalty is something in itself, on three accounts it is said to be from God, namely on account of its matter, which God makes, as the matter of fire; and on account of the nature, namely that such a thing cannot be in such a thing without suffering: which nature is from God, and on account of the judgment [0445A] of God, by which such a penalty is inflicted on such a fault. Yet we do not say that God is the cause of that penalty; for this would be to say that God had made the fault of that penalty, because he is not the cause of the corruption; which if it were not, he who is punished would not suffer anything. Therefore a penalty of this kind, which is something, is said to be from God on three accounts. On account of the matter, and the nature, and the judgment. But according to two, namely the fault and the corruption, it is not from God. But that penalty which is sin, on two accounts can it be said to be from God, namely on account of the judgment of God, and on account of the nature, which is that no one can so sin, without being punished by that very thing. But the fault and the corruption are in no way from God. It is not therefore to be said that by God's operation or impulse anyone is cast headlong into sin; [0445B] nor even into that which is penalty; but the Scriptures which seem to hold this are to be referred either to permission, or to the withdrawal of grace. Some also refer them to the opening of the way, because wickedness conceived within cannot go out unless a way is opened for it, as Nebuchadnezzar had first conceived malice within, and God, by exposing to him the Jewish nation, opened a way for him, and thus he exercised the malice which he before had shut within. But others refer it to the closing of the way, because God closed all the other ways, that it might not be permitted to go out, so that it has not another by which to go out. Hence one is said to be cast headlong into sin, because he does not close this way by which it goes out, as the others by which it does not go forth. Whence it is clear that God is not the cause why it comes forth by this, but he himself who [0445C] goes out; but why it does not go out by another, God is the cause, and why also rather by this than by another, is from both; why he does not sin worse, God is the author; but why so much, and not less, is from him. Just as, if someone were in a tower, wishing to cast himself down through a window, and some other closed all the windows except one, and that man cast himself down through it. See that not through another: he who closes the rest is the cause that it is through this one, not he who closes; but he himself, who cast himself down. But that it is rather through this than through another, is from both. There are also those who refer them to occasion, as when the Lord entered Jerusalem, the whole city was stirred, and the malice before conceived, having taken occasion, was excited to envy; and thus to persecution. [0445D] Therefore it is referred either to permission, or to the withdrawal of grace, or to the opening of the way, or to the not-closing of the way, according to some, that which is said: *He gave them up to the desires of the heart, and to the passions of shame,* etc. [n: (Rom. I.)] But we rather say all of them, than any one of the several, even if all do not everywhere concur; yet always three, namely permission, and the withdrawal of grace, and the not-closing of the way, do concur; but the opening of the way, and occasion, are not found in all.

QUESTION XLVI. How is God said to give them up to the passions of shame, since they not only suffer nothing there, but even take delight? Solution. On account of the effect; for because eternal penalty follows, [0446A] the passions of sin of this kind are called penalties. Or even in the present life they suffer. For they cannot so sin, without suffering, without nature being harmed, corrupted, and deprived of some good, and defiled; and thus it is true that they already suffer in the present.

QUESTION XLVII. It is asked again what evil it brings upon the reprobate, to be given up to a reprobate sense, since they are not going to repent. For those who are blinded, so that they put light for darkness, and darkness for light, seem to sin less through such blinding, since they do nothing against conscience; whence what is a greater sin in itself, is less to him by whom it is done, since it is less done against conscience. Therefore since these sin less against conscience on account of the reprobate sense, it seems to profit them to be thus blinded. Solution. Such a necessity [0446B] of ignorance does not have excuse, because it comes from a perverse will.

QUESTION XLVIII. How does God not sin by working in their hearts, so that they are inclined to evil? Solution. If the aforesaid things be called to mind, the response is plain: for by doing this he does not send in malice.

QUESTION XLIX. How can it be defended, that God does not act cruelly in condemning this man, who, all grace being withdrawn from him and left to himself, cannot but sin: for if he does not avoid what he cannot avoid, what fault is it to him? or if for this he is condemned, it seems that he is dealt with unjustly. Solution. That impossibility is inexcusable, which proceeded from the fault and vice of his own will; because he first cast himself headlong, although [0446C] now he sins unwillingly; yet God justly condemns him for his sins. *For it is just that he who is in filth should be filthy still [n: (Apoc. XXII)]*.

QUESTION L. How does God not consent to those who sin, since he knows the evil, and can forbid it, and the expositor says: To consent is not to correct when you can. Solution. God corrects and reproves those who sin in many ways, both by natural reason, and by written law, and through his ministers, and through his own or others' scourges: whence he is by no means to be said to consent to those who sin.

QUESTION LI. Whether those who do and those who consent sin equally. Solution. In matters of this kind, which can vary according to different causes, a general judgment [0446D] cannot be given: for according to the intention such things are to be judged.

[n: (Rom. 3)] QUESTION LII. *According to his hardness, he is impenitent,* etc. It is asked what it is to repent. Solution. Penitence is a compunction of mind concerning things past, and a resolution concerning the future, or a grief, because he made a resolution which he will no more carry out. For what else is it truly to repent, than to bewail things committed, and, by resolution, not to commit further things to be bewailed? Penitence is also called satisfaction for sins. Whence the priest is said to enjoin penance. Likewise penitence is of one kind late, of another kind fruitless. Fruitless, which does not profit: as that of Judas, and of the senseless who say: *We senseless ones,* etc. [n: (Sap. V.)]. Late, which is wont to be done tardily when there is no time. Thus it is said: Truly [0447A] penitent is he who is contrite in heart, and confesses with the mouth, and renders worthy satisfaction.

QUESTION LIII. *When it is said in the day of wrath,* etc. It is asked concerning the day of judgment, why it is called the day of wrath rather than of mercy; for as the evil are to hear: *Go into eternal fire,* so the good are to hear: *Come, blessed of my Father,* etc. [n: (Matth. XXV.)] Solution. In that it is called the day of wrath, provision is made for us, that we may always fear that day, and by fearing may beware of the eternal burning. Note that it is called day not for time, but for manifestation. Likewise it is to be noted that all will come to judgment, both good and evil. Of the good, some to judge, namely the very good; others to be judged, as the less good. Likewise of the evil, some are already judged, whose damnation [0447B] is already certain; others yet to be judged, whose damnation is uncertain.

QUESTION LIV. How is it to be understood concerning the saints, that they will judge the evil. To which some respond that this is nothing other than that the excellence of their glory will show how great a penalty they are worthy of who persecuted them, or did not imitate them. Or they are for this reason called judges, because in the judgment itself the eternal reasons will be made manifest to them, according to which the judgment will be made, and the merits of each, concerning which the judgment will be made. According to that of Daniel: *The judgment sat, and the books were opened [n: (Dan. VII)]*, namely of the reasons and merits: those therefore whose penalty or glory is not yet so plain, are to be judged in the day of judgment.

[0447C] QUESTION LV. It is asked again concerning that *according to the impenitent heart,* concerning the sin against the Holy Spirit, what it is, and why it is called irremissible. Whether because it cannot be forgiven; or for this reason because it will never be forgiven, although it could be; or because it is scarcely, and rarely, and with difficulty forgiven. Solution. Some say that the sin against the Holy Spirit is out of envy to disparage the divine goodness: of which sin so great is the stain, that those who so sin can never submit to the humility of penitence. Others say that despair or impenitence is called the sin against the Holy Spirit. Others that it is to sow discords among brethren with the torches of envy. But to those who say that this sin can be forgiven, the objection is made thus: If this is possible, that such a fault be forgiven, from this supposition no [0447D] impossibility follows; but the truth says that *this sin is not forgiven, neither in this world nor in the world to come [n: (Matth. XIV)]*; but if it were forgiven, it would be false that it is not forgiven: which is impossible, because the Truth cannot lie. Of this sin no one is certain: yet this is known, that if anyone has sinned it, he will never obtain pardon.

QUESTION LVI. How does he *render to each according to his works,* since this man's works are good, and his intention evil, and that man's works evil, and his intention good, since the name of the work is imposed from the disposition: will he be condemned for good works, because the intention is evil, or saved for evil ones, because the intention is good? Solution. Intention does not suffice in all cases for works to be called good, but it is always required that they be [0448A] good to him by whom they are done: for if the works be good in themselves, and the intention evil, he is punished for the evil intention, not for the good works; nor will he be rewarded for them, because they are made useless to him through the evil intention. But for this, that the works be evil to him, an evil intention is not required; for the intention can be good or evil, while the works are evil: whatever the intention may have been, from the fact that the works are evil, it harms him who did them: but in those things which are indifferent, that is, as far as in themselves neither good nor evil, judgment ought to be referred according to the intention.
