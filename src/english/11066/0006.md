## I. ON THE EPISTLE TO THE ROMANS. (cont.)

QUESTION LXVIII. It is asked concerning circumcision — to whom it was commanded, and why, and why in the genital parts and not in others, and why to males and not to females, and what efficacy it had, and why baptism succeeded it. Now circumcision is the cutting off of that little skin which is over the male parts; whence that skin too is called the foreskin (*praeputium*); from which the gentiles are called foreskinned (*praeputiati*), because they are without pruning, that is, cutting. It was first commanded to Abraham; whence [0451C] he and his whole household was circumcised. Nor was this command made to all in common or generally, but only to the Jews. Whence it is said: *The soul that shall not have been circumcised shall perish from his people [n: (Gen. XVII)]*. The people of God is the people of the Jews. This was commanded with reason, for God willed his people to be set apart from others, and their hearts to be circumcised both from vices and from concupiscences, and that he might show this in outward things, he commanded them to be circumcised in the flesh: and in the genital parts rather than in others. For since there are two things by which fellowship is chiefly contracted among any people, namely marriage and food, in both God willed his people to be set apart; whence he forbade to the gentiles a common food, namely [0451D] swine's flesh. He also commanded them to be circumcised in the genitals, lest they should pollute those sanctified parts by some intercourse with unclean women, and that they might keep the flesh, as it were the gate of our birth, uncorrupted from vices. And the gentile women, seeing all the males cut, would avoid marriages with them. It was commanded to males alone for this reason, that no one who was without sin was to be born of male seed; but from a woman he was.

QUESTION LXIX. It is likewise asked: of what efficacy was it? And some say that it was of the same [efficacy] as baptism is now, except that it did not send to the kingdom. But if this is so, then in circumcision there was remission of sins; therefore justice, and thus from the law, which the Apostle denies. Solution. We say that in circumcision [0452A] there was not remission of all sins, but only of original sin; but in baptism there is made remission of all, and moreover a conferring of virtues. But whether baptism had this full efficacy before the Passion is wont to be asked. And some indeed say that it did not. Yet it can be said that after Christ's death, upon those who were purified before the cross, there was conferred by the sacrament of baptism itself as much as upon those also who were baptized afterward.

QUESTION LXX. Next it is asked why baptism succeeded circumcision. Solution. For three reasons: for its gentleness, for its comeliness, and for the imperfection of circumcision itself. For it was hard to be circumcised, and more seemly to be washed with waters than to be cut with a knife: baptism is also more general, being suited not only to hands, [0452B] but also to women, and to Jews as well as to gentiles.

QUESTION LXXI. It is asked how the Apostle here says that *to be circumcised is profitable,* when in what follows he says: *If you be circumcised, Christ will profit you nothing [n: (Galat. V)]*. Solution. This is said according to the state of the different times. For in the time of the law it was profitable to observe it; in the time of grace it does not profit according to the letter, but rather harms.

QUESTION LXXII. It is asked of what state it is said: *What then is more,* etc. Solution. Of the former, in which the Jews were before the incarnation of the Lord.

QUESTION LXXIII. Likewise it is asked what it pertains to the matter that he says [this], since those to whom he was writing were not in that state, but in the state of faith. Response. That we, [0452C] who have believed from among the gentiles, might not be proud against the Jews.

QUESTION LXXIV. It is asked: is the assumed man a liar? It seems that he is, because he is a man: and every man is a liar. For one is called a liar, not because he lies or sins, but because he is changeable, *and never remains in the same state [n: (Job XIV)]*, and through sins can dissolve away. All of which some presume to assert even concerning the assumed man, which they attempt to affirm thus. If the assumed man could have not been assumed by the Word, he could have sinned; but he could have not been assumed, therefore he could have sinned: which seems [to follow], if the antecedent be conceded; but all concede that he could have not been assumed. Solution. It is not to be conceded [0452D] that that man could have sinned: for when one says "that man," by "that" the personal property is noted, in which it is impossible for him to sin; but in the consequent, when it is said, if that man could have not been assumed, he could have sinned, that personal property is not understood, but, without regard to it, the human nature is given to be understood. Likewise by another way they wish to prove the same. Christ, when he came into Jerusalem, could have gone into Galilee, and if he had then gone into Galilee, he would not now suffer; and if he did not suffer, he would not fulfill the command of the Father, by whose command he suffered: but he could then have not suffered in Jerusalem, and been disobedient, and so have sinned. Solution. He could indeed thus have not gone to Jerusalem. He could therefore have not fulfilled the precept of the Father: this does not follow — for if he did not then suffer, which [0453A] was possible; whence he says: *I have power to lay down my life, and to take it up again [n: (Joan. X)]*: if this, I say, were the case, that he did not then suffer, it would not be the command of the Father that he should then suffer there; but that these two should be at once is impossible: that the Father should have commanded him to suffer then, and that he himself should not then suffer: this is the common solution.

QUESTION LXXV. It is asked concerning this hypothetical: *If our iniquity commends the justice of God,* etc. Whether it is true, and whether the consequent necessarily follows from the antecedent, and how it is to be understood. Solution. Our iniquity commends the justice of God: this is understood in two ways: either that, by its own quality, it makes the justice of God more commendable, which is false; and according to this understanding the consequence would be true: [0453B] if this were so, God would be unjust, who punishes sins. It can also be understood that from the comparison of our injustice the justice of God appears more commendable, and this is true; but according to this understanding the consequence is not true, nor does the consequent follow from the antecedent: for the false never follows from the true.

QUESTION LXXVI. It is asked how this is to be understood: *why am I still judged as a sinner?* — whose voice is it, of one converted or of one not converted? If a converted man speaks, he is not judged as a sinner; if one not yet converted speaks, God is not yet glorified in his lie. Solution. It is the voice of one converted, and again fallen.

QUESTION LXXVII. It is asked whether God wills evil [0453C] to be done, since he himself uses our evils for good and for his glory, just as we abuse his good things unto our own reproach. Solution. He does not will evil to be done. For it is not to be conceded that he wills evil to be done, or wills it not to be done: for if he willed evil to be done, he would be the author of evil, since, when he wills something to be done, it comes to be by no other than himself as author. Likewise, if he willed it not to be done, and yet it were done, something would resist his will. There are, however, those who say that God wills evil to be done, but according to these "will" is said in several ways, namely for permission, for good pleasure, for command, and for prohibition. He wills evils to be done, that is, he permits them, not that he is their author.

QUESTION LXXVIII. Whether it is good that evil should be? [0453D] Solution. "Good" is said in several ways: the expedient good, the good by its own quality, etc. Likewise "evil" is said in many ways, namely that which harms, that which is corrupted: a depraved act and a depraved will, which is sin — the privation of every good; any disorder whatever is called sin. When therefore it is said, it is good that evil should be, it must be diligently seen what is understood by the name of good or of evil.

QUESTION LXXIX. Since he says: *We have charged that all are under sin,* etc. It does not seem to be a necessary argument for demonstrating that the Jews do not excel the gentiles; for both were under sin, even mortal sin: for it could have happened that these were under a lesser, and those under a greater sin: whence they seem more worthy and fit to receive grace than the rest. [0454A] Solution. If both were under sin, namely mortal, which the Apostle notes, who says "under sin" as though pressed down and enslaved: it is clear that neither did they merit grace, but punishment. Wherefore if grace is conferred on them, namely faith and the other gifts, this is not from their merits, but from the sole goodness of God: therefore, so far as lies in themselves, neither do they excel. We do not deny, however, that those who were under a greater sin were less fit to receive grace: with whom it was dealt more mercifully than with the others, who were under a lesser sin. *But where the offense abounded, grace did more abound [n: (Rom. V)]*: even though the grace of these was not greater than that of those in itself, yet grace superabounded, according to the number of the men. It is not [0454B] to be discussed in this place whether the Jews were subject to a greater sin than the gentiles.

QUESTION LXXX. It does not seem to be true which is said: *There is none that understands, or that seeks after God.* For many in that people had understanding of God, namely that he is one, that he is Creator, and almighty: for if one is called "not understanding" because he did not understand perfectly, then the men of grace also, because they do not yet understand perfectly except through faith, can be called "not understanding." Solution. One can be called "not understanding" who, though he know God in majesty, does not yet know him in humility and mercy. Or one is said "not understanding God perfectly" who, though he have some knowledge, does not have it through the experience of charity [0454C]; or "not understanding God perfectly," namely because he alone is the Creator of all, and moreover is the author of all justice, which the Jews do not understand: establishing their own justice, they are not subject to the justice of God; nay, in a certain manner they make themselves God, when they say that they are justified by their own power without the grace of God, asserting themselves to be authors of justice, which God [alone] can do. Whence the Apostle says that *in the Gospel the justice of God is revealed, not of man (Rom III)*. And elsewhere: *But now without the law the justice of God is made manifest [n: (ibid.)]*. And if at times the justice of man is read of, as David often says *my justice:* it must be so understood that it is the man's who receives, and the same is God's who gives.
