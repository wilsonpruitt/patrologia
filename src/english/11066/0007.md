## I. ON THE EPISTLE TO THE ROMANS. (cont.)

QUESTION LXXXI. It is asked concerning that which is said: [0454D] *Whatever the law says, it says to those who are under the law,* etc. Even if it speaks to the Jews, do all things therefore pertain to the Jews, because they were said to the Jews? Solution. It is to be understood thus: It so speaks to them that the things which it says pertain to them. Which again seems to be false: for many things are said about the gentiles in the law. Whence one must understand "whatever it says" without determination and distinction, from which it is certain whether the discourse is made to the gentiles: so that it speaks about those gentiles, I say, who are of the law, in such a way that what is said pertains to them. To which opinion it is objected thus: Were not David and many other just men, and yet were under the law? and if this is so, how does that which is said pertain to them, "there is none that understands"? Solution. To be "under the law" notes two things, namely to seek justice [0455A] from the law, and to lean wholly upon it, as those who out of fear observe it outwardly; and according to this David and the other just men were not under the law. For they did not seek to be justified from the law itself, but from the faith of Christ yet to come; and thus they were men of the Gospel, not of the law. Those also are said to be "under the law" to whom the law was given, and who do the works of the law: and according to this David and those like him were under the law, because to them the law was given, and they kept it for the sake of those to whom the law was necessary, lest by their example they should despise the law. It is to be noted that there are three kinds of men, namely men of the natural law, of the written law, and of grace. Men of the natural law are said to be those who have the natural law alone, and nothing is superadded. Men of the written law are those to whom the written law was given, [0455B] and they have nothing superadded. Men of grace are those to whom grace itself was given. Or otherwise: Men of the natural law are those who seek to be justified by their own powers; men of the written law are those who lean upon the law, supposing that the law justifies. Men of grace are those who seek justice and salvation from nowhere else but grace alone.

QUESTION LXXXII. Since it is said: For by the works of the law shall no flesh be justified, it is asked concerning Moses and David and the other just men who lived in the time of the law, whether they are just from the works of the law: which seems so, because they did those [works] out of charity. Solution. By faith alone in [the one] to come, so that those ancients were just not from the works of the law. Note that "works of the law," according to some, are said [to be] those which were instituted with the law and terminated with the law, [0455C] namely the ceremonial, which were not instituted for justification, but for the signifying of things to come; but according to others "works of the law" are said [to be] those which are done from fear alone, and not from love: concerning which it is clear that they do not justify: whence the law is said to restrain the hand, and not the mind.

QUESTION LXXXIII. Why do the works of the law done with charity not justify as the works of the Gospel do — do not the moral precepts, when fulfilled, justify? From these it seems that the works of the law justify. Solution. Not whatever we are bound by a precept of the law to do is called a work of the law; but that which was instituted with the law and terminated with the law. Or, what is better: works of the law are those for whose fulfilling the law suffices, which are done [0455D] from fear alone of temporal punishment, which justify no one.

QUESTION LXXXIV. It is asked what is the difference between the works of the law and [those] of the Gospel; for if the works of the Gospel are done from the fear of temporal punishment alone, they confer justice on no one, just as neither do the works of the law. Solution. To the works of the law belong only outward things: whence the law forbids the hand. To the works of the Gospel belong also inward things, such as the inner affections and motions, in which justice consists.

QUESTION LXXXV. It is asked concerning that which is said: *Through the law the knowledge of sin,* etc. — of what sin has knowledge been made through the law? For "sin" is said in several ways, sometimes [as] guilt, sometimes [as] the punishment of sin. Whence God is said to bear our sins, [0456A] that is, the punishment for sin: sometimes [as] a victim for sin, whence *Christ was made sin for us [n: (II Cor. V)]*, that is, a victim for sin. Sometimes also it is said [to be] satisfaction for sin, or a penance enjoined for sin: any disorder whatever of anything whatever is also called sin. Whence the lyre-player is said to have sinned, if he always strays upon the same string. Likewise "sin" is said [to be] the work of sin, and the guilt of sin: which, according to some, is other than the fault, according to others the same as the fault. It is asked, therefore, of what sin knowledge has been made through the law? Solution. Some say that knowledge of that sin which is in the will has been made through the law, because before the law the sin which is in the deed was alone believed to be sin, and concupiscence was not known to be [0456B] sin.

QUESTION LXXXVI. It is again asked why the law does not justify, since it thus makes sin manifest. For what else does the Gospel do than make sin manifest, teaching what is to be followed and what avoided — which the law also does? Likewise, some wish to prove that the law leads to perfection by the words of the Lord, who concerning a precept of the law answered the young man: *Do this, and thou shalt live [n: (Luc. X)]*. Likewise: *Thou shalt love the Lord thy God,* etc. [n: (Matth. XIX.)] This is a precept of the law, but this, when observed, justifies; therefore the law leads to perfection. Likewise Bede says: The law, observed in its own time, conferred not only temporal things, but also eternal. Solution. That a response may be made to all questions of this kind, we say that the law is called a commandment without grace, [0456C] which never confers salvation; but the Gospel is called a commandment with grace, which justifies and leads through to eternal life.

QUESTION LXXXVII. *But now without the law there is justice,* etc. It is asked how both are true, that the justice of God is made manifest without the law, and is testified by the law and the prophets? Solution. The Apostle says that the justice of God is without the law, not that it is made manifest without the law.

QUESTION LXXXVIII. Likewise, when it is said: *The justice of God through faith in Jesus Christ,* it is asked concerning this virtue of faith, whether it can be had equally by the good and by the evil? Solution. In the answer to this question the moderns seem to disagree. For some say that this virtue of faith is had only by the good, and in no [0456D] way by the evil: for it is that [faith] which works through love. And therefore, they say, where there is no love, neither is there faith. But to others it seems that it is had equally by the good and by the evil. For what else is it to have faith, than to believe those things which are to be believed? But all that this good man believes, this evil man also believes; how then does he not have the same faith, especially since Augustine says that faith can be had without charity, and the Apostle: *If,* he says, *I have all faith, so as to remove mountains, but have not charity, I am nothing [n: (I Cor. XXXI)]*: how would he say this, if faith could not be had without charity?

QUESTION LXXXIX. *Justified freely through the grace of Christ.* How does he say *freely through grace?* Would not either "freely" or "through grace" suffice? It seems that one of them [0457A] is superfluous. Solution. He says "freely," that is, without any merit of ours; "through grace," that is, through his gratuitous gifts. For often he confers many things on us through grace indeed, yet not without any merit of ours: which nevertheless does not come about without his grace.

QUESTION XC. *Through the redemption which is in Christ Jesus,* etc. It is asked why God redeemed man through his own death, whom he could have freed by a word alone? Solution. Although another mode was possible to God, yet none was more suited to our misery: because in the same [death] he both conferred on us a remedy, and furnished an example of humility and of love. The remedy is considered in this, that the devil laid his hand upon him who was free from sin: in whom he found nothing that was his own. Therefore he deservedly [0457B] lost those whom he seemed to hold by a certain right, [namely] those believing in him who, through his own death, was made the cause of salvation to all who obey him: for he suffered not for himself, but for us, granting us his merits, so that for them there might come to be for us what would come to be for himself, if he were in need. To humility he provoked us in this, that, descending from the secret bosom of the Father's majesty, he so emptied himself as to take the form of a servant [n: (Philipp. III)]. To charity he invited us in this: that, though he is the Lord of glory, such and so great, he died in such a manner for the impious and for sinners. Note that if he had redeemed man by a word alone, no injury would have been done to the devil. Or if God had reformed the human race through an angel, salvation would not on that account be ascribed to the angel. For God works many things through the angels [0457C] which nevertheless are to be attributed not to the angels, but to God: and therefore he redeemed us through himself, not through another, because the merits of no other could be so great as to suffice for the redemption of the whole world.

QUESTION XCI. It is asked whether God could have made a more fitting mode of redemption? If it be said that he could not, it seems that the power of God has a limit, and is not immeasurable: if it be said that he could, how is this [mode] the most fitting? Solution. Although in this he has a limit, it is nevertheless not to be conceded without qualification that he has a limit. Or, although this mode is the most fitting to our misery, it is nevertheless not necessary that it be the most fitting absolutely.

[0457D]QUESTION XCII. It is asked to whom our price was given, whether to the devil or to God? Solution. It was given to God, not to the devil: because no injury was done to the devil, since he was nothing but as it were a jailer, nor would he even wish to receive it, that he might lose the man: but it was not to be given to one unwilling, lest injury should be done to him.

QUESTION XCIII. It is asked from whom man was redeemed? Solution. From the devil, from sin, from torment. Moreover he is reconciled to God: and this is the twofold efficacy of the blood of Christ.

QUESTION XCIV. It is asked in what the power of the devil has been diminished through the death of Christ. For as before he had the power of tempting the good and the evil, so also now. Solution. It has been diminished not according to essence, but according to [0458A] efficacy: because he cannot prevail as much as before, especially because powers of resisting have been given to man, and the stronger man is for resisting, the weaker is the enemy for assailing.
