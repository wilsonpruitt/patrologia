## I. ON THE EPISTLE TO THE ROMANS. (cont.)

QUESTION XCV. It is asked, when it is said *unto the showing of the just one,* etc., concerning the ancient just, who were held in hell, whether their sins had been remitted to them through faith and penance: and if they had been remitted, why were they held in hell: for without the remission of sins they were not just. Solution. All sins had been remitted to them through faith and love, but they obtained the full effect of remission. Twofold is the effect of remission, namely, to be without punishment, and to enjoy glory; only the one of these they had before the death of Christ: [0458B] because they did not feel actual punishment, the other they had not yet received, because they did not see God. Again it is objected: Were they not just, and so worthy of glory? Why then was not that given to them of which they were worthy? Solution. Their justice was not so great as to suffice for obtaining life without the death of Christ, nor even were their sins remitted simply, except under a certain expectation and pledge of one to come, who should make satisfaction for them. Whence the Apostle says that Christ died not only for the remission of present sins, but also of preceding ones; *because he is the lamb who was slain from the origin of the world [n: (Hebr. IX)]*.

QUESTION XCVI. Whether God could damn them with eternal punishment, since they were just, and he could not justly punish the just — for what is unjust, God [0458C] cannot do: and if he could not punish them, in what did he sustain them? Solution. It was necessary that satisfaction be made for original sin, and since they themselves could not make satisfaction unless another made satisfaction for them, God could justly punish them. Yet it is not necessary to concede that he would punish the just. For this would be after the death of Christ: for they are not to be called just simply, but only in comparison with the death of Christ: for they did not have so great an actual justice from which they could justly demand eternal life, just as neither do we without the death of Christ: yet Scripture calls them just, because they had as much as God demanded, since what was lacking to them Christ was to supply.

[0458D]QUESTION XCVII. When it is said: *By what law of deeds?* etc., it is asked why the Mosaic law, the written law, is called the law of deeds, and not the Gospel: but the law of the Gospel [is called] the law of grace and the law of justice: for as that one has works, so does this. Solution. The Mosaic law is called [the law] of deeds, because it only commands the things which are to be done, and does not confer the grace through which the things commanded may be fulfilled: and therefore the killing letter is called a commandment without grace. But the Gospel indeed commands the things which are to be done: yet moreover it confers the grace through which the things commanded may be fulfilled. Or for this reason: the written law is called the law of deeds, because the men of the law placed their whole justice in the works of the law: but the law of faith or of grace is so called, because the men of grace place the whole sum and efficacy [0459A] of their salvation in grace alone: knowing that, as no one is saved from the justice of works, so no one is justified from the works of justice. For justice is not from good works, but good works from justice.

QUESTION XCVIII. *Is he not also of the gentiles,* etc. The Apostle proves that God is [God] of the gentiles, because he is their creator. But thus it seems that he is the God of stones. Solution. He is indeed the creator of stones, but he is the creator of the gentiles in another way, whom he created to his own image and likeness: whence he is deservedly called [God] of the gentiles, as one truly to be worshiped by them.

QUESTION XCIX. Whether the works which precede faith are entirely useless, or whether they profit for anything; for it seems that they profit nothing, because the whole life of unbelievers is sin. Solution. Good works which are done [0459B] before faith, even if they do not profit for meriting life, are nevertheless of avail for receiving it, as it seems to some, as appears in the case of Cornelius.

QUESTION C. *Do we then destroy the law through faith? God forbid: but we establish the law.* It is asked how this is true, when elsewhere he says: *If the things which I have destroyed I build again, I make myself a transgressor: and through the law I am dead to the law [n: (Galat. II)]*. There he says that he has destroyed the law: here he says that he does not destroy it, but establishes it. Solution. To destroy the law is taken in two ways. Whence here the Apostle says that he does not destroy the law, that is, he does not show the law to be useless in its own time, and to be of no avail, nor that it is not to be fulfilled spiritually. Elsewhere he said that he destroyed it, that is, he preached that after the fulfillment of the truth it ought to cease as regards [0459C] the carnal things.

QUESTION CI. *Abraham believed God, and it was reputed to him unto justice [n: (Rom. IV)]*. It is asked why he says *it was reputed,* as though it were not the true justice which he had through faith, but something which was reputed unto justice: for if you owed me a horse, I would not fittingly say: Give me a horse, and I will repute it for a horse; but I could suitably say: Give me an ass, and I will repute it for a horse. Solution. If man had not sinned, he would have justice in every respect, which consists in the fulfilling in every respect of the precepts of God, so that he would desire nothing at all against reason, and would love God with his whole heart; but after sin, and on account of sin, man could not have this perfect justice [0459D] to which eternal beatitude is deservedly owed: but God through his grace gives man faith, which likewise, through the same grace, he reputes for that perfection: as if he had the perfection of justice.

QUESTION CII. *But to him who works, the reward is not imputed,* etc. It is asked of what works he here treats: whether of the inward, or of the outward. Outward [works] are such as to clothe the poor, and the rest of this kind, which many do not perform, although they have the time for performing them, such as contemplative men, whence they seem unworthy of salvation, if the discourse here be about works of this kind. Inward works are such as to believe, to love, to pray, which are common to all the faithful, because without them there is no salvation, concerning which, if it is here treated, how is faith without works reputed unto justice for him [0460A] who does not have the time for working? Solution. He treats of outward works, which are required of those who have the means of doing them, and for those who do not have the means of doing them, the will is reputed for the deed.

QUESTION CIII. *According to the purpose of the grace of God,* etc. Here it is wont to be asked concerning grace and merit. For it seems either that the whole is from grace, so that merit confers nothing; or, if anything is from merit, that not the whole is from grace. But that the whole is from grace, the Scriptures testify. Whence the Apostle: *What hast thou that thou hast not received? [n: (I Cor. IV.)] By the grace of God I am what I am [n: (I Cor. XV)]*. And that: *Grace for grace [n: (Joan. I)]*. What then is to be said? To say that merit is nothing is the error of the Manicheans, just as to assert that the whole is from free will is the error of the Pelagians. Solution. [0460B] When it is said that the whole is from grace, merit is not excluded, since merit is from grace. And therefore it must be seen what the grace of God works in us without us, and what it works in us not without us. And so prevenient grace, which is also called operating, heals the free will, freeing it from the yoke of sin: and this it does in us without us: then the healed will is not idle, nor does it receive the grace of God in vain, which it works not by itself, but with grace; nay, the grace of God cooperates with the free will: whence it is called cooperating; and the same work or merit is said to be from grace and from will: for it does not work separately, but together. Whence, although the whole is from grace, it does not follow that nothing is from merit, or from free will; as if someone should find a little child lying in the mud [0460C] and unable to rise, and should lift him up, then hold his hand so that he might walk, the walking itself would be from both, just as the lifting of him is from the finder alone and not from the child: so, from prevenient grace alone it is that we will the good, just as from subsequent grace — I do not say alone, but also from free will, healed and deliberating through grace — it is that we work the good.

QUESTION CIV. *Blessed are they whose iniquities are forgiven.* It is asked concerning original sin what it is, of which the doctors discourse somewhat obscurely. For some say that original sin is the guilt of eternal punishment, that is, the debt and liability by which we are bound over to punishment: but according to this original sin is not [0460D] a fault, but a punishment. But that it is a fault, the authorities testify, which one must concede. But others say that original sin is the tinder of sin: concupiscence, or concupiscibility: the law of the members, the law of the flesh, the languor of nature, the tyrant who dwells in our members: the innate vice which makes the child apt to lust, the adult lusting: by these and other names original sin is called.

QUESTION CV. Why it is called "original" is wont to be asked. Solution. Because it is drawn from the vicious condition of our origin.

QUESTION CVI. Likewise it is asked why it is imputed to posterity? Response. Because the intercourse of the parents does not [0461A] come about without lust, nor the conception of the children without sin.

QUESTION CVII. How is it destroyed in baptism? Solution. Wholly, as to the guilt, it is mitigated, and it is weakened as to the act or the affection. But others say that original sin is the privation of a certain original justice which man would have, if he had not sinned: and therefore, because he is deprived of justice, he is deprived also of glory, nor will those who are punished for original sin alone suffer any other punishment, except that they will forever lack the vision of God. Likewise, since the soul is not from transmission (*ex traduce*), but the flesh alone [is], it is asked how this sin is drawn through the flesh: for the flesh itself cannot without the soul have fault, which is not, nor can be, except in a rational creature: for that which is not [0461B] capable of justice, [is] neither [capable] of sin.

QUESTION CVIII. How then, in the propagation of offspring from the parents, does that pass through the flesh which cannot be in the flesh alone without the soul? Solution. Sin is said to pass, because its cause passes, which is a certain pollution and uncleanness which the soul finds in the flesh when it is infused into it, and by which it is polluted: whence, although the soul alone lusts, yet the soul is not said to lust, because the soul lusts from the flesh.

QUESTION CIX. Whether the soul is ever such as it was created by God: for if it was created clean by God, and from the moment it was joined and coupled to the flesh it was unclean, it follows that it is never such as it was created by God. Solution. It can be conceded that it never [0461C] was altogether such as it was created by God, as if I had given you a clean apple, and you received it with unclean hands: it would be true that you never have such an apple as I gave you.

QUESTION CX. It is wont to be asked why the guilt of original sin is imputed to the soul, created clean by God. The doctors say that this question is insoluble; yet it can be said that it is not unjust that glory be not given to the soul not having justice: for this is to be subject to the guilt of original sin, which is to be deprived of original justice. What wonder, then, if such a fault be punished with such a punishment?
