## I. ON THE EPISTLE TO THE ROMANS. (cont.)

QUESTION CXI. *And he received the sign of circumcision, and the seal of justice,* etc. Concerning circumcision [0461D] it was said above. But here it can be asked whether Abraham obtained any benefit from circumcision. Solution. The doctors say that through it he was only shown to be just, not made [just]. Did it then have greater efficacy in his posterity than in Abraham himself? Which seems so, since to them it was given as a remedy at least for original sin; but to Abraham himself it was given only for the showing of justice.

QUESTION CXII. Likewise it is asked whether from circumcision remission of original sin was given to those children who were held by no other [sin], how they were not justified through it, and thus from the law. The solution of this question is clear from what has been said.

QUESTION CXIII. It is asked concerning the children who died before [0462A] the eighth day, on which circumcision was performed, whether they were damned or saved. Solution. The same judgment is concerning those uncircumcised as concerning the unbaptized, namely that they are damned by the guilt of original sin alone. But if it be asked concerning those children who died as soon as they were born, before circumcision, perhaps they were succored by the faith of their parents.

QUESTION CXIV. *For not through the law was the promise,* etc. It is asked how the Apostle says: the promise was not *made* to *Christ* through the law, *but through the justice of faith;* was not the promise made in the law and through the law, namely in David, to Christ himself? Likewise: What is it for the promise to be made to Christ through the justice of faith? Solution. The Apostle does not say thus, that the promise was not [0462B] made through the law, but through the justice of faith; but thus, that the promise was not made — that he should be the heir of the world — through the law, but through the justice of faith.

QUESTION CXV. Likewise it is asked how the promise was made to Abraham through justice; was it by the merit of his own faith? but if by the merit of Abraham's own faith the promise was made, then by the same merit also [was] the fulfillment of the promise itself, how then [is it] by grace alone? Solution. The letter must not be construed thus: The promise was made through the justice of faith; but thus: The promise was made to Abraham, that he should be the heir of the world, through the justice of faith: through which he himself was made the father of believers, and believers are made the sons of Abraham, that is, just men and heirs. The expositor here says otherwise than we.

QUESTION CXVI. Because it is said: *The law works the wrath of God.* [0462C] It is asked how this is to be understood. Is not the law good, and wrath evil? How does that which is good work that which is evil? Is not that whose effect is evil itself also evil? How then does such a cause have such an effect? Solution. The law does not work wrath immediately, and by its own quality, and as an efficient cause, but as it were by occasion; because if the law had not been given, the wrath would not be so great, which was increased through the transgression of the law. For many things are said to effect certain things, not because they effect them, but because without them they would not come to be; whence Christ too is said to be *set,* not only *unto resurrection* by cause, but also *unto ruin [n: (Luc. II)]* by occasion. And the Apostle is said to be, not only *the odor of life,* but [0462D] also *the odor of death [n: (II Cor. II)]*. According to the same reasoning one must be vigilant concerning like cases.

QUESTION CXVII. How does the Apostle prove that the law works wrath, saying: *For where there is no law, neither [is there] transgression?* For this too does not seem to be true. Where there is no law, cannot there be transgression of the natural law, or of the Gospel? How then [is it] true, where there is no law, neither [is there] transgression? But if it be so expounded, where there is no law, neither [is there] transgression of the law, by the same argument it is proved that where there is no Gospel, neither is there transgression of the Gospel. Solution. By the name of "law" the Apostle often designates a commandment without grace; but by the name of "Gospel" is always understood a commandment with grace: whence it stands true, where there is no law, that is, a commandment without grace, neither [is there] transgression. [0463A] Likewise, where there is a commandment with grace, there is not transgression, but fulfillment of the commandment.

QUESTION CXVIII. Likewise, when it is said: *Who against hope believed in hope.* It is asked concerning the faith of Abraham, which the Apostle here praises: which is reputed to him unto justice: what or of what thing it was? For if the words of the Apostle and of Genesis are diligently considered, it seems that that faith, by which he believed that offspring was promised to him by God, was reputed to him unto justice; but if this is so, then some faith besides the faith of Christ is a justifying faith. But if it be said that he then had the faith of Christ, it is asked whether two faiths justify, or why justice is rather said to be from that one than from this? Solution. The Apostle commends the faith of Abraham, by which he believed all things which were to be believed: by which faith, among other things, [0463B] he believed God to be truthful in the promise of offspring.

QUESTION CXIX. How is the generation of Isaac said to be against nature? What is nature? Is it not a certain power of creatures, implanted by the Creator, through which like things are procreated and propagated from like? Did not nature there work? Was there not a certain tempering in the works of the parents in the act and from the mingling of flesh? how [then is it] against nature? Solution. When the Author of nature works in nature beyond the accustomed course of nature, that is, not according to the lower causes, but according to the higher, then something is said to come about against nature, but it would more properly be called above nature.

QUESTION CXX. *Most fully knowing that whatever [0463C] God has promised, he is able also to do.* The expositor says that by much beholding of the divine power he knew God to be almighty, and the Apostle [says he was] "most fully knowing," etc., which seems to be harmful to faith; because faith has no merit, to which human reason furnishes proof. Likewise, if he knew him to be God, what great thing [is it], if he believed him to be almighty; or if he did not know him to be God, how did he believe him? Solution. That beholding of the divine power which Abraham had was not from human reason, but from the constancy of faith: by which he was as certain as if he most fully knew, or saw.

QUESTION CXXI. But as to what is asked, what great thing it was that he believed God to be almighty, since not only the good, but also the evil believe that? [0463D] We say that to believe that was then a great thing, when almost the whole world was straying in the worship of demons, nor had the faith of one God yet been preached, or manifestly declared by the Scriptures, as [it is] now.

QUESTION CXXII. *And not only was it written,* etc. It is asked how the Apostle says that this was written on account of Abraham, that faith was reputed unto justice? does he obtain any benefit from the fact that he is praised in the Scriptures, or imitated by us? Solution. Perhaps the Apostle does not say this because it profits him, but it is to be understood thus: And not only, etc., that is, this was written not only that it might be shown whence Abraham himself was [0464A] just, but that it might be demonstrated to us also in what alone we can be justified, namely in the grace of faith, and nowhere else.

QUESTION CXXIII. *He was delivered up for our offenses,* etc. So the authority says. It is asked by whom he was delivered up? And it is certain that [he was delivered] by God the Father, by Judas and by the Jews, and by Pilate. But it is asked whether that which was done by God and by the Jews was good or evil? If it be said good, then the Jews did good. But if it be said evil, then God did evil. Likewise: Did not the Jews do only evil, and God only good? how then did God and the Jews do the same? Solution. The work or deed of the Jews is taken equivocally for the act and for the passion; the act of the Jews [was] only evil; the passion [0464B] of Christ, which followed from their act, was good. Therefore the doctors sometimes unite God and the Jews, even in the same deed or work, on account of the passion of Christ, which came to pass both from the action of the Jews and from [that] of God; sometimes they distinguish [them] on account of the diversity of the acts. But if it be asked whether the work of the Jews was good, distinguish thus: Their act [was] evil, the passion which they inflicted was good.

QUESTION CXXIV. Likewise it is asked why the Apostle distinguishes thus, saying: *That Christ was delivered up for our offenses, and rose again for our justification,* since both — both the passion and the resurrection — both free from sins and justify? Solution. Although each of them is a cause, yet [not each is] a figure.

QUESTION CXXV. *And not only so, but also [sic: sedet] we glory [0464C] in tribulations [n: (Rom. V)]*, etc. How does the Apostle glory in tribulations, which are bitter, and not to be sought for their own sake? Solution. Not on account of the tribulations themselves, but on account of their effect, is one to glory in them. But it is objected that by the same reasoning one ought to glory in sins, not for their own sake, but for the good, which they often [produce], as when they bring about humility, whence the Prophet: *Before I was humbled, I offended [n: (Psal. CXVIII)]*. Solution. Tribulations by their own quality cooperate with the inner grace unto the attaining of patience, but sins do not; rather God by his mercy makes some good come forth from our evils.

QUESTION CXXVI. When the Apostle says: *But hope does not confound.* It is asked concerning hope, what it is, and whether it is had without charity? Solution. Hope is the certain expectation [0464D] of future goods, which differs from faith in this, that faith is about things past, present, and future, both good and evil; but hope [is] only about things future and good. But it seems that it is not had without charity: for without good works and a good life, which is not without charity, to hope for future goods is not hope, but rather presumption, as Augustine says. Likewise Augustine seems to assert the same, that hope precedes charity. Solution. One is the hope of pardon, another is the hope of glory; the first is had even by the evil, the second perhaps only by the good.
