## I. ON THE EPISTLE TO THE ROMANS. (cont.)

QUESTION CXXVII. *The charity of God is poured forth,* etc. It is asked whether the charity by which God loves us, and that by which we love God, is the same? Solution. There are those who [0465A] say that it is the same; whom Augustine meets, expounding this passage and saying that here the discussion is of the charity by which we love God, just as there, *God is charity [n: (I Joan. IV)]*, the discussion is of the charity by which God loves us: which he would not say, if it were the same. Likewise elsewhere he says: I call charity a movement of the mind toward loving God for his own sake, and the neighbor for God's sake. God is not a movement, therefore there is a charity which is not God. Likewise charity can be increased and diminished, but God cannot, therefore there is a charity which is not God. And so it must be firmly held that the name of charity is said equivocally of God, when it is said God is charity, and of a certain virtue, when it is said, a certain virtue is charity, or the charity of God is poured forth in our hearts. The Apostle [0465B] Paul almost everywhere designates by this name the virtue which is not God, but from God. John by the same name signifies God himself. But as to that which Augustine says, that fraternal charity, by which we love one another, is God: we concede it, saying that God, [who is] charity, makes us also love one another, but by the mediation of charity, just as he makes us believe by the mediation of faith; for from whom faith is, from the same is charity, as Augustine says. But it is objected: God is charity in us, and also charity which is a virtue; therefore there are two charities in us, or we love God by two charities. Solution. They are not on that account two. Just as the sun illumines us and the ray of the sun, therefore two illumine us — it does not follow, because this would be apart and separately: for the sun through the ray, and God through charity [0465C] the virtue, illumines or enlightens us.

QUESTION CXXVIII. It is likewise asked whether charity, once had, can be lost? And some say that it cannot, because it is written: *Charity never falls away [n: (I Cor. XIII)]*. Solution. This was said of perfect charity, or it was said that charity never falls away for this reason, that it is had in the present and in the future.

QUESTION CXXIX. Charity is that fountain of which it is said: Let the fountain of thy water be thine own, and let no stranger share it with thee. It is asked who the strangers are, if not the reprobate, and those who are to hear: *Go, ye cursed, into eternal fire [n: (Matth. XXV)]*, etc. But many of the number of such love for a time, and thus as strangers share [0465D] this fountain. Solution. Strangers are so called according to foreknowledge — of whom he is not now speaking — but according to present injustice, namely all who do not love now, whether they be elect or not; and these, as such, do not share the fountain of love.

QUESTION CXXX. How is this true, which is said: *Christ died for the ungodly.* Did not Christ die only for those to whom his death is profitable? but it is profitable only to the godly, therefore he died only for the godly. It seems, therefore, that he did not die for the ungodly. Solution. He died for the ungodly, that is, for the love of those who were formerly ungodly, and through faith were made godly; and so it is true that the death of Christ was made for the ungodly, and that it profits none but the godly.

[0466A]QUESTION CXXXI. *We are reconciled to God through the death of his Son.* It is asked how this word is to be understood. Was he perhaps angry at us before the death, as at sinners, according to that: *Thou hast hated all who work iniquity [n: (Psal. V)]*; and through the death of the Son was he appeased toward us, and did he then first begin to love? But if this is so, how did he choose us before the foundation of the world? Did he not choose while not loving; or how, being not appeased, did he deliver up the Son for us? Solution. The wrath of God is called not a passion of the mind, but a just vengeance; and when such wrath is ended — which was done through the death of Christ — God is said to be appeased toward us, and we to be reconciled to him; nor yet, because he reconciled, did he love; but because he loved, he reconciled. Of that justice by which Christ conquered it has been said above [0466B], and of the manner of the redemption. Nor is it needful to do again what is done: let only the words of Augustine be briefly set down, by which he plainly expresses it. He says therefore: This is the justice by which Christ conquered the devil. The devil, a lover of power and a deserter of justice, slew Christ, in whom he found nothing worthy of death. Whence it is just that those whom he held should be let go free, believing in him who was slain without any evil desert. He willed not, therefore, to use power against the lover of power, but against the deserter of justice he willed to use justice, that he might instruct us how we must fight against the same enemy; namely, not by power, but rather by justice, and so we shall be victors.

QUESTION CXXXII. *Therefore as through one,* etc. It is asked why he says rather that sin entered into the world through one man than [0466C] through one woman, since the beginning of sin was rather in the woman than in the man? Solution. He kept the order of custom, as Augustine says, because posterity is wont to be named not from the woman but from the man. Or for this reason, because man and woman are one flesh; and therefore whatever was done by him or by her is said to pertain to the first man. Or otherwise: if the man had not sinned, God would perhaps have provided him another companion, of whom he would have begotten offspring innocent and conformed to himself, or by his merit would have forgiven the sins of the wife; but because he sinned, through him not undeservedly is sin said to have entered into the world.

QUESTION CXXXIII. It is likewise asked why sin is said to have entered through man and not through the devil, [0466D] since it was first in him, and through him entered also into the first man, whence it is written: *By the envy of the devil death* entered into the world [n: (Sap. II)]. Solution. In one way through the devil, in another way through man did sin enter. For through the devil by imitation alone, and not by propagation. Through man it entered not by imitation alone, but also by propagation. It is to be known that the Pelagians said that original sin entered through Adam by imitation alone and not by propagation: which if it were true, he would have called not man, but the devil, the author of sin: whence also they said that in baptism original sin is not forgiven to little ones, because according to them nothing is contracted in the newborn: but the catholic faith does not hold this, but preaches this, and the Scriptures testify to this, that, just as [0467A] Christ, beyond the example of imitation, works illumination and justification inwardly and secretly, by which grace alone he inserts into his body the regenerated little ones who cannot imitate him; so Adam, beyond the example of imitation, by the taint of his concupiscence corrupted all who were to be born of him through concupiscence.

QUESTION CXXXIV. Julian asks thus: He does not sin who creates, he does not sin who generates, he does not sin who is generated: through what chinks, then, amid so many safeguards of innocence, do you feign that original sin has entered? To whom Augustine answers thus: The Apostle says: *Through one man sin [sic: inravit] into this world [n: (Rom. V)]*; what does he seek more openly? what does he seek [sic: *nculcatius*]? what does he seek more plainly? why does he seek a chink, [0467B] where he has a most open door?

QUESTION CXXXV. It is likewise asked whether original sin is from the will or from nature: if from the will, the will is evil; if from nature, nature is evil. To whom Augustine responds: Every evil work proceeded from an evil will as from a root, but no evil preceded the evil will itself, but it arose first in the angel, then in man, both of whom were the good work of God; and thus in the good, evil arose, which had no efficient cause, but a deficient cause.

QUESTION CXXXVI. It is likewise asked whether original sin is from the will. Solution. It is indeed from the will of the first parents, whence it too can be called voluntary.

[0467C]QUESTION CXXXVII. *In whom all have sinned,* etc. We were not yet; how then could we, who were not yet, sin? Solution. In him we all sinned, that is, in him was done that from which we all should sin. Or thus: From him we drew the cause of sin; or thus: We are all guilty of that sin which he committed.

QUESTION CXXXVIII. It is also asked how we all were one man in Adam? Solution. That is, from him, who was one man, we descend by propagation.

QUESTION CXXXIX. It is asked again how from that small mass, which was in Adam, so many and so great things could descend, or how so small a particle could be divided into so many parts, that the body of each may thence materially consist. This question is wont to be raised concerning those *seven loaves,* with which the Lord satisfied so many thousands of men without the addition of anything [n: (Matth. XV)]. Solution. This was done by the law of propagation. For that particle which was in the loins of Adam, from the time it was separated in the generation of his son, was increased and multiplied in itself into the perfect stature of a man: from which again a small particle was separated in the second generation, which again was increased and multiplied in itself: from which again, thus multiplied, another was separated, and so on thereafter by the law of propagation it grew into so great a multitude without any extrinsic addition, or change of foods into it. It is agreed [0468A] that God can make from one atom whatever weight he will.

QUESTION CXL. If therefore one atom were thus multiplied in itself into something great, in which there were infinite atoms, it is asked whether that one would be all these, or one of the number of the others, or whether these are that one. Solution. It is not necessary to concede that that one is all these, but that it is multiplied and increased into these; nor that all these are that one, but from that one, or that they were in that one — just as that grain grew into a tree, nor is that grain the tree, but the tree is from it, or materially in it. Expressions of this kind are to be noted carefully. It is to be noted that there are several kinds of changes: sometimes essence is changed into essence without change of accidents, as in the sacrament of the altar; [0468B] sometimes a change of properties takes place without change of essence, as when water was made wine; sometimes a change takes place according to quantity alone, into something greater, as in the seven loaves. But in what manner the rod of Moses was changed into a serpent — whether according to properties alone, both substantial and accidental, or according to essence — is not certain to me.

QUESTION CXLI. It is written: *God did not make death.* Likewise: *Death and life are from God [n: (Sap. I)]*, which seems to be contrary: for nothing temporal is from God which he himself has not made; how then did he make death, and not make it? Solution. God did not make death, that is, the cause of death, namely sin; but that penalty which is called death, according to some, God did make: and it is numbered among the good works, [0468C] because it is just; and everything just is from God.
