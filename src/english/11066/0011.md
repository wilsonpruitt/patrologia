## I. ON THE EPISTLE TO THE ROMANS. (cont.)

QUESTION CXLII. *But not as the offense, so also the gift.* It is asked how the Apostle says that the gift of Christ abounds unto more than the offense of Adam: for not more are saved through the grace of Christ than are damned by the offense of Adam. Solution. This superabundance of grace is not to be considered in the number of persons; but to those very ones to whom the gift of Christ is profitable, it confers more in good than the offense of Adam harms those whom it harms in evil; because from Adam not all damnation [comes], but that alone by which those are punished who lie subject to original sin alone, apart from that by which those are punished who have added their own offenses; but the grace of Christ not only frees from original sin, but also from the things superadded, advancing unto justification, and at length unto eternal life.

QUESTION CXLIII. Whether actual sins are from the sin of Adam? Which seems so to some, because from the tinder of sin we are prone to sinning. Solution. Such proneness is not an efficient cause, even if it be a cause without which it would not come to be, as it pleases some; but neither is it required, since it was neither in the devil, nor in the first man before the fall, and yet there was sin in them without it: for it was not the cause of sin, but sin was the cause of it.

QUESTION CXLIV. It is asked whether the other actual sins of Adam are imputed to his posterity? Solution. The Apostle says by *the offense of one,* he does not say by offenses, *many are made sinners:* in which he intimates that not many, but one alone is imputed.

[0469A]QUESTION CXLV. It is asked whether the sin of Eve too is imputed to us? Solution. Note the words of the Apostle saying by the offense of one, he does not say of two; or if it is imputed, for the aforesaid reasons, he says that through one man sin entered into the world, or by the offense of one.

QUESTION CXLVI. It is asked whether the actual sins of nearer parents are imputed to their posterity? Which seems so, because the Lord says: *I am the Lord thy God, a jealous [God]: visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation [n: (Exod. XX)]*, etc. Solution. The sins of parents, even of nearer ones, are not imputed to the children, unless they themselves make the sins of the fathers their own by imitation; nor then are they punished because [they are] parents, but because they themselves sinned. According to another Scripture: [0469B] *The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father [n: (Ezech. XVIII)]*, etc.; but the contradiction which seems [to lie] between these two authorities, the doctors sufficiently elucidate.

QUESTION CXLVII. But if it be asked for what reason the sin of the first parents, and not of the nearer ones, is imputed. Solution. Because that one despoiled us; the other sins of others, finding us as it were naked and despoiled, could not take anything away from us: for original sin, as has already been said, deprives only of a certain original justice.

QUESTION CXLVIII. Man, of himself and through himself, before sin was able to sin; but to advance of himself and through himself, without the help of grace, he was not able; therefore he was more prone to evil than to good; but he was not yet [0469C] other than such as God had made him: therefore God made him more prone to evil than to good. Solution. Before the fall man was not prone to sin; nor did God make man such that he was prone to sinning, but such proneness afterward came in from sin; nor even did he have the power of sinning from God, but from his own nothingness; not from the good which he had received, but from its limit; not because he had received so much, but because he had not received more, as has already been said above concerning the same.

QUESTION CXLIX. *As through the disobedience of one,* etc. It is asked whether man, first, from the good which he had before the fall, without the addition of others, could obey grace and fulfill the commandment given to him? If it be said that he could: therefore, from that which he then had he could [0469D] advance: which is denied by almost all. Likewise, if it be conceded that he could not obey without the help of grace, what then was his fault, if he did not do what he could not do without grace, and grace was not conferred; nor was it his fault that it was not conferred. Solution. He did not sin because he did not do what he could not do; but because he did not do it when he could.

QUESTION CL. It is likewise asked whether Christ, by obeying God the Father, merited anything? Some wish to prove that he merited nothing, because neither according to his humanity nor according to his divinity. God, they say, cannot receive anything from anyone; or through anything that he does acquire something else not previously due [0470A]: therefore he cannot merit anything. Likewise, according as he is man, he is good and loves; but he cannot not be good, or not love: therefore of necessity he is good or loves; how then can he merit? Solution. To merit has a twofold signification: for one is said to merit when through a good work he is made worthy of something of which he was not previously worthy — according to which signification it seems to us that neither according to his divinity nor according to his humanity did Christ merit anything, even in his very death. One is also said to merit when he does some good which is worthy of remuneration — according to which God too is said to merit, when he bestows benefits on us for which we are bound to praise him for eternity; and Christ, according to [0470B] his humanity, in his passion merited for us the entrance of eternal life. Beforehand indeed he merited many things for us; but the passion alone was not only precious, but the price of the world.

QUESTION CLI. *The law entered in, that [sin] might abound,* etc. It is asked whether the law is the cause of evil, which seems so, because it is the cause of the abounding of the offense. Solution. 'That' [ut] is sometimes causative, as: I [go] to the market, that I may buy a toga; sometimes operative, as: he placed man in paradise, that he might keep it, etc.; sometimes it is consecutive, as: he went outside, so that he died; sometimes also it denotes occasion, as: the law entered in, that sin might abound. For the Jews received a more abundant occasion of sin from the law, which was good, not the cause of evil; but their vice was the cause of this evil, [0470C] just as if I take occasion of envying from another's knowledge; which [knowledge] is not the cause of my grief, but my own vice.

QUESTION CLII. *Where the offense abounded, grace also superabounded.* Did not the offense superabound in the Jews, because [of] the transgression of the law; and yet grace did not also superabound, because they were blinded. How then is it true, where the offense abounded, grace also superabounded? Solution. He does not say: Wherever the offense abounded, grace also superabounded; but 'where' indefinitely, which is to be understood as regards those who believed, in whom, before they believed, the offense abounded; because besides the other sins the transgression of the law was in them: but grace forgave all; moreover it conferred faith and charity — not [0470D] as regards those who died in their sins, in whom the offense abounded, and not grace.

QUESTION CLIII. *What then shall we say: Shall we remain in sin, that grace may abound? [n: (Rom. VI.)]* Concerning these things it is asked what it is to remain in sin. Solution. Not to repent of sin, or, by a certain torpor of mind, persevering in sin, to await grace.

QUESTION CLIV. *Whoever of you have been baptized in Christ,* etc. It is asked what sins are forgiven in baptism? Solution. Augustine says that not only past or present [sins], but even future ones.

QUESTION CLV. But it is asked how future [0471A] sins, which are not yet, of which no one repents, nor is yet held [liable] for them, because no one is yet guilty for them — how, I say, are they forgiven? Solution. Future sins are said to be forgiven in baptism for this reason, that through the grace given there they are guarded against, or for this reason, that afterward he who receives this sacrament more easily obtains pardon.

QUESTION CLVI. Whether sins are forgiven to one who approaches feignedly? Solution. Augustine says that yesterday's day is loosed, and whatever there was over and above fraternal hatred, in the very hour of being baptized; but they return at once because he does not repent.

QUESTION CLVII. It is asked how what Augustine says is true? Is he not, from the fact that he does not repent, a member of the devil? how then is he a member [0471B] of Christ? And if this is so, how are his sins forgiven him? he has not faith, he has not the spirit of Christ; therefore he is not a member of Christ, nor are his sins forgiven him. Solution. Some say that the aforesaid words of Augustine are to be understood thus. Yesterday is loosed, etc., that is, baptism has such efficacy even in him who approaches with a heart not contrite, that, unless the feigning remained in him, he would obtain pardon of all offenses, and afterward he will be able to obtain it, if he be willing to repent of his feigning.

QUESTION CLVIII. *Our old man is crucified,* etc. It is asked whether the old man, and the outer man, and the new man, and the inner man are the same? Solution. Not the same, because the outer man is called that which we have in common with the animals, the inner man that which [0471C] we possess in common with the angels. But the old man pertains to both; for not the outer man alone, but also the inner is old *por* fault, concerning which old [man] we must treat in the present chapter.

QUESTION CLIX. It is asked, therefore, what the old man is? They say that the tinder of sin is so called; but it is truer that the tinder itself is called a part of the oldness. But it seems to us that oldness is understood according to two [things], namely fault and penalty. Now of penalty, one kind is eternal, another temporal; but of fault, one kind is original, another actual. Likewise original fault consists in the tinder itself, and in its act, and in the guilt of both. Again, actual fault is of one kind venial, of another mortal. Venial consists in three [things], namely [0471D] consent, act, and the guilt of both. Similarly mortal is understood by the same three modes. Behold the elevenfold number of transgression, in which the old man is regarded; for there is one pair in penalty, one triad in original fault, another in venial fault, a third in mortal fault, and thus three triads of faults with the pair of penalties constitute the number eleven. It is to be seen, therefore, according to what the old man is crucified. According to eternal penalty he is wholly destroyed in those who are Christ's; according to temporal penalty he is weakened and mitigated. Similarly according to mortal fault he is nonetheless utterly destroyed; but according to venial, mitigated, not yet fully taken away; further, according to original guilt [0472A] wholly not imputed; but according to act and the original tinder so crucified, that it does not have dominion.

QUESTION CLX. It is asked whether in Abraham the tinder of sin was crucified through the death of Christ? Some say that through faith in the death of Christ, then to come, it was in him also weakened.

QUESTION CLXI. It is likewise asked whether in the Apostle himself it was so weakened and crucified, that he never consented to its movements. For how did he not consent to the movements, when he committed venial [sins]? Likewise the venial [sins] which he committed — were they not voluntary, and so he willed them? how then did the tinder of sin not draw the Apostle to consent to sin? Solution. The Apostle, although he committed [them] voluntarily, nevertheless did not will the sin. [0472B] For a sin is not called voluntary from the fact that someone willed it; but because it proceeded from some will. Nor did he consent to the tinder. For to consent is to do, from deliberation and diligence, what the movements suggested: and so to consent seems even to be mortal.
