## I. ON THE EPISTLE TO THE ROMANS. (cont.)

QUESTION CLXII. *That you obey the concupiscences.* It is asked what difference there is between obeying the concupiscences and presenting the members to sin. Solution. To obey the concupiscences is to consent in mind to carnal delights. To present the members to sin, so that they may be weapons of iniquity, this is to fulfill iniquity itself in deed.

QUESTION CLXIII. Whence it is asked why, after the first, he forbids the second; for in forbidding to obey [0472C] the concupiscences, he also forbids presenting the members to sin; for he who does not consent, does not act. Solution. He forbids the work also after the consent, for this reason: that if it should sometimes happen to consent in mind, yet such consent may not proceed into act, but rather be cut off. But some understand by 'to obey the concupiscences' the operation; by 'to present the members to sin,' the consent; and thus fittingly, after the prohibition of the operation, follows the prohibition of consent.

QUESTION CLXIV. *Sin shall not have dominion over you, for you are not under the law, but under grace.* Does not sin have dominion over many who are under grace? How then is it true: Sin shall not have dominion, etc. Solution. To you, who are obedient to grace, [0472D] there is already given a power by which you can resist sin, that it may not reign. Whence it is clear that if at any time it resumes its strength so as to have dominion, this comes about by our own fault.

QUESTION CLXV. *I speak a human thing on account of the infirmity.* It is asked what the Apostle calls 'human,' namely light, and easy to do, which belongs to a grown justice and not a perfect one. Solution. He himself shows it, subjoining: As you presented your members to serve uncleanness and iniquity unto iniquity, so now present your members to serve justice unto sanctification. Which is, briefly and openly, to say: Serve justice with that love with which formerly, without compulsion, by delight alone, you served uncleanness. Which is therefore called 'human,' because more is owed to justice than [0473A] to sin: for no one ever so loved sin, that he would not fear to die for it. But since we are prone to evil, which is done without labor and without any difficulty, and moreover with great sweetness and [sic: *immmoderata*] delight, but to good [we are] slow and sluggish, which is not done except with great labor, and huge difficulty, and often little or no delight; because the way of the virtues is arduous and hard, strait and narrow.

QUESTION CLXVI. But it is asked how the Apostle says it is a human thing to serve justice with as great a love, and as great a delight, as we formerly served iniquity, since this seems to be perfect and consummate? Solution. It is indeed true that when we see someone serve justice with love, we say that he is perfect, especially in this time [0473B] when the holy [man] has failed: yet as regards the truth itself, he is imperfect, unless for justice's sake he despise not only other things, but even death itself. It belongs, therefore, to a grown [adulterinae] justice, and not a perfect one, to do the commandment with love, with the resolve of dying for Christ, even if he does not yet have so great a charity or constancy from which he could endure death itself; and this degree suffices for salvation, and is required; because without it no one is worthy of glory. But it belongs to a perfect and consummate justice to have only virtue [enough], which may suffice for enduring death for the truth and the love of justice. Peter is seen to have had the first degree when he said: *Even if I must die with thee, I will not deny thee [n: (Matth. XXVI)]*. But he did not yet have the second, [0473C] which he attained through the coming of the Holy Spirit.

QUESTION CLXVII. *While the husband lives, the woman is bound by the law of the husband [n: (Rom. VII)]*, etc. It is asked concerning David and the other just, whether, while the law was living, that is, holding its standing, they were bound to keep the law, and whether they are to be called adulterers, because they were with another, that is, with Christ, in whom they believed, and by whom they sought to be justified? Solution. The law indeed is not laid down for the just [man], being as it were the schoolmaster of little ones; yet David with the other just were bound to keep the law on account of those to whom the law was necessary, lest by their example they should transgress it. Nor because they were with Christ through faith and love are they to be called adulterers: but [0473D] if, the legal sacraments being despised, they should already presume to introduce the sacraments of the New Testament, then they would seem adulterers.

QUESTION CLXVIII. *For when we were in the flesh, the passions of sins,* etc. It is asked what he calls 'passions,' whether the first movements, or something else. Solution. It can be said [that he means] the inborn concupiscences, which are the cause of sin, and, in those who are not in Christ, even mortal sin. Or the first movements, which are not imputed to the reborn; which he therefore calls 'passions,' because they harm nature, and deprive it of some good.

QUESTION CLXIX. *What shall we say? Is the law sin? God forbid!* It is asked whether the law itself makes [one] sin, or teaches [it], and so is evil. Solution. It is not [0474A] evil, but good; nor is it the cause of sin, even if it may seem the occasion.

QUESTION CLXX. *For I knew not concupiscence,* etc. It is asked of what concupiscence the Apostle says this. And the doctors say, of that which is forbidden in the Decalogue. Concerning which again it is asked what that is? For by the name of concupiscence there is sometimes signified the vice of concupiscence, namely the tinder of sin; sometimes the first movement, which is called propassion; sometimes the second movement, which is called passion, or delight; sometimes the consent; sometimes the outward endeavor; which, then, does the law forbid, saying: *Thou shalt not covet? [n: (Ibid.)]* Solution. In the response to this question the modern doctors disagree. For some say that the law forbids the first movements; but others [0474B] say the consent; others expound thus: Thou shalt not covet, that is, know concupiscence to be evil, and avoid it as much as you can, and so, according to this, nothing is forbidden. But whatever is contained in the Decalogue is either a precept or a prohibition: whence it must be that something is forbidden when it is said: Thou shalt not covet. To those who say that consent is forbidden, the objection is made thus: the Apostle says: *I do what I will not; but if this is so: I consent to the law by not doing, but by not willing (ibid)*.

QUESTION CLXXI. It is asked, therefore, what that is which the law forbids, and the Apostle, unwilling, was doing? but these say that the law forbids consent; yet it is clear that the Apostle was not consenting, because, if he consented to concupiscence, he would in no way consent to the law, [0474C] but would act against it. But Augustine shows openly what it is that the Apostle was unwilling [to do], and yet was doing. Namely, he felt the movement of concupiscence, but nevertheless did not consent to it — nay, he willed not to feel it. Whence it is clear that the law forbids the first movements of concupiscence.

QUESTION CLXXII. But it is asked again why God forbade what no one ever could avoid? For the law seems not only useless, but even irrational, since it interdicts what is impossible to avoid. For this reason the aforesaid doctors are unwilling that the law forbid the first movements. To whom the objection is made thus: Did not the law command to love God with the whole heart? But this precept, as Augustine says, [0474D] no one in the present can fulfill: if therefore something is commanded which cannot here be done by anyone, what wonder if something is forbidden which cannot be avoided?

QUESTION CLXXIII. It is asked, therefore, why God either forbade what cannot be avoided, or commanded what cannot be fulfilled. For he seems cruel, or not fair. Solution. That he might humble the proud, and illumine the blind, and thus prepare [him] worthy to receive grace. In this, that he forbade what no one can avoid, and commanded what no one can do, he shows what the perfection of divine justice is, and what God can justly exact from man. Moreover [he shows] what kind of [being] man was before sin, namely such a one as, with grace helping, would covet nothing without any difficulty, [0475A] and would love God with his whole heart: and of what kind he was made through fault, namely weak, carnal, unable not to covet, or to love God perfectly. What then remains, except that man, no longer presuming upon himself, take refuge in grace, and say: Lord, answer for me, for I am weak. And so not cruelly, but by a great dispensation, God commanded what cannot be done except by the sole mediator.

QUESTION CLXXIV. It is asked where the law thus forbids concupiscence in general; for it is not found where the law makes a prohibition except concerning the property or the wife of a neighbor. Solution. In the particular the general is understood.

QUESTION CLXXV. It is asked whence it comes about that we strive toward the forbidden, and that which is forbidden pleases the more [0475B], and becomes sweeter from the prohibition itself? Solution. Human nature is so created that it naturally desires liberty: which by prohibition seems to be diminished, and reduced to servitude; whence, as much as it can, immediately when the prohibition is made, it resists, striving against [it].

QUESTION CLXXVI. It is asked in what person the Apostle speaks when he says: *But I am carnal [n: (Rom. VII)]*, in his own, or in a general one? For if he speaks in his own person, how does he say that he is carnal, since he was spiritual? but if he speaks in a general [person], so that what he says befits every just and unjust [man] of any time whatever, how does it befit the unjust to hate evil, and to will good, and to consent to the law of God? Solution. Augustine says that he speaks in his own person [0475C], saying that he is carnal on account of the movements which he felt, and the same [man] was spiritual, not consenting to those disordered movements. But it cannot be denied that he says certain things which do not befit his own person, such as that: *I lived once without law [n: (ibid.)]*. There are those who say that he speaks in the general person of the just [man]; but how does it befit the just [man]: *But I am dead? [n: (Ibid.)]* But we say that the Apostle speaks in such wise that certain things befit the man of the natural law, certain things the man of the written law, certain things the man of the law of grace; but what befits whom is to be carefully noted; but it is of our purpose to pursue the questions, not to expound the letter.

QUESTION CLXXVII. *For what I work I understand not,* [0475D] etc. It is asked how he says that he does not understand what he works, since through the law he had known sin? Solution. 'I understand not' is put for 'I do not approve.'

QUESTION CLXXVIII. But when he says: *For not what I will, this I do;* it is asked how he says that he does not will what he does; is not that which he does a sin, and every sin is voluntary, and thus he seems to will what he says he does not will? Solution. There is a threefold willing, namely of nature, of fault, and of grace; whence the same [man] can will according to the flesh, and not will according to the mind.

QUESTION CLXXIX. *But if what I will not, this I do,* etc. It is asked what that is which the Apostle says he wills not, and yet does, the law forbidding [it]? [0476A] Solution. To covet: for the law forbids to covet, and the Apostle wills according to the mind not to covet, and yet according to the flesh he was coveting.

QUESTION CLXXX. But it is asked how he says that he does [it], and does not work [it]: for the same [holds]: if he does something, he works it, and if he does not work it, neither does he do it. Are affirmation and negation at once true of the same thing? Solution. He does [it] according to the flesh, and does not work [it] according to the mind.
