## I. ON THE EPISTLE TO THE ROMANS. (cont.)

QUESTION CXCV. Concerning which it can be asked, whether it is a gift of the Holy Spirit; which if it is, it is good; but it seems evil, since on account of penalty it makes [one] serve. Solution. This [fear] is good, having a good effect, namely, to restrain from an evil work; but this, that the evil [0479D] will remains, is not from it, but from the vice of man. But according to others, servile fear is that by which hell is feared: by which good is done, but not well: which is called initial according to the earlier [authors], and, according to the later ones too, is the beginning of wisdom: which prepares a place for wisdom, and leads to charity. Initial fear is also spoken of in another way, when that which was hard begins to be loved, and this is as it were midway between servile and filial, having something of both. According to both opinions, filial fear is that by which not penalty is feared, but lest the bridegroom be offended, or depart — lest we offend, lest we be without God: which is the companion of perfection.

QUESTION CXCVI. But which of these was in [0480A] Christ is wont to be asked? That the worldly was not [in him], since it is evil, is agreed: likewise neither the servile nor the initial, since neither could be in perfect charity: therefore either the filial, or none, seems to have been in him; but filial is that by which we fear to offend; but did Christ fear to offend, or to be separated? Solution. Filial fear was in Christ not according to the effect which it has in the present in us, but according to that which it has in the angels, or will have in the future in the saints; but according to reverence, which is love mixed with subjection, whence the Apostle: *And he was heard for his reverence [n: (Hebr. V)]*. Likewise, did not Christ fear penalty, namely death, *whence Jesus began to be afraid [n: (Marc. XIV)]*: by what fear, then? was it perhaps filial, since no other was [0480B] in him? Solution. By natural fear he began to be afraid, which is not contained in the aforesaid division; because it is equally in the good and the evil: which is called natural, not because it is concreted with nature, but because from corruption it grew somehow into nature: which, with the other penalties, Christ took upon himself.

QUESTION CXCVII. Servile fear was given in the law; but servile fear, according to Augustine, is that by which hell is feared: therefore fear of hell was given in the law: but does not fear of hell restrain not only the hand, but also the mind; whereas the law [restrains] the hand only, and not the mind? Likewise, Augustine says that servile fear is never with charity; but does not everyone who fears eternal penalty have fear of hell? but someone who has charity begun, [0480C] still fears penalty, and thus it seems that servile fear is in charity. These questions I raise, not that I may solve them, but that I may rouse the reader to inquire with me.

QUESTION CXCVIII. It is asked still concerning servile fear, whether it makes [one] a servant of Christ, or of the devil. Solution. Neither of God nor of the devil, but it makes [one] a servant of penalty; because in some way it takes away liberty, and does not allow [one] to do the work which he wills, on account of penalty.

QUESTION CXCIX. *And coheirs of Christ,* etc. It is asked whether Christ is an heir, and if he is, according to which nature; for it seems that according to divinity he is not to be called an heir, since an inheritance is the firm possession of a successor upon someone's dying: but neither does the Father die, nor does the Son succeed him, because both [0480D] are from eternity: how then [is he] an heir? Solution. Thus indeed among us an inheritance has [its] being through succession; but not so in God: for *the Son, because he has [his] being from the Father, has also all things which he has from the Father [n: (Joan. I)]*; but not conversely: therefore the Son is the heir of the Father: but according to his humanity he received the inheritance, according to the fullness of which we all have received.

QUESTION CC. It is asked what it is to be coheirs of Christ. Solution. Partakers of the same inheritance. But according to this it seems false that we are coheirs; because the possession of the nations is the inheritance of Christ. Whence: *Ask of me, and I will give thee the nations for thy inheritance [n: (Psal. II)]*: of which inheritance we are not partakers, [0481A] and thus we are not coheirs. Solution. The inheritance of Christ, according to which we are coheirs, is eternal life, not the possession of the nations.

QUESTION CCI. *If yet we suffer with [him].* It is asked how one is to suffer with Christ, who now does not suffer. Solution. In two ways we suffer with Christ: either by calling to memory his sufferings, which he endured for us, and thus by compassion grieving with him, or by enduring tribulations in his likeness, with him, and on account of him. Compassion is sometimes the name of a natural affection, sometimes of the consent to it, which is a virtue.

QUESTION CCII. *The sufferings of this time are not worthy [to be compared],* etc. It is asked whether the merits of the saints suffice for attaining the future life: for if they have true [0481B] justice they are worthy of the crown; but that the fathers of the New Testament are truly just, the Apostle himself intimates, saying: *For the rest, there is laid up for me a crown of justice, which the just judge will render to me: and not only to me, but also to all who love his coming [n: (II Tim. IV)]*. But here he seems to say that the tribulations which the saints endure do not suffice to merit the future glory, which is to be revealed: for this is [what it means], that the sufferings are not worthy [to be compared] to the future glory. Solution. The Apostle does not deny that the merits of the saints suffice for attaining glory, but for meriting so excellent a glory they are not worthy [to be compared]; because God from his grace will superadd more than our merits have merited, which is, briefly, to say: our merits are least [0481C] in respect of the rewards. For the creature is subjected to vanity. Note that vanity is threefold: the first is of mutability, the second of mortality, the third of iniquity; and man is all vanity, that is, subjected to every vanity.

QUESTION CCIII. *Not willing, but for the sake of him who subjected it in hope.* It is asked what the creature subjected to vanity does not will: if it does not will evil, the will is good: but then that which follows is nothing, 'But for the sake of him,' etc.; but if it does not will good, it sins, since it ought to will good. Solution. 'Will' is spoken of in several ways; for sometimes it is called the natural affection, as there, 'not what I will, I do'; sometimes the consent to it, according to which the will is said [to be] damnable or to be rewarded; sometimes the horror of the flesh, as there: [0481D] *Not as I will, but as thou [wilt] [n: (Matth. XXVI)]*, and in this way it is taken here, and the sense is: Although the penalty which I endure is bitter, yet I take delight to endure it for the sake of Christ.

QUESTION CCIV. *Every creature groans,* etc. It is asked how this is true, since a stone is a creature, and yet does not groan? Solution. 'Every,' here, does not gather the individuals of the kinds, but the kinds of the individuals, and the sense is: Every creature, that is, man, in whom is every kind of creatures. For there are three species of creature, namely corporeal, and animal, and spiritual: which all are in man, and thus every creature groans in man.

QUESTION CCV. *And not only it, but we also,* etc. [0482A] Are not the good understood by the name of creature? how then does the Apostle distinguish between the apostles, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, and the creature, as though they themselves were not good? Solution. By the same reasoning by which, between the creature and the sons of God — although the sons of God are creature — he distinguishes between the creature and the apostles, although they themselves are creatures, making a distinction which is regarded not in a diversity of another quality, as the glosses, diligently inspected, declare.

QUESTION CCVI. *Hope that is seen is not hope,* etc. It is asked what it is that is signified by the name of hope, when it is said: Hope that is seen; and from what it is removed, when it is said: It is not hope. To that which is not hope, how does the name of hope befit, or is it fitted? Solution. Just as equivocally faith is called that by which one believes and that which is believed; [0482B] so equivocally hope is called that by which one hopes, and that which is hoped for. And thus hope that is seen, that is, the thing hoped for, is not the hope by which we hope, namely that virtue. Or otherwise, hope that is seen, that is, that which is of a visible thing, is not our hope, namely, by the merit of which we are already made saved.

QUESTION CCVII. *The Spirit himself asks for us with unspeakable groanings.* It is asked, since the Spirit needs nothing, nor suffers any straits, how he is said to ask or to groan. Solution. What the saints do, with him as author, is attributed to the Spirit: he asks, therefore, or groans, because he makes us ask, or groan.

QUESTION CCVIII. Whether the Spirit asks for something, and does not obtain [it]? Whenever we ask for anything out of charity, [0482C] does not the Spirit himself ask in us; but often we ask for him who lies in crime, and we are not heard, and thus it seems that the Spirit, by whose teaching we pray, is not heard. Solution. There are two species of just prayer: either when we ask what is to be asked, or when we ask where it is to be asked; but sometimes we ask where it is to be asked, yet not what is to be asked, and the Spirit then teaches how it is to be asked, but does not teach what is to be asked, and therefore we do not obtain [it]. But as often as we ask what is to be asked and where it is to be asked, we obtain [it].

QUESTION CCIX. *We know that to those who love God all things work together unto good.* Do not some love for a time, and afterward fall and thus are damned? But to these [0482D] not all things work together unto good, and thus it seems that to those who love God not all things work together unto good. Solution. He does not say simply that to those who love God all things work together unto good, but *to those who love and are called saints according to purpose:* for some love, and yet are not called saints according to purpose, just as some are preordained to life, yet do not yet love; and to neither do all things work together unto good, but to those who love and are called saints according to purpose.

QUESTION CCX. It is likewise asked how the very sins of such [persons] work together unto good for them. Solution. They rise up more humble and more learned.

[0483A]QUESTION CCXI. But would they not, if they had not fallen, but had then done good, be better than they now are; as this corrupted [woman], if she had, along with those good things which she now has, also virginity, would she not be of a higher degree and of greater merit? Solution. It is indeed true that if Mary Magdalene, with so great devotion, had also virginity, she would be of greater merit; but her fall cooperated toward a certain perfection, and perhaps if she had not fallen so basely, she would never have loved the heavenly physician so much: yet [one is] not to sin, that grace may abound.

QUESTION CCXII. It is likewise asked how the sins of the evil work together unto good for the good. Solution. Nothing is done in the world that does not confer something of usefulness [0483B] to the good of the universe; and thus all things, whether goods, or evils, whether one's own, or another's, work together unto good for the good.
