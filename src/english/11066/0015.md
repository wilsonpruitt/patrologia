## I. ON THE EPISTLE TO THE ROMANS. (cont.)

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION CCXIII. ⟦s2⟧ Whether all things work together unto evil for those to be damned? ⟦s3⟧ That their own evils harm them is certain; but whether the good things which they sometimes do work together unto evil for them can be asked, which seems [so]: because it is written: *It is better not to know the way of truth, than after knowing it to turn back [n: (II Petr. II)]*. ⟦s4⟧ Solution. ⟦s5⟧ Not all things work together unto evil for those to be damned; because for the good things which they sometimes do, they will be punished less. ⟦s6+7⟧ But that which is said: It is better, etc., was not said of those to be damned who were once just; it was said of heretics.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION CCXIV. ⟦s2⟧ *Whom he foreknew, he also predestinated.* ⟦s3⟧ It is asked whether foreknowledge is the cause of predestination, [0483C] just as predestination is the cause of calling, calling the cause of justification, and justification of glorification — which seems [so] according to the supposed order: but since each is eternal, how can one be the cause of the other? ⟦s4⟧ Solution. ⟦s5⟧ Although each is eternal, yet one can be the cause of the other; just as the Son and the Holy Spirit, though each is eternal, yet the Son is the cause of the Holy Spirit; so, according to some, foreknowledge pertains to knowledge, and thus to the Son; predestination to election; election to will, which pertains to the property of the Holy Spirit: and therefore, just as the Son is the cause of the Holy Spirit, so also foreknowledge is the cause of predestination, which it precedes naturally, not in time.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0483D]QUESTION CCXV. ⟦s2⟧ Whether foreknowledge and predestination are the same in God? ⟦s3⟧ For the solution of this question it must be known that the divine essence, though it is one and simple, yet on account of various effects obtains diverse names; for it is called wisdom, knowledge, foresight, providence, disposition, predestination. ⟦s4⟧ But wisdom and knowledge are concerning all things past and present and future, good and evil, and foresight concerning the same; providence concerning things to be governed, which is sometimes taken for foresight; disposition concerning things to be done; predestination concerning those to be saved; and thus in God, as to essence, foreknowledge and predestination are the same.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION CCXVI. ⟦s2⟧ Whether foreknowledge would be in God, if [0484A] there were no future things? ⟦s3⟧ That [it would] not, they seek to prove thus. ⟦s4⟧ If there were no future things, God would not foreknow any future things, and if this were so, foreknowledge would not be in God; but foreknowledge is the divine knowledge, and this is the essence of God, and thus it seems, if there were no future things, that God would not be; which God forbid! ⟦s5⟧ Solution. ⟦s6⟧ Foreknowledge does not simply signify the divine essence, but designates about it a relation with respect to future things: whence that expression, if there were no future things, foreknowledge would not be in God, is understood in two ways. ⟦s7⟧ For if it be said: If there were no future things, foreknowledge would not be in God, that is, if there were no future things, there would be no objects of the divine knowledge whence it would be called foreknowledge, the understanding is true. ⟦s8⟧ But if it be understood thus: If there were no future things, foreknowledge would not [0484B] be in God, that is, the knowledge by which he foreknows future things, the understanding is false.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION CCXVII. ⟦s2⟧ Whether God's foreknowledge is the cause of future things, or future things of foreknowledge? ⟦s3⟧ There would be no future things, unless God had foreseen [them]; and thus it seems that foreknowledge is the cause of future things. ⟦s4⟧ Whence Augustine: God knows all creatures not because they are; but they now are, because he knows [them]. ⟦s5⟧ But since he knows equally good and evil things, it seems that foreknowledge is the cause both of evils and of goods. ⟦s6⟧ But Origen says: A thing will not be for the reason that God knows it to be future, but because that thing is future, God foreknows [it]: this seems to be contrary to that earlier opinion of Augustine. ⟦s7⟧ Solution. ⟦s8⟧ Sometimes "knowledge" is taken for good pleasure, and then it is the cause of future things; but only of good things: [0484C] thus [it is] that Augustine took [it] above; but sometimes it signifies knowledge or cognition alone, and then it is not the cause of future things, but is related equally to goods and to evils; and thus Origen took [it]; and thus foreknowledge is not the cause of future things, but neither are future things the cause of foreknowledge, unless it be called a cause without which [there is] not [the thing].

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION CCXVIII. ⟦s2⟧ Whether foreknowledge imposes on future things a necessity of coming to pass? ⟦s3⟧ Which seems [so], because if God foreknew something to be future, it cannot not come to pass; and if this is so, whatever he foreknew will necessarily come to pass. ⟦s4⟧ Likewise it seems that God's foresight could be deceived; because if that which is foreseen can come to pass otherwise than it comes to pass, it can come to pass otherwise than it is foreseen, as if God foresaw that someone would read today: [0484D] it is conceded by all that such a one, who is to read today, can not read, and thus it can happen otherwise than is foreknown. ⟦s5⟧ Solution. ⟦s6⟧ This is the common one: these and similar [statements] can be expounded by conjunction and by disjunction; the sense of the conjunction is this: If God foresaw, it is necessary [that it] come to pass, that is, both cannot be at once, that God foresees and that it does not come to pass, and thus it is true. ⟦s7⟧ The sense of the disjunction is this: This future thing cannot come to pass otherwise than in the manner in which it comes to pass, and than it is foreseen, and this is false. ⟦s8⟧ Likewise, if it came to pass otherwise than is foreknown, the divine providence would not be deceived; because then this would not have been foreseen, but the other thing which would then come to pass. ⟦s9⟧ To the aforesaid solution it is objected thus: If God foresaw something, it will come to pass; [0485A] this hypothetical [proposition] is necessary: whence if the antecedent is necessary, so is the consequent; but the antecedent is necessary, because what is foreknown cannot be not foreknown, and thus it seems that whatever is future is future by a certain necessity. ⟦s10⟧ Which also seems able to be proved otherwise: God from eternity by his eternal Word said of every future thing, that it will be; but it is impossible for God to lie: but if that which he said to be future did not come to pass, it would follow that God were a liar, and so what is future will necessarily come to pass; whence Augustine: As it is necessary that what was should have been, so it is necessary that what is future should come to be. ⟦s11⟧ And elsewhere, speaking of God: Whose will, he says, is necessity, because, if he wills, it will necessarily be. ⟦s12⟧ Solution. ⟦s13⟧ There are some who [0485B] concede that God can not-have-foreseen what he foresaw from eternity: and therefore they say that the antecedent of the preceding hypothetical is not necessary, and therefore neither is the consequent. ⟦s14⟧ But how what they say is true, I do not see. ⟦s15⟧ Likewise let them answer the second objection: how God is not a liar, if that which he foretold to be future does not come to pass; or how the will of God is necessity; or how what Augustine says is true, [that] it is necessary that what is future should come to be. ⟦s16⟧ But we, following Augustine, say that there is a twofold necessity: one which is regarded according to lower causes, which is sometimes impeded by the higher ones; and another which is understood according to higher causes, which it is impossible not to be fulfilled. ⟦s17⟧ But of those things which are future, some come to pass equally both according to higher causes and according to lower causes, some [0485C] only according to higher causes: but whatever is future, as regards the necessity which is understood according to the totality of causes, cannot be hindered from coming to pass. ⟦s18⟧ But the usage of speaking is formed according to the lower causes, because they are more known to us. ⟦s19⟧ Whence we often say that something can be, and the same can not be, because those causes permit both; but according to the higher causes only one can be, namely what God wills, what he disposes, what he foreknew from eternity and foretold by his Word to be future, and it can in no way be hindered from coming to pass: regarding these causes, Augustine said that what is future will necessarily come to be.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0485D]QUESTION CCXIX. ⟦s2⟧ Likewise it is asked whether God's knowledge can be increased or diminished. ⟦s3⟧ That it can, they seek to prove thus: God knows that this man will read, but he can not read: therefore God can not-know that this man will read. ⟦s4⟧ Or thus: This man is not going to read, but he can read: therefore God can know that this man will read, who is not going to read: but it can come to pass that he read, therefore something can be known by God which is not. ⟦s5⟧ Solution. ⟦s6⟧ God's knowledge is immutable, nor can it be increased or diminished: yet some concede that God can know what he does not know, and not-know what he knows, and know more things than he knows, not knowing how to respond to the aforesaid objections. ⟦s7⟧ But we say that God cannot know what he does not know, nor not-know what he knows, nor know more things than he knows; because if [0486A] some things which will never come to be were to begin to come to be, they would not thereby begin to be known by God, who from eternity knows all things perfectly, not only those which sometimes come to be, but whatever can come to be: for he knows both by what causes they can come to be, and what they would be like, if they came to be. ⟦s8⟧ For he does not now have a fuller knowledge of the world than he had from eternity: nor now a lesser knowledge of those things which will never come to be, though they could come to be, than he would have if [it] came to be. ⟦s9⟧ Yet it is a great question whether any such thing is in mere possibility, which is never in act: for that which does not have a cause in God, how can it come forth into act? ⟦s10⟧ But of this, which we have now touched upon incidentally, it must be spoken elsewhere. ⟦s11⟧ But responding to the aforesaid objections, we say that this [statement], God knows that this man will read, posits that this man [0486B] is to read and that God knows this: and therefore, since he is not going to read, it is not to be conceded that God knows that this man will read — not because God does not know something: for whether he read or not read, God does not therefore know or not-know more or less. ⟦s12⟧ The same judgment [holds] of similar cases. ⟦s13⟧ It is also to be noted that when it is said that he who is to read can not read, or that he who is not to read can read, this is said according to the lower causes; but if we look to the higher causes, what is future will determinately be, and not the other, nor will it be able not to come to pass: yet because usage is formed according to the lower causes, which compel neither but permit both, we say that both can be and not be, although in truth it is true that the one is future, and not [0486C] the other.
