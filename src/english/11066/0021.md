## I. ON THE EPISTLE TO THE ROMANS. (cont.)

QUESTION CCXCVI. *Providing good things not only in the sight of God, but also in the sight of men,* etc. It is asked what it is to provide good things in the sight of God. Solution. In the secret of the heart, that is, to do those things which conscience dictates ought to be done for God.

QUESTION CCXCVII. But since God requires of us that we provide good things also in the sight of men, how does he divide between this and that? Solution. The sense is: The Apostle commands that we do good things also so circumspectly that the weak be not scandalized, but [0504D] receive a good example of profiting; and in this very thing he represses the proud presumption of certain men, who care nothing about the scandals of the weak, considering only what is lawful, not what is expedient.

QUESTION CCXCVIII. *If thy enemy be hungry, feed him,* etc. In the law it is written: *Thou shalt love thy friend, and shalt hold thy enemy in hatred [n: (ibid.)]*. Which seems to be contrary to that precept: *Love your enemies [n: (ibid.)]*, and to this: If thy enemy be hungry, feed him. Solution. It is not a precept in the law to hold enemies in hatred, but a permission; and it is, according to some, a counsel, not a precept, to love enemies in the Gospel, and thus there is no contrariety. Understanding which, David says: *If I have rendered evil to them that repaid me evils, let me deservedly fall away empty before my enemies [0505A] [n: (Psal. VII)]*.

QUESTION CCXCIX. *There is no power but from God,* etc. [n: (Rom. XIII.)] Concerning the power of the good it is agreed that it is from God; concerning the power of the wicked it is asked whether it is from God. If it is from God, it is good, because God is the author only of good things; but how is the power of the wicked good, since through it evils are done, which power also is often called in Scripture iniquitous and unjust? On the contrary, that the power of the wicked is from God, Scripture testifies: how then is it evil? Solution. The power of the wicked is good, and from God; but nevertheless it is called evil because of the malice of those who abuse it, just as the law indeed is good, but nevertheless an occasion of evil, and the light of the sun, and many like things.

QUESTION CCC. It is asked what he calls power. [0505B] Solution. Some say that it is the ordinance of God, from which some indeed have to be set over others. Others say that it is the very persons constituted in eminence, as kings and princes, who must be obeyed in all things that pertain to their power. But if they enjoin anything which is against God, they are not to be heard.

QUESTION CCCI. It is asked whether the power of sinning is from God. Which is proved thus: The power of Pilate, by which he could crucify the Savior, was from God, as is had in the Gospel [n: (Joan. XIX)]: therefore the power of sinning is from God. Likewise, on the contrary, God, from the fact that he is omnipotent, cannot sin: therefore to be able to sin is not to be able, nor is the power of sinning a power, but an impotence. Solution. The power of sinning is called [0505C] a certain force of doing something which cannot be done without sin, or a certain dignity from which someone can lawfully do that which is not done without sin: as the power of Pilate was indeed good, namely from God, from which he had the power to crucify Christ, just as from royal power someone can exercise tyranny, and under the appearance of ordained power rage against his subjects: yet to crucify Christ, or to exercise tyranny, is not a power, but rather an impotence, which is often called the power of sinning, which is present from a defect, or limit, of good: which also, if it was in the first man before sin.

QUESTION CCCII. *He that loveth his neighbor hath fulfilled the law,* etc. It is asked whether love of neighbor and love [0505D] of God are the same. If they are not the same, how is love of neighbor the fullness of the law? how is the whole law restored in love of neighbor? how are the three precepts of the first table, pertaining to God, fulfilled by love of neighbor? Likewise, if this and that are the same, why was one precept given separately concerning love of God, and another concerning love of neighbor? Solution. Love is the name of a virtue, and thus it can be said that the love by which we love God and neighbor is the same; and it is the name of a motion of the mind, and thus love of God is one thing, love of neighbor another, and love of God is greater, and love of neighbor less, since love of God is contained in love of neighbor: for he who loves God, it follows that he loves his neighbor, and conversely; and therefore the one is put for both [0506A] commandments. For love of neighbor is a motion of the mind toward the neighbor for God's sake: how then can there be love of neighbor without love of God? For love of neighbor is a certain matter in which love of God is exercised, which lies more hidden, whose effect appears in love of neighbor. Love of God is a motion of the mind toward God for his own sake.

QUESTION CCCIII. But since love of God is worthier than love of neighbor, why did the Apostle rather mention love of neighbor than love of God, saying that it is the fulfilling of the law? Solution. Because love of neighbor appears more in daily life and conduct.

QUESTION CCCIV. It is asked whether love of neighbor can be without love of God. Which seems so, because [0506B] someone can love his neighbor not for God's sake, but for something else. Solution. It is not to be conceded that anyone loves his neighbor, unless he love him for God's sake: for otherwise to love is not to love, but rather to hate; because *he that loveth iniquity hateth his own soul [n: (Psal. X)]*; and thus neither does he love another, who hates himself.

QUESTION CCCV. It is likewise asked whether we ought to love one another for the sake of eternal life: which if it be granted, it seems that we do it for our own advantage, and thus we are hirelings. For Ambrose says: he who serves in hope and desire of the heavenly fatherland is a hireling. Solution. The neighbor is to be loved for the sake of eternal life, nor is anything on that account preferred to God, because he himself is eternal life: and in him there is not [0506C] anything outside him besides himself, and therefore it is not fitting to extend the aforesaid words of Ambrose from their place; because they seem to sound against the usage of the Church: yet it seems to intimate this, that if anyone, thinking eternal life to be something apart, besides God, should do good, attending only to his own advantage, not because God willed this to be done, he would have to be called a hireling.

QUESTION CCCVI. It is asked whether all are to be loved equally. Solution. Some say that according to affection all are to be loved equally, but not according to effect, nay rather some more, some less, according to that which is written: *He set in order charity in me [n: (Cant. II)]*. To others it seems that even according to [0506D] affection some are to be preferred to others, as the better to the less good, and parents to strangers. And so charity is ordered according to affection and effect: which can be done in many ways; sometimes the affection ought to be unequal, sometimes the affection equal, and the effect unequal, and conversely.

QUESTION CCCVII. It is asked where the precept, or doctrine, is given, how a man ought to love himself. Solution. When a man is taught how God is to be loved, in this very thing he is taught how a man ought to love himself. For what is it to love oneself, but to love one's own good? but what is the good of man, but God? He therefore who loves God, in this very thing loves himself; and in as much as he loves God, in so much does he love himself.

[0507A]QUESTION CCCVIII. It is asked whether a man ought to love his neighbor as much as himself. Because it is written: *Thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself [n: (Matth. XXII)]*. Solution. "As" notes a likeness, not a quantity, according to some, when it is said: Love thy neighbor as thyself, that is, unto that unto which thou lovest thyself, that is, unto this, that he may have God, and as much as thou canst, give thy labor that he may do those things whereby he may be saved. But what has already been said above concerning charity need not be repeated. For not all things can, or ought to, be said everywhere.

QUESTION CCCIX. *Now him that is weak in faith,* etc. It is asked how he takes "weak." Solution. He here understands "weak" not according to constancy, but according to the knowledge of faith.

[0507B]QUESTION CCCX. *He that is weak, let him eat herbs. And let every one abound in his own sense.* It is asked how the Apostle persuades that the man who distinguishes foods ought to be left to himself, since it is evil to distinguish foods, and to think this one clean and this one unclean, when he ought rather to be drawn back from such an error, for this seems to concede it to him, since this is to foster him in error. Solution. He permits the lesser evil to be done, that the greater evil may be avoided: for it would be a greater evil to eat against conscience than to abstain from certain foods, when one thinks he must abstain from them: for whoever acts against conscience, sins.

QUESTION CCCXI. It is asked whether he who thus distinguishes foods sins. Does he not live according to the doctrine of the Apostle? if this too is so, how does he sin? Solution. [0507C] The Apostle does not here teach that it is to be done as though it were good, but he shows that it is a lesser evil: for it is a lesser evil to err out of good zeal than manifestly to sin against conscience: wherefore, lest he incur a greater sin, he is rather to be borne with than provoked. But there are certain things which even in the New Testament are forbidden, as that no one eat the blood of animals, or use things strangled. From certain things also we abstain, because it is not necessary to use them, as from horse-flesh, not because it would be as good to eat as beef; but because there is no need. From certain things we abstain, because they are harmful, as the serpent, the toad, and the like: which indeed are venomous, and therefore deadly to those eating them.

[0507D]QUESTION CCCXII. It is asked how they distinguished foods. Did they establish some uncleanness in these more than in those, on account of which they refused to eat them? If this is so, they were casting the fault back upon God through the medium of the creature, and thus sinning unto death. But if they sinned unto death, how does the Apostle bid them be tolerated in their death? Solution. They erred and sinned, but venially, not unto death; for they abstained from certain foods, not because they believed some uncleanness to be in them, but because, being placed under the law, they had been accustomed to abstain from them, and it was grievous for them to lay aside the custom.

QUESTION CCCXIII. It is asked to whom what the Apostle here says pertains. Solution. What he says seems to pertain to prelates, *Now him that is weak receive [take up* ]* [0508A] [n: (Rom. XIV.)] Likewise he seems to say certain things in this chapter which seem to pertain to the weak, as that: *He that eateth not, let him not despise him that eateth; and he that eateth, let him not judge him that eateth [n: (ibid.)]*: for by these words he seems to instruct the weak.
