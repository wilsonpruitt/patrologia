## I. ON THE EPISTLE TO THE ROMANS. (cont.)

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION CCCXIV. ⟦s2⟧ *To his own Lord he standeth or falleth.* ⟦s3⟧ The Apostle seems, when he says this, to take away from us all judgment concerning the good: for, since a man is good from intention and charity alone, which do not lie under human judgment, how can we judge anyone to be good? ⟦s4⟧ Solution. ⟦s5⟧ The Church judges only of manifest things, and not of hidden things: for it does not judge that charity is in someone, because then it would judge that to be which it did not know to be, but that the signs of charity, which are in him, show charity to be in him. ⟦s6⟧ Likewise, [0508B] when it judges or condemns anyone, it does not judge that he is guilty of the crime of which he is accused, but that the signs concur in his accusation, on account of which it gives a just sentence against him. ⟦s7⟧ If it be objected that the witnesses are false by whom he is convicted, and the accusation false, because he is not held guilty of the crime imputed to him, we say that the accusation is true and the witnesses true, even if he is not guilty of that crime; because the accusation is called true, that is, irreproachable, when it is made in judicial order. ⟦s8⟧ Likewise we say that the Church condemns the guilty, although the crime be not in him of which he is accused, because we take him as guilty there when convicted in judicial order.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION CCCXV. ⟦s2⟧ Doubtful things are to be turned to the better part, as the expositor says. ⟦s3⟧ It is asked, then, how [0508C] we ought to turn ambiguous things to the better part: for since I am equally in doubt about a good thing, whether it be good, as about an evil thing, whether it be evil, I seem to act no less indiscreetly in judging it to be good than in judging it to be evil; but since neither is defined and certain to me, I ought to define neither: how then ought we to understand what is said, to turn ambiguous things to the better part? ⟦s4⟧ Solution. ⟦s5⟧ To believe, to judge, or to turn, is here taken for to wish: for this the Apostle counsels, that when we see ambiguous things being done, we wish them to be done with good intention, and not with evil: for with what intention they are done, since this cannot be known by us, we ought not to judge what we cannot know.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0508D]QUESTION CCCXVI. ⟦s2⟧ Likewise the expositor says: He who thinks evil to be evil is deceived; but he who thinks evil to be good is not deceived: is not evil's being evil true? and evil's being good false? ⟦s3⟧ How then is he who thinks that which is true deceived, and he who thinks that which is false not deceived? ⟦s4⟧ Solution. ⟦s5⟧ Without prejudice to a better opinion, we say that this is to be so understood: He who thinks evil to be evil is deceived, is slowed in his course, because he despises and spurns it, or at least loves it less. ⟦s6⟧ But he who thinks evil to be good is not deceived, that is, in the way of conduct he is not offended, nor slowed, because in that he believes it to be good, he loves it the more, and thus in a certain manner profits by erring. ⟦s7⟧ It is to be noted that there are certain things manifestly good, certain things manifestly evil, [0509A] concerning which it is lawful to judge, even if we do not know with what mind they are done. ⟦s8⟧ But there are also middle things, which can be good and evil, concerning which to judge is perilous, and forbidden by the Lord: and these we ought to turn to the better part, as the blessed Jerome says: If you see a priest raising his hand over a woman, say that he does this to bless her. ⟦s9⟧ Which authority is to be understood according to the exposition above. ⟦s10⟧ Of manifest goods, however, we can rarely judge, since a just man can do almost nothing which a wicked man does not also do, as miracles, signs, and the like. ⟦s11⟧ He fasts, prays, and the rest of such things, which are signs of good. ⟦s12⟧ *Let every one abound in his own sense [n: (ibid.)]*, that is, let him be permitted in his own conscience, if it is not unto death, lest, called back from it, he sin more grievously.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0509B]QUESTION CCCXVII. ⟦s2⟧ *We shall all stand before the judgment seat of Christ,* etc. ⟦s3⟧ It is asked how the Apostle says: We shall all stand, since elsewhere it is read written: *You shall sit upon twelve seats, judging the twelve tribes of Israel [n: (Matth. XIX)]*. ⟦s4⟧ If the saints will sit in the hour of judgment, how will they stand? or if they will stand, how will they sit? ⟦s5⟧ Solution. ⟦s6⟧ They are said to stand out of reverence for the supreme judge, they are said to sit as being themselves also about to judge.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION CCCXVIII. ⟦s2⟧ *Nothing is common but to him who esteemeth any thing to be common.* ⟦s3⟧ The Apostle seems to intimate that the amount of guilt is to be assigned according to the amount and quality of conscience, so that if anyone believe a venial sin to be mortal, committing it against conscience, he sins unto death: even [0509C] in lifting a straw from the ground, if he lift it while conscience dictates this to be a sin unto death. ⟦s4⟧ And thus from this it seems it can be held that every sin which is against conscience is unto death. ⟦s5⟧ For whoever acts against his conscience, even in committing a venial sin, seems to despise God; because he prefers himself to God. ⟦s6⟧ Solution. ⟦s7⟧ In a venial sin which is against conscience, some sin unto death, others venially: for if anyone commit a venial sin against conscience thus: that he believe himself to be eternally punished if he commit it, he sins unto death, and despises God. ⟦s8⟧ But if from some infirmity, his conscience dictating to him that he is not to be damned for it, although he go forth with it, he sins venially; nor does he [0509D] prefer himself to God, nor despise God. ⟦s9+10⟧ If anyone object that he sins against conscience, and thus the committed sin is pleasing, and thus he sins unto death; because he sins unto death, authority attesting: *Every one to whom sin is pleasing,* etc. ⟦s11⟧ Solution. "To please" is taken for "to bring into use": and whoever brings any venial sin into use is guilty of death, as Augustine says.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION CCCXIX. ⟦s2⟧ *It is good for a man not to eat flesh, and not to drink wine,* etc. ⟦s3⟧ Jerome against Jovinian uses this argument: It is good not to drink wine, therefore it is evil to drink; and if it is evil to drink wine, everyone sins who drinks wine, and the sentence of the Apostle seems false: *I know, and am confident in the Lord Jesus, that nothing is common through him [n: (Rom. XIV)]*. ⟦s4⟧ Solution. ⟦s5⟧ To drink wine according to [0510A] Jerome is called evil by occasion, because from the drinking of wine luxury easily is born: and the sense of the argumentation is: if by abstinence from wine evil is avoided, from the drinking of wine an occasion of evil is easily taken. ⟦s6⟧ The Apostle says therefore: It is good for a man not to eat, etc., that is, it is good, by abstinence from flesh, or wine, or any other food, to avoid that the neighbor be offended. ⟦s7⟧ Likewise the Apostle could have said the same of beans, and peas, and the like.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION CCCXX. ⟦s2⟧ *All that is not of faith is sin.* ⟦s3⟧ It is asked how faith is here taken, whether for the judgment of conscience, or for the catholic faith. ⟦s4⟧ Therefore the questions are to be considered according to each opinion. ⟦s5⟧ All then that is done against faith, that is, the judgment of conscience, is sin. [0510B] The judgment of conscience is that whereby we believe something is to be done for God's sake, against which it is not to be done, as if anyone believe even that a man is to be killed if he can: and if he do it not, he sins even unto death, as some say. ⟦s6⟧ But to others it seems not to be unto death, unless he himself, who believes this is to be done for God's sake, thinks that he sins mortally, and does it not.

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION CCCXXI. ⟦s2⟧ It is asked, therefore, since this man with this intention kills a just man, since he ought to do this, whether in doing this he merits? ⟦s3⟧ For whoever does for God's sake what he thinks is to be done for God's sake, merits, but this man does for God's sake what he believes is to be done for God's sake: therefore from this he merits. ⟦s4+5⟧ But that he ought to do this is established from this; that if he leave it undone, he sins mortally. [0510C] Solution. ⟦s6⟧ Some plausibly say that here he has put himself in a strait, and so whether he go forward or draw back, he falls: as if someone see fire before him, and feel a precipice of water behind, he incurs peril on either side. ⟦s7⟧ But to others it seems that there are two things here, error and zeal: and that which is of error is evil; but that which is of zeal is good, and has merit. ⟦s8⟧ For there is a good will, but that in which it is exercised is evil; in this, then, he sins, in that he merits.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION CCCXXII. ⟦s2⟧ All the life of unbelievers is sin, as the expositor says. ⟦s3⟧ But do not even unbelievers do certain good things, as to sow fields, to build houses, to feed parents: which, although they are not worthy of eternal life, yet are laudable, and in no way [0510D] sins: how then is all the life of unbelievers sin? ⟦s4⟧ Solution. ⟦s5⟧ He calls unbelievers those acting against faith, not those not having faith.

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION CCCXXIII. ⟦s2⟧ Now let us pursue the other part of the question according as faith is taken for the catholic faith. ⟦s3⟧ The Apostle therefore, saying: *All that is not of faith is sin [n: (Act. X)]*, seems to hold that no one not having faith does anything which profits him: which seems not to be true, since Cornelius, not yet having faith, praying, was heard, and elsewhere it is written: *They who faithfully administer the affair of Babylon will deserve at some time to be freed from Babylon.* ⟦s4⟧ Solution. ⟦s5⟧ The Apostle intends to say this, that nothing profits anyone not having faith toward attaining eternal life, unless he come to faith: [0511A] nay, according to some, all good things also are harmful to such a one; because good things mingled with evils are wont to harm, and the more one abuses virtues, the more he offends. ⟦s6⟧ Whence Augustine: The least goods are better than the greatest, when they are mingled with evils.

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION CCCXXIV. ⟦s2⟧ It is asked here, since all that is not of faith is sin, whether a Jew sins in saying that Christ is God, when he says this under compulsion: for in saying this he seems to lie, since he speaks against conscience; whence he seems to sin unto death in saying it. ⟦s3⟧ Solution. ⟦s4⟧ It does not seem to sound well to the ears of the faithful, that a Jew in saying that Christ is God sins unto death; nor do we say this, though some seem to assert it; because to lie is to speak that which the judgment of conscience must dictate [0511B] ought not to be said. ⟦s5⟧ Or to lie is properly to speak what is false with the intention of deceiving: whence where there is no falsehood, neither is there a lie, yet someone can be guilty of a lie even in speaking the truth. ⟦s6⟧ No virtue is true, save that which is formed by the recognition of eternal truth; not even in the best morals, as appears in the philosophers, who flourished in the best morals, yet shone with a false virtue, because they lacked the knowledge of eternal truth.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION CCCXXV. ⟦s2⟧ *For Christ did not please himself. [n: (Rom. XV.)]* It is asked how this is to be understood. ⟦s3⟧ Is not the will of the Father and of the Son himself one? how then could he please the Father, and not himself? ⟦s4⟧ But that he pleased the Father in all things, there is no one who doubts. ⟦s5⟧ Solution. ⟦s6⟧ He did not please himself according to [0511C] the infirmity of the flesh, whose affliction he did not refuse, according to what he says elsewhere: *I came not to do my own will [n: (Joan. VI)]*, that is, he came not to fulfill that affection which he had from the nature of the flesh, but he subjected it to the command of reason, which reason in all things obeyed the divine will.

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION CCCXXVI. ⟦s2⟧ *But the gentiles, for mercy,* etc. ⟦s3⟧ God in a certain manner bound himself by promise to the Jews; but to the gentiles he passed over by mercy alone: yet he did both of grace, both that he promised himself to those, and that he passed over to these. ⟦s4⟧ Yet it is said, on account of the things promised to the Jews, truth; but to the gentiles, mercy, because by grace alone and not by the promise of God he came to them, and took them up.
