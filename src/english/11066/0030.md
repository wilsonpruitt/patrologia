## II. ON THE FIRST EPISTLE TO THE CORINTHIANS. (cont.)

QUESTION CXVI. *If I should have all faith, but charity,* etc. [n: (I Cor. XIII.)] Here the Apostle manifestly shows that faith, and the other gifts, cannot be had without charity. It is asked therefore, first of all, of what faith he here treats — whether of the catholic faith, or of another? But not of another, because by another mountains cannot be transferred from place to place, as by this of which he here treats: therefore he here treats of the catholic faith. Whence it is established that it can be had without charity, and thus can be had by the wicked, which many deny. But according to these, the catholic faith is a faith working [0535B] through love. Of whom it can be asked whether they call it one thing, or two — faith working through love — but this whole cannot be one? For he who has faith thus not only believes, but also loves. But these two cannot be in the wicked; yet so far as it is simply in faith, this whole can be even in the wicked, even in the devil. For what does this good man believe that this evil man does not believe? Does not this evil man, or even the devil, believe that Christ died, and was raised again from death, and the other things which are to be believed, which are necessary to faith? But it is objected, according to this, that the devil has the catholic faith, and thus becomes a catholic. Solution. "Catholic" is said in two ways: both he who lives in catholic wise, imitating the life of Christ; and he is called catholic who believes all [0535C] things that are to be believed, whether he have charity or not. Certain ones concede that even the devil, according to the other acceptation, can be called catholic — which sounds grievously to our ears, especially since that knowledge which he has of Christ is rather from subtlety of nature than from the inspiration of the Christian faith.

QUESTION CXVII. *Charity is the proper fountain of good things,* etc. It is asked whether charity can be had by those who are to be damned. Are they not strangers, who do not share in the proper fountain of good things? Solution. Charity is called the proper fountain of good things for this reason, that no one can at once have charity and be evil.

QUESTION CXVIII. *Charity never falleth away,* etc. [0535D] It is asked whether charity, once had, is never lost. For if it never falls away, and is never lost, then those who are to be damned can never at any time have charity. Solution. Charity is said never to fall away for this reason, that it is had both here and in the future; but had in the present it is lost, and once lost is again recovered.

QUESTION CXIX. *Knowledge shall be destroyed,* etc. It is asked how he says that knowledge is destroyed in the future. Shall we not have in the future the knowledge of those things of which we now have it? We shall indeed have it, and much greater than we have in the present: how then shall knowledge be destroyed? Solution. Certain ones say that knowledge shall be destroyed as to its partiality and imperfection, so that it be not partial and imperfect. But [0536A] this is for it to be increased and perfected, and not destroyed; because in the same way it could be said of charity, that it is to be destroyed. For as we know imperfectly, so we love imperfectly; and when that which is perfect is come, that which is in part shall be done away.

Solution. Certain ones say that not knowledge, but its enigmatic and shadowy mode is to be destroyed. But again, by the same reasoning, charity too seems to be destroyed: whose mode, namely of imperfection, is in the future to be done away. To this it is answered that it is true that the mode of imperfection shall be taken away from charity, but not every mode. For in the future the neighbor shall be loved for his own sake, and for God's sake, as he is loved in the present. Others also say that the act of knowledge shall be destroyed in the future. But charity, [0536B] which now is — neither its act, nor any mode of it — shall be destroyed in the future; but faith and hope shall be wholly done away; but knowledge in part shall be destroyed [sic: *destrueter*]: of which there shall be neither act nor mode.

QUESTION CXX. But there is another question, which presses us more: it is true, and it cannot be denied, that charity in the present is imperfect in comparison with the future; but the Apostle proves by such an argument that prophecies shall be done away, and that knowledge shall be destroyed: *We know in part, and we prophesy in part: but when that which is perfect is come, that which is in part shall be done away:* since therefore charity is in part, by a like argument it too shall be done away, when that which is perfect is come. Solution. But we say that [0536C] knowledge, or prophecy, is said to be in part by one reason, and charity imperfect by another reason. For from the fact that some know, and some do not know, prophecy or doctrine has place in this present, where one teaches and instructs another. But in the future, where all shall be taught of God, when all from the greatest to the least shall know God fully and perfectly, then the teaching of men shall have no place: when no one shall say to his brother: Know God. And therefore knowledge, that is, doctrine, shall be done away. But charity is not in this way said to be in part. For it does not have being for this reason, that some love and some love not: nay, it shall be much more truly when all shall love perfectly. Note: an enigma is an obscure likeness; and as in the present every creature is as it were [0536D] a kind of mirror in which God is seen, so in the future God himself shall be the mirror of every creature, in which all things shall be seen more truly than in themselves. Hence it is, according to some, why knowledge is to be destroyed: because this shadowy mode of knowing, which we now have, when full knowledge comes will be no more.

QUESTION CXXI. Faith and hope cannot be lacking to charity; but faith and hope can exist without charity. Yet certain ones say, as was said above, that faith cannot exist without charity: whose error is here destroyed. For when he says: *If I should have all faith, so that I could remove mountains, and have not charity, I am nothing:* he manifestly intimates that even perfect faith can be had without charity. [0537A] And the expositor, in the aforesaid words, manifestly refutes them, saying that faith and hope can exist without charity.

QUESTION CXXII. But concerning hope it can be asked how it can exist without charity. Is not hope a confidence of future goods, coming from preceding merits? But this cannot be without charity. And so it seems that hope cannot exist without charity. Likewise, if hope is, as it seems to others, the expectation of a future advantage, it will now be presumption rather than hope, if I am certain that he will reward, though there be no merits. To this it can be answered that hope is twofold: of reward, and of meriting: but the hope of meriting cannot exist without charity, while the hope of reward can (not) be had without [0537B] charity.

QUESTION CXXIII. *He that speaketh in a tongue, speaketh not to men, but to God [n: (I Cor. XIV)]*. It is asked what it is to speak in a tongue. Solution. Some say that to speak in a tongue is to speak in parables; others, in an unknown tongue. Augustine says that the utterance of signs, which the Spirit forms in the spirit of man, is said to be speaking in a tongue. Whence the Apostle says: *The Spirit speaketh mysteries [n: (Ibid.)]*. For to speak in a tongue and to speak in the spirit he says to be the same. The Holy Spirit, in the primitive Church, formed for the conversion of the faithful the signs of heavenly secrets in the minds of the faithful, and the utterance of such things the Apostle calls speaking in tongues. But it is to be noted that those who spoke in tongues sometimes understood, sometimes did not understand. But [0537C] someone will say: If he who speaks in a tongue understands what he says, then this is not to speak in a tongue, but to prophesy. Solution. Although he himself understand, unless he expound to others, he does not prophesy, but only speaks in a tongue. Note that certain things are done in the Church only for adornment, not so much for necessity: among which is contained speaking in tongues.

QUESTION CXXIV. *You shall be speaking into the air,* etc. From these words certain ones judge that to speak in tongues is to speak in diverse kinds of languages; but this is not true: nay, the Apostle brings this in to show that to speak in tongues without interpretation has little usefulness, just as to speak in all kinds of languages would make rather confusion than edification [0537D] for those who understood none of them.

QUESTION CXXV. *If I know not the power of the voice.* It is asked what he calls the power of the voice. Solution. He calls the signification of the voice the power of the voice.

QUESTION CXXVI. It is also asked why speech of this kind is said to be done by the tongue. Solution. This is so because what is in the tongue is in the voice and in the utterance; but what is in the heart is in the understanding; because in the heart is intelligence: whence, because this speech is only in the utterance, and not in the intelligence, it is therefore said to be done by the tongue; by the spirit also, and not by the mind. For the spirit is a power of the soul lower than the mind, in which the images of things are confusedly apprehended. For it is an imaginary and confused apprehension of visible things, without discernment of the properties of those things which [0538A] are apprehended: such as was that vision of Pharaoh concerning the cows and the ears of grain: for he saw only images. But Joseph had in his understanding the revelation of God concerning these things.

QUESTION CXXVII. It is asked what words thus uttered signify, when someone thus speaks in a tongue — to set down this example: *He that soweth went forth to sow his seed.* For if this is the signification of the voice, it was true: it is asked therefore whether that is said by it which is properly signified by it: if it be said to be so, it is inferred, therefore, that something false is signified by it. To this certain ones respond, saying: The signification of the voice is not to be sought in such things, but only that of the things, which pertains to allegory. Others say, to speak more freely, that it does indeed properly signify something false; [0538B] yet a falsehood is not spoken by it: because the statement is not there terminated; for the end of the speech is not that first signification of the voice, but the second, which is called allegorical. But it can be said that the speech itself signifies neither true nor false: for the things there signify only the truth, which is there mystically understood. Nor do I deny that there is there a signification of the voice, but only incomplexly, because this word, "man," signifies a man, and so of the rest; but they do not signify complexly; for they do not signify conjointly, so that the true or the false is signified, but so that they may signify those things by which the truth is signified. Or it can be said that the proposition itself, from the property of the things which are signified, is taken up [0538C] to signify that which is mystically understood, as this word, "seed," signifies the word of God, from the property of the thing which it signifies.
