## II. ON THE FIRST EPISTLE TO THE CORINTHIANS. (cont.)

QUESTION CXXVIII. *Christ died for our sins,* etc. [n: (I Cor. XV.)] It is asked whether Christ, according to the flesh, had the necessity of dying? That he had the necessity of dying seems to be the intent of the authority upon that passage: As *it is appointed unto men once to die, and after this the judgment: so also Christ was offered once [n: (Hebr. IX)]*; so, that is, by the same necessity and law of nature by which the rest die. Likewise the authority says, *because it was his own will he was offered [n: (Isai. LIII)]*. Therefore by will alone: how then by necessity? Solution. It is established that in Christ there was no cause of dying, because no sin; yet, as certain ones will have it, among [0538D] the other penalties, he took up voluntarily also the necessity of dying; and thus this necessity does not impede the will, which, as he received it when he willed, so he laid it down when he willed: for if, they say, some greater benefit had not been conferred upon the assumed flesh, it would necessarily be subject to the law of nature — which necessity also some understand by mortality.

QUESTION CXXIX. *I am the least of the apostles,* etc. It is asked how the Apostle calls himself the least of the apostles, since he is of greater merit than the merits of the others: for he labored more than all. Solution. He says this according to his former state, not according to the present, in which he was not the least of the apostles, but the greatest. But it is objected: For he does not [0539A] say, I was the least, but *I am the least of the apostles:* therefore, since he is not among the least, but rather among the first, it seems that he lies, and thus sins unto death: because *the mouth that belieth killeth the soul [n: (Sap. I)]*. Solution. The sense is: I am the least of the apostles, that is, I do not prefer myself to others, but rather others to myself: for indeed all his sins, past or present, hidden or manifest, he had before his eyes; but the simplicity and innocence of others he considered, and thus, according to this consideration, he felt more humbly of himself: for as the proud man, if he has any goods, always attends to them, and to the infirmities of others, whence he judges himself alone great, despising others; so the humble man without ceasing sees his own infirmities, but weighs the goods [0539B] of others: hence it is that the saints, though they be greater, feel themselves the more humble, nor is it a deceiving opinion, because the goods which they have they judge to be not their own, but the gifts of God; but the evils to which they are subject they know to be their own.

QUESTION CXXX. *By the grace of God I am what I am,* etc. It is asked of what grace he speaks. Indeed, of operating and cooperating grace, or preventing grace and subsequent grace, it has already been said in what precedes: for preventing, or operating, grace is one and the same, which works in us without us, namely by preparing and healing free will, that it may will the good, which the Apostle notes, saying: By the grace of God I am what I am; but subsequent, or [0539C] cooperating, grace is one and the same, which works in us, not without us, by following after and helping, lest we will in vain, which the Apostle notes, saying: *And his grace in me hath not been void, because I have laboured more than all: yet not I, but the grace of God with me.* From this therefore, that he says: *I am what I am by the grace of God,* the error of the Pelagians is destroyed, who said that free will suffices for meriting salvation. Likewise, from the fact that he adds, *and his grace in me hath not been void,* he shows that man can do something from free will, which certain heretics deny, saying that man can merit nothing. But the Apostle demonstrates that man of himself can indeed do nothing, but only from grace supervening: for it is needful that man be prevented by grace; then free will, [0539D] already prevented by grace, ought to cooperate with grace itself: whose nature is such that it can shine back and cooperate, as the eye touched by the ray of the sun can see. What therefore man works, is from grace, with which he cooperates. For a certain grace, as has already been said, works without the help of man, because it pricks the mind and rouses it; but man without grace can neither consent to grace, nor effect anything else, but leans upon grace drawing and leading, and thus, helped by grace, merits. From which it appears that it is not grace only when man does anything of good, but also from free will, although the whole is the work of grace, either by itself, or working with man. And so there are certain things from grace alone, and certain things from grace and man.

[0540A]QUESTION CXXXI. *If there be no resurrection of the dead, then Christ is not risen again.* It is asked concerning the hypothetical, whether it be true, and how it is to be understood. Solution. It is true, and is thus understood: If it were impossible for the dead to rise, as certain heretics said, neither is it possible that Christ rose; or if the saints were not to rise, neither would it be true that Christ rose; because all that he did in the flesh — by dying, by suffering, by rising — he did for us.

QUESTION CXXXII. *If Christ be not risen again, our faith is vain.* It is asked how the Apostle says that our faith is vain, and *we are more miserable than all men [n: (I Cor. XV)]*, if there will be no resurrection of the dead, or of bodies. For since [0540B] the soul is immortal, and it alone is capable of the vision of God, and thus it alone can have beatitude, can we not be blessed without the resurrection of bodies? And to the same:

QUESTION CXXXIII. It is asked, to what end will be the resurrection of bodies, since there will be no need of them where Christ will be all in all, nor can they themselves receive the beatitude which will be of the soul alone? Solution. It is to be seen diligently what is said, and according to what. For that which he says: We are more miserable than all men, he says not according to the soul, but according to the body. For here the Apostle suffered greater misery than others. But the resurrection of bodies is, that we may glory also in the second robe: for that brightness [0540C] which will be in the body will be for the increase of beatitude itself, so that what before it had for misery, it may now have for glory and adornment. The Apostle proves the resurrection of the dead by the resurrection of Christ: which was done for this reason alone, that the resurrection of bodies might be believed, and might come to pass: *When he shall have delivered up the kingdom to God and the Father [n: (Ibid.)]*, that is, when he shall lead the Church — in which he now reigns through faith, through the knowledge which it had of the Son — to the knowledge and vision of the Father.

QUESTION CXXXIV. *Till he put all his enemies under his feet.* It is asked how he says that Christ must reign: *Till he put,* etc. [n: (Ibid.)] Will he not reign forever, and especially when his enemies are subdued? Solution. In like places, "till" is put [0540D] for "forever": for if he reigns then, when he has adversaries, it is established that he will reign when all are subject to him, and thus he will reign forever.

QUESTION CXXXV.-- *Then the Son also himself shall be subject unto him that put all things under him.* It is asked according to what nature he says the Son is subject to the Father. For if according to the divine, he will be less than him: which is false, because according to the divinity he is equal to the Father. Likewise, if this be said according to the human, then according to it all things are subject to him: and according to the human he is Lord of all: wherefore also Creator; and thus according to the same he seems to be equal to the Father, according to which he is less than him: whence it is read: Equal to the Father according to the divinity; less than the Father according to the humanity. Solution. This can [0541A] well be understood according to both natures, namely the divine and the human: according to the human all things are subject to him, according to which he was exalted to the equality of the Father, when the human nature was united in one person to the Word consubstantial with the Father, according to which he himself also received the fullness of gifts; of which *fullness we all have received [n: (Joan. I)]*, and thus are subject to him. Likewise, according to the divine nature, certain ones understand it thus, that the Son is subject to the Father, because from him he has being, from whom he has whatever he has. According to that: *My doctrine is not mine [n: (Joan. VII)]*. Likewise: *The Father is greater than I [n: (Joan. XIV)]*. Which some will have understood according to the divine nature. In a word of this kind a distinction is noted, because the Son is from the Father, not the Father from the Son [0541B]; whence the Father is called the principle of the Deity, because he is from none, and from him is both the Son and the Holy Spirit. For the Father is a principle not from a principle; the Son a principle from a principle; the Holy Spirit proceeding from both. But things of this kind are to be said only at a fitting place and time, lest the weak incur scandal.

QUESTION CXXXVI. *In unequal brightness there will be equal joy.* It is asked, if the brightness is unequal, how can the joy be equal? Will not the quantity of joy be according to the quantity of brightness! Will not the brightness itself be the joy itself? Likewise, if one is more blessed than another, the one will have greater joy than the other: how then will the joy be equal? Likewise, will not the joy of all [0541C] be that of each? How then, in unequal brightness, will there be equal joy? If the same coin be given to all, how is the brightness unequal? If the joy will be equal, then the joy of Peter will be the joy of Martin. Solution. One thing is the joy of experience, another that of the will; as the joy of Peter is of remuneration, and of experience, but for Martin it is not of experience, but of affection. For the good of Peter pleases him as much as it does Peter himself, yet he does not feel and experience in himself so great a beatitude as Peter feels. And so the beatitude is different according to quantity, although it be the same according to quality. Just as, therefore, at the health of someone recovering from infirmity I rejoice as much as he does, by affection, though not by experience, because I do not feel in myself the health which he experiences. [0541D] As two are covered by the same bed, yet one is warmer: so in the one vision of God, one will rejoice more intensely than another. But no inferior will envy any greater, nor will he desire for himself the greater joy of the superior, because each one will have as much as he wills, otherwise he would not be blessed. There is life without death, knowledge without error, love without offense. There will be seen the end of our desires, namely God without end, he will be loved without weariness, he will be praised without fatigue.

QUESTION CXXXVII. *It is sown a natural body,* etc. It is asked whether the body has animality, that is, sensuality, from the soul, or the soul from the body? Solution. Neither can the body have animality except from the soul; nor would the soul be animal, that is, having [0542A] sensuality, unless joined to a body. And so animality seems to be known from the conjunction of both; the soul alone, however, feels through the body, but the body receives it, and does not itself feel. For man was first so created that from creation he was passible, yet he would never have suffered, unless he had sinned. Whence he is also said to have been immortal before sin, because he was able not to die, for he was able not to sin, since, if he had not sinned, he would not die.
