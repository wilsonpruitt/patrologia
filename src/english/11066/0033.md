## III. ON THE SECOND EPISTLE TO THE CORINTHIANS.

[0543]

[0543C] *[n: (II COR. I.)] Paul an apostle,* etc. This is the second epistle, which is addressed to the Corinthians. Now the Apostle sometimes writes two, sometimes one epistle only; but neither, when he writes one, does he say anything diminished and imperfect, nor, when he writes two, anything superfluous, as here can be seen. For this epistle, which follows, is the consummation and confirmation of the preceding. For in this second one he admonishes those to be corrected who had not yet been corrected through the preceding epistle. He marks them, that in almsgiving they were sparing. He commands the corrected fornicator to be received. Whence it appears what is the matter of this epistle, and also what the intention. The matter is the special state of the Corinthians, in which they then were. But the intention is to recall them to the unity and integrity of faith. But in this [0543D] special state he signifies and forms the general state of the Church, and invites all to the unity of faith. He sends beforehand, after his manner, and that of others writing epistles, salutations of those to whom he writes, courting benevolence: whence also he says, Paul an apostle, joining a name of humility and a name of dignity, that humility as companion may temper the excellence of dignity, without which every virtue is empty and vain. Likewise, of Jesus Christ: Jesus is a name of the person. Now many were called by this name, as Jesus son of Nave, Jesus the great priest, but all by way of designation. But Christ alone substantively, because he had both the name and the thing: who was truly the Savior of the world. For Jesus is interpreted *Savior.* But Christ is a name of the person, [0544C] which embraces both natures. For it is a name of office, like priest, soldier, and is interpreted *anointed.* In the Old Testament two persons were anointed, the royal and the priestly; but Christ was anointed with a royal anointing, who according to the divine nature was able to rule his own; but according to the human nature received the power of offering, who offered himself to God the Father: whence from the royal and priestly office he is called Christ. The will of God is taken in many ways, as was already said above, and to say it again will not be superfluous. For the will of God is called the disposition itself, and the good pleasure. Whence: *Whatsoever he pleased he hath done [n: (Psalm. CXXXIV)]*. The will of God is also called counsel, or precept: whence it is said: *God will have all men to be saved [n: (I Tim. II)]*, that is, [0544D] he counsels and commands them to do those things by which they may be saved, such as prohibition, or permission, and the like.

QUESTION I. *Whereas when I was thus minded, did I use lightness?* It is asked therefore whether the Apostle lied, in promising that he would come, when he did not come: for he himself said, I will come, and he did not come: therefore with him there was "it is" and "it is not," that is, affirmation and negation of the same thing, and thus a lie, and thus he is guilty of lying. Solution. A lie is a false signification of the voice with the intention of deceiving: whence he who says something false which he thinks true is not to be judged a liar, since he is rather deceived than deceives. But whoever, with the intention of deceiving, says what is true or false, is guilty [0545A] of lying: whence it is gathered that someone saying what is true is guilty of lying, or lies; and that someone says what is false, and yet does not lie or is not guilty of lying.

QUESTION II. But it is asked concerning those who lie piously, whether they are guilty of lying, as those Egyptian midwives? For from the intention of deceiving they pronounced something false. Solution. Those who thus lie sin, and while they provide for the lives of others, acting against their own conscience, they offend the truth, and incur the peril of their own soul.

QUESTION III. Likewise it is asked concerning those who speak falsehood in jest. Solution. Sometimes so to jest is evil, sometimes not; but if from habit, then it is a sin.

[0545B]QUESTION IV. *To some indeed the odour of death unto death,* etc. [n: (II Cor. II.)] It is asked whether the odour of death is good or evil, since the Apostle says that he is an odour, to some unto death, to some unto life: for if good, how unto death? Likewise, if evil, how good to God? Solution. The Apostle was nothing but a good odour, and yet by this good odour some were dying, that is, they took occasion through envy, as the law is good, and yet an occasion of evil, because of transgression.

QUESTION V. *Not that we are sufficient,* etc. [n: (II Cor. III)]. Here it is asked how the Apostle says that we are not sufficient to think anything of ourselves, since we can both think and do evil of ourselves. Likewise, since certain things we can do naturally, which are neither for reward nor for punishment [0545C] — and these are those which were given us from the first creation, as to raise a finger, to bend it, to lay it down, and the like. Solution. The Apostle here treats of those goods which have merit with God, which can in no way be done without grace supervening and helping. Whence *His mercy shall prevent me [n: (Psal. LVIII)]*, *and his mercy shall follow me [n: (Psal. XXII)]*. For grace prevents the will, that it may will, and follows after, lest it will in vain. Whence the Apostle here destroys the error of those who said that the beginning of good cannot naturally be without grace, but that the consummation of good is from ourselves: in this that he says, but our sufficiency is from God. For from free will we have the faculty of working well, yet we cannot use this faculty except [0545D] by the help of grace. For free will is through fault so depressed that it cannot use its own power, unless it be raised up by grace and helped, as, when I have the power of riding, yet I cannot have the exercise of this power without a horse.

QUESTION VI. *The letter killeth, but the spirit quickeneth,* etc. It is asked how the letter is said to kill: does it command such things as, when observed, kill? How then will that stand which the Apostle says elsewhere, that *the law is holy, and the commandment holy, good and just,* if the precepts kill? Likewise, if it be said that the letter without the spirit, that is, without grace, kills, the same can seem to be said of the Gospel. [0546A] Likewise, if it be said that the letter kills for this reason, that not all things can be observed there according to the letter: let him not perish who has observed them. In the same way it can be said of the Gospel. For if this, *except you eat the flesh of the Son of man, and drink his blood,* etc. [n: (Joan. VI)], be observed according to the letter, that is, as the letter sounds, so that we understand eating and drinking thus, this, as elsewhere, is foolish and damnable. Solution: The letter without the spirit kills, that is, without grace, because it is an occasion of death, as knowledge without charity puffs up. But the letter of the Gospel is not without the spirit. Or, as was already said elsewhere: *By the letter, or law, is understood the commandment without grace:* which always kills: because, increasing concupiscence, it adds on transgression. By the Gospel [0546B] is understood the commandment with the spirit, that is, grace: whence the Apostle calls the law *the ministration of death:* the Gospel, *the ministration of justice.*

QUESTION VII. *And not as Moses put a veil,* etc. It is asked concerning the veil, whether it blinds. Solution. One thing is the figured veil, which is the reading of Moses: which was figured by the veil that Moses, speaking to the children of Israel, put over his face. Another thing is the veil of blindness, which is placed over the heart of the Jews: both veils are taken away through Christ. In figure of which thing the veil of the temple was rent in the passion of Christ.

QUESTION VIII. *But we all, beholding the glory of God with open face, are transformed into the same image,* [0546C] etc. It is asked what he calls glory, and what image? Solution. The glory of God, which we behold, and the image, into which we are transformed, he calls the same, namely Christ, who is the glory, and the uncreated image of God, as man is the glory, and the created image of God.

QUESTION IX. *Who is the image of the invisible God [n: (II Cor. IV)]*. It is asked why the Son is called the image of the Father. Solution. That it may be shown that he is so from the Father, as to be shown through all things like and equal to him.

QUESTION X. But if it be asked why the Holy Spirit, since he is from the Father, and like and equal through all things, is not called the image of the Father as the Son is. It is answered that image, equality, and likeness pertain more to the property of the Son than to [0546D] the property of the Holy Spirit. For those things which are born, not those which proceed, are wont to be alike. It is to be noted that upon image and equality follows likeness: because wherever there is image, or equality, there is likeness, but it is not converted. Likewise, image neither infers equality, nor is inferred from it, because there can be image without equality, and equality without image.

QUESTION XI. Having the same spirit of faith. The expositor says upon this passage that the times are varied, not the faith, because whatever we believe, those ancients also believed, and conversely. Whence it is thus objected: Abraham believed that Christ was to be born, and we believe that he is born: but it is one thing to be [0547A] about to be born, another to be born: therefore he believed one thing, and we another? Solution. Whatever we believe, and the ancients, etc., that is, the same things are subject to our faith and to theirs: yet it does not follow that the same thing which is now past was past in their time; or that what was then future is now future. Likewise, if it is objected: Abraham believed that Christ was to be born, but now it is false that Christ is to be born: therefore Abraham believed something false. Solution. As it seems to us, the "what" is interpreted by the "of what kind." For when it is said: Abraham believed that Christ was to be born, the sense is, he had faith concerning the nativity of Christ, which was then future; but in the assumption, when it is said, now it is false that Christ is to be born, the sense is: the nativity of Christ is not future: whence [0547B] from those two premises it in no way follows that Abraham believed something false.

QUESTION XII. *But though our outward man.* It is asked whether the outward man and the inward man are two men, and whether the outward man and the old man, and the inward man and the new man, are the same. Solution. As the old man and the new man are not two men, but one, although according to one thing he be called old, according to another new, so the outward man and the inward man are not two men, but one and the same, so called according to diverse things. Nor is the old man the same as the outward man; nor is the new man the same as the inward man. For the old man consists in fault and punishment, which two are found not only in the outward man, but also in the inward man. But the new man [0547C] is understood according to justice and glory: which two also pertain to the inward man. But the outward man is called whatever we have in common with the beasts; the inward man, what is common to us with the angels.
