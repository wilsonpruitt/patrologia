## VI. ON THE EPISTLE TO THE PHILIPPIANS.

[0575]

[0575C] *[n: (Philip. I.)] Paul and Timothy,* etc. This epistle the Apostle writes to the Philippians, who are Macedonians. For Philippi is a metropolis city in Macedonia, which is a certain province in Greece, which Philip the father of Alexander built, calling it by his own name. But these, having received the word of preaching from the Apostle, were firm in the faith, and did not receive the false apostles. These therefore he fortifies against a twofold war, namely of tribulations and of false preachers. And so the intention of the Apostle in this epistle is to exhort the Philippians to patience against tribulations, and to constancy against the false apostles. The mode is such: after the customary manner he sends a salutation beforehand. Then he gives thanks for them, imploring for them greater goods, that growing in virtues they may come to perfection. Then [0575D] he admonishes them to the patience of tribulations by his own example and Christ's. Afterward, that they beware of the cunning of the false apostles. At last he interposes an admonition of mortality, and near the end he says that he rejoices concerning the grace which the Romans had sent him through Epaphroditus.

QUESTION I. *For what? so that by every means, whether by occasion or by truth, Christ be announced.* It is asked how the Apostle permits the wicked to preach the evangelical doctrine, since elsewhere it is written: *To the sinner God hath said: Why dost thou declare my justices [n: (Psal. XLIX)]*? etc. Likewise: *Praise is not comely in the mouth of a sinner [n: (Eccli. XV)]*. Likewise: He whose life is despised, it remains that his preaching be neglected. Solution. In such a case the preacher is rebuked on his own account, not on account of others. For what he preaches profits not himself, but others: [0576C] whence the praise of God in the mouth of sinners is not comely. The hirelings therefore are to be tolerated. Whence the Lord: *Upon the chair of Moses have sat the Scribes and Pharisees: what they say, do ye [n: (Matth. XXV)]*, etc. And so the preachers who preach for the truth are to be loved; the hirelings, who from occasion and temporal advantage announce the truth, are to be permitted; thieves and robbers, or wolves, are to be guarded against, nay, to be fled from.

QUESTION II. *What I shall choose, I know not,* etc. It is asked how the Apostle is in doubt which he should choose, whether to remain in the flesh, or to be dissolved and to be with Christ [n: (Philipp. I)], since he knows that the stronger the fight, the greater the crown. Did he not know that it is of greater merit to fight than to rest? Solution. Affection drew him to both: therefore he says that he knows not which [0576D] he should choose. The Apostle therefore doubts from affection, not from ignorance. But then it is asked: How does he say that it is much better to be dissolved and to be with Christ. Solution. "Better" is here set down for "sweeter" and "safer."

QUESTION III. *To you it is given not only that you believe in him, but also that you suffer for him,* etc. It is asked whether to suffer itself is a good, and whether it is a gift of God? for if it is, it is to be desired. Solution. From him who is the faith of believers is the endurance of the suffering. But the punishment itself in itself is not good, nor to be desired for its own sake, nor ought it to be numbered among the gifts of God. Yet to suffer for Christ is good and to be desired; because it has great merit. Nor does the Apostle say simply: To you it is given to suffer; [0577A] but thus: To you it is given to suffer for Christ.

QUESTION IV. *Who being in the form of God [n: (Philipp. II)]*. It is asked what "form" here denotes. Solution. Form is sometimes set down for representation, sometimes for the truth of the thing, as here: Who being in the form of God, that is, in the truth of the divine essence and in the equality of substance; for as a man begets a man, a dog a dog, so God begot God, equal in all things, not unequal: whence the Son also is said to be this which the Father is; and since this can be understood according to person and nature, Augustine shows the understanding of this saying, saying: The Son is therefore said to be that which the Father is, because as the Father is God, so also the Son, and as the Father is omnipotent, so also the Son, and as the Father is immutable, so also the [0577B] Son. For therefore the Father and the Son are one, and supremely one, of whom there is no diversity of nature or of will. Scripture therefore says both: that the Son is equal to the Father, and that the Father is greater than the Son, but this according to the form of a servant, and that according to the form of God.

QUESTION V. *And in habit found as a man,* etc. In how many ways is something had? Solution. In four ways something accedes to another so as to be had: either so as to change and not be changed, as wisdom; or so as to change and be changed, as food; or so as neither to change nor be changed, as a ring; or so as not to change but to be changed, not from its own nature, but from some prior form, as a garment when it is put on; it does not change the man, but is changed; not from its own nature, but it receives an appearance and form which [0577C] cast aside it did not have; according to which comparison and likeness the incarnation is understood: for the form of a servant so acceded to the form of God that it did not change or convert it, but the form of a servant itself was changed, not from its own nature, but into a more excellent and worthier state than it had been before. All this therefore which Augustine says of habit he adduces for this reason, to show that when the Word was made flesh it was not changed or converted into man; just as neither is a man changed into a garment when he puts it on. But this, which certain ones add of their own, that as a man when he puts on his garment does not become something, so neither did God, when he took the form of a servant, become something: this, I say, they do not have from the words of Augustine, [0577D] nor of any other saint, since it is false: for if they wish to hold the likeness adduced from the garment in all respects, they must concede that, as a man does not become a garment when he puts it on, so neither did God become man when he clothed himself with the form of a servant. For if Christ is nothing but that which he was from eternity, he had no being common with his mother: therefore not consubstantial with his mother; because nothing substantial is said of both. For when it is said: Christ is man, and the Virgin mother of the Lord is man, the same is not signified by this name, man, according to these new heretics. Of these things it has been spoken more fully upon the Epistle to the Romans.

[0578A]QUESTION VI. *Who humbled himself, becoming obedient,* etc. It is asked whether Christ merited anything. Solution. He merited for us, not for himself. For we by his merit were made worthy of eternal life; but he by his death acquired for himself nothing of which he was not worthy before. Yet it can be said: Truly by the humility of his passion he merited the brightness of the resurrection, namely impassibility and immortality. I do not say that Christ, unless he died, would not become immortal and impassible; but I say that such a merit was worthy of such a reward.

QUESTION VII. It is asked how at the same time in the same soul there could be the highest beatitude and sadness: for before the passion these two were in Christ. For that there was beatitude according to the mind [0578B] from the time he was man, is to be believed: otherwise, how he would be true God, I do not see. Likewise that there was sadness is established, since he himself says: *My soul is sorrowful even unto death [n: (Matth. XXVI)]*. Solution. As in Paul there was a lower power and a higher power, so in Christ there was a lower power, namely a motion of the soul, or a certain affection, which shrank in horror from death: yet the higher reason, free in its beatitude, flourished. For the affection of the flesh in him, as in the rest, did not impede the reason in anything, so as to contemplate the divinity less.

QUESTION VIII. Likewise it is asked how he did not have greater beatitude after the resurrection, when he put on immortality and impassibility? Solution. He did not then have more as to the mind, but what [0578C] he had before, he then possessed more tranquilly, just as a king possesses his kingdom more tranquilly and securely when the enemies cease to harass it.

QUESTION IX. *For which cause he gave,* etc. Did he not have before the name which is above every name? How then does he say, For which cause he gave? for not only according to that he is God was this name given to him from eternity according to nature, but also according to that he is man it was given from his conception to the man assumed according to grace. Solution. A thing is then said to be made, when it becomes known: for what he had before, then first after the resurrection became known to men and demons.

QUESTION X. Likewise it is asked to whom this name was given, [0578D] whether to God, or to man? Solution. Augustine says that to man and not to God was given the name *which is above every name [n: (Philipp. II)]*, namely that he be God and the Son of God by the grace not of participation, but of union, and that the man assumed have by grace all that God begotten has by nature. By grace therefore the man has it that he is a son, not a son of grace, but a son of nature. Ambrose says otherwise, that to God and not to man was given the name which is above every name.

QUESTION XI. But do Augustine and Ambrose think contrary things upon this? Solution. One is the gratuitous giving, of which Augustine speaks; another the natural, [0579A] of which the blessed Ambrose speaks. It was given to the man that he be God by personal union immediately in his conception: for when God was made man by condescension, the man too was made God by grace, not an adoptive God, but a natural God, an eternal God, an omnipotent God. But to God begotten it was given from eternity to be God by nature. And so there is no contrariety in understanding between the doctors of truth.

QUESTION XII. *We who serve God in spirit,* etc. [n: (Philip. III.)] It is asked with what service. Note that the service which is owed to God alone is called *latria*; whence also one is called an idolater who exhibits to idols the worship of God, and the abomination is called idolatry, and the house an idol-temple, and the sacrifice an idol-offering. But the service by which through charity [0579B] we are bidden to serve one another is in Greek called *dulia*: yet both are named by the Latin word "service."

QUESTION XIII. *A Hebrew of Hebrews, according to the law a Pharisee.* It is asked whence the Hebrews are so called. Solution. The Hebrews are called from Heber, not from Abraham, as it once seemed to the blessed Augustine, which he afterward retracted: which is clear from this, that Abraham himself is in a certain place called a Hebrew: which would not be, if this name were derived from him. Likewise Abraham is interpreted *father of many nations;* the Hebrews, *those passing over.* It is established therefore to be true that the Hebrews are called not from Abraham, but from Heber: who alone in the division of tongues retained the Hebrew [0579C] language.

QUESTION XIV. *According to the justice which is in the law.* It is asked how he says that he conversed according to the justice which is in the law, without complaint, since elsewhere he says that he walked with others in the desires of the flesh while he was under the law, whence he also names himself a child of wrath? Solution. Both are true, both that he walked according to the desires of the flesh, and yet that he conversed without complaint according to the justice which was in the law — which makes one serve through the fear of punishment, not through love; which restrains the hand, not the mind: such was the justice in the law.
