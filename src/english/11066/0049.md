## X. ON THE FIRST EPISTLE TO TIMOTHY.

[0593]

[0593C] *Paul an apostle [n: (I Tim. I)]*, etc. The Apostle writes this epistle to Timothy, who was the son of a certain faithful woman, begotten of a gentile father: and since he was not circumcised, and was himself a gentile, and the brethren who were in Lystra and Iconium gave good testimony of him, Paul wished this man to set out with him: and therefore that he be circumcised on account of the Jews who were in those places. And he was learned as well in the divine Scriptures as in the liberal arts. This man the Apostle created bishop: therefore, leaving him in Asia, he instructs him in this epistle concerning the episcopal office, namely how he should resist false apostles and how he should instruct the Church, what sort of priests or deacons he should ordain; what sort of widows he should honor, and how he should conduct himself in the Church, or how he should rule it. And the intention of the [0593D] Apostle in this epistle is to instruct Timothy concerning the office of the episcopal dignity. The manner is such: first he salutes him; then he warns him to resist the false ones; afterward he instructs him concerning the episcopal office, teaching what sort of priests and deacons he ought to ordain. Then what sort of widows he ought to receive; afterward he instructs him concerning the manner of correption; but at the end he warns him to avoid profane novelties.

QUESTION I. *Of God the Father our Saviour,* etc. Since the Son is frequently called in Scripture Saviour, who alone died for our salvation, not the Father, not the Holy Spirit, it is asked why the Apostle refers our salvation to God the Father, saying here: Of God the Father our Saviour. Solution. God the Father, in that he deigned to send us his Son, and to bestow the Holy Spirit in the sacraments of the New Testament, [0594C] is the author of our salvation and therefore is called Saviour.

QUESTION II. *And of Jesus Christ our hope.* Likewise it is asked why he refers hope specially to the Son, saying, and of Jesus Christ our hope, since we hope and believe in the whole Trinity. Solution. We therefore refer our hope to Christ, because he rose from the dead, and ascended into the heavens; because through his resurrection we hope to attain to the glory of the future resurrection.

QUESTION III. *Grace, mercy and peace,* etc. It is asked why, beyond his wont, the Apostle in this salutation puts three, saying, grace, mercy, and peace. Solution. The nine preceding epistles he wrote in common to the Churches. But this one specially to Timothy [0594D] his fellow-bishop; therefore he puts three. By mercy understanding the same thing which in the others he understood by grace, namely the remission of sins; by peace, the tranquillity and foretaste of the future life; but by grace he understands the donation of the Holy Spirit, wherewith the ministers of Christ are armed.

QUESTION IV. *That they should not attend to fables,* etc. It is asked what the Apostle here understands by fables? does he perhaps so call the words of the law, and the divine oracles in this place? and elsewhere he says: *Avoid profane and old wives' fables [n: (I Tim. IV)]*. Solution. By fables he here means the doctrine of those who preach that the law with grace is necessary. Or by fables he here means the traditions which the Jews hold unwritten, and one transfuses into another by speaking, which they call deuterosis: wherein they say, and believe, that God first created two wives, from whom [0595A] they weave infinite genealogies of men, bringing forth unfruitful questions. Concerning which traditions, foreign to the sacred Scriptures, the Lord says in the Gospel: *Why have you made void the commandment of God for your traditions? [n: (Matth. XV.)]*.

QUESTION V. *Now the end of the commandment is charity, from a pure heart, and a good conscience, and faith unfeigned.* Here the Apostle defines charity, as the expositor says. Whence it can be asked what is the definition here given, whether this: the end of the commandment? or this: from a pure heart, and a good conscience, and faith unfeigned? Solution. The definition of charity is, to be from a pure heart, and a good conscience, and faith unfeigned.

QUESTION VI. Likewise it can be asked whether this definition fits all charity. Which if it be granted, no one [0595B] is having charity who is not perfect, because consummating and perfecting the law; because charity is the end of the commandment, that is, perfection and consummation. For if 'end' is here put for consummation. But elsewhere it is put for consumption, as when it is said, the bread is finished. Elsewhere for a boundary, as when it is said: Here the field ends. Likewise, who can have a pure heart and a good conscience without charity? does not cleanness of heart, and a good conscience, proceed from charity? how then does charity proceed from a pure heart? Solution. It seems to me that here the definition of perfect and consummated charity is assigned, and thus this definition does not fit imperfect charity. First is given love, which cleanses the heart, and from which good [0595C] works are done, and from which is born a good conscience; at length, the heart being cleansed, and a good conscience procured, charity is perfected and consummated, which is the end of the commandment. These things I say without prejudice to a better opinion.

QUESTION VII. *The law is good, if a man use it lawfully.* It is asked what it is to use lawfully? Solution. As the expositor says, he uses the law lawfully who, understanding it spiritually, through it recognizes the disease, and seeks the physician, and who knows it to have been given for a time, and forsakes it for Christ. It is to be noted that the unjust man uses the law lawfully, when he understands why it was given, and by fear of it flees to the grace of Christ, that he may become just. But the just man uses the law lawfully, when by terrifying he imposes it upon the unjust.

[0595D]QUESTION VIII. *The law is not made for the just man.* Were not David and other just men, who were under the law, bound to keep the law, how then is the law not made for the just? Solution. The law is not imposed upon the just man, so as to have dominion over him, and to coerce him by fear, and the just man is not under the law, but rather with it, as a friend of the law.

QUESTION IX. *Because I did it ignorantly,* etc. The expositor says that this 'because' can note the cause or the consequence. [sic: *Ssd*] it is asked how ignorance, which is a fault, can be the cause of divine mercy. Solution. There is a certain ignorance from infirmity: from which if anyone act against God out of good zeal, out of the affection which he has, he merits to be freed from that ignorance.

[0596A]QUESTION X. *He came into this world to save sinners,* etc. It is asked concerning little ones, who are born of baptized parents, whether they pertain to the sinners for whose saving Jesus came into the world. For these have no actual sin, and how can they contract original sin from parents, which the parents themselves have not; because it is forgiven them? Solution. It is to be known that original sin is so forgiven in baptism, not that it be not at all, but that it be not a fault. But the vice of corruption remains even in the baptized, who beget, not from that whereby they are reborn, but from the oldness of corruption, through which original fault passes into the little ones: whence they too need a remedy. Whence also the physician himself says: *Suffer the little ones to come unto me [n: (Marc. X)]*.

[0596B]QUESTION XI. *Of whom I am the first.* Were not Cain and Saul before Paul? how then is Paul the first among sinners? Solution. Paul was first not in order of time, but in greatness of iniquity. To which solution it is thus objected: Did not Paul sin through ignorance, and many others knowingly? but it is a greater sin to sin knowingly than through ignorance: therefore Paul was not the first among sinners in greatness of iniquity, since others, especially those who sinned against the Holy Spirit, are much worse than Paul even according to his first state, in which he was a blasphemer, a persecutor, and contumelious. Solution. Paul, considering the greatness of his own offenses, judged himself worse than all sinners, [0596C] although in the eyes of God it was otherwise.

QUESTION XII. *Now to the king of ages, immortal, invisible, the only God, honor and glory for ever and ever. Amen.* The expositor says that the man assumed by the Word is king of ages from that time in which he was assumed by the Word; but if the man assumed is not man, nor God, nor person, as certain men have dared to profess, how is the man assumed, from the time that he is assumed, and not before, king of ages? But we say that the man assumed by the Word is man and God: the Word itself, by whom he was assumed, both began in time to be king of ages, namely when he began to be assumed by the Word. For when God began to be man, man also began to be God, as many authorities attest.

[0596D]QUESTION XIII. *Who will have all men to be saved [n: (I Tim. II)]*. Since God is omnipotent, and no one can resist the divine will, how are not all saved, since God wills all to be saved? Or is it perhaps his will that the reprobate be saved? Ambrose thus resolves it: God wills all to be saved, if they themselves will. But do not many will to be saved, who nevertheless are not saved? how then is it true, God wills all to be saved? And therefore others thus expound: God wills, etc., it would please him, if all were saved, or all, that is, of every kind of men some. Or thus: To all he offers grace, through which, if they will, they can be saved. But how is this solution true? Are there not many who are and were, who did not even hear the word of preaching? Or he wills all to be saved, [0597A] that is, he makes the holy to will that all be saved. Or thus: He wills all to be saved, that is, no one is made saved, unless he himself will it. In like manner is understood that: *Who enlighteneth every man [n: (Joan. I)]*, that is, no one is enlightened except by him, and all are made alive in Christ, according to the same manner it behooves to be understood. Thus concerning the ambiguous diverse opinions are given.

QUESTION XIV. *One mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus.* Concerning the mediator it has already in part been said above. But here let it suffice to consider this which the expositor says, that in so far as he is man, he is mediator, not in so far as he is Word. But is he not mediator between the angels and God the Father, and also between the Holy Spirit, in so far as he is Word, though otherwise between the angels and God the Father, and otherwise between the Holy [0597B] Spirit and the Father? Solution. It can well be granted that, in so far as he is Word, he is mediator between the rational creatures and God the Father; but he is not mediator between God the Trinity and men in so far as he is Word, but in so far as he is man like to God in justice, to men in mortality. For if, in so far as he is Word, he were mediator between men and God the Trinity, then there would already be something mediate between himself and another, because the Word between himself and men; which is not to be granted. Someone will say: Christ is now not mortal, how then is he mediator between mortal men and the immortal God, since he was therefore called mediator; because he is like to men through mortality, and to God through justice? Solution. Even now Christ reconciles us to God the Father as the best [0597C] mediator, through that which he did for us in the days of his flesh, that is, of his mortality.
