## XIV. ON THE EPISTLE TO THE HEBREWS.

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0607]

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0607C] *In many ways [n: (Hebr. I)]*, etc. ⟦s2⟧ Paul, the excellent doctor, the Apostle of the Gentiles, wishing to honor his ministry. ⟦s3⟧ According to which he says in the Epistle to the Romans: *As long as I am the Apostle of the Gentiles, I will honor my ministry, trying if by any means I may provoke to emulation my own flesh [n: (Rom. XI)]*. ⟦s4⟧ To the churches of the Hebrews he writes this epistle, treating of the eminence of Christ according to both natures, and of the uselessness of the Mosaic law; building up in many ways that the faith of Jesus Christ, without the legal observances, suffices for justice and salvation. ⟦s5⟧ But that the legal observances, after Christ's passion, [0608C] not only do not profit, but even hinder, excluding the estimation of certain Hebrews, who, confessing Christ, thought that the legal observances were to be kept; and by their authority had led into this error even some who had come to Christ from heathenism; therefore the Apostle, providing for the Gentiles, lest thenceforth they be drawn into this error by the authority of the Hebrews, provoking the Jews also to emulation, commends the grace of God made to the faithful in this time through Christ the true high priest, showing the law to be reprobated. ⟦s6⟧ The intention, therefore, of the Apostle [0609A] in this epistle is to show the eminence of Christ, and the sufficiency of faith, as well as the insufficiency and uselessness of the law.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ The manner of treating is such. ⟦s2⟧ First he proposes that the words of Christ are to be heard as those of the prophets, and further, by comparing him with the prophets, and preferring him; because in him God spoke as in the prophets, and he is greater than they. ⟦s3⟧ Then he commends him alternately according to both natures, namely the human and the divine, afterward he compares him with the angels, and prefers him, interweaving many things about his excellence according to both natures. ⟦s4⟧ Then he compares him with Moses and prefers him. ⟦s5⟧ Then by many reasons and authorities he declares that the grace of faith is to be carried through for the shadow of the law; and the priesthood of Christ to the Levitical [0609B] priesthood, and the New Testament to the Old; and he shows that its one sacrifice is to be set before the many sacrifices of that; because there is the shadow, here the truth. ⟦s6⟧ Finally he sets down a description of faith, commending it by many testimonies. ⟦s7⟧ But toward the end he adds a moral instruction.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION I. First it is asked, why to this epistle, as to the others, he did not prefix his own name, which is Paul; why also he was silent about the name of dignity, which is Apostle? ⟦s2⟧ Solution. ⟦s3⟧ Because he was odious to the Hebrews, to whom he seemed a destroyer of the law, he kept silent his name, odious to them, lest the ill-will toward his name, prefixed, should exclude the usefulness of the reading that follows; knowing also their pride, and showing his own humility, he was unwilling to put forward the dignity of his order by naming [0609C] himself Apostle, but, keeping silent the merit of his office, himself humble toward the proud, he did not name himself apostle, lest the proud be indignant. ⟦s4⟧ But someone will say? ⟦s5⟧ Does not the Apostle write to the faithful who were at Jerusalem, to whom the name of Paul was not odious, nor did they envy his dignity as though he were proud; how then is it true that he kept silent his proper name or the name of dignity because it was odious to the Hebrews, since to those to whom he wrote he was not odious, but beloved? ⟦s6⟧ Solution. ⟦s7⟧ Among those to whom, as an excellent preacher of grace, he was much pleasing, there were certain zealots of the law, who thought and preached that the law was to be kept together with grace, and to these the name of Paul was odious. ⟦s8⟧ For they found no one so contrary to their false opinion as Paul, whence they persecuted [0609D] him as much as they could. ⟦s9⟧ It is to be noted that there were some saying that this epistle was by no means the apostle Paul's, because his name is not prefixed to it as in all the others, and also because it shines with a more splendid and more eloquent style than the others; but that it was either Luke's, or Barnabas's, or Clement's. ⟦s10⟧ To whom Jerome thus responds: If it is therefore not to be called Paul's, because it is not inscribed with his name, then neither is it any of theirs, nay of no one at all, since it has no one's name in the title, which was done for the aforesaid cause already given. ⟦s11⟧ But that it shines with greater eloquence than the others is not to be wondered at, since it is natural for each one to be more capable in his own language than in a foreign one; since therefore he wrote this one [0610A] alone in the Hebrew language, but the others in Greek, what wonder if it shines with greater eloquence?

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION II. ⟦s2⟧ *In these days.* ⟦s3⟧ It is asked what days he calls them. ⟦s4⟧ Solution. ⟦s4⟧ The Apostle calls the time of grace "days" on account of the eminent doctrine of faith and the knowledge of salvation, whence elsewhere: *Behold, now is the day of salvation [n: (II Cor. VI)]*.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION III. ⟦s2⟧ *Whom he hath appointed heir of all things,* etc. ⟦s3⟧ It is asked, according to which nature Christ is here called heir of all things. ⟦s4⟧ Solution. ⟦s5⟧ It can well be said that according to the divine nature he is here called heir, that is, possessor, and Lord of all things, that is, of all nature. ⟦s6⟧ Or according to his humanity he is called heir of all things, namely of those to be saved, or of Jews and Gentiles. ⟦s7⟧ For this is the heir of the world, that seed in which all nations are blessed, [0610B] to whom the Father speaks, saying: *Ask of me, and I will give thee the nations for thine inheritance [n: (Psal. II)]*.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION IV. ⟦s2⟧ *By whom also he made the ages.* ⟦s3⟧ Did not God the Father make all things through the Son, visible and invisible, mutable and immutable? ⟦s4⟧ Why then does the Apostle not say: By whom he made all things, but "by whom he made also the ages"! ⟦s5⟧ Solution. ⟦s6⟧ These visible and mutable things are more known to us; and therefore in these we more admire the power of God than in those which are unknown; and this is the reason why he rather named the ages specially.

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION V. *Who, being the brightness of glory,* etc. ⟦s2⟧ It is asked why he uses this likeness, and elsewhere another? ⟦s3⟧ Solution. ⟦s4⟧ The Apostle, wishing to show that, although the Son is from the Father, yet he is coeternal with him, [0610C] uses a proportional likeness of temporal things, saying: who, being the brightness of glory, as if he were to say: as the brightness of fire, although it be from the fire, yet is coeval with the fire, and would be coeternal with it, if it were eternal; for the fire was never without brightness; so the Son, although he be from the Father, yet is coeternal with him, because the Father was never without the Son. ⟦s5⟧ Likewise, wishing to show the identity of nature which the Son has with the Father, he uses a proportional likeness of other things, calling the Son the figure of the substance, because he could not demonstrate both in the same things. ⟦s6⟧ For in creatures nothing is found which has being from another, and is of the same nature or substance, and which is not preceded by it. ⟦s7⟧ But the Son so has being from the Father, that he is coeternal with him; as the brightness [0610D] is coeval with the fire, and is of the same essence or nature with the Father. ⟦s8⟧ As the man begetting, and the man begotten of him — for as a man cannot beget except that which he himself is, that is, because a man begets a man, so God begets nothing other than what he himself is, that is, God begets God. ⟦s9⟧ Reader, diligently observe these words of the Apostle, and the exposition of the holy ones upon them; and thou wilt notice how both the Sabellian heresy and the Arian is here manifestly destroyed.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION VI. ⟦s2⟧ *And upholding all things by the word of his power.* ⟦s3⟧ It is asked how God is said to uphold all things by the word, since to uphold does not pertain to the property of the Word. ⟦s4⟧ Likewise, whether he is beneath all things, and not rather over and above them, that he should be said to uphold them. ⟦s5⟧ Solution. [0611A] He is said to uphold all things by the word rather than by power for this reason, that in upholding — that is, in containing, and governing, and preserving — he may be understood to sustain no labor or difficulty, he who is more inward than every thing, because all things are in him; and higher than every thing, because he is above all; and more ancient than every thing, because he is before all; and newer than every thing, because he is after all, that is, after the beginnings of all things.

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION VII. ⟦s2⟧ *He sitteth at the right hand,* etc. ⟦s3⟧ The expositor says that the man assumed was exalted even to equality with the Father, which is understood by the "right hand": therefore it is asked whether it is to be granted that the man assumed is equal to the Father; but since the Son says: *The Father is greater than I [n: (Joan. XIV)]*: which is to be understood [0611B] according to that which is assumed, how is the same equal to the Father, since the Son according to it is less than the Father? ⟦s4⟧ Solution. ⟦s5⟧ That man, inasmuch as he is man, is less than the Father, and not equal: but inasmuch as the man assumed is God, the Son of God, he is not less than the Father, but equal. ⟦s6⟧ It is therefore to be seen what is said of what, and in respect of what, as often as discourse occurs about Christ; yet there are many who do not grant that the man assumed is God, as has already been said above.

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION VIII. ⟦s2⟧ *Being made so much better than the angels.* ⟦s3⟧ It is asked according to which nature he here speaks of Christ: that according to divinity he is better than the angels, there is no question; but yet he was not made better than the angels, but rather born. ⟦s4⟧ By this therefore, [0611C] that he says "made," we are compelled here to understand of Christ according to his humanity, namely that he was made better than the angels. ⟦s5⟧ But to this seems contrary what is found in the Psalm: *Thou hast lessened him below the angels [n: (Psalm. VIII)]*. ⟦s6⟧ How, according to the same nature, can he be better, and less? ⟦s7⟧ Solution. ⟦s8⟧ He was made less than the angels by the mortality and passion of the flesh; and he is greater and better than the same by the fullness of grace, from which the angels themselves also receive.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION IX. ⟦s2⟧ *I have this day begotten thee,* etc. ⟦s3⟧ It is asked of what day he speaks. ⟦s4⟧ Solution. ⟦s5⟧ Augustine says that this can be understood of that day on which Christ was born according to the flesh. ⟦s6⟧ Yet it can be understood more divinely of his eternal generation: whence he says "I begot," [0611D] lest it be understood as new; for "today" is said of a past begetting; and thus it is intimated to be eternal, in which nothing is past, as though it had ceased to be, nor future, as though it were not yet.

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ QUESTION X. But again it can be asked how this can be expounded of the temporal generation of Christ, since God the Father did not beget Christ according to human nature? ⟦s2⟧ For as Christ has no mother according to his Deity, so neither a father according to his humanity. ⟦s3⟧ Since therefore he is not his father according to this nature, how can it be said that he begot him according to it? ⟦s4⟧ Solution. ⟦s5⟧ To beget does not always denote a natural generation, but sometimes a gratuitous one, as to thee: *Of his own will he begot us by the word of truth [n: (Jac. I)]*. ⟦s6⟧ Because, therefore, the temporal generation of Christ [0612A] was the work of the whole Trinity, it can not unfittingly be said that the Father begot him even according to his humanity. ⟦s7⟧ But someone will say: Therefore Christ is a son of grace, and so adoptive? ⟦s8⟧ Solution. ⟦s9⟧ It is not true: by grace indeed that man was made a son, not of grace, but of nature.
