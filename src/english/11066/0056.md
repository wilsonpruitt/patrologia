## XIV. ON THE EPISTLE TO THE HEBREWS. (cont.)

[0615C]QUESTION XXVI. *But now we see not yet all things subject to him,* etc. The prophet says, thou hast subjected all things to him: which the Apostle, expounding, subjoined, he left nothing not subject to him; how then does he say here: We see not yet all things subject to him? Solution. David, after his manner, uses the past for the future out of certitude, saying: Thou hast subjected all things to him. The Apostle, understanding this, said above that the world was to be subjected to him: and here he says: We see not yet all things subject to him; as if he should say: already in part the prophecy is fulfilled, in part still to be fulfilled. But someone will say: do not all things serve the divine dispensation, whose will nothing can resist? and thus already, willing or unwilling, all things are subjected to him. How then does the Apostle say, We see not yet all things subject to him? Solution. Subjection [0615D] is one kind general, another special. Likewise one hidden, another necessary, another voluntary. According to the general, and the hidden, and the necessary, all things are already subject to God; according to the voluntary, not yet are all things subject which shall be subjected. Not yet does every tongue of things heavenly, earthly and infernal confess that the Lord Jesus is in the glory of the Father [n: (Philipp. II)]. Not yet are all enemies made the footstool of his feet [n: (Psal. CIX)]. All which indeed shall wholly come to pass in the future; therefore the Apostle says: We see not yet all things, namely voluntarily, or openly, subject to him. Note that only God is greater than the human mind, not any of the angels. Angels indeed can be greater divine ones than men as regards the body and the mind [0616A] weighed down by the corruption of the body [n: (Sap. IX)]: but not as regards the very purity of the mind, in which it was assumed by the Word.

QUESTION XXVII. *That by the grace of God he might taste death for all,* etc. The death of Christ did not profit all; how then did he taste death for all? Solution. The whole is here reduced to a part: namely, for all the predestined he tasted death. Or it is therefore said that he tasted death for all, because, as far as in him lies, the death of Christ suffices for all.

QUESTION XXVIII. *It became him for whom are all things, and by whom are all things,* etc. It is asked how it became God the Father to perfect the author of the salvation of the faithful through the passion of death. Wherein consists this [0616B] fittingness, since it rather seems unfitting that the Lord of glory should die? Solution. If Christ did not die, man would perish: and if this were so, God would not be glorified by the whole; and this would be unfitting, since all things were made for God to be glorified. Likewise, if Christ did not die, man would not be saved, and thus the divine predestination would not be fulfilled, and this would be unfitting, that it should be made void. Nor was it unfitting that the author of salvation should die for us, since he is our neighbor, and brother, and it befits a neighbor to provide for the advantage of his neighbors as much as he can; whence it follows:

QUESTION XXIX. *For which cause he is not ashamed to call them brethren,* etc. It is asked whether Christ is therefore called our brother, because he was made a partaker of our nature, [0616C] taking the form of a servant [n: (Philipp. II)]. But since the nature assumed by God is common to good and bad, it seems that Christ is not only the brother of the faithful but also of the unfaithful. Solution. Christ is not called our brother only because he shares in our nature, but also because we have received of the fullness of his grace. Therefore, for anyone to be called a brother of Christ, it behooves that he be joined to him by nature and by grace.

QUESTION XXX. *And he himself,* namely Christ, *in like manner partook of the same,* namely of the children, *or of flesh and blood.* But it is asked how Christ partook of the children? Solution. That is, he was made a child consisting of soul and flesh, as the expositor says. Or he partook of flesh and blood, that is, he was made [0616D] man, and this "in like manner," that is, passible and mortal.

QUESTION XXXI. *That he might destroy him who had the empire of death.* Since death is a punishment justly inflicted by God, and thus is not unfittingly said to be from God, it is asked how the devil is said to be the author of death. For the same thing does not seem able to be from God and from the devil. Solution. Because death happened from sin, which the devil persuaded, therefore he has the empire of death, or is said to have it; because he was the cause of death, therefore he was called its author: whence it is said that God does not make death, that is, its cause has not its being from God. Note that this is the justice by which we were redeemed. For because the devil shed the blood of one who was not a debtor, [0617A] he was commanded to give back the debtors; and because he shed the blood of the innocent, in whom he found nothing, he was commanded to withdraw from the guilty, whom by a certain right he seemed to possess.

QUESTION XXXII. The expositor says that unless it were a man who conquered the devil, not justly, but by violence would man be taken from him. But had not the devil done injury to God, who first fraudulently deceived the servant, and afterward violently possessed him? What injustice then would God do, if by the sole word of his power he should snatch man from the hand of the most unjust invader? Solution. All the works of God are just, but in some there is also manifest power, and justice lies hidden; in some also hidden power lies, and justice is manifest. If therefore Christ in our liberation had used [0617B] manifest power and hidden justice, it might seem to some less discerning that man was taken from the devil not justly, but by violence: but that all iniquity might stop its mouth [n: (Psal. CVI)], our Lord used in our redemption manifest justice and hidden power.

QUESTION XXXIII. *That he might deliver them, who through the fear of death,* etc. It is asked to whose servitude they were subject, whom the Lord delivered? Solution. The devil, before the coming of Christ, obtained the effect of his wickedness through death: by the fear of which he cast down those whom in no other way he could cast down. For they gave all things for their life. Whence in Job it is read: *Skin for skin; all that a man hath he will give for his life [n: (Job I)]*. And so, conquered by the fear of death, they yielded, and succumbed to temptation, [0617C] until Christ came, who by dying and rising took away the fear of death from their midst. Whence the saints deride death, and desire to be dissolved, and to be with Christ [n: (Philipp. I)]. Or it can be understood of the fear of punishment, whereby the Jews served under the law, whom Christ, the law being emptied, through grace liberated from the burden of the law and from servile fear.

QUESTION XXXIV. *For nowhere doth he take hold of the angels,* etc. It is asked whether God could have assumed the angelic nature into the unity of the person as he did the human? Solution. It seems to us that he could, but would not; wherein the dignity of our race is understood: for to man, who had received less than the angels in his creation, more was conferred in his glorification through [0617D] grace. For human nature does not adore the angelic nature above it, but rather conversely.

QUESTION XXXV. *Wherefore he ought in all things to be made like unto his brethren.* Was he not by grace alone made like unto his brethren in this, that he was born man, that he was reared, suffered, died? how then does the Apostle say that this "ought" to be, since this is not a debt, but a gratuitous gift of God? Solution. He does not say simply "ought," but added, *that he might be merciful [n: (Hebr. II)]*: for by dying he could not have mercy on us, unless he first became passible and mortal. Or he therefore says "ought," that is, it was fitting for him to be made like unto his brethren. For by this, that he bore our infirmities and death for us, he provoked us to humility, and kindled charity in us.

[0618A]QUESTION XXXVI. *For in that he himself hath suffered and been tempted, he is able,* etc. Was he not before his passion, nay before his incarnation, able to help the tempted? for his power was not augmented through the passion; what then is it, "in that he himself hath suffered, he is able to help those who are tempted"? Solution. After the passion he well knows even by experience what that temptation is, and the tribulation of those who suffer: whence with much alacrity he stretches out his hand to compassionate; therefore he is said to be able to do these things, and perfectly to know them. For he knows who they are who suffer for him, he knows when and how and to whom he ought to stretch out his hand. Whence the Prophet: *The Lord is become a refuge for the poor in his opportunities, in tribulation [n: (Psal. IX)]*.

[0618B]QUESTION XXXVII. *By so much hath he greater honor than the house who framed it [n: (Hebr. III)]*, etc. Does not often he who dispenses in the house have greater glory in it than he who framed it? how then does the Apostle say: By so much greater, etc. Solution. This is to be understood of the spiritual house, as if he should say: Christ as Lord and Creator is greater than Moses the servant and minister.

QUESTION XXXVIII. *According to the day of temptation,* etc. It is asked by whom temptation is made. Solution. Man tempts, that he may know what he is ignorant of, according to which acceptation temptation is a certain soothing foretaste of the mind, running before, for the recognizing of something which before was unknown. God tempts, that he may prove through affliction and tribulation, that he may crown the proved. [0618C] The devil tempts, that he may cast someone down into sin: whence it is said: *And lead us not into temptation [n: (Matth. VI)]*, that is, suffer us not to be cast down into sin. The flesh tempts, when we feel its inordinate motions. The world tempts, when it provokes us through vanity to the love of itself.

QUESTION XXXIX. *Wherefore I was offended,* etc. Another translation has "I was near." How then, if offended, was he near; and if near, how offended? and if both readings cannot stand, which rather is to be held? Solution. He was near by applying the scourges of correction; because it is the part of a neighbor to correct his neighbor. He was offended and angry, because even through the scourges they would not repent. Or toward the good for forty [0618D] years he can be understood angry in this; because for so long a time he did not bring them into the land of promise: this opinion nevertheless the following text does not seem to approve. But toward the wicked he can be understood near for forty years in this, that for so long a time he bore with them, nor wholly destroyed them.

QUESTION XL. *If they shall enter into my rest.* It is asked by what figure this is said. Solution. It is the figure which is called aposiopesis, and the sense is: If this shall be, anything impossible will be, or let me no longer be believed in anything. Note that there are four Sabbaths, or rests. The first rest is that of God, of which it is written in Genesis: *And God rested on the seventh day [n: (Gen. II)]*; the second rest is the land of promise; the third, the quiet of the mind; the fourth is the eternal. The first and [0619A] fourth are of God; the second and third of man. The first is a figure of the fourth, the second of the third.
