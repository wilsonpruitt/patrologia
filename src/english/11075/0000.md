## PROLOGUE.

[0997B]

Since every word of the divine page has this intent, that it may persuade man to the good of humility and, more earnestly, urge him to shun the evil of pride — especially since in the one lies the beginning of salvation and of life, in the other the beginning of ruin — it seems necessary to display outwardly to the cultivator of the virtues the fruit and efficacy of humility and of pride themselves, as though a certain visible form, so that the imitator of either kind, of pride or of humility, may recognize from the quality of the fruits what reward he attains from the pursuit of the one or the other. And so we set before every convert, both the untrained and the newcomer, two little trees, unlike in fruit and in growth, with a few small definitions of the vices or virtues appended, so that from their root the yield of the fruits may lie open, [0997C] and that the one drawn on by the fruit may discern which tree of the two is to be chosen. And indeed pride is the root of the fruit of the flesh, humility the fruit of the spirit. This diversity, once the roots are inspected, shows to the one who moderately seeks their fruit what he should desire. Finally, the old Adam sets himself upon the summit of the vicious tree. The new Adam obtains the headship of the spiritual yield. If therefore you join the more excellent to the worse — that is, the good to the evil, side by side — you will understand strongly what stands out among these, what outweighs. For when the qualities of contraries are set side by side, the estimation of the better will quickly lie open. Therefore, once the roots, branches, and fruits have been examined, let it be for your judgment to choose what you will.

## CHAP. I. *On the three theological virtues, and the four cardinal virtues born from humility.*

[0997D]

For the foundation of all the virtues, as has been said before, is humility, *because everyone who humbles himself [0998B] shall be exalted [n: (Luc. XIV)]*. And *to the poor in spirit the kingdom of heaven is opened [n: (Matth. V)]*. Now the first of the virtues, faith, draws near to humility, because *it is impossible to please God without faith; and the just man lives by faith [n: (Hebr. XI)]*. But of what profit is faith to you, if by hope you do not seek what by faith you embrace? *For what we do not yet see we hope for, and hope does not confound [n: (Rom. V)]*. And because by hoping we love what we already hold by faith, charity succeeds to hope, and thus faith, which works through love, rightly goes forward. But how these three virtues — that is, hope, faith, charity — are to be distinguished and held, prudence teaches and instructs. Justice adorns and completes. Fortitude retains and strengthens; and lest they run out into excess and lest they be improperly confined within their bounds, temperance moderates and discerns. [0998C] If therefore you add these four virtues to the three above, this sevenfold number of virtues brings to those who carry them out the fullness of sevenfold grace, through which the framework of the vices is dissolved, the body of the devil is overcome, and by the path of justice the fount and origin of all the virtues is approached.

## CHAP. II. *On the comparison of equals among themselves and with unequals — as of virtues with virtues, of baser things with vices, and of virtues with vices.*

If you join like things to like, the equality of the parts sets forth the excellence of each part. But if you set unlike things together, the diversity of the one is the more manifest. And thus the conjunction of equals keeps off the difference of the individual parts, and the diverse quality of each seems to grow [0998D] from both. Compare white with black, the wise man with the fool, the proud with the humble, the evil with the good, and from these very distant contraries the proof of its own nature [0999A] seems to accrue to each kind. So consider the fruits of the aforesaid trees. For of how great a bitterness the root of pride is, together with its shoot, is easily perceived from the abundant sweetness of holy humility. And so, whoever has set out to war down the furies of the vices by the opposition of the virtues, let him know that it belongs to the matter to consider their natures or qualities by a subtle examination; and once these are inspected, in humility's triumphs their whole force will be weighed. Therefore humility, which by the primacy of the virtues holds the highest place, is girded with a sevenfold crown of the theological and cardinal virtues. From these the other virtues, derived as though from a certain fount, bring in by their carrying-out a certain perfection, and display the prize which is promised indeed to those who fight, but bestowed upon those who conquer. [0999B] Against this humility runs pride, the summit of the vices, which in its phalanx displays the matter of death, and for its followers a sure ruin. Therefore let it not weary us to consider the retinue of each, differing in nature or in effect, so that, the vices being reproved through reason, one may be able to triumph through virtue.

## CHAP. III. *On pride, and its companions.*

Pride is a certain craving for glory and preeminence, of singular excellence and of a mind swelling above every company. Its principal retinue are these:

Lust, gluttony of the belly, avarice, sadness, anger, envy, vainglory.

These seven principal vices (to say something drawn from secular learning) we may call subalternate [0999C] genera (in a certain manner), that is, so that they are species in relation to what is above them — that is, to pride, from which they proceed — but genera in relation to what is below them, that is, to the vices issuing from them. For example, we say that the evil of pride is a certain substantive genus; and the vices flowing from pride, certain substantive species, as are animal and man, horse, etc., but only in a certain respect. Just as we say that night multiplies the darkness, and that silence covers malice, when in the one it is the light, in the other the voice, that has been suspended — and many things like these. But let no one here be offended by this paint and outward coloring, which I have set down for this reason: that I may the better display the fount and the stream — that is, pride with its appendages. Now therefore, with the little definitions appended, [0999D] let us look at the array and retinue of the malice of pride.

## CHAP. IV. *On vainglory, and its companions.*

Vainglory is an unfitting agitation of both the inner and the outer man, set in motion by the desire of pleasing. In its retinue are hypocrisy, disobedience, boastfulness, the presumption of novelties, arrogance, loquacity, obstinacy.

Hypocrisy is to cloak by art a vice hidden under the simulation of virtues.

Disobedience is, out of the hardness of an obstinate mind, to refuse to comply with him whom you ought to obey when he commands just things.

Boastfulness is, the arrogance of the mind driving it, vainly to proclaim and promise greater things about yourself than there are.

[1000A]The presumption of novelties is, with singular swelling, to attempt something new against the character of one's own order or of one's powers.

Arrogance is to peddle what you do not have, and to boast of what you have not received, out of the desire of pleasing men.

Loquacity is that by which inward levity is shown through a foolish outpouring of words.

Obstinacy is, out of an irrational and inflexible stubbornness of mind, to refuse to yield to the better.

## CHAP. V. *On envy, and its companions.*

Envy is that which renders the mind burning in grief on account of another's good and honor, unwilling to see these things. In its retinue are hatred, affliction, exultation, malice, detraction, bitterness, whispering.

[1000B]Hatred is that which, more sullenly and tenaciously, in the hope of revenge, bitingly conceals the movement of an angered mind, and pursues with ill-will the man himself, not only his vices.

Affliction amid prosperity is the torment of a hating mind, descending from the advancement of one who pleases it less.

Exultation amid adversity is the display of immoderate gladness, from another's downfall, on account of a hatred once conceived.

Malice is the desire for another's harm without hope of obtaining any good from it.

Detraction is the biting and envious diminishing of another's glory and repute, through words or signs.

Bitterness is a certain solitude of a distracted mind, [1000C] void of spiritual joy, and a sickening at another's good.

Whispering is that by which, by a certain hiss of hateful flattery or of disparagement, one is stirred to the hatred of another.

## CHAP. VI. *On anger, and its retinue.*

Anger is the drawn-tight fury of a roused mind, burning with the lust of avenging. Its companions are clamor, blasphemy, contumely, lamentation, rashness, frenzy, indignation.

Clamor is the loud and shameless upbraiding of a crime committed.

Blasphemy is a cursing hurled at another with a certain shameful irreligion of the abusive; [1000D] or it is the diminishing of a divine or human name, done by swearing or by reproaching.

Contumely is the making public of another's disgrace, with the spite of grief and under the command of bitterness.

Lamentation is that which, the mind embittered, makes satisfaction upon itself with weeping for the vengeance it cannot take upon a superior.

Rashness is the undisciplined sally of an insolent mind, void of reason.

Frenzy is the unbridled madness of an impatient and powerless mind, over the adversities conceived within.

Indignation is a certain turbid contempt toward an inferior, arising from insolent ill-will.
