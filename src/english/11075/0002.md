## CHAP. XVIII. *On charity, and its retinue.*

Charity is an affection of the mind, and a devout love, extended fervently and in due order toward God and neighbor. Its companions are graciousness, peace, piety, meekness, liberality, mercy, indulgence, compassion, kindness, concord.

Graciousness is that in which the affectionate cultivation of mutual good-will, of the one toward the other, is shown.

Peace is the ordered tranquillity of minds agreeing in the good.

[1005A]Piety is a disposition helpful to all, arising from the sweetness of a kindly mind.

Meekness is that by which the tranquillity of mind of the meek or modest man does not, by anyone's wickedness, overstep the bound of its own quiet.

Liberality is that by which a free mind, in the bestowing of its possessions, is constrained by no niggardliness.

Mercy is a clement and equal regard toward all, and the inclination of a compassionate mind toward the afflicted.

Indulgence is the remission of another's guilt, descending from the consideration of oneself.

Compassion is that by which, from the grief of a neighbor, a certain affliction is engendered in a sympathizing mind.

Kindness is the fervent regard of a loving mind, with a certain splendid sweetness [1005B] of manners.

Concord is a certain unbroken and united embrace of minds, meeting together in what is right.

## CHAP. XIX. *On love, and its twofold aspect.*

We daily sow a discourse on love, in the hope that it may perchance sparkle in our hearts, and the fire blaze up, making a flame — either consuming the whole, or purging the whole. For from it is everything that is good, and everything that is evil is from it. One fount of love, springing up within, pours in two streams. The one is love of the world, cupidity; the other is love of God, charity. For the heart of man is the midpoint, whence the fount of love breaks forth. And when through appetite it runs down to outward things, it is called cupidity; but when it directs its desire to inward things, it is named charity. [1005C] Therefore there are two streams that flow from the fount of love: cupidity and charity. And cupidity is the root of all evils, and charity the root of all goods. But we find that this, which so manifoldly divides our desires and leads the one heart into diverse things, is nothing other than love. And since it is [1006A] a motion of the heart, according to its nature single and one, but according to its action divided — when it moves itself inordinately, that is, toward the things it ought not, it is called cupidity; but when it is ordered, it is called charity. We wish to commend a great matter, if only we are strong enough for what we wish. Almighty God, who needs nothing, because he himself is the highest and true good, who is; who can neither receive from another anything by which he might grow, since from him are all things, nor lose anything of his own by which he might fall away, since in him all things immutably subsist — he created the rational spirit by charity alone, by no necessity, that he might make it a partaker of his blessedness. Furthermore, that the same might be fit to enjoy so great a blessedness, he made love in it — in a certain manner rendering, through this, [1006B] the spiritual palate sensible to the taste of the inward sweetness, so that through this love, namely, it might savor the gladness of its own happiness, and might cleave to him with untiring desire. Through love, therefore, God joined the rational creature to himself, so that, ever cleaving to him, it might, in a certain manner, by affection suck from him the good by which it was to be beatified, and drink of him by desire, and possess him by joy. Therefore by love the rational creature is united to its Maker; and it is the bond of love alone that binds them both unto him, and the more happily the more strongly. For which reason also, that an undivided fellowship and a perfect concord might be had on both sides, the bond was doubled into the charity of God and of neighbor, so that through the charity of God all might cleave to the One, and through the charity [1006C] of neighbor all might become one toward one another; so that what each, of that One to whom all cleaved, could not contain in himself, he might more fully and more perfectly possess in another through the charity of neighbor; and the good of all might become the whole of each.
