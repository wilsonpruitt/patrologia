## PROLOGUE.

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0977A]

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ To his most dear lord and father H. Receive, I pray you, this little gift of my love with that same kindness with which I know myself to have offered it in devotion. ⟦s2⟧ I have sent to your charity a brief treatise on the power of prayer, written though it be in unpolished speech, in which you have set out distinctly in what ways the mind of one praying is kindled through compunction amid the words of his prayers; and it is shown also why those Scriptures which have not the form of a petition are brought forth by us, in the course of praying, when we ask for our own salvation or for that of others. ⟦s3⟧ Being about to ask for prayer, therefore, I have written concerning prayer; and the more perfectly you have already tasted its sweetness through long zeal of piety, the more gladly ought you both to receive the gift and [0977B] to bestow the gift. ⟦s4⟧ Farewell.

## CHAP. I. *That from the consideration of our own misery and of the divine mercy we ought to be stirred up to pray to God.*

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ With what zeal and with what affection God is to be prayed to by us, we can consider from our own misery and at the same time from his mercy. ⟦s2⟧ For what would more effectually rouse a man to the zeal of prayer than the misery and calamity of so great evils, to which he is bound over and by which he is pressed? ⟦s3⟧ And what would more pleasantly draw him to the practice of prayer than the mercy of his Maker, which amid his evils he so often experiences? ⟦s4⟧ Lest, then, the human mind be sluggish in praying, on this side it is compelled by its own need; and lest it pray tremblingly and mistrustfully, on that side it is called forth by the loving-kindness of God. ⟦s5⟧ By these two wings — namely, the misery of man and the mercy [0977C] of the Redeemer — prayer is lifted up; for while the mind by the alternate consideration of these unceasingly rouses itself to devotion, it is borne aloft by a certain impulse of spiritual desire, and flies. ⟦s6⟧ So necessary, then, is holy meditation to prayer, that prayer cannot be perfect at all if meditation does not accompany or precede it. ⟦s7⟧ For those also who neglect to consider their own evils are easily deceived through ignorance, either by asking nothing or by asking otherwise than they ought; or else, by asking less worthily than they ought, they grow lukewarm through sloth. ⟦s8⟧ First, therefore, it is necessary, if we wish to pray to the Lord prudently and profitably, that we exercise our mind with unbroken meditation, and in the consideration of our misery learn what it is necessary [0977D] for us to ask; but in the consideration of the mercy of our God, with what desire we ought to ask. ⟦s9⟧ Let us consider how brief our life is, how slippery the way, how uncertain death. ⟦s10⟧ Let us consider that we entered this life weeping, that we pass through it with sorrow, and that we are to go out of it with mourning. ⟦s11⟧ Let us consider with how great bitternesses it is mingled, if anything even sweet or pleasant along the way of this life sports with us as it meets us; [0978A] how deceitful, how suspect, how unstable, how transitory is whatever the love of this world brings forth, whatever temporal show and beauty promises. ⟦s12⟧ Some evils there are with which we are born, and from which, in the course of time, we are set free, whether by the hidden grace of God or by manifest diligence. ⟦s13⟧ But others come with us into this life, and do not depart from us before death. ⟦s14⟧ There are likewise other evils which grow upon us in time, and again in the passing of time go away. ⟦s15⟧ And others we contract after our birth, and do not rid ourselves of before death. ⟦s16⟧ And who could enumerate all the evils of this life? ⟦s17⟧ For, that we may pass over those evils which oppress all men in common, of what sort do we reckon the secret perils of individuals to be, of which each man is conscious to himself? ⟦s18⟧ If anyone [0978B] should be willing to consider what evils he has done from his earliest years, and what he has endured, and should set before his eyes the times past and the days of his life, and should diligently consider how many empty labors he has borne, how often for the love of this life he has sweated in vain, how often in toilsome attempts he has found deceptive outcomes, and after long runnings to and fro has not yet attained any rest at all — he will know what he can judge concerning this life. ⟦s19⟧ But when he considers the hardship of this life, let him attend also to what the pleasantness and sweetness of the heavenly country is, and let him weigh what he has found, what he has lost, where he lies, whence he has fallen, so that from both he may understand how much he must mourn in this exile. ⟦s20⟧ For hence Solomon says: *He that addeth knowledge, addeth also sorrow [n: (Eccle. I)]*, because the more a man understands his own evils, the more deeply [0978C] he sighs and groans. ⟦s21⟧ Lastly, it remains that in the remembrance of our evils we call to mind also the mercy of God, because we seek his compassions for the future with the more confidence if we consider how he has had mercy upon us in the past. ⟦s22⟧ Let us call back to memory the good things which he has bestowed, and that he has often mercifully snatched us out when we were set in perils; and that he could never be overcome by our sins so as to withhold his mercy; that he admonished of himself those who had forgotten him, called back those who had turned away from him, kindly received those who came to him, pardoned the penitent, guarded the persevering, held up those who stood, raised the fallen, changed evil delights into bitternesses, yet again bestowed his consolations upon those thus wholesomely embittered. ⟦s23⟧ And lastly he restores rest and perfect peace to those purged by tribulation — he who was never wanting to sinners, [0978D] to correct them, nor to the just, to guard them. ⟦s24⟧ Whoever has exercised his mind with meditations of this sort before the time of prayer comes to prayer neither, perhaps, unprovided nor lukewarm. ⟦s25⟧ For assiduous meditation begets knowledge; and knowledge, once begotten, drives out ignorance and begets compunction; and compunction, once begotten, puts sloth to flight and [0979A] begets devotion; and devotion perfects prayer. ⟦s26⟧ It is knowledge, when a man is enlightened unto the recognition of himself. ⟦s27⟧ It is compunction, when from the consideration of his own evils the heart is touched with inward grief. ⟦s28⟧ Devotion is a loving and humble affection toward God, which is generated out of compunction. ⟦s29⟧ For the mind, terrified by the greatness of its own evils, and mistrusting its own strength, turns itself to God. ⟦s30⟧ And it seeks his protection the more ardently, the more plainly it sees that nothing has been left to it outside God in which it might trust. ⟦s31⟧ Devotion, therefore, is a turning to God with a loving and humble affection. ⟦s32⟧ For it is humble from the consciousness of its own weakness; it is loving from the consideration of the divine clemency. ⟦s33⟧ This has within itself the three principal virtues: faith, hope, charity. ⟦s34⟧ For a man would not turn himself to God by devotion [0979B] unless he believed that he could be saved by him, and hoped that he was willing to show mercy. ⟦s35⟧ Lastly, unless he loved that mercy more than his own evils, he would by no means, fleeing them, choose a refuge for himself in him. ⟦s36⟧ He believes, then, and hopes, and loves. ⟦s37⟧ He believes in his power, hopes in his mercy, loves his keeping. ⟦s38⟧ Prayer, therefore, is nothing else than devotion of the mind, that is, a turning to God through a loving and humble affection, supported by faith, hope, and charity. ⟦s39⟧ But because the mind is kindled through devotion in many ways, and again because that same devotion of the mind is uttered by the voice in various ways, we ought to distinguish severally certain kinds of prayer, that we may be able more plainly to point out its power.
