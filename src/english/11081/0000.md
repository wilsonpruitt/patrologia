## On the Sacraments of the Natural Law and of the Written Law

[0017C]D. What was there before the world was made? M. God alone. D. How long was he before? M. From eternity. D. Where was he, when there was nothing besides himself? M. Where he now is, there he was then also. D. Where is he now? M. He is in himself, and all things are in him, and he himself is in all things. D. When did God make the world? M. *In the beginning [n: (Gen. I)]*. D. Where was the world made? M. In God. D. Out of what was the world made? M. Out of nothing. D. How was the world made. M. First, in matter, all things were made together; but afterwards, in this form in which the world is now beheld, all things were composed and set in order through six days. D. How could matter be before form? M. That matter was not [0017D] altogether without form; yet it is said to have been unformed at first, because in the first condition of itself it did not yet have so seemly and so fitting a form as it now has. D. Why did God will first [0018C] to create unshaped matter — as though he could not have created it together with its form? M. He willed first to show what a creature was of itself, and what it had afterwards received from the Creator's kindness; and therefore he first created the essence, and afterwards put the form into it. D. To whom was he showing this, when as yet there was no one who could see it? M. There were already then angels to whom this might be shown, that they might see outwardly in the things founded what they should inwardly recognize in themselves; lest they too should think that the glory they had received was to be ascribed to themselves, and not to their founder. To us also this is shown; for we, although we did not see it when it was done, have nevertheless heard that it was done, on the report of sacred utterance. D. Where, or of what sort, was that first matter before [0018D] it was formed? M. It lay confused in the very place where it now subsists in order. For the earth, set in the same place in which it now is, that is, in the middle place, was enwrapped on every side by the three remaining [0019A] elements, that is, by water and air and fire, still confused together in one murk; which confusion, of course, encircling the earth round about, occupied upward as much space as the whole body of creatures now embraces. Even then the earth had a form of some kind, more excellent than the rest, but, wrapped and overlaid, what it was could not yet appear. The remaining three, as has been said, were in every way confused and undigested, hanging in the manner of a kind of cloud in that space which now air and heaven hold. This shadowy murk Scripture named darkness: which in the beginning, spread out above by the gathering of the waters, hung over the face of the deep (which was not yet filled).

D. What formation was made on the first day? M. Light was made [0019B] on the first day [n: (Gen. I)]: which indeed had already before been created materially among the rest; but now for the first time, that it might be able to shine and to give light, it was separated and marked off from the others. D. Of what sort is that light to be reckoned to have been? M. There are those who assert that it was a luminous cloud, which (as the sun does now) was carried round encircling the earth; and while, rising, it lit up the upper parts of the world, it made day, but descending at its setting to the lower regions, by the absence of its light it made night. From this they will have those first three days at the outset of creatures — before the sun and the other luminaries were founded — to have been marked off and named. D. Where must that light be said now to be? M. They say that it is now dispersed round about in the upper parts of the aether, and could still be seen if the greater brightness of the sun did not [0019C] hide it; but that at night, out of that light, there are still shown to those who consider carefully, as it were, certain luminous remnants of a most thin cloud scattered against the firmament, which the greater brightness of the day wholly conceals from the eyes of beholders.

D. What formation was made on the second day? M. The firmament was made on the second day [n: (ibid.)], which indeed had itself also been created materially before; but until now it remained undistinguished and mingled in that universal confusion. But now, that is, on the second day, drawn out of the mingling and reduced to form, it was set to divide between the upper waters and the lower waters; namely in such wise that, coming into the middle of that confusion which hung in the manner of a cloud about the earth, it should in a certain way [0019D] cut through it, and, embracing it round about with its own circuit, should shut one part beneath itself and separate the other part outside — that is, that by those very waters which in the division remained outside or above, the firmament, set in the middle between the waters and the waters, should be wholly encompassed round about, while the waters which were left within or below should all be shut in on every side by that same firmament. Part of the waters, therefore, remained in the division above the firmament; the other part, enclosed beneath the circuit of the firmament, took possession of the whole space of the air, filling it round about. D. Is it to be believed that there are still waters above the firmament? M. So it is thought; because Holy Scripture also in divers places plainly confirms this, as is that testimony of the Psalmist: [0020A] *Let the waters that are above the heavens praise the name of the Lord [n: (Psal. CXLVIII)]*.

D. How can waters stand above heaven? M. In icy fashion, or (what is the more credible) after the likeness of that first chaos they are firmly suspended there, hanging in the manner of a light cloud, such as the nature of all waters is thought to have been in the beginning.

D. What was the formation of the third day? M. Those waters which had remained hanging beneath the firmament were compacted together; and pressed down at length by their weight they were gathered *into one place [n: (Gen. I)]*, that is, into that great deep which before they had covered over: whence even now they are drawn off to all parts of the earth through their [sic: *thracones*] and their channels. But when the waters had been gathered, [0020B] the *dry land [n: (ibid.)]* began to appear, that is, the earth, which before lay hidden, wrapped in that murk and confusion; and God clothed it, as it appeared, with its own shoots: and so the work of the third day was completed.

D. What formation was made on the fourth day? M. The luminaries were founded in heaven, that is, the sun and the moon and the stars; that they might shine over the earth, and give it light, and by their course mark off the times [n: (ibid.)].

D. What formation was made on the fifth day? M. The birds and the fishes were made out of the waters. The birds were then placed in the air; the fishes remained in their original seat [n: (ibid.)].

D. What formation was made on the sixth day? M. The beasts and the other living things which live upon the earth were created out of [0020C] the earth. But when all things had been consummated and prepared, last of all (yet on that same sixth day) man was made, who should have dominion over every creature. D. Why were the birds, which were placed in the air, not made out of the air itself, as the fishes out of the water, and the beasts likewise and man out of the earth? M. Because the air has not such bodily substance that a living thing could be made of it as it can of the earth. D. Why was man made last of all? M. Because man was to be set over every creature, it was fitting that his dwelling should first be prepared, and that afterwards he himself, when all things had been ordered, should be brought into the round world as its possessor and ruler. These, therefore, are the works of the founding which God made and completed in six days: creating before every day, [0020D] disposing and ordering in the first three days, setting out in order in the three following: on the first discerning, on the second distinguishing, on the third disposing, on the fourth adorning the heavens, on the fifth the air and the water, on the sixth the earth. D. Why did God make his works in six days, and rest on the seventh? M. The number six, because it consists of its own parts, is a sign of perfection; and therefore God willed to complete his works in six days, that he might show that in the things he had made he had neither set down anything superfluous nor left anything imperfect. But when the six days had been completed, on the seventh day at last he rested [n: (Gen. II)], that is, he ceased from work; not that he himself, as though wearied, had need of rest, but that he might promise us a rest to come, if we should study to do good and perfect works according to our own manner and measure. D. How [0021A] is God said to have rested on the seventh day, when he himself says in the Gospel: *My Father worketh until now, and I work? [n: (Joan. V.)]* M. God is said to work in many ways. For there is the work of creation, and there is also the work of disposition, there is the work of propagation, and there is the work of governance. The work of creation he made before every day; the work of disposition he completed in six days; the work of propagation he does not forsake until the end of the age; from the work of governance he will not cease unto eternity. D. If God first rested on the seventh day, what then did he do from the beginning of the first day, after he had made the light, until the rising of the day following, when he was making the firmament? M. If you will look at the matter more closely, you will find that God by no means rested on the first day, after the beginning of light, from every work whatsoever; [0021B] because not only did this belong to his work, that the light was made, but also that it was directed, until, the compass of its circuit being run through, it consummated its course. First therefore, that he might begin his work from what was perfect, he made the light rising; and thence directing it, he completed first the evening, then the morning, that is, the first day. D. Is it to be understood so of the works of the remaining days? M. In these matters I would rashly affirm nothing; because, although the like might be said of the work of the second day — that he first made the firmament between the waters and the waters, and then directed its course until the rising of the third day — yet, since we have not yet found any certain experience concerning the motion of the firmament, let us, if you please, loose the knot of this question in another way. For it can not unfittingly be said [0021C] that the Lord did not so continue the succession of his work in those six days as though he had left off the work for no interval of time in the meanwhile; but that, since he passed over no day in working, on the seventh day he then at last rested from all work in such wise that he neither thereafter created other things beside those which had already been made, nor disposed them otherwise than they had already been disposed (so far as pertains to the order of the universe). For neither did he afterwards add anything to the number of the universe, nor did he change the order of the [sic: *unversitatis*] in anything. D. And what of this, that new souls are still created daily. M. Yet nothing new is said to be adjudged; because those souls themselves, although created out of nothing and only now first beginning to be, were nevertheless in a certain way created even then in their likeness. [0021D] And that I may briefly discharge for you what seems to me to be said thereon: all things were then made, so that nothing should afterwards come to be which had not before been created either in matter or in likeness.
