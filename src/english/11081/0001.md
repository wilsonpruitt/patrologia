## On the Sacraments of the Natural Law and of the Written Law (cont.)

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ D. I see still further in these works matters worthy of inquiry, which indeed, though unwilling, I am compelled to pass over; lest perchance, if I detain you longer than is right upon such things, I fail to reach the other matters that remain, which are more necessary. ⟦s2⟧ Now therefore, if you please, let us return to man, that you may open to me — for I greatly desire it — the cause of his creation. ⟦s3⟧ For it is assuredly fitting that we, who already believe that all things were made for his sake, should also not be ignorant why he himself was made. ⟦s4⟧ M. God, who is the highest and true and perfect good, was from eternity so blessed in himself and through himself that his glory and beatitude, because it was [0022A] eternal and unchangeable, could not be diminished, and, because it had been full and perfect, could not be increased. ⟦s5⟧ Needing nothing, therefore, but willing to share with others the good which he himself was and by which he was blessed, and to make others blessed in it and through it, by no necessity but by charity alone he created the rational creature, that is, rational spirits; and he commanded that they should in part persist in their own purity, and in part, clothed with flesh, should quicken the human body to life and to sense. ⟦s6⟧ To those spirits, therefore, which were to be joined to bodies, he appointed a place and a habitation on earth; but for those which were to remain in their own purity he set a dwelling in heaven. ⟦s7⟧ The one sort indeed he set on high by favor; the other he disposed below by dispensation, that both he might one day, through obedience, [0022B] carry these up from the lowest to the highest, and might, through obedience, confirm those in the highest. ⟦s8⟧ D. Why did God will to join spirits to bodies, since they would have been of greater excellence persisting in their own purity? ⟦s9⟧ M. He willed to show in them by creation what he was afterwards to do in them by favor. ⟦s10⟧ For since among created things nothing is more excellent than spirit, nothing lower and more corruptible than earth, when to a body made of earth and having corruptible matter he granted a rational spirit, that is, a rational soul, he joined in a certain way the highest things to the lowest in one fellowship and society; showing that what the body was to the spirit, this in a certain way the spirit was to itself. ⟦s11⟧ And just as one condition of creation had joined the excellence of the spirit to the weakness of bodily nature, [0022C] so one favor of loving-kindness was to join the lowliness of the spiritual creature to the excellence of the Creator. ⟦s12⟧ He made, therefore, as has been said, the body of man out of the slime of the earth, and breathed into it a rational soul which he created out of nothing [n: (Gen. II)], that living obediently in the body it might one day be raised, together with the body, to the fellowship of those spirits which lived without a body, that is, of the angels; and that he might lift both alike to a share in his own glory, so as to glorify both the spirit in himself and the body in the spirit; and that, as far as he had before inclined the highest things by dispensation when he founded them, so far he might now exalt the lowest by favor. ⟦s13⟧ D. Why did God not create men all at once, as he had created the angels? ⟦s14⟧ M. Because he willed that there should be one beginning of the human race. ⟦s15⟧ D. Why [sic: *Qnare*] did he will this? ⟦s16⟧ M. For [0022D] the beating back of the pride of the devil. ⟦s17⟧ For since the devil proudly desired to be another beginning apart from God, in order that his haughtiness might be the more confounded, man received this, so that he should have therein also the dignity of the likeness of God: that as God stood forth as the beginning of creation to all things, so man should be the beginning of generation to all men; and that all men, while they knew themselves to be from one and to be one, might all love one another as though they were one. ⟦s18⟧ D. Why was woman made? ⟦s19⟧ M. For a help unto generation. ⟦s20⟧ D. Why was she made out of the man? ⟦s21⟧ M. Because if she were made from elsewhere, there would not be one beginning. ⟦s22⟧ D. Why was she made out of his side? ⟦s23⟧ M. That it might be shown that she was created into a fellowship of love; lest, if she had been made out of the head, she should seem to be preferred to the man for dominion, [0023A] or, if out of the feet, to be subjected to him for servitude. ⟦s24⟧ Since, therefore, she was being prepared for the man neither as mistress nor as handmaid, but as companion, she had to be brought forth neither out of the head nor out of the feet, but out of the side, that he might acknowledge that she was to be set beside him, whom he had learned to have been taken from beside him: *God therefore sent a deep sleep upon Adam, and took one of his ribs [n: (Gen. II)]*, and from it fashioned the woman. ⟦s25⟧ D. Why did he will to take away the rib from him sleeping rather than waking? ⟦s26⟧ M. Lest he should be thought to have drawn it from him by way of penalty. ⟦s27⟧ D. How could the woman be made out of a rib alone? ⟦s28⟧ Did God add anything else to that rib from without for its increase, or create together with it something new, or did the rib itself begin to be multiplied in itself? ⟦s29⟧ M. If, for the completing of the woman's body, God added to that rib an increase from without (since [0023B] that which was added was much greater than the rib itself to which it was added), then Scripture ought rather to have said that the woman was made out of that from which she received the greater number of the parts of her substance. ⟦s30⟧ Likewise, if that which came to the rib for its increase is believed to have been made out of nothing, why do we say that she was made out of the man, and not rather out of nothing, since that part of her which was made out of nothing is much greater than that which passed over from the substance of the man into her body? ⟦s31⟧ It remains, therefore, that we believe that that rib was multiplied into itself and received no increase from without. ⟦s32⟧ D. How can it come about that any thing grows to which nothing is added from elsewhere? ⟦s33⟧ M. It is more to make something out of nothing than to multiply a small substance into itself.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0023C]D. It is certain: wherefore go on with what remains. ⟦s2⟧ M. Having made them, then, outside paradise, he placed them in paradise, forming them by the precepts of life and of discipline. ⟦s3⟧ D. Why was man, who was to have his dwelling in paradise, made outside paradise? ⟦s4⟧ M. That he might not impute the benefit of God to nature, but to grace. ⟦s5⟧ D. By what precepts did God form man, when he set him in paradise? ⟦s6⟧ M. By two, that is, by the precept of nature and the precept of discipline. ⟦s7⟧ For God prepares two goods for man: one temporal and the other eternal, each of them full and perfect. ⟦s8⟧ The one, that is, the temporal good, he set full within him; and the other, that is, the eternal, he set full before him. ⟦s9⟧ To that which he had given he applied as a guard the precept of nature; to that which he had set before him the precept of discipline opened [0023D] the way. ⟦s10⟧ The precept of nature contains precept, prohibition, and concession. ⟦s10⟧ The natural precept was the discernment by which it was breathed into him what things were necessary for his nature; the natural prohibition was the discernment by which it was naturally implanted in him what things were harmful; the concession was the discernment of those things which were indifferent, that is, which he might use or not use without disadvantage. ⟦s11⟧ If therefore man, instructed by the natural precept, had guarded against negligence, the divine providence would have permitted him to be oppressed by no violence. ⟦s12⟧ For man was able, through the precept of nature, to keep the good which he had; but through it he could not obtain the good which he did not yet have. ⟦s13⟧ For it could be no merit to a man if he either avoided [0024A] harmful things or sought wholesome ones, or used indifferent things toward either alternative; since in all these he would be regarding not so much the affection of the one commanding as the advantage of the one obeying. ⟦s14⟧ And so, that man might be able to merit something, it was necessary that the precept of discipline should be added; but this could be taken neither from the precept nor from the prohibition, lest, if God commanded or forbade the same things, there should be, as has been said, no merit for the one obeying; or, if he changed the commandment, he should become contrary to himself. ⟦s15⟧ It behooved, therefore, that it should be taken from the concession, yet in such wise that by that very precept the concession should not be changed — that is, that the precept should not corrupt nature, but that disobedience, not nature, should injure the transgressor. ⟦s16⟧ But since the precept of discipline had to be formed out of the natural concession, it was the more fitting [0024B] that this should be done according to prohibition, lest the devil should be able to bring a charge against the obedience; and in one thing only, not in many, lest the disobedience should be able to be excused by man. ⟦s17⟧ If therefore man had stood fast in this obedience, after the time appointed by God he ought to have been carried over, without the pain of death, to that highest good which had been prepared for him in the heavens, there to live eternally with all his offspring in the fellowship of the blessed angels. ⟦s18⟧ D. Why did God will that there should be need in man? ⟦s19⟧ M. That from it he might take occasion of exercise; and that that outward need might show him his inward need. ⟦s20⟧ D. Before sin, was man mortal or immortal? ⟦s21⟧ M. Both by power, neither by necessity; and afterwards to be both [0024C] by necessity, neither by power: the one first through fault, the other thereafter through grace.
