## On the Sacraments of the Natural Law and of the Written Law (cont.)

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ D. Where is paradise? ⟦s2⟧ M. Why do you ask what you see? ⟦s3⟧ Toward the east is your entrance; this is the tree of life which you behold. ⟦s4⟧ D. Why was it called the tree of life? ⟦s5⟧ M. Because such is the power in it that its fruit can defend the life of man from death and from the trouble of every infirmity. ⟦s6⟧ D. What is the tree of the knowledge of good and evil? ⟦s7⟧ M. That in which Adam transgressed. ⟦s8⟧ D. Why was it so called? ⟦s9⟧ M. Adam, before he sinned, knew good and evil: good indeed both by knowledge and by experience, but evil by knowledge alone. ⟦s10⟧ But after he touched the forbidden tree, he began to know evil then by experience also; yet it was not the nature of the tree but the disobedience of the man that conferred the knowledge, [0024D] since through the experience of evil he came to know also how strictly the good had had to be held fast. ⟦s11⟧ And so this name is to be believed to have been given to the tree not from the nature which it had, but from the occasion which it afforded. ⟦s12⟧ D. How did man transgress? ⟦s13⟧ M. The devil, seeing that man was ascending by obedience to the place whence he himself had fallen by pride, envied him; and because he could not harm him by violence, he turned himself to deceit. ⟦s14⟧ But lest his deceit, if it were too much hidden, could not be guarded against at all, he was not permitted to tempt through any other animal save through the serpent, that the natural cunning of the serpent might betray the malice of the tempter; that through the covering which was outside, man might know how greatly he ought to have guarded against the [0025A] craft of him who was ruling within. ⟦s15⟧ D. How was man tempted? ⟦s16⟧ M. By outward temptation only. ⟦s17⟧ D. What is outward temptation? ⟦s18⟧ M. The persuasion of someone, made either by sign or by word, that he to whom it is made may be inclined to consent unto sin. ⟦s19⟧ D. What is inward temptation? ⟦s20⟧ M. The motion of the first delight, by which the mind is driven to sin. ⟦s21⟧ And this is the more difficultly overcome, because, assailing us from within, it is strengthened against us out of what is our own. ⟦s22⟧ Therefore the devil did not merit pardon, because he sinned by no temptation; but man, who fell when struck by an outward temptation, was [sic: *crat*] so much the more grievously to be punished, by how much the lighter the impulse by which he had been laid low. ⟦s23⟧ And yet, because in falling he felt some violence, so to speak, therefore at length the grace of God raised him up unto pardon. ⟦s24⟧ But we, the more grievous the violence of temptation we suffer [0025B] in sinning, by the lighter expiation are we renewed unto the grace we had lost. ⟦s25⟧ D. In what order was man tempted? ⟦s26⟧ M. The devil, because he saw that the woman, as being weaker and less strong in reason, could the more easily be circumvented by fraud, first approached her with a question, wishing to draw out her mind, that from her answer he might gather how he ought thereafter to address her. ⟦s27⟧ D. Why did the woman not shudder at the serpent speaking to her? ⟦s28⟧ M. Because she believed that, being newly created, it had received the office of speaking from God. ⟦s29⟧ D. How did he address her? ⟦s30⟧ M. *Eat: and you shall be as gods, knowing good and evil [n: (Gen. III)]*. ⟦s31⟧ He tempted by three things: by gluttony, by vainglory, by avarice. ⟦s32⟧ Now gluttony is the immoderate appetite of eating; vainglory, the love of one's own praise; avarice, [0025C] the immoderate appetite of having. ⟦s33⟧ By gluttony he tempted when he said: *Eat.* ⟦s34⟧ By vainglory, when he said: *You shall be as gods.* ⟦s35⟧ By avarice, when he said: *knowing good and evil.* ⟦s36⟧ D. I would that you should unfold the very root of this sin: and as you have shown me in what order it was committed, so you should also show what it is. ⟦s37⟧ M. In the first man God had set two things, namely the appetite for the just and the appetite for the advantageous; the one, that is, the appetite for the just, according to will; the other, that is, the appetite for the advantageous, according to necessity. ⟦s38⟧ And he appointed that the former should be voluntary for this reason, that in it man might earn merit, either by keeping it well when he could forsake it, or by forsaking it ill when he could keep it. ⟦s39⟧ But the latter he willed to be necessary for this reason, that in it man might be recompensed, [0025D] either unto punishment if he forsook the former, or unto glory if he kept the former. ⟦s40⟧ And therefore to the one he inseparably joined its own effect, that is, justice; but to the other he set forth its own effect, that is, advantage, as separable; so that man, by losing the one alone, should lose the affection of both the one and the other; or, by keeping the one alone, should keep the effect of both the one and the other. ⟦s41⟧ But man did only that which he could; and because he forsook the appetite for the just of his own accord, therefore he justly lost both justice and advantage; keeping only the appetite for the advantageous, unto the increase of his unhappiness; because, just as it would have been an increase of glory for one persevering in justice not to be able to grow lukewarm from the love of the good he possessed, so now it is a heap of misery not to be able to restrain himself from the appetite for the good (although he cannot [0026A] obtain it). ⟦s42⟧ D. Did man forsake the appetite for justice willing or unwilling? ⟦s43⟧ M. If he forsook it unwilling, it is rather to be called violence than fault; but if the will is said to have gone before in him, then that very forsaking of justice is denied to have been the first sin; nor will it be possible to find whence such a will came to him, that he should will to forsake the good; because he could neither will anything of himself, nor did he receive from God the power to will anything beside the advantageous and the just. ⟦s44⟧ D. Yet, since he forsook it, he must be believed to have willed what he did. ⟦s45⟧ M. You would say this rightly if to cease from something were to do something. ⟦s46⟧ But since he did not begin to will what he formerly did not will, but only ceased to will what he formerly willed, it is not necessary that he be said to have done this either willing or unwilling. ⟦s47⟧ Yet he willed something, by willing which, because with that [0026B] he could not will, he ceased to will this which he had willed; nor did he will the one because he ceased to will; but rather he ceased to will this because he willed that. ⟦s48⟧ D. Why then is he rather said to have sinned in that he ceased to will this, than in that he began to will that? ⟦s49⟧ M. Because if he did not cease to will this, then, willing either that or anything else, he could never have sinned. ⟦s50⟧ D. What then is justice? ⟦s51⟧ M. Measure in the appetite for the advantageous. ⟦s52⟧ D. What is the appetite for justice? ⟦s53⟧ M. The love of measure. ⟦s54⟧ D. What then did he will, that he should cease to will this? ⟦s55⟧ M. He willed something beyond measure. ⟦s56⟧ D. Was not this willing too a sin? ⟦s57⟧ M. It was not a sin that he willed this, but that in willing this he lost the measure of willing, that is, the appetite for justice. ⟦s58⟧ D. What did he will beyond measure? ⟦s59⟧ M. He desired the likeness of God. ⟦s60⟧ And so man sinned in [0026C] this, that he forsook the appetite for the just, and he was punished in the appetite for the advantageous which he kept. ⟦s61⟧ D. How? ⟦s62⟧ M. He was punished doubly in the appetite for the advantageous: both when he is restrained from those things which he desires in an ordered way, and because he is let loose to desire disordered advantages. ⟦s63⟧ In the one is punishment; in the other is punishment and fault. ⟦s64⟧ D. Whence does it come to man that he desires disordered things? ⟦s65⟧ M. From the punishment of sin. ⟦s66⟧ D. I ask the manner. ⟦s67⟧ M. God had made the first body of man sound and whole. ⟦s68⟧ Whole indeed, because nothing was lacking to it for perfection; sound, because nothing was in it for corruption. ⟦s69⟧ But in both he had set such a condition that neither wholeness nor soundness could be preserved without support. ⟦s70⟧ And so among created things he ordered certain things outwardly which could avail to preserve and safeguard its soundness or wholeness; [0026D] and the care and office of providing these he laid upon the spirit which presided over the body. ⟦s71⟧ But lest, in carrying that out, he should be pressed by a servile condition if he fulfilled it from mere command and not also from love, God gave him an affection whereby he should love his own body, that as he loved its wholeness and soundness, so also he should willingly provide all things which availed to preserve it. ⟦s72⟧ Again, lest either there should be labor in seeking those things, or peril in taking them, he tempered by a fixed measure both the need and the carnal appetite; so that few things could suffice him for sustenance, and moderate things for satiety. ⟦s73⟧ And so the prudence of the flesh would have been labor as well as delight and exercise, if man [0027A] had kept justice. ⟦s74⟧ But because the spirit, lifted up above, would not restrain its appetite within measure, therefore it deserved that now, scattered below through endless labors, it should not be able to keep measure in the appetite of its own flesh. ⟦s75⟧ For God broke by mortality the wholeness of the human body, and on this account it now needs more sustenance than before, both to repair its soundness so far as it has perished, and to preserve it so far as it has remained. ⟦s76⟧ Hence is begotten that hard necessity whereby nature extends its appetite beyond order; so that it may not only guard nature, but also drive out the vice of nature. ⟦s77⟧ For as it is naturally given to the spirit to love the advantage of its own flesh, so also it naturally has this, that it cannot love its disadvantage. ⟦s78⟧ Yet it must be known that this necessity of concupiscence [0027B] is not therefore no fault because it is a necessity; because, that this necessity should be, not necessity but will was the cause. ⟦s79⟧ This, however, can be truly said of all things: that the soul desires nothing, however disordered it may seem, save on account of some advantage, either of preserving a good or of removing an evil; as, for example, when it desires intercourse, it longs to quench the itching of the flesh; when it desires food or drink, the burning of gluttony. ⟦s80⟧ D. If every appetite desires what is advantageous, and if to desire one's own advantage, both in preserving a good and in removing an evil, is given by nature, why is this concupiscence reckoned to man for a fault? ⟦s81⟧ M. He does not sin because he desires his own advantage, but because he desires it in a disordered way. ⟦s82⟧ D. Is he not able to disorder, not to desire? ⟦s83⟧ M. He is not able. ⟦s84⟧ D. [0027C] Why is he not able? ⟦s85⟧ M. Because of his weakness. ⟦s86⟧ D. Why has he been weakened? ⟦s87⟧ M. Because God broke the wholeness of nature by the passibility of mortality. ⟦s88⟧ D. Why did he do this? ⟦s89⟧ M. I see where you are tending. ⟦s90⟧ D. What, nevertheless, was the cause? ⟦s91⟧ M. The fault of man. ⟦s92⟧ D. Therefore through his own fault he came to this necessity. ⟦s93⟧ M. It is so.
