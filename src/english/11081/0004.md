## On the Sacraments of the Natural Law and of the Written Law (cont.)

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ D. Will the judge, then, give counsel to the accused? ⟦s2⟧ M. He will lay aside [0028D] for the time being the person of the judge, and will take up the person of the counsellor. ⟦s3⟧ D. When will he come? ⟦s4⟧ M. In the last times. ⟦s5⟧ D. Why does he defer his coming so long? ⟦s6⟧ M. That he may first permit man to make trial of his own failing. ⟦s7⟧ First will come the patriarchs and the prophets and the lawgivers; there will come the just and the wise; they will seek and will not find; they will labor and will fail; many will promise, and none will show. ⟦s8⟧ But when all have failed, and, after many promises and many words brought forth, have fallen silent, then he himself will bring forth one word and will give counsel. ⟦s9⟧ D. What counsel will he give? ⟦s10⟧ M. Am I privy to the secrets of God? ⟦s11⟧ This counsel is secret and hidden from the ages. ⟦s12⟧ D. Yet in the last times it is made manifest. [0029A] Besides, if we wish to belong to the lot of the redemption, it is just and seemly that we be not ignorant of the manner of that redemption of ours. ⟦s13⟧ M. This counsel lay in this: how man could justly be either snatched from the power of the devil or reconciled to God. ⟦s14⟧ D. I greatly desire to know how this could be done. ⟦s15⟧ M. Consider first the case of man; and then you will easily be able to see by what justice he has been set free. ⟦s16⟧ D. And I long to have that very thing shown me. ⟦s17⟧ M. The case of man stands against two, that is, against God and against the devil; wherefore let it not irk you if I set forth this same case as it were under the shaping of three persons. ⟦s18⟧ D. You have set me wholly in expectation. ⟦s19⟧ M. Three come into the case: man, God, and the devil. ⟦s20⟧ The devil is convicted of having done injury to God; because [0029B] he both fraudulently led away his servant, that is, man, and violently held him. ⟦s21⟧ Man is convicted of having done injury to God; because he both despised his precept, and, setting himself under another's hand, brought upon him the loss of his own servitude. ⟦s22⟧ Likewise the devil is convicted of having done injury to man; because he both first deceived him by promising good things, and afterwards harmed him by inflicting evils. ⟦s23⟧ Unjustly, therefore, the devil holds man, but justly is man held; because the devil never deserved to oppress man as subject to him; but man deserved through his own fault that he should be permitted to be oppressed by him. ⟦s24⟧ For although he did not know that what the devil promised was false, he was nevertheless not ignorant that he ought not to have desired it (even if it were true) against the will of the Creator. ⟦s25⟧ Justly, therefore, is man subjected to the devil [0029C] so far as pertains to his own fault; but unjustly so far as pertains from the deceitfulness of the devil. ⟦s26⟧ If therefore man had such an advocate that the devil could be brought into court against him, man would justly gainsay his lordship; because the devil had no just cause why he should claim a right over man for himself. ⟦s27⟧ But no such advocate could be found save God. ⟦s28⟧ But God was unwilling to take up the case of man; because he was still angry with man for his fault. ⟦s29⟧ It behooved, therefore, that man should first appease God, and so thereafter, with God as his patron, enter with confidence into the case with the devil. ⟦s30⟧ But he could not reasonably appease God unless he restored the loss which he had brought upon him, and made satisfaction for the contempt. ⟦s31⟧ But man had nothing wherewith to make worthy recompense to God for the loss inflicted, [0029D] because if he rendered anything of the irrational creation, it would be less than the rational creature taken away. ⟦s32⟧ But neither could he render a man for a man; because he had taken away one just and innocent, and found none but a sinner. ⟦s33⟧ Nothing, therefore, did man find whereby he could appease God toward himself; because whether he gave what was his own or himself, it would not be a worthy recompense. ⟦s34⟧ God therefore, seeing that man could not by his own power escape the yoke of just damnation, had mercy on him; and first went before him freely by mercy alone, that thereafter he might set him free by justice: that is, because man had not of himself the justice of escaping, God gave man justice through mercy. ⟦s35⟧ For neither would the rescue of man be perfectly reasonable, unless it were done justly on both [0030A] sides — that is, that as God had justice in requiring man, so man too should have justice in escaping. ⟦s36⟧ But man could never have had this justice, unless God had bestowed it upon him through mercy. ⟦s37⟧ In order, therefore, that God might be appeased by man, God gave freely to man what man should render to God out of debt. ⟦s38⟧ He gave, therefore, to man a man, whom man should render to God for man; who (that a worthy recompense might be made) should be not only equal to the former, but even greater. ⟦s39⟧ That, therefore, a man greater than man might be rendered for man, God was made man for man, and man gave himself to man that he might receive himself from man. ⟦s40⟧ God the Son of God was incarnate, and to men was given the God-man Christ, as Isaiah says: *A child is born to us [n: (Isa. II)]*, and so forth. ⟦s41⟧ That, therefore, Christ was given [0030B] to man, was the mercy of God; that Christ was rendered by man, was the justice of man. ⟦s42⟧ For in the nativity of Christ God was justly appeased toward man; because such a man was found for man as should be not only, as has been said, equal to man, but even greater. ⟦s43⟧ Therefore in the nativity of Christ the angels announce peace purely [cj: *munde*; read *mundo*, "to the world"], saying: *Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men of good will [n: (Luc. II)]*. ⟦s44⟧ But it still remained for man that, as by restoring the loss he had appeased the wrath, so also by making satisfaction for the contempt he should become worthy to escape the punishment. ⟦s45⟧ But this could not more fittingly be done than that he should willingly and obediently take up the punishment which he did not owe, that he might become worthy to be snatched from the punishment which he had deserved through disobedience. ⟦s46⟧ But this punishment sinful man could not [0030C] pay; for whatever punishment he took up, he would bear it only worthily and justly (on account of the guilt of the first contempt). ⟦s47⟧ That, therefore, man might justly escape the punishment he owed, it was necessary that such a man should take up the punishment for man as had owed no punishment. ⟦s48⟧ But no such man could be found save Christ. ⟦s49⟧ Christ, therefore, both by being born paid man's debt to the Father, and by dying expiated man's guilt; so that, when he himself bore for man the death which he did not owe, man might justly escape for his sake the death which he did owe, and the devil might now find no place for bringing a charge; because both the devil ought not to have had lordship over man, and man was worthy to be set free. ⟦s50⟧ And so he made both our judgment and our cause. ⟦s51⟧ Whence the Psalmist: *For thou hast made my judgment and my cause* [0030D] [n: (Psal. IX)]. ⟦s52⟧ He made our cause; because he paid the debt to the Father for us, and by dying expiated the guilt. ⟦s53⟧ But the judgment; because, descending to the lower regions and breaking the gates of death, he set free the captivity which was held there. ⟦s54⟧ The judgment of the devil against God, therefore, was made at once in the beginning of the age; the judgment of man against the devil, in the passion of Christ. ⟦s55⟧ The judgment of man against God is deferred unto the end of the age. ⟦s56⟧ And therefore man, if he shall first have willed to be reconciled to God through Christ, can await his judgment without fear of damnation. ⟦s57⟧ D. Here you ought fittingly to have distinguished the modes of judging, so that by comparison with the others I might be able both more plainly to discern and more truly to approve the things which have here been said concerning judgment. [0031A] M. There are four judgments, namely according to foreknowledge, according to cause, according to operation, according to retribution. ⟦s58⟧ According to foreknowledge we were judged before we were; according to cause we are judged from the time we begin to be, when we are good and evil; according to operation we are judged when by our works we show outwardly what sort we are inwardly with ourselves; according to retribution we are judged when, in receiving the wage of our works, we shall know what sort we were foreseen from eternity with God. ⟦s59⟧ Of these four judgments two are hidden, the judgment according to foreknowledge and the judgment according to cause, and therefore in them human judgment is not admitted; two are manifest, that is, the judgment according to operation and the judgment according to retribution, [0031B] and in one of these, that is, in the judgment according to operation, the power of judging in the present is given by God to men; but the other, that is, the judgment according to retribution, will be judged not by men, but by God in the presence of men. ⟦s60⟧ But the judgment according to foreknowledge is hidden and unchangeable; the judgment according to cause, hidden and changeable; the judgment according to operation, manifest and changeable. ⟦s61⟧ The judgment according to retribution, manifest and unchangeable. ⟦s62⟧ The judgment according to operation is in a certain way an image, a sacrament of the judgment according to retribution; because the one manifests the cause and hints at the foreknowledge, but the other will manifest the cause and will confirm the foreknowledge.
