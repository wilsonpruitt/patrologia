## On the Sacraments of the Natural Law and of the Written Law (cont.)

D. I would that you should now describe to me one by one the army of so great a king: who and of what sort his soldiers were from the beginning; what arms, what enemies they had; where and when, and what sort of wars they waged; how they conquered; what rewards at the last (the victory being consummated) they received. M. After the first parent of the human [0033A] race, exiled from paradise for the fault of disobedience, came into this world, the devil, exercising a tyrannical right over him, possessed him violently thereafter as he had first fraudulently seduced him. But the providence of God, which was disposing him unto salvation, so tempered the rigor of justice by mercy that it permitted him indeed to be oppressed by that one for a time, and yet, lest he should be oppressed by him forever, prepared for him a remedy out of the very punishment. And so already then, from the very outset of the world, God set before man the sacraments of his salvation, whereby he might seal him under the expectation of the sanctification to come: so that whoever should receive them with right faith and firm hope, for the sake of obedience to the divine institution, might, even though set under the yoke, belong to the fellowship of liberty. He set forth, therefore, [0033B] an edict, forming and instructing man, that whoever should choose to await him as savior and liberator ought to approve the vow of that same choice of his in the receiving of his sacraments: « The devil also set forth his own sacraments, whereby he might bind his own to himself, so that, by how much the more plainly he distinguished them from those who refused his rule, by so much the more securely he might hold them. The human race, therefore, soon began to be divided into contrary parts, some receiving the sacraments of the devil, others receiving the sacraments of Christ: and there were made two households, the one of Christ, the other of the devil. » The devil therefore, seeing that the offspring of the human race, which he had corrupted in its root, although it came forth wholly with corruption in being born, did not nevertheless persist wholly in that same corruption, being renewed in being born again, [0033C] determined to press by persecution, through those whom he possessed, those whom he was losing; presuming then so much the more to inflict violence, by how much he remembered himself to have prevailed before by fraud. But this contrivance, the craft of the malign one, was the dispensation of divine mercy: namely, that man, confirmed through grace, might defend against him the justice which, seduced before by him, he had forsaken. Wherein, without doubt, the pride of the tyrant is also fittingly confounded, in that now he does not prevail through torments to overcome a man mortal and weak, whom before, surrounded by no weakness, he seduced by persuasion alone. By an intention so diverse, therefore, one work is accomplished, when the devil exhorts his soldiers to persecution [0033D] and violence, but Christ exhorts his own to patience. And since the devil, from the beginning, whether through himself or through his own, attacks the good for no other end than to take away their justice, and the good demand nothing else through this fight than that they may not lose their justice, it is assuredly plain that he, when he cannot bring what he wills to effect, is always overcome by the good, even if he seem to have prevailed outwardly. Therefore, when the soldiers of the devil inflict violence outwardly, the soldiers of Christ conquer inwardly through patience. D. Are the sacraments of Christ, then, arms? M. Not only arms, but also medicines. D. How? M. They are medicines, because they heal the sick; arms, because they protect the sound. D. What is a sacrament? M. The sign of a sacred reality. D. In how many ways are sacraments made? M. [0034A] In three; that is, by things, by words, by deeds. By things, as in water, in bread, in wine, in salt, in ash, in oil, in fire, and in the rest of this kind; by words, as is the invocation of the Trinity, and the other words having divine power which are uttered in blessings or consecrations or adjurations; by deeds, as is the little sign of the cross, the bending of the knees, the spreading out of the hands, and all other such things as there are. D. Why is a sacrament called the sign of a sacred reality? M. Because through that which is discerned visibly without, another thing invisible within is signified. For example: as through water, the remission of sins; through fire, the ardor of charity; through oil, mercy; through salt, wisdom, and so forth. D. Shall man, then, seek salvation in such things, since he himself is rational, and more worthy in his own condition than things without sense? How [0034B] can these things give salvation and heal the languors of souls, since they are themselves bodily, having nothing beyond a visible appearance? M. There is altogether something else there beyond that which is discerned by bodily eyes; and it is that very thing which invisibly cleanses the stains of sins and heals the languors of souls. D. Whence, then, comes that power? M. Not from the nature of that which is there discerned visibly, but from the operation of the Holy Spirit, who works invisibly in that visible thing. D. Could not the Holy Spirit have furnished this same power for the restoration of man without the showing forth or the support of bodily things? M. He assuredly could, if he willed. D. What cause was there, then, that these things should be instituted, that man should be bidden to seek salvation as it were in these things which are lower and less worthy, since [0034C] God alone is he from whom man both ought to seek salvation and can hope for it? M. Man is bidden to seek salvation in these things, not from these things; in such wise that, when he has obtained it, he may know that he has received it from him who bade him seek it in these things. D. Tell me, then, why God commanded man to seek salvation in things of this kind. M. That man might be humbled and instructed. Humbled indeed, while he is compelled now, lying below, to seek his good in what is lower, which before, when he stood, he despised to receive from him who was above him. Fittingly, therefore, is the medicine of subsequent humility applied to the disease of preceding pride: namely, that the man who before had despised God (ruinously puffed up in himself) should afterwards, healthfully humbled, willingly lay himself down even beneath things perceptible by the senses, [0034D] apart from God. Sacraments have also been set before man for teaching, that through those things which are done outwardly he may be able to recognize what is shown forth to him inwardly. D. Is it to be believed, then, that in *ommni* sacrament there is more than that alone which can be seen and perceived by bodily sense? M. There are two things there: one which we perceive outwardly by bodily sense, which is properly called the sacrament, that is, the sign of a sacred reality; the other which we do not see but believe, which is rightly named the reality or the power of the sacrament. D. What difference is there between a sign and a sacrament? M. A sign signifies only from institution; a sacrament also represents from likeness. Likewise a sign can signify a reality, not confer it. But in a sacrament there is not only signification, but also efficacy; namely, that [0035A] it at once both signifies from institution, and represents from likeness, and confers from sanctification. D. Can a sacrament ever be taken for a sign? M. Improperly it is called a sacrament for the signification alone. D. What if we should say that those figures of the Old Law were only signs of things to come, and not sacraments, and that the spiritual grace (which was given in them) was conferred by the mediation of those sacraments which were to come? M. If anyone shall have denied that the earlier sacraments had the effect of sanctification, he does not seem to me to think rightly; yet in this we without doubt prefer the dignity of the later ones, that those were both visible things and signs of visible things; but these, although they are visible, are themselves the sacraments only of invisible grace. Therefore the sacraments of grace [0035B] first receive sanctification into themselves through the invocation of the Trinity, and thereafter confer sanctification, so that they may be sanctifying out of sanctification, and may confer this out of their own sanctification, given them from heaven, which those were wont to confer through these out of the signification of these alone.

D. What are those things without which salvation could not be had from the beginning? M. There are three, that is, faith, and the sacraments of faith, and good works: which from the beginning seem to have been set before man for the obtaining of salvation. These the soldiers of Christ have always used: receiving strength in faith arms in the sacraments of faith, darts in good works, with which they fought against the devil. Now just as neither strength can profit an unarmed man contending against a strong armed man, nor arms without powers, nor darts, [0035C] so indeed faith without works and the sanctification of the sacraments, and again the sacraments without faith, or works, do not have the effect of salvation.

D. What is faith? M. Paul says *that faith is the substance of things to be hoped for, the argument of things not appearing [n: (Hebr. XI)]*. D. How do you think this is to be taken? Whatsoever things either are to come, and are not yet; or, being past, have now ceased to be; or, being present, hide the truth of themselves from our senses or knowledge: all these things, so far as pertains to us, in a certain way are not; but because through faith a certain certitude is born in us concerning all these things as though they were existing and present, not undeservedly is faith itself called both the substance of things not existing and the argument of things not appearing. [0035D] Further, this is to be considered, that by faith alone no one can please God unless charity be joined to it, so that it may be, namely, faith working out of love. Nevertheless faith is in a certain way the foundation and beginning of good in man, out of which charity is born, and out of charity hope is generated; because faith pertains to thought, but charity is love, hope is confidence.

D. I would that, the definition of these being briefly set forth, they might be more plainly distinguished for me. M. Faith is a voluntary certitude concerning things absent, set above opinion and below knowledge. Charity is the love of God for his own sake, and the love of one's neighbor for God's sake. Hope is confidence in the good things to come, out of the grace of God and a good conscience. In this also faith and hope seem to differ: [0036A] that faith can be concerning things present and past and at the same time things to come; hope, concerning things to come alone; faith, concerning our own things and those of others; hope, only concerning our own. Faith, concerning good things and evil; hope, concerning good things alone.
