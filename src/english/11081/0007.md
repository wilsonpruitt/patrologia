## On the Sacraments of the Natural Law and of the Written Law (cont.)

D. What is the sacrament of faith? M. In two ways can the sacrament of faith be understood: either that very sacrament which is faith; or the sacrament of faith, that is, of the faithful, which the faithful use and by which they are sanctified. D. For what reason is faith called a sacrament? M. Just as, when the sacrament of baptism is spoken of, no other sacrament is signified than that very thing which is baptism, so also by the sacrament of faith we are sometimes wont to understand faith itself, which is a sacrament. Further, all the rest are called sacraments of faith for this reason, that they were instituted for the sanctifying of those only who have faith. [0036B] But faith itself, as has been said, is a sacrament, for this reason: that, as in baptism and in the other sacraments one thing is that which is shown outwardly, and another the spiritual power which is exhibited within; so the faith is one thing by which *now,* as the Apostle says, we behold God as it were *through a glass in a dark manner [n: (I Cor. XIII)]*; and another is the manifest vision by which we shall hereafter see him, beholding him *face to face [n: (ibid.)]*. Faith, therefore, is the sacrament of the vision to come; and that vision itself is the power of that sacrament which is faith. D. I would now be taught: what things pertain to faith? M. There are two: the Creator and the creature. Sound faith ought so to distinguish these that it neither attribute to the creature the divinity of the Creator, nor ascribe to the Creator the weakness of the creature; that is, that it neither enclose God within time, [0036C] nor stretch out the creature with eternity. For whichever of these be done, faith must needs suffer loss. And it follows that faith, when it has rightly divided between the Creator and the creature, should rightly study also to offer to the Creator his own honor by rendering it to him: namely, that it confess that all things were made by him and through him, but that he himself subsists not from elsewhere but from himself, without beginning and end. Then follows the third: namely, that him whom it confesses to be the Creator of all things it should also believe and await as the savior of men. And, to sum up briefly, these are the things than which right faith could never have less: that one God be believed, the maker of all things and the savior of men. For those who, worshipping the creature for God [0036D] and deceived by a twofold error, believed either that the creature was eternal or that God was temporal — what else are they to be said to have done than to have attributed the excellence of the maker to the work and the weakness of the thing made to the Creator? Again, the philosophers of the Gentiles, because they had not faith concerning the Savior, although they were not in the least mistaken in distinguishing between the Creator and his work, are nevertheless by no means to be called faithful, because they had not the faith of the Savior. D. What sort of faith had the ancients concerning the Savior who was to come, especially those who, existing about the first beginnings of the age, had not yet received the Scriptures in which his advent was promised? M. It is not necessary that we should say that to all who were then faithful his coming in the flesh, his cross and resurrection, had been manifestly revealed; since even [0037A] the apostles, who saw him present in the flesh (whose faith and justice is preached by the Savior himself), are read to have been ignorant of this. Yet they believed themselves to be saved through him in whatever way and at whatever time it should please him; but that they were ignorant that they were to be redeemed by his death (excepting a few to whom this had been revealed by God) is plainly shown from this, that Peter strove to gainsay the Lord when he foretold his own death [n: (Matth. XVI)]; and all the other apostles are read rather to have despaired at his death, so much so that they themselves, already despairing on account of the cross, said: *But we hoped that it was he that should have redeemed Israel [n: (Luc. XXIV)]*. For they who had hoped for redemption while he was living, but afterwards despaired when he was dying, plainly show that they knew not that they were to be redeemed [0037B] through his death.

D. Could this suffice unto salvation for those earlier men, that they should believe him the creator of all things and await him as the savior to come? M. This could then suffice for them, together with the other things without which, as has been said, faith could not please: at least in those who had time for working well, that is, with the receiving of the sacraments and the exercise of good works, through charity and hope strengthened by faith. D. Did faith, then, afterwards increase? M. In two ways faith increases: by constancy and by knowledge. Some have a faith great in knowledge, and small in constancy; others small in knowledge and great in constancy. But with God a great constancy of faith is more precious than great knowledge. The ancient [0037C] saints, therefore, for the most part had a faith small in knowledge and great in constancy; just as, on the contrary, many now have a faith great in knowledge and small in constancy.

D. What sacraments were there under the natural law? M. Oblations and sacrifices and tithes. D. For what reason are these called sacraments? M. Because they are signs of a sacred reality. D. Of what? M. Of the remission of sins. D. How? M. The substance of man is twofold: one inward, which he himself is; the other outward, in the things which he possesses. God willed, therefore, that man should make an oblation to him out of his outward substance, so that, by a just recompense, while he devoutly rendered that which he had outwardly received from God, he might, by God's bestowing, recover that which he had inwardly lost of himself. Through faith, therefore, and through oblations or sacrifices they then received the remission [0037D] of sins, whoever, putting off the old man, that is, Adam, were made members of the new, that is, of Christ. For oblations and sacrifices, faith mediating, wrought the same in them which circumcision afterwards wrought under the written law, or baptism under grace; namely, that they too, justified from all sin whether original or actual, should be reckoned among the members of him by whose faith and sacraments they were sanctified. For indeed the passion of the Savior conferred upon the preceding sacraments, that is, either upon oblation under the natural law or upon circumcision under the written law, the same power and efficacy of sanctification which it confers upon those that follow and succeed: with this alone excepted, that sacrifices and oblations under the natural [0038A] law were not made unto the necessity of salvation, as circumcision was under the written law and baptism under grace, but unto the worship of devotion, faith alone then justifying and purifying believers. And again, with this also excepted in the case of circumcision, that those earlier fathers, however just and pleasing to God, could not enter the gate of the heavenly kingdom before the victim was given; but were kept only in the bosom of Abraham, that is, in a tranquil rest, until he should come who, dying and rising again for us, should through the flesh he assumed ascend the heavens, and open the way to all believing in him and following him.

D. If the same power was in those sacraments which is in these, what was the cause that those should cease and these succeed? M. A more manifest truth is declared in these; [0038B] and it was just that, as the acknowledgment of faith grew with the gradual advance of time, so also other sacraments should be instituted, which, even if they conferred no other sanctification of spiritual grace, should nevertheless more manifestly display the same. For indeed there was the same Savior, the same faith, the same grace: there of one to come, here of one exhibited. But because he through whom salvation was given was still far off, the signs of that same salvation had to be obscure, that gradually, as his advent drew near, both faith might increase the more and grace might manifest itself the more evidently. Therefore first through oblation, and afterwards through circumcision, and at the last through baptism, the sacrament of expiation and justification was instituted to be formed; because the form and likeness of the same cleansing is indeed hinted at secretly in oblation, [0038C] but is more evidently expressed in circumcision, while through baptism it is manifestly declared. For man received the first justification, that for the remedies of salvation he should offer to God a part of his outward substance and retain a part for his own use, so that in this deed he might learn to ascribe to God those things which were inwardly good, but to impute to himself those which were evil. But this sign, taken from afar, taught man rather what he was than what was being done. Therefore, when the time had come in which the truth had to be declared, there was given to him as a remedy the sacrament of circumcision, where, while from the inward substance of man a part is cut away — not indeed naturally superfluous, but the figure of that which is superfluous — that circumcision of sin which grace accomplishes in the inward man is now more manifestly [0038D] shown. But since circumcision cuts off outward filth and the excesses which are without, whereas washing cleanses even inward stains, therefore, when the manifest truth had come with Christ, circumcision had to cease, that that sacrament might succeed which could declare the perfect cleansing of man and the inward whiteness of the soul (which in it and [sic: *ot*] through it the sacrament obtains). Those first sacraments, therefore, seem to be as it were a certain shadow of spiritual grace; but those which followed afterwards as it were a certain image, and (if it must be so said) having the lineaments of truth. But these are now not shadow or image, but the body of truth; in which (because the effect of salvation is fully given, and evidently [0039A] shown) as the spirit in a living body betrays itself by outward motion, so in them the life of invisible grace is most clearly demonstrated.

D. It remains now that you open what things are to be said concerning works. M. There are three kinds of deeds: some are altogether good, which can never lawfully be passed over; some are altogether evil, which can never lawfully be committed; some are indifferent, which, whether they be done or not done, are neither fault nor merit. Those, therefore, which are so constituted that they can never lawfully be done, these only did the natural law forbid; but those which can never lawfully be passed over it alone commanded, leaving all indifferent things to either alternative. But concerning those which were either to be commanded or to be left free, it set one precept only in the heart of man: [0039B] *What thou wilt for thyself, that do thou to others; what thou wilt not for thyself, do not thou to others [n: (Matth. VII; Tobiae IV)]*, that from the consideration of himself, namely, man might learn how he ought to show himself toward his neighbor. But afterwards, when the law had entered in, and was setting forth a stricter discipline of living it began to except certain things out of those which were indifferent, and out of these to place some by precept, some by prohibition, on one side only; so that, just as to the first man in paradise God had excepted out of the natural concession one showing forth for the precept of discipline, so now, to his sons, who did not stand in one truth but were scattered through various errors, he formed not one precept of discipline out of the natural concession, as for men to be proved, but many, as for men to be corrected and restored; that by how much the more they learned [0039C] to temper themselves in indifferent things, by so much the more lightly they might be able to guard against things not conceded.
