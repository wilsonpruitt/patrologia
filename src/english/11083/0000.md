## PREFACE.

[0845D]

To G., the prudent and religious searcher of the divine word among all others and above all others: HUGH, a sinner. I have found that it has sometimes happened that, in the handling of difficult matters, either the folly of the reporter corrupted the hearer, or the rashness of the hearer brought the reporter into ill repute. Wherefore it seems to me that equal caution must be taken, that above all there, where nothing else can be held without danger, we should neither easily presume to assert what is ours, nor rashly to judge what is another's. For I know « how many, » as blessed Augustine says, « dreams the human heart brings forth; » and that by the same judgment by which it is deceived in the knowledge of itself, it will look upon the other things also, which are outside itself, not rightly. For what do you think this is, that men are wont to hold such different views about the truth of things? [0846C] Is truth a matter of names? Behold (that we may for the moment be silent about [0846D] God), what is it that dialectic holds so many differing and so opposed — not to say perverse — opinions? Have they all known the one thing that is, but out of a love of deceiving invented differing accounts? I do not think so. But they relate their five dreams, and by that opinion by which they were themselves first deceived in themselves they afterwards, unknowing, lead others astray. And as blessed Job says to his friends, who were arrogant and presumed of themselves more than was fitting [n: (Job XII)], they now think that wisdom not only will die according to them, but has also arisen from them. It is fitting therefore that this very thing should make us timid in our own assertion, that in the assertion of one truth we already see so many to be at variance. For because truth is seen from afar, it brings forth differing judgments; and each man has as much power over it as [0847A] he himself is. For in us, that which we perceive concerning the knowledge of things — and in that manner the understandings of the heart give indication toward the truth about the things that are without — in the manner in which the mind inwardly shapes itself in its representation. Whence it is necessary that, while the mind, inwardly corrupted, is wrongly affected, the understanding too is deceived in its judgment of the things that are without. This is the very cause which is wont sometimes to breed a controversy of words even where there is unity of meanings; which nevertheless then becomes the more pardonable, when it has not been obstinate. This, therefore — we who seem to be handlers of the word of God and searchers of the truth — if perchance we ever find it among ourselves (as commonly happens), let us return the more quickly to one and the same mind, lest, if we begin obstinately to defend our own sayings, we be thought to have held some other view. That which your charity [0847B] has deigned to ask of me concerning the soul of Christ, I have learned was long since debated among certain wise men; and I have come to know what those thought whom we judge to be the more worthy of belief, partly from others reporting about them, partly from themselves reporting about themselves. But I, since I am wont to be assailed rather often by this question, profess myself to be such a man as would not wish anything on this matter either to be inquired into by all so curiously and superstitiously, or to be asserted so confidently. Whence now too I am hemmed in by a twofold consideration in this matter, since I see that I owe both silence to your prudence, and an answer to your goodness. Amid these things the very difficulty of the matter also presents itself, which perhaps cannot be discussed by me as compendiously as is fitting, since, if I pass the measure in speaking, I fear to seem superstitious. Yet I wish, and much more do I wish, to be convicted in some [0847C] part of ignorance rather than to seem a despiser of charity, and to have unpolished words rather than a perverse heart
