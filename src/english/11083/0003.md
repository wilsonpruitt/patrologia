## THERE FOLLOWS *The determination of the question concerning the soul of Christ, whether it had knowledge equal with the divinity.* (cont.)

For as to that which you brought in — that if it had knowledge equal [0853C] with God, it would also have equal beatitude — how it fails to hold together there is no need to say at present, since we take away the equality of comparison where we demonstrate the singularity of unity. And it is one thing altogether to have the same, and another to be the same; because to have belongs to grace, to be belongs to nature. But suppose a multitude of wisdoms or of kinds of knowledge does admit comparison: does equal wisdom make equal beatitude? Then greater wisdom makes greater beatitude, and less makes less; and demons are more blessed than men by as much as they go before them in knowledge. And what shall I say of charity? *Knowledge puffs up, charity builds up [n: (I Cor. VIII)]*. Not all, therefore, who are wiser can straightway presume upon a greater love [0853D]; because it is one thing altogether to see, and another to love. *Now,* he says, *they have both seen and hated both me and my Father [n: (Joan. XV)]*. Yet it matters nothing here whether an equal love accompanies equal knowledge of anything whatever; because where wisdom is one, just as there can be no comparison, so neither can there be an equality of diverse things. Afterward you added and said: Since wisdom is a great and spiritual good, then, if the soul of Christ has wisdom equal with God, it will be false that God has a greater sufficiency in every good than his creature has. And next: As a creature cannot to an equal Creator, so neither the good of the one to the good of the other. But this is well and truly said where it applies. Where is the good of a creature found to be other and different from the good of the Creator? But where [0854A] it is one and the very same, what can be greater and less? For what other true good of a creature, and chiefly of a rational creature, is there besides God? Or what other good of God is there besides himself? If therefore there is a true and only good of the creature, it is God; and the good of God cannot be anything other than himself. Is God therefore less or greater than himself, because he himself is alike the good of his creature and his own good? Let there cease then at last so frequent a repetition of comparison, where there is the singularity of unity. Then, to prove the assertion you had set forth, you brought forward the words of the Apostle, by which you think to establish that the soul of Christ had less wisdom than God. For he says: *For who among men knows the things that are of a man, but the spirit of a man which is in him? So too the things that are of God no one [0854B] has known but the Spirit of God [n: (I Cor. II)]*. Therefore, as it seems, the soul of Christ did not know them, if no one has known them but the Spirit of God. Unless perhaps someone should wish to answer us that the soul of Christ too was the Spirit of God? Yet what are those things of God which no one has known but the Spirit of God? What but those of which it is said in the same place: *O God, the eye has not seen, besides thee, the good things which thou hast prepared for them that love thee [n: (Isa. LXIV)]*. Because *neither has eye seen, nor ear heard, nor has it ascended into the heart of man [n: (I Cor. II)]*, etc. These things therefore no one knows but the Spirit of God; and what is it that follows: *But to us God has revealed them through the Spirit,* the holy one? What Spirit? He adds: *But we have not received the spirit of this world, but the Spirit of* Christ, *that we may know the things that have been given us by God [n: (ibid.)]*. Therefore, as the Spirit of God is the Spirit [0854C] of Christ, those also have known them to whom these things have been revealed through the Spirit. Therefore much more has the soul of Christ known these things, and known them fully and perfectly — the soul which received the fullness of that very Spirit. *Not,* for he says, *by measure does God give the Spirit [n: (Joan. III)]*. *And we saw his glory, the glory as of the only-begotten from the Father, full of grace and truth [n: (Joan. I)]*. We too, whoever of us are counted worthy to know these things, and however much we are counted worthy to know, receive of his fullness not the fullness, but a portion of grace. *Father,* he says, *I have manifested,* or revealed, *thy name to the men whom thou gavest me [n: (Joan. XVII)]*. What is the revelation of a man, but the breathing-in of knowledge? Did he then reveal to others a name of which he himself had not the knowledge? God forbid! [0854D] Again: *But when that Spirit of truth shall come, whom I will send to you, he shall teach you all truth [n: (Joan. XVI)]*. Why did the Master himself not first learn all truth from that Spirit of truth, whom he sent to the disciples to teach all truth? But he sent the Spirit, and did not lose the Spirit; whose gifts he distributed to faithful men as he willed, and himself retained the fullness. He pours him into others, but has not poured him out from himself; filling others, not emptying himself; not diminishing himself, and enriching others. Therefore what the Spirit of God knows, the soul of Christ also knows through the Spirit of God, and knows fully, because it had the fullness of the Spirit of God.

Lastly you bring forward into the midst certain words of blessed Ambrose, and, lest they should seem to run counter [0855A] to your assertion, you show how they are to be understood or determined. For he says « that the soul of Christ has by grace all the things which God has by nature. » To which words you think that something must (well-nigh) be added, lest, if perhaps without that qualification we should say that it has utterly all the things that are of the deity, we seem to attribute to a creature — which had a beginning — eternity, and — which has *termimum* — immensity, and — which had mutability and could lie subject to passibility — immutability, and the rest of the things known to belong to the divine nature alone. But this determination, although it does not seem wholly to be rejected, is nevertheless far less suitable than the more convenient exposition to which this opinion may be fitted. For the soul of Christ [0855B] is said to have by grace all the things which God has by nature for this reason: because the fullness of the deity in the Word was united to it in time by grace, which fullness was in God from eternity by nature. This soul has in time what it has, because it received it; he had it from eternity, because he was it. It received it, not that, being changed, it should pass over into the nature of that which it received, but that, being lifted up, it should ascend into fellowship with it. This one received what it did not have; he could neither receive what he did not have, nor did he cease to be what he was. What then did the soul of Christ receive? The fullness of the deity, the fullness of wisdom, the fullness of power. It received the whole of what God is. How did it receive it? Not by a turning of nature, but by the firmness of person. It received it, not because it was essentially changed into it, but because it was ineffably united [0855C] to it. It received it, not that it might essentially become it, but that it might be personally one with it. Yet it received the whole, and has the whole which he did not receive and yet has. Does the soul of Christ then have eternity, if it has the whole that God has? Why not? Behold, let it be said — although it is not necessary in all cases to admit the use of words to this effect: it has immensity, it has eternity; it has by grace all the things that God has by nature. How does it have them? Perhaps, because we say that it has eternity, because we say that it has immensity, are we to think this: that what began to be lacks a beginning; that what has received circumscription is unbounded? Is it also, because it has God, that it should be God? Or because it is not God, does it therefore not have him? In what way then does it have him? But it has him as personally united, not as naturally [0855D] implanted; it has the whole — not that a creature could be eternal or immeasurable, but because it is united to the eternal and the immeasurable. For what is eternity or immensity? Is it an accident? Are then the eternity and immensity of God accidents? God forbid! When therefore it is said, God has this by nature, it is understood that God is this. But when it is said, the soul of Christ has this by grace, it is understood that it has been united to it. Therefore the soul of Christ has wholly by grace [0856A] that which God is by nature. Nor yet does it follow from this that his own work is equal to the Creator; because, while it received the immensity of grace, it did not lose the condition of its nature. It received what it did not have, but it did not cease to be what it was. It received the fullness of wisdom, in which and through which it might be fully and perfectly wise; yet it did not receive that it should be wisdom. And what need is there to pursue each point? Briefly I will show you what seems to me must be held in this matter. We believe that the full and perfect and whole wisdom of God was in the soul of Christ; and that out of that same wisdom the same soul was fully and perfectly wise — not by participating in it, but by possessing it whole, which it singularly conceived and ineffably comprehended? Why ineffably comprehended? Because it received according to fullness [0856B]. John *a lamp* was, *burning and shining [n: (Joan. V)]*; he had comprehended the light, because he was not darkness. But because he had comprehended it in part, therefore he could not comprehend that ineffable comprehension in Christ, but he says: *Of whom I am not worthy that I should loose the latchet of the shoe* of him [n: (Joan. I)]. Therefore John could not loose the latchet of Christ. For it was within him to receive of the fullness; it was above him to comprehend the fullness. And so what he knew he was able to say; what he had not conceived he chose to believe, and did not presume to discuss. He therefore was unwilling to loose the union, lest it be presumed to compare us to that unity. What does it seem to you? Do not those wish to loose the latchet of the incarnate Word who divide the unity of the ineffable mystery by a difference of comparison? The soul [0856C] of John therefore received of the fullness; the soul of Christ comprehended the fullness. It comprehended it, I say, not in equality of nature, but in fullness of grace and by the property of unity. Behold, I have said to you who asked what I owed also to you who commanded — what I judged the more to be approved in this assertion. But in doubtful matters, if I have no strength of manifest authority, I wish indeed to be so received by others that they help me while I seek, and do not refute me when I find. But whoever persist in this, so as to contend in asserting that the wisdom by which the soul of Christ was wise was one thing, and another the wisdom which was united to the soul of Christ — I do not wish to prejudge anyone; let them see for themselves in what sense they assert this, lest perhaps it be a carnal sense, pronouncing its own things rather than true ones. This [0856D] one thing I affirm without doubt: that either there was in the soul of Christ no other wisdom besides the divine, or, if there was another, it was not such as that one. He who does not believe one who believes this, does not believe well. To the rest I do not compel, but persuade; in these things, he who believes me believes with me; he who does not believe me, let him believe himself, until he come where he may believe with me. Yet so that meanwhile each one may take care to abound humbly in his own sense, not to presume arrogantly. Farewell.
