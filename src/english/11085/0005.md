## CHAP. II. *That the most holy and perpetual virgin Mary conceived not of a man, but of the Holy Spirit, whom she also bore, the only-begotten of the Most High Father, Christ, God and man.*

The first part of the proposition having therefore been proved, in which it was said that Mary did not change the purpose of virginity in her conjugal consent, our discourse must now pursue its own order to the exposition of the second part, in which it was said that she conceived not of a man but of the Holy Spirit, without lust. The judgment of the first part indeed (because it was more caught up in the question) [0870D] needed a longer disputation. But this second, since less doubt has been raised about it, requires not so much proof as exposition. And so it must first be asked how that which was said — that blessed Mary conceived of the Holy Spirit — ought to be [sic: *in elligi*]. For we know, and all of us have by now learned from the most frequent practice of nature, that when a woman is said to have conceived by a man, nothing else is recorded than that she has received the substance of flesh, for the generating of flesh, through the intercourse of flesh. And this substance of flesh, poured over from the flesh of the man through that intercourse of flesh, becomes one flesh with the flesh of the woman, so that what is to be born, truly taking its origin from the substance of both, is generated by him through her and also by her; therefore a woman conceives by a man when from [0871A] the flesh of the man, through intercourse of the flesh, she receives seed for generating flesh. Nor does she conceive anything other than what he is by whom she conceives, or than she herself is who conceives; and what she conceives, that she brings forth. What, then, shall we say? Did the Holy Spirit pour into the womb of the Virgin the seed of birth out of his own substance? How then could a spiritual substance supply the seed of flesh? Or was it perhaps not seed of flesh that was conceived, and yet flesh that was born? What do we say? How did Mary conceive of the Holy Spirit? And if she conceived of the Holy Spirit, how was the Holy Spirit not the father of Christ? All these things need great consideration, lest perhaps in a matter difficult and very obscure the human understanding presume something beyond its own possibility. Nor are these things to be doubted because they are obscure, [0871B] nor is anything to be defined rashly because they are to be believed. Let us ask, then, what Mary conceived. For either she did not conceive flesh, and did not beget flesh; or if she begot flesh, she conceived flesh. For what she conceived, this she begot? But how could that which is not flesh supply flesh, or flesh not beget flesh? Let us consider in what way this perplexed inquiry, or manner of asking, may soundly and fittingly be understood. And first let us look at how the work of nature is completed in that conception which is carried through according to the accustomed and usual operation of nature. In parents generating carnally and, through intercourse of the flesh, fashioning out of flesh the flesh that is to be born, nature on both sides supplies substance for the creating of the offspring, so that from the flesh of both [0871C] may proceed the flesh which is to be formed in the flesh of one only. Yet this tribute (which nature lays out as a kind of debt, by the appointment of the Creator, for accomplishing the fabric of the human body) is so entirely required of her without any necessity or compulsion, that she is inclined to render it by nothing but love alone, and, if I may so speak, by a spontaneous charity. For there is nothing except love alone that can wring this debt from nature; but when she has been persuaded by a spontaneous charity, then surely, from both sides, she runs willingly and gladly to meet herself for the completing of the work. Indeed whatever is snatched violently from one unwilling is shown to be altogether unsuitable and [0871D] ineffectual for the effect of a cause of this kind. Love alone, therefore, is what can persuade nature, and in a manner compel her while willing, to sow offspring. And indeed in the woman the love of the man, and in the man the love of the woman, is wont to bring this about: that, since in either alone nature is not sufficient for herself, each may come to the other's help through love, so that what neither could do by itself, each may do by itself together with the other. Therefore the seed of human offspring, which is to be formed by the woman alone, is conceived, being sown at once by man and woman. And this indeed, as has already been said, nature works in the woman through love of the man, and in the man through love of the woman. For which reason too a woman is said to conceive only by a man, although she receives from herself as well as from the man that whereby she is made fruitful. And rightly does she conceive by the man alone, [0872A] because that which is conceived is in the one case taken from the flesh of the man, and in the other case is shown through love of the man. And so she conceives by the man not only that which she receives from the man, but she conceives by the man this also, which she receives from herself out of love of the man. This we wished to set down first, on account of what was said, that Mary conceived of the Holy Spirit. Mary conceived, then, of the Holy Spirit — not that she received the seed of the birth from the substance of the Holy Spirit, but because through the love and the operation of the Holy Spirit nature supplied substance to the divine birth out of the flesh of the Virgin. For because in the heart, in which the love of the Holy Spirit burned singularly, therefore in her flesh the power of the Holy Spirit was working wonders. And of him whose love in her heart admitted no partner, the operation [0872B] in her flesh had no precedent. This alone, therefore, did the Virgin conceive, which she received from her own flesh through the love and the operation of the Holy Spirit; and of this alone she begot a son, without the admixture of a man's seed. It was not, then, lust of the flesh that wrought the conception in the Virgin, who neither received seed from the flesh of a man, nor conceived from her own flesh through love of a man, but through the love and the operation of the Holy Spirit. Nor is the Holy Spirit himself on that account to be called the father of Christ, because his love wrought the Virgin's conception, since he did not bestow the seed of the birth upon the Virgin out of his own essence, but supplied substance to the Virgin herself, out of her own flesh, through his love and power. Christ, therefore, was both born of the Virgin, because he received the substance of flesh from the flesh of the Virgin. [0872C] And he was conceived of the Holy Spirit, because she, being a virgin, conceived from her own flesh alone, without the admixture of a man's seed, through the operation and love of the Holy Spirit. Hence it is that to that same Virgin who was to bear, it is said through the angel: *The Holy Spirit shall come upon thee, and the power of the Most High shall overshadow thee [n: (Luc. I)]*. For the Holy Spirit came upon the Virgin, that by his operation the flesh of Christ might be formed from the flesh of the Virgin. And the power of the Most High overshadowed her, lest, while supplying the substance of flesh, she should be burned with carnal concupiscence. And thus indeed we think that what is said — that the Virgin conceived of the Holy Spirit — is to be understood. Thus also it can be established that she is reported to have felt no lust in conceiving.

## CHAP. III. *That Mary bore Christ without detriment of pain and without detriment of virginity.*

[0872D]

There now remains this last of the three things that had been set forth: that we should show that she also bore without pain, and suffered no detriment of virginity in bearing. I once heard that a virgin conceived, but that a virgin did not bear, because such a one conceived from a man that which she bore. For deservedly was her integrity corrupted in the childbearing, because her virginity was defiled in the conceiving; and it was just that what had not been conceived without lust should not be borne without pain. In no way would childbearing bring pain, if conception had not felt lust. For if the fault of unlawful delight did not defile her who conceives, never would the penalty of pain torment her who bears. For [0873A] that first mother of our race and of our sorrow, after she had conceived an evil delight, only then heard that she should bear children in sorrow — so as to show openly that a woman does not bear with sorrow because she conceives by a man, but because she conceives with lust. For who could say, if man had not sinned, whether human childbearing, even when conceived by a man, would either corrupt integrity or violate chastity? And indeed, if chastity were not violated, why should integrity be corrupted? let him say who can. But the blessed Virgin Mary — that the second restoration might be above the first creation, and that the grace of the Savior might overcome the nature of the race — not only conceived without lust, but did not even receive her offspring from the seed of a man; and on this account she brought forth her son without pain, and with the glory of integrity [0873B] remained a virgin after the birth.
