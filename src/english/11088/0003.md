## CHAPTER III. ON PRAYER AND FASTING.

*Be instant in prayers at the appointed hours and times.*

To pray before the time is foresight. To pray at the appointed time is obedience. To let the time of praying pass by is negligence. Prayer ought to be the more frequent, the more useful it is. We ought to pray instantly and ardently. For great are the good things which God promises us. [0891B] For *eye hath not seen, nor ear heard, neither hath it entered into the heart of man, what God hath prepared for them that love him [n: (I Cor. II)]*. He does not wish his good things to grow cheap through ease in seeking and finding them. Precious and [sic: *desirabilis*] merchandise seeks out an eager trader. Therefore the Lord exhorts us in the Gospel, saying: *Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and you shall find; knock, and it shall be opened to you [n: (Matth. VII)]*. For the kingdom of God is not promised to the idle, the lukewarm and the negligent, but, as Scripture says: *The kingdom of heaven suffers violence, and the violent bear it away [n: (Matth. XI)]*. God loves instancy and importunity in prayer. If the good things are great, the desires ought not to be small. With the greatness of the rewards the greatness of the desires ought to agree. But if at the first tears the Lord does not hear, not even so [0891C] is one to desist from seeking and knocking; but rather one must persevere with such instancy, and the Saviour must be besought with so obstinate a cry, until he fulfils the desire of him who prays. But if any of us feels that his conscience or his thought or his action is defiled with the filth of vices, he ought to implore the mercy of the Redeemer with continual laments, and to ask the intercessions and help of the saints; and if after the example of the woman of Canaan we persist in praying and remain fixed, the grace of our Redeemer will be at hand, to correct all our errors in us, to sanctify unclean things, to calm troubled ones. For he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, to cleanse us from all iniquity, if we cry to him with the assiduous voice of the mountain. And that the brethren may be able to pray secretly and [0891D] quietly, it is therefore subjoined:

*Let no one do anything in the oratory except that for which it was made, whence also it received its name, so that if perhaps some wish to pray outside the appointed hours (if they have leisure for it), those who think something is to be done there may not be a hindrance to them.*

Therefore our Lord cast the sellers and buyers out of the temple, saying: *My house shall be called the house of prayer [n: (Matth. XXI)]*; to teach that in the Lord's house nothing else is to be done but to pray. For to this end every church is established, that in it prayer be made. For it is the house of God and the gate of heaven. There sinners are reconciled, and the just are sanctified; there the divine mysteries are accomplished and the heavenly sacraments are handled. [0892A] Whence Solomon says: Lord, if thy people shall be converted and shall pray toward thy sanctuary, thou wilt hear them and wilt forgive their sins [n: (III Reg. VIII)]. And therefore it is above all to be guarded against, that nothing be done in the sanctuary which either offends the eyes of the divine majesty or hinders those who wish to pray there. But in what manner we ought to pray is shown when it is added below.

*When you pray God with psalms and hymns: let that be turned over in the heart which is uttered in the voice.*

The mind of those who sing psalms in church ought to agree with the voice, that the word of the Apostle may be fulfilled: *I will sing with the spirit; I will sing also with the mind [n: (I Cor. XIV)]*. Perseverance in praying only then deserves to be fruitful, if what we pray with the mouth we meditate with the mind. For often we pray and yet direct the heart elsewhere, nor do we recall the very thing which [0892B] we are saying. But God does not hear that prayer to which he who prays does not attend; which is often wont to happen at the devil's prompting. For knowing the usefulness of praying, and envying us the grace of obtaining, he sends upon those who pray tumults of thoughts, that he may divide the mind from prayer and take away the fruit of prayer. Against his malice we ought to have constancy of mind, so that the more the crowd of thoughts assails us, the more sturdily our mind may remain fixed in the uprightness of its own state. For it greatly helps toward purity of prayer if in every place and time we restrain ourselves from unlawful acts; if we always chasten hearing and tongue alike from idle words; if we accustom ourselves to walk in the law of God and to search his testimonies with the whole heart. Whatever things we are more often wont to do, [0892C] to speak, to hear, these same things must needs run back to the mind as to their accustomed and proper seat.

*And sing nothing except what you read is to be sung. But what is so written as not to be sung, let it not be sung.*

For it is not fitting that ecclesiastical chant should be made according to the judgment of various men, but it is firmly to be kept according to the writings and institutions of our elders. Likewise the other customs of the monastery are to be ordered with authority and discretion. But if anything must be changed or established, this ought not to be done quickly or lightly; nor by the judgment of only two or three, but with the brethren called together it is to be ordered as the sounder part of the canonical congregation shall have decreed. [0892D] And it is to be known that ecclesiastical authority is in all things more to be imitated than reason, since authority is always of obedience and of humility; but reason is sometimes of presumption. And this is to be kept in the order, that maturity and gravity be always held. *Did I use lightness,* says the Apostle, *that with me there should be yea and nay? [n: (II Cor. I.)]* It is indeed honorable and religious not to vary anything easily or lightly.

*Tame your flesh by fastings and by abstinence from food and drink, as far as health permits.*

The flesh indeed lusts against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh; but, lest the flesh be able to prevail, spiritual men ought by the strength of the spirit to restrain that same concupiscence; because, [0893A] when the flesh is tamed, the spirit is strengthened. But when there is added as far as health permits, the virtue of discretion is commended. For good things themselves perish unless they are done with discretion. So much, then, ought each man [sic: *quisquae*] to tame his flesh by abstinence, as far as the health of nature permits. He who afflicts his flesh beyond measure kills his own fellow-citizen. If he refreshes it with more food than is fitting, he feeds an enemy. So then it is to be nourished that it may serve; so tamed that it may not grow proud. In all abstinence this is always to be attended to, that the vices be extinguished, not the flesh.

*But when someone cannot fast, let him nevertheless not take any food outside the hour of the meal, unless he is sick.*

The first vice of the seven principal vices is [0893B] gluttony. The temptation of this vice sometimes comes from small and cheap things. For the first man was tempted not by a pig, but by an apple; Esau not by a hen, but by a lentil dish. Our Saviour himself not by flesh, but by bread. And there are three kinds of this vice. One kind is when a man uses food or drink immoderately. Another kind is when a man desires food or drink that is too dainty. The third kind, when a man wishes to take food or drink outside the hour. We believe "outside the hour" to be up to the third hour. For up to that hour it is not fitting for a brother to eat or drink unless he be sick. To the sick man it is lawful at every hour. For to the infirm no law is set. For those not fasting, and yet not sick either, we understand the right [time] for taking refreshment to be from the third hour to the sixth; but for those fasting, from [0893C] the ninth hour to evening. Since the Apostle commands that *all things be done honorably and according to order [n: (I Cor. XIV)]*. Nor is it only in food and drink that one must be temperate; but the appointed hours also must be observed, lest we give anyone a stumbling-block from our life. It is exceedingly unseemly if we live reprehensibly, we who ought to show others the pattern of living rightly.
