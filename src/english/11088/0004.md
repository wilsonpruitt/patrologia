## CHAPTER IV. ON THE REFRESHMENT OF BOTH MEN.

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *When you come to table, until you rise from it, hear without tumult and contention what is read to you according to custom, lest your jaws alone take food, but let your ears also hunger for the word of God.*

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0893D]That man hunger for the word of God who desire to hear. ⟦s2⟧ Many hear the sacred reading who nevertheless withdraw empty from that very reading; where they have no affection, they feel no savor: whatever they hear they easily lose. ⟦s3⟧ It is a judgment for them to hear what they hear. ⟦s4⟧ On the contrary it is written: *Blessed are they who hunger and thirst after justice, for they shall be filled [n: (Matth. V)]*. ⟦s5⟧ Therefore they shall be filled because they first hungered. ⟦s6⟧ For by hungering a man is made worthy to be filled afterward. ⟦s7⟧ First, therefore, we ought to prepare our heart to hunger for and to desire the word of God; the divine grace will be at hand to flood us within and to refresh us with spiritual sweetness and savor, and to delight us to sing, How sweet are thy words to my jaws, [0894A] O Lord, above honey to my mouth, and *Good for me is the law of thy mouth above thousands of gold and silver [n: (Psal. CXVIII)]*. ⟦s8⟧ We ought to be partakers not only of the bodily table but of the spiritual, nor to take only the food of the body but of the soul. ⟦s9⟧ And by how much the soul is better than the body, by so much the more ought the foods of the soul to delight us than those of the body. ⟦s10⟧ As often as divine Scripture is read or expounded to us, we ought to think that it is spiritual food, which is sent to us by God to refresh our hearts lest we fail on the way, and to strengthen us against the temptations of the devil and the perils of this world. ⟦s11⟧ Such food we must hunger for unceasingly and hold continually in mind, that we may be able to say with the Prophet: *In my heart have I hidden thy words that I may not sin against thee [n: (ibid.)]*. ⟦s12⟧ He who hides the sacred [0894B] word in his heart, to him it is the life-giving food of the soul, which defends from death, that is, from sin, and moreover leads through to eternal life. ⟦s13⟧ Now let us hear what he subjoins concerning the infirm.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *Those who are infirm from their former manner of life, if they are treated otherwise in their diet, this ought not to be troublesome to others, nor seem unjust to those whom some custom has made stronger.*

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ As if he were openly saying, the stronger ought not to be saddened if they see the infirm treated more mercifully; nay rather, because they are stronger, they ought to keep the rigor of abstinence in themselves, and to bear the weakness of others with an even mind through compassion.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *Nor let them think those richer, because they take what [0894C] they themselves do not take, but let them rather congratulate themselves, because they are strong for what those are not strong for.*

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ For it is better to abstain than to take more. ⟦s2⟧ For the one is of strength, the other of infirmity. ⟦s3⟧ Each man ought to congratulate himself who by the house of divine grace is so sound and vigorous that the common food and drink of the others can suffice him, who seeks nothing dainty, nothing [sic: *it*] superfluous. ⟦s4⟧ For by how much the less each man craves, by how much the more straitly he lives, by so much the more blessed does he stand. ⟦s5⟧ For strait and narrow is the way which leads to life. ⟦s6⟧ Let him rejoice, therefore, who is able to live sparingly for Christ's sake. ⟦s7⟧ For a sparing life kills the vices, extinguishes lust, nourishes the virtues, strengthens the mind, lifts the mind to heavenly things.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *And if to those who have come from more delicate ways of living to [0894D] the monastery something of food, of clothing, of coverings is given, which is not given to others who are stronger and therefore happier; those to whom it is not given ought to consider how far those men have come down from their worldly life to this life, although they have not been able to attain to the frugality of the others who are firmer in body.*

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Why ought they to consider these things? ⟦s1⟧ Namely, that they may recognize from reason that it is just that those who have come down further should receive more. ⟦s2⟧ And when they have recognized this to be just, let them not wish all to receive what they have seen a few receive. ⟦s3⟧ For those who can do less are to be borne with. ⟦s4⟧ And this is what he subjoins.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *Nor ought all to wish to receive what they see a few receive [0895A] more abundantly, not because they are honored, but because they are borne with; lest a detestable perversity befall, that in monasteries, where as far as they can the rich become laborious, the poor become dainty.*

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ It is exceedingly detestable that a poor man should there become dainty, where a rich man craves to become laborious, that is, abstinent. ⟦s2⟧ Which nevertheless we often see done or happening in many. ⟦s3⟧ For the more noble and the more delicate many have been in the world, the more abjectly and strictly do they afterward live in the monastery. ⟦s4⟧ And very many, the more [sic: *abjectores*] and of poorer condition they were, the more do they afterward seek self-exaltation and dainties in the monastery. ⟦s5⟧ But, as we have said, it is exceedingly detestable and perverse that a poor man should there seek delicacies, where a rich man for the love of God strives by abstaining [0895B] to labor. ⟦s6⟧ The one serves the belly, the other the spirit; the one by the broad and spacious way makes for punishment, [sic: *site*] by the strait and narrow way makes for glory.
