## CHAPTER IX. ON THE CLEANNESS OF CLOTHES AND OF BODIES. (cont.)

Above he enjoins that they be instant in prayers at the appointed times. Times therefore must be distinguished in which we ought to pray, and in which we ought to read. Concerning work also he admonishes elsewhere that no one work anything for himself, but that all your works be done in common. These three things the rule commends to us, which are greatly necessary for our soul: namely prayer, reading, work. For by prayer we are cleansed, by reading we are instructed, by work we are made blessed, as the Holy Spirit says in the Psalm: *For thou shalt eat the labors of thy hands; blessed art thou, and it shall be well with thee [n: (Psal. CXXVII)]*. But because he says that the books are to be asked for on each several day, [0912A] frequency of reading is commended. The servant of God ought to read often. For divine reading is greatly useful. For through reading we learn what we ought to guard against, what to do, whither to press. Hence the Psalmist says: *Thy word is a lamp to my feet [n: (Psal. CXVIII)]*. Perfection, sense and understanding are increased. Reading instructs us for prayer and for work, and forms us for the contemplative life and the active. Therefore in the Psalm: *Blessed is the man,* it is written, *who in the law of the Lord* meditates *day and night [n: (Psal. I)]*. These are the arms — namely prayer, reading and work — by which the devil is beaten off. These are the instruments by which eternal blessedness is acquired. By these arms vices are suppressed; by these nourishments virtues are fed. But even if there is a pause from reading, the work of the hands ought to follow, because [0912B] idleness is the enemy of the soul, and the ancient enemy easily snatches into vices the man whom he shall have found free from reading or from prayer. For by the practice of reading you will learn both how you yourselves are to live and how you are to teach others; by the work of the hands and the mortification of the body you will both deny nourishment to vices and relieve your own necessities, and will have wherewith to hold out something to those suffering want.

*But when garments and shoes are necessary for the needy, let those under whose keeping the things that are asked for lie not delay to give them.*

This is what was said above, that they serve their brethren without murmuring. Lest at any time, by delaying to give what is necessary, they make the brethren murmur and be an occasion of sin. Let them therefore be eager to serve their brethren [0912C] for God's sake, and to minister necessaries at fitting times. And let them do this without murmuring, without sadness, without delay, with joy and cheerfulness, because *God loves a cheerful giver [n: (II Cor. IX)]* — so that they may have everything that is needful for them according to the manner of the congregation and the possibility of the place, and that for those who serve there may be a greater reward thence from the Lord. But in the clothing and the shoes of clerics a fitting measure must be kept, that they be not costly or glossy beyond measure, nor too abject and mean; having neither more trimness than is seemly, nor more untrimness than is right. Let them be such, then, as to commend at once religion and decency. Let the brethren, therefore, to whom necessaries are ministered, take care that they seek nothing [0912D] importunately, nor anything that does not befit their religion. For whatever superfluous thing they have is deadly to them. Let them therefore weigh that the things they spend are the offerings of the faithful for the redemption of sins. And therefore let them not glory in using such things, but rather fear what was said of the priests in the Old Testament, that they must bear the iniquity of the people. And therefore let them with great fear be solicitous over those whose gifts they share, because the danger is great. But it is more wholesome, as has already been said above, that the poor of Christ should always be in need of less than have more. For whence shall we be poor unless we bear penury for God's sake with an even mind? As much, therefore, as we remember that we have committed unlawful things, so much ought we to abstain from lawful ones. For through the narrow [0913A] gate we must enter, that we may be able to attain to the breadth of heavenly joys. For the straiter the life we lead here, the greater the glory we shall possess in heaven.

*But have no quarrels, or end them as quickly as possible, lest anger grow into hatred and make a beam out of a mote, and make the soul a murderer. For so you read: He who hates his brother is a murderer [n: (I Joan III)]*.

Thus do vices often grow little by little. Indeed, when we are unwilling to restrain the tongue from an idle word, we slip away into harmful ones; thence we sometimes fall into murmurings and detractions; very often even as far as quarrels and contentions; thence to anger and hatred; and, according to Solomon's word, while we neglect to correct the least things, we slide down to greater [n: (Eccli. XIX)]. While therefore [0913B] vice arises out of vice, iniquity is drawn out at length. Hence the prophet says: *Woe to you that draw iniquity with cords of vanity! [n: (Isa. V.)]* And the Psalmist: *They have lengthened out their iniquity: the just Lord will cut the necks of sinners [n: (Psal. CXXVIII)]*. First of all, then, sin must utterly be guarded against. For it is better to guard beforehand than to amend. Because if it happens that one sins through frailty, that fault is easily corrected which is quickly recognized. Hence it is that the Lord says of the woman to the serpent: *She shall crush thy head [n: (Gen. III)]*; the serpent's head is crushed when sin is corrected there where it is born. Each one, therefore, ought to be solicitous to be eager to amend himself at once there where he has known himself to have gone astray. As great as is the difference between a mote [0913C] and a beam, so great is the difference between anger and hatred. For hatred is inveterate anger, which has taken on so much, as it were from age, that it might deservedly be called old. For it can happen that, if you are angry with a man, you may also wish him to be corrected; but if you hate him, you cannot wish him to be corrected. By anger the eye of the mind is troubled; by hatred it is even extinguished. Whence the Apostle: *He who hates his brother is in darkness [n: (I Joan. II)]*. To no angry man does his own anger seem unjust. Whence from all indignation one must quickly return to the gentleness of meekness.
