## Flowers, or Sentences, from St. Bernard (cont.)

Gladly will I go without any spiritual gain, however great, which cannot be acquired except with scandal. *Epist.* 82.

It is a fair and wholesome order that you should first carry yourself the burden which you lay on another to carry. *Epist. 221, n.* 3.

The power of the voice is the deed; but prayer earns grace and effectiveness both for the deed and for the voice. *Ibid.*

A false Catholic does more harm than a true heretic. *Serm. 65 in Cant., n.* 4.

It is no unprofitable exchange to have left all things for the sake of him who is [1202B] above all things. *Tract. de cont. mun. ad cler., c.* 1.

The coveting of the world does more harm than its substance. *Ibid.*

The reason for fleeing riches is that they can scarcely, or never, be possessed without love of them. *Ibid.*

The human heart easily clings to everything it frequents. *Ibid.*

He who purposes to leave all things should remember to number himself also among the things to be left. *Ibid., n.* 2.

True love has its reward — but that reward is the thing loved. *Tract. de dilig. Deo, n.* 17.

If one of the people goes astray, he perishes alone; the error of a prince involves many. *Epist.* 117.

True joy is that which is conceived not from the creature, but [1202C] from the Creator. *Epist.* 114.

The vicious man does not shun the consciences of the vicious. I, *De consid., cap.* 10.

Where all are filthy, the stench of one is not noticed. *Ibid.*

To those who dwell on them, honors are alluring; to those who weigh them, they are a thing of dread. *Tract. de off. et mor. episc., cap.* 7.

True charity is not empty of reward, and yet it is not mercenary. *Tract. de dilig. Deo, n.* 17.

He keeps a good stronghold who guards his own body. *Serm. 2 de Assumpt., num 2. A common proverb.*

Misery alone is free from envy. *Serm 5 de verb. Isa.*

Cursed is he who makes his own portion worse. [1202D] Lib. I *De consid. cap. 5, et serm. de septem panib.*

He gives his hand to the seducer who makes a pretense of giving it to his teacher. *Serm. 77 in Cant.*

He who lets the sheep go into the pastures without a keeper is a shepherd not of sheep but of wolves. *Ibid., num.* 6.

The life of the poor is sown in the streets of the rich. IV, *De cons., cap.* 2.

Ambition, the cross of the ambitious, tormenting all, pleases all. III, *De consid., cap.* 1.

Trifles in the mouth of a layman are trifles; in the mouth of a priest they are blasphemies. Lib. II, *De consid., cap.* 15.

A vain heart forces the mark of vanity upon the body. *Apol. ad Guil., cap.* 9.

Soft garments indicate softness of mind. *Ibid.*

[1203A]The Church shines in her walls, and goes cold in her poor. *Apol. ad Guil., cap.* 11.

*1573* At the expense of the needy, the eyes of the rich are served. *Ibid.*

I fear the teeth of the wolf more than the rod of the shepherd. *Tract. de vita et off. episc., n.* 35.

Hippocrates teaches men to save their souls in this world; Christ, to lose them. *Serm. 35 in Cant.*

The pleasure of the throat, which today is reckoned at so high a price, scarcely takes up the breadth of two fingers. *De conv. ad cler., n.* 13.

The insatiable love of riches torments far more by desire than it cools by use. *Ibid.,* 14.

The use of riches falls to others, generally; to the rich falls only the name and the anxiety. *Ibid.*

[1203B]The demon lies in wait chiefly for perseverance alone, which alone of the virtues he knows to be crowned. *Epist.* 24.

A guard upon the mouth is always useful — provided it does not shut out the grace of affability. IV, *De consid., cap. 6, n.* 23.

The headlong tongue is to be bridled everywhere, but most of all at a banquet. *Ibid.*

That bearing is more fitting, if you are severe indeed in act, serene in countenance, and grave in word. *Ibid.*

There is no need of ladders for one who already holds the throne. V, *De consid., n.* 1.

To disparage, or to listen to one disparaging — which of these is the more damnable, I could not easily say. II, *De consid., n.* 22.

Deliberating foresight holds judgment in suspense. *Serm. [1203C] de S. Magdal., n.* 1, now in volume V.

Religious sorrow mourns either another's sin or its own. *Ibid., n.* 2.

[1204A]Learn from those things which he was made for you, how highly God has valued you. *Serm. 1 de Epiph.*

*1574* It is the utmost madness that we are unashamed toward obscene things, and blush at penitence: wrongly prone to wounds, and bashful toward remedies. *Serm. in Circumc.*

For the sake of preserving humility, the divine mercy is wont so to order things that the more a man advances, the less he reckons himself to have advanced. *Serm. 15 de div., n.* 4.

Both are pleasing to God: the sinner stricken with compunction, and the just man devout; but the ungrateful just man is as displeasing as the sinner untroubled. *Serm. de div.,* 4.

The healthy man does not know what the sick man feels, nor the full man what the fasting man suffers. *Tract. de grad. hum., n.* 6.

Humility is a glorious thing — with which pride itself also [1204B] seeks to cloak itself, that it may not become cheap. *Ibid., n.* 47.

Unhappy is that victory by which, overcoming a man, you succumb to a vice. *Exhor. ad mil., n.* 2.

The impious walk round about, naturally seeking that by which they may end their appetite, and foolishly spurning that by which they might draw near to the end. *De dilig. Deo, n.* 19.

The soul that loves God seeks no reward of its love other than God; or if it seeks something else, it loves that, not God. *Ibid., n.* 17.

By natural desire everyone seeks the highest good. Do you wish to arrive? Begin to leap across. *Tract. de cont. mun. ad cler., n. 33. For if you are entangled in the lowest things, you will not arrive.*

The way is winding, and cannot be walked; you will arrive more easily by despising all things than by obtaining them. *Ibid.,* 33.

[1204C]A wretched exchange, and full of the utmost madness: to shun human labor, and to choose everlasting gnashing with the devil. *Ibid.,* 27.
