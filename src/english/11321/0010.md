## 31.

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Again the Bridegroom withdraws from the bride, and hides himself in the secret of his heaven — he who was already thought to be held in the bed of conscience. ⟦s2⟧ Returning therefore, as she is wont, to the maidens, and keeping in her heart the tenor of her thought by which she had addressed the Bridegroom, likening him to the apple tree among the trees of the woods: *Under the shadow,* she says, *of him whom I had desired, I sat down, and his fruit was sweet to my throat [n: (Cant. II, 3)]*. ⟦s3⟧ The shadow between the deity of Christ and us is his body, under which the bride sits, while modestly and humbly she contemplates the mystery of his Incarnation: [0431C] and his fruit is sweet to her throat, because it does not pass into her belly, that is into satiety, as is that word of Job, *Nor dost thou suffer me to swallow down my spittle [n: (Job VII, 19)]*. ⟦s4⟧ For with the sacrament of the word of God there is scarcely one who can be satiated. ⟦s5⟧ We can taste, we cannot be satiated: because the healthier the taste, the stronger the appetite. ⟦s6⟧ Sweet therefore, says the bride, is his fruit to my throat, showing me from the sweetness of his example how much I must suffer for his name. ⟦s7⟧ Therefore I return to you, therefore I do not desert you. ⟦s8⟧ Nay more, *the king brought me into the wine cellar,* where I learned what the wise of the world know not, namely to be over others in charity. ⟦s9⟧ For the Bridegroom has, as it were, three treasuries of his riches: [0431D] a garden or paradise of pleasure, a cellar, and a house or bedchamber. ⟦s10⟧ The garden has things pleasant and things useful: the pleasant remain in the garden; the useful are carried down into the cellar: and from the cellar the king is served therewith in the house or bedchamber. ⟦s11⟧ For the garden contains the historical sense; the cellar, the moral; the house, the mystical. ⟦s12⟧ The garden contains the histories of the New and Old Testament, of the present and the future age; the cellar, the moral instruction of Christ; the house, the glory of the eternal reward. ⟦s13⟧ In the garden, the creation of all things that are; in the cellar, the reconciliation of all men who are saved; in the house, the confirmation and glorification of all the reconciled. ⟦s14⟧ The garden is divided into three times; the cellar, into three merits; the house, into three rewards. ⟦s15⟧ For the garden has three times, of planting, of maturity, [0432A] of gathering. ⟦s16⟧ The time of planting is as in Genesis and in the Prophets and the scriptures of the Old Testament, concerning the creation of heaven and earth, and the first beginnings of the world. ⟦s17⟧ The time of maturity is when in Christ and the Gospel the maturity of human conduct appeared, so that it is no longer said, *An eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth [n: (Deut. XIX, 21)]*: but, *Whosoever shall strike thee on the right cheek, offer him the left also [n: (Matth. V, 38, 39)]*; and many other things, which, though they are to be understood simply and as it were historically, yet pertain to the maturity of life. ⟦s18⟧ The time of gathering is the time of the future age, of which *280* we pronounce simply, and as it were historically: He who has done good things, shall go into life everlasting; but he who evil things, into death everlasting [n: (Matth. XXV, 46; Joan. V, 29)]. ⟦s19⟧ In the garden, the walking [0432B] is the overshadowing of examples. ⟦s20⟧ But the fruit of the garden is carried down into the cellar. ⟦s21⟧ In this garden, as was said above, the bride sat under the apple tree, while she humbly contemplated the historical series of the humanity of Christ; but his fruit would not have been sweet to her throat, unless she had entered the cellar. ⟦s22⟧ Whence at once there follows:

## 32.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *The king brought me into the wine cellar [n: (Cant. II, 4)]*. ⟦s2⟧ For the fruit of the garden, as was said, is carried down into the cellar: the pleasantness remains in the garden, the usefulness is to be sought elsewhere; if thou wilt find it, enter the cellar. ⟦s3⟧ It has three merits, as it were three cells of morality: the disposition toward prelates, which is discipline; the disposition toward equals, which is justice or friendship; the disposition toward subordinates, which is charity. ⟦s4⟧ And that we may say it more briefly [0432C] and more clearly, it is that one may know how to be under, to be alongside, to be over. ⟦s5⟧ The first cell is full of odors; the second, of savors; the third, of grace. ⟦s6⟧ For the first profits more by examples; the second, by moral or mystical studies; the third, by the charisms of the Holy Spirit. ⟦s7⟧ For this is the wine cellar. ⟦s8⟧ In it the friends drink, the most beloved are inebriated. ⟦s9⟧ For none before God is higher in rank than that of prelates, who, full of the wisdom of God, and bearing his stead among their subordinates, from the limit of possibility reach mightily to the limit of impossibility, and between these two dispose all things sweetly. ⟦s10⟧ These, for their brethren, according to the Apostle, wish to be anathema from Christ [n: (Rom. IX, 3)], but from Christ the beatitude, not from Christ the justice. ⟦s11⟧ For to us Christ is each, [0432D] both beatitude and justice; but so much the more gloriously do they acquire beatitude, as for beatitude's sake they are unwilling to forsake justice. ⟦s12⟧ For these words of the Apostle are of affection, not of effect. ⟦s13⟧ For this inebriation, by which the most beloved are inebriated when for the love of God and of the brethren one casts away his inmost things, this is the wine cellar, into which the bride merited to be brought, when she learned to be over others in charity. ⟦s14⟧ This cell is beside the house of the king. ⟦s15⟧ Often therefore thence she enters the house of the king, and the secret of the bedchamber, serving the Lord the Bridegroom, both by herself and through her subordinates, from the good things committed to her both of the garden and of the cellar. ⟦s16⟧ For whether prelate or subordinate, whoever has put on this affection of charity has free access hither. ⟦s17⟧ Whence the bride, above, when she desired to be drawn after the Bridegroom, and yet herself to draw the maidens [0433A] after her, saying, *We will run after thee to the odor of thy ointments:* at once, as she herself declares, *the king brought* her *into* these *cellars,* that thence she might receive wherewith to refresh them, lest they fail in the course; and in order, from the garden to the cellar, from the cellar to the king's bedchamber, he might bring them in; now indeed through the affection of contemplation and of love, but afterward through the effect of beatitude. ⟦s18⟧ Moreover the house has one reward of the Lord, save that through this it is divided into three rewards, that we may know, what, how great, in what manner; that is, what God is, body or spirit; how great, created or uncreated; in what manner he is to be found.

## 33.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *He brought me* therefore, says the bride, *into the wine cellar,* that is into the fullness of charity, in which, lest, [0433B] inebriated with the new wine of the Holy Spirit, I should do anything amiss when I returned to my subordinates, *he set charity in order in me [n: (Cant. II, 4)]*. ⟦s2⟧ Charity has two things, affection and act. ⟦s3⟧ Of the affection: *Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with thy whole heart, and with thy whole mind, and with all thy strength [n: (Matth. XXII, 37)]*; in which all kinds of affection are comprehended. ⟦s4⟧ Affection therefore is owed to God principally, secondarily to the neighbor, whom in God we ought to love as ourselves. ⟦s5⟧ For ourselves first, because to us is granted the primacy, that first we love ourselves, then our neighbors as ourselves. ⟦s6⟧ But to the charity of God there is owed a certain purgatory act, that is one purging us to the vision of God, such as are vigils, fasts, meditations, readings, compunctions: and an act is owed also to the neighbor, such as [0433C] the works of mercy, and the rest that the necessity of the active and social life demands. ⟦s7⟧ But the affection of God we ought in all things to prefer to the affection of the neighbor, yet not always the act of God to the act of the neighbor. ⟦s8⟧ But even in ourselves we ought often to prefer the act of the charity of the body to the act of the charity of the soul; and the act of the charity of the soul to the act of the charity of God, that is to contemplation. ⟦s9⟧ Yet all these things when necessity demands, which has no law. ⟦s10⟧ For there is a law of charity, and there is a necessity of charity. ⟦s11⟧ The law of charity commands the act of the charity of God to be preferred to the act of the soul, and the act of the soul to the act of the body. ⟦s12⟧ The necessity of charity often [commands] all things contrary to these. ⟦s13⟧ Hence it is that many say, few understand: Necessity has no law. ⟦s14⟧ There proceeds the order [0433D] and rule of charity, which commands one to love his own body and another's less than his own and another's soul; but to love his own soul and another's more than his own body or another's: his own soul not more than another's, but another's as his own. ⟦s15⟧ This as far as regards the affection of charity. ⟦s16⟧ But the act of charity itself is often ordered otherwise. ⟦s17⟧ For often, as was said, in the act the body is preferred to the soul, the soul to God. ⟦s18⟧ But if thou first succor thy brother, set with thee in the same necessity, of the same merit and usefulness, thou dost abound in charity; if thyself, thou dost not sin. ⟦s19⟧ For in that it is said *as thyself [n: (Matth. XXII, 39)]*, the primacy is granted to thee: but if thou be of unequal merit, the better is to be succored. ⟦s20⟧ And though we ought to love all equally, yet not without cause do we make private prayers [0434A] for particular ones: not that we are unwilling to profit all, but because we believe our prayers to profit especially those who commit themselves to us, or because our hearts are the more stirred up. ⟦s21⟧ And so in this order the Bridegroom *set charity in order in me.*
