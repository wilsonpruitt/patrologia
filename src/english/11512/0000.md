## Commentaries on the Canticle of Canticles

who work in me shall not sin, and who make me plain
shall have life everlasting [n: (Eccli. XXIV)].


## NUNCUPATORY EPISTLE

[1001]To the most reverend Father in Christ, the venerable Lord Henry Spichernagell, abbot of St Pantaleon and [1002] most deserving principal president of the holy Union of Barsfeld, and also to the very reverend and most ample [1003A] Lords Henry Libler, John Munch, Paul Vrechen, Henry Goermann and Gabel Scaffenius, most worthy abbots of the monasteries of the order of Saint Benedict of the same observance at Saint Martin, Brawiler, Tuitium, Gladbach and Graffscafft, his lords and prelates to be revered.

Your very reverend and most ample Lordships will wonder, and not without cause, why this commentator on the *Canticles of Canticles of Solomon* has lain so long under a bushel, and only now for the first time looks upon the light: to which, in all likelihood, I [answer] with that Jew Mordecai pressing his beloved Esther, and who knows whether you have come to the kingdom for this, that this Wolbero might be made ready at such a time? Wherefore I did not judge it enough for one soul alone, if such an author, [1003B] written, should only lie hidden in the house of St Pantaleon, unless, given over to the anvil, he should come forth into the light, opportune upon some new occasion, to profit the use and the spiritual taste of very many.

And what, you will say, is this occasion? It is now the third year since, at the time of the annual chapter celebrated in the monastery of Saint Pantaleon on the Kalends of September, sacred to blessed Aegidius the abbot, your consecrated heads too were newly adorned with the pontifical infula. By no words or writings am I able to comprehend how much joy grew from this for your dearest confreres, and with how great gladness I too was filled by it, in my own name as much as in the public one, and how heartily I desired to congratulate you, after I had understood that from our most holy lord [1003C] Urban VIII this episcopal authority had been superadded to your very reverend Lordships, and that pontifical mitres had been placed upon your heads in solemn order by the reverend lord bishop of Cyrene. For why should I not congratulate your Lordships on a glory so holy, so honourable and so new? a glory, I say, with which, beyond all ambition, as I am persuaded, you have been gifted and endowed by the sole merit of your most illustrious virtues (inasmuch as you are numbered among the chief pillars of our sacred Union of Bursfeld, and establish it by unswervingly supporting it)?

What was I to do? Filial love [1003D] demanded that I go down to acclaim this new honour of the reverend lord my prelate; gratitude required that I go likewise to meet [you], to venerate the tiaras of your very reverend Lordships; the examples of others provoked [me]. But I did not wish to appear empty in the sight of the highest priests, nor was there even a gift at hand that I might offer. When suddenly, God so inspiring, I remembered this commentator Wolbero, your co-abbot, and was delighted; and because very many of the religious, enticed heretofore [sic: *antebac*] by the reading of his most sweet interpretations, grieved exceedingly with me that commentaries so pious and profound lay hidden among the other furniture of our library, and that such a treasure was not communicated to more, I set about it with confidence, to testify my [1004A] readiness toward your very reverend Lordships and my zeal to deserve well, which I had long had among my wishes; and I not only reviewed the *Commentaries* of our Wolbero, but also, reviewed, arranged them into a better order, and I began to move every stone that, so arranged and renewed, and to be inscribed with the names of your very reverend Lordships, they might be put under the type. For the rest, since, as Solomon himself attests, of making many books there is no end, and so new things are wont to smile upon the wits of this age more than old ones, there was perhaps something I feared: lest either the antiquity of this commentator, or, more truly, the smallness of my own person, from which the book, not a little renewed, might not go forth, should be an obstacle, whereby it should pass less happily into the hands of many. [1004B] But, animated by the authority of your very reverend Lordships, I thought that I should easily be freed from a like fear, nay, from the detraction of all the Zoiluses, who are not wont to be wanting to good endeavours: both because into the Lord's storehouse and barn not always the hundredfold or sixtyfold, but more often the thirtyfold fruit is brought in; and because, to be protected by your Aaronic horns, it returns from its prelate at the same time, the course of the year having run, as that in which your abbatial almuces were changed into pontifical mitres.

Wherefore, most reverend lord president, my most revered prelate, receive thou this work, such as it is, of thy least client, superadded to my pastoral cares and hitherto drawn out [sic: *protacta*] by the various incursions of soldiers standing in the way; and because, according to the saying [1004C] of the Wise Man: *A man who loveth wisdom maketh his father glad [n: (Prov. XXIX)]*: gladden thy spirit, through this endeavour of mine, in the wisdom and spiritual canticle of this book, and refresh thy infirm and aged limbs, weakened by many labours endured for the monastery and our Union of Bursfeld, but not yet broken. Receive you also, venerable prelates, this small token of my regard toward you, Wolbero your co-abbot, humbly dedicated by me to your very reverend Lordships; and let him be a sharer in your new honour, who stood forth as the eminent narrator of the ancestral, and equally monastic, religion. That the same, under you as prelates, may grow and be propagated in the begun increase of spiritual discipline, [1004D] I will unceasingly entreat God, best, greatest, and our most holy lawgiver Benedict, and the patrons of the same order. May he also deign, by his most patient kindness, to keep your very reverend and most ample Lordships, with all committed to them, surviving and unharmed for as very long and as very happily as may be. So he cherishes and prays, from the pastorate of Laugel, on the very [day] of blessed Aegidius the abbot, in the year 1629.

Of your very reverend and most ample Lordships the humble servant and brother, Henry ERSMERS of Grave, a Pantaleonite, of the order of St Benedict.

## EPIGRAM TO THE AUTHOR AND THE READER OF THESE COMMENTARIES.

[1005A] That thou mayest outlast the times of the Pylian, most learned writer,
And that thy fame may be known in every place,
To commit this excellent work to the press has seemed
Fitting to many, and not without praise of thee.
Go forth then secure, Wolbero; it befits not
That the so pious writings of thy genius lie hid.
Whom the sweet love of composing canticles now touches,
[1006A]Lo, he has Wolbero, under whose leading he may compose.
Not such canticles did the Dircaean bard play,
When at the touch of the lyre the boar leapt up.
Wolbero, thou deservest; he sings a chaste life,
He tells the mystic words of the Bridegroom with the bride.
Go now, whoever thou art that lovest the nuptials of the soul and of Christ:
Whom thou mayest win to thyself, lo, the paranymph is here.


## BROTHER HENRY GRAVIUS TO THE KIND READER.

*The Commentaries of the venerable lord abbot Wolbero, although they were composed and inscribed by the author himself for devout virgins consecrated to God, who once so requested, and he wished them to lie hidden with the nuns alone, and not to come forth into the common light unless, corrected by someone, they had turned out a finished and masculine work: I would not have thee persuaded, kind reader, that thou hast obtained this very thing by my labour. For this is the virtue of those who truly think humbly of themselves: to do or to write things great and splendid, and nonetheless to think meanly and moderately of themselves and of what is theirs. In this kind of virtue our abbot Wolbero shines forth in no common way. in whose commentaries, most ancient indeed, but gathered by the assiduous study of nine years, I am so far from having met with anything shapeless, or womanish, or wanting a more correcting file (although in the dedication addressed to his religious sisters, and in other prefaces of this work, he seems to hint at this very thing), that this little work ought deservedly to seem conceived by a divine rather than a human spirit, and traced by the pen. Wherefore I would not have it hidden from thee, whoever thou art that shalt be the reader of this work, that I have added or mixed little of my own into these Commentaries, save where ambiguity of sense, looseness of periods and disjunction of verses had seemed to want some such thing. These perhaps were the things that might have offended a delicate reader in the ancient Wolbero. Since these have been sufficiently relieved by my labour and effort, [and] through I know not what carelessness or haste of printer or corrector some errors have crept in here and there, which it is permitted to see corrected at the end of this book, take this labour of mine, such as it is, in good part; and while thou enjoyest it, receive it to thy profit, and receive kindly this perpetual monument of this author. Farewell*.
