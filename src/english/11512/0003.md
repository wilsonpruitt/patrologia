## GENERAL PREFACE OF WOLBERO ON THE CANTICLE OF CANTICLES OF SOLOMON.

[1009]

[1009D]In Holy Scripture many canticles are spoken of, which, differing by a diverse kind of causes, have been sung now generally, now specially, according to place and time; place, because either in the fatherland or in a foreign land; time, because either in peace or in captivity. For there is spoken of the *Canticle of Moses, the Canticle of Anna, the Canticle of Habacuc the prophet,* or others of this kind; but all these are simply canticles. Here, however, they are called *Canticles of Canticles,* because they surpass all kinds of canticles by the majesty of mystical understanding.

And the other canticles, indeed, have a cause why they were [1010D] sung, as Moses sang the *canticle of praise* for the victory celebrated, when Pharaoh was drowned with his chariots and horsemen in the Red Sea. Anna too sang a canticle to the Lord for fruitfulness bestowed, the reproach of barrenness being taken away. Ezechias too sang a canticle for health restored to him. Therefore the several canticles were, for each one, a thanksgiving for a benefit conferred on him by God. But this canticle represents the harmony of that highest and perfect charity, by which, all the cares [sic: *curi*] of worldly things being lulled to sleep, every faithful soul [1011A] is joined to her Creator as a bride to the bridegroom with the ineffable sweetness of love; in whose embraces she dwells with such sweetness of peace and quiet that there is nothing she desires besides him, nothing she would set before so magnificent a grace; because certainly there is nothing that resists the spirit which, the war of the flesh trodden down, with the flesh subjugated to it, keeps sabbath in Christ in the same happy concord. For a great part, and a Sabbath to be desired, that is, rest, is when the flesh does not lust against the spirit, but, the tumult of the vices being stilled, the mind enjoys such sweetness of inward tranquillity that, desiring nothing else than the Lord, it wills and is able to say the *Canticle of Canticles*, that is, to have true and perfect charity. For charity towers above all the virtues, as the *Canticle of Canticles* [1011B] goes beyond all canticles. But ascents are to be disposed in the heart, so that, as by certain steps, one may arrive at singing the *Canticles of Canticles;* that just as he who enters the holy place still has need of many things to be able to enter the Holy of holies; and he who celebrates Sabbaths needs far higher things to be able to arrive at the Sabbaths of sabbaths: so he who sings canticles ought ever to advance to greater things, to be able to sing the *Canticles of Canticles.*

For seven canticles are found in the divine page, the causes of whose singing mystically indicate to us, as by certain steps, the progresses of the soul, which are ruled through the grace of the sevenfold Spirit. [1011C] For we must go out of the Egypt of this world and cross the Red Sea through baptism, or through the tears of penitence, that, the attendant of the vices being drowned, we may first sing the canticle with Moses: *Let us sing to the Lord, for he is gloriously honoured; the horse and the rider he hath thrown forth into the sea [n: (Exod. XV)]* [var: Vulg. *magnificatus est … dejecit*, "is magnified … hath cast down"]. Then, the tumult of worldly business being left behind, the spiritual solitude is to be walked through until we find the well which the fathers dug, by whose doctrines and examples being informed in the manner of living rightly, we may sing together the second canticle, which the children of Israel sang for the well they had found. *Let it ascend,* they sang together, *the well which the princes dug, and the leaders of the multitude prepared, in the giver of the law and with their staves [n: (Num. XXI)]*. And when we have come to the neighbourhood of the land of promise, [1011D] preparing ourselves to possess that blessed land, and inviting with us the spiritual and the carnal, let us sing with Moses the third canticle: *Attend, O heaven, and I will speak; and let the earth hear the words from my mouth [n: (Deut. XXXII)]* [var: Vulg. *Audite, caeli, quae loquor: audiat terra verba oris mei*, "Hear, O heavens, the things I speak: let the earth hear the words of my mouth"]. But that we may inhabit this land spiritually, the enemies of the soul must be fought down, against whom we must war with our whole intention, that, these being put to flight, we may sing together the fourth canticle with Debbora: *The remnants of the people are saved, the Lord hath fought among the mighty [n: (Jud. V)]*. The enemies then being overcome, let us sing to the Lord the fifth canticle with David, for our escape and deliverance, that canticle namely which David sang when he was delivered from the hand of all his enemies and from the hand of Saul, and said: *The Lord is my rock [1012A] and my strength and my saviour [n: (II Reg. XXII)]*, and the rest. After, namely, the motions of spiritual adversities having been repressed and all the cares of worldly things set aside, we shall at last have been placed in the longed-for quiet of mind, we must rise and hasten with the progresses of the soul to merit the embraces of that heavenly Bridegroom; the canticle of which intention we shall sing in the sixth place with Isaias: *I will sing to the beloved the canticle of my cousin, of his vineyard [n: (Isai. V)]*. When, namely, by the progress of this angelic conversation, we shall have flown up to such a height on the wings of holy contemplation that, dwelling now in the embraces of the Bridegroom himself, we can say with the Apostle: *But our conversation is in heaven [n: (Philip. III)]*. Then at last we shall sing the seventh canticle, [1012B] which is called not only a canticle but the *Canticle of Canticles*, when, resting from every worldly work, we shall have begun to keep sabbath with the bridegroom himself in continual quiet.

Now three things are to be considered in this book: the matter, the intention, the final cause. The matter is the espousals of Christ and the Church, or of each faithful soul, which consist in true and perfect love of him; which love, namely, is shown by the mortification of the vices and the delight of the virtues and the observance of his commandments. The intention is to exhort those who read and hear so to study to be united to Christ through charity that they may be worthy to be called by the name of bride. The final cause is thence to attain the highest good, which is God, of which good it is written: *That which neither [1012C] eye hath seen, nor ear heard, nor into the heart of man* have those things ascended *which God hath prepared for them that love him [n: (I Cor. II)]*.

It is to be heeded, namely, that in this book nothing is to be understood carnally according to the letter, but the whole is to be referred to the spiritual sense, because *the letter killeth, but the spirit quickeneth [n: (I Cor. III)]*. For because God, speaking of himself to the human heart, could not be grasped, he mercifully moves and recalls it through enigmas, and through known things secretly insinuates to it the love of unknown things, so that, since it had to be addressed allegorically, through the exterior things which it knew it might understand the interior things, which it ought to love and keep. For here words as it were of bodily love are set down, where kisses, breasts, cheeks, thighs are named; that the soul, shaken out of its torpor [1012D] by accustomed speech, may grow warm again, and through the words of the love which is below may be stirred to the love which is above. In which the sacred description is not to be made void nor despised, but the mercy of God is to be considered, and in this the ampler, because, to kindle us to the embraces of sacred love, he inclines himself even to the words of our infirmity. But whence he humbles himself in speech, thence he exalts us in understanding, because from the speech of this love we learn how we should serve in the love of the Godhead. Greatly, or skilfully, must we look to it, lest, when we hear exterior things, we remain in those exterior things by our perceiving, and the machine which is set to lift should itself rather press down and weigh down. We ought therefore in the exterior words to seek whatever is within, and, speaking of the body, to become outside the body; [1013A] we ought to come to the nuptials of the bridegroom and bride with the understanding of charity [nt: (to come with the wedding garment, lest, if we lack it, into the outer darkness (Matth. XXII) )] we be driven back into the blindness of ignorance.

It is to be known besides that Solomon put forth three books, the first of *Proverbs,* the second *Ecclesiastes,* the third the *Canticles of Canticles,* which are placed, each under each, beneath the three parts of philosophy, namely Physics, Ethics, Logic, that is, natural, moral, rational science. For in *Ecclesiastes* he disputes of the natural state of things; in *Proverbs* he discourses of the honesty of morals; in the *Canticles of Canticles* he sings of the rational disposition of divine mysteries. For by these three modes, as by certain steps, the progress of the mind is led, that is, by naturality, [1013B] morality, rationality. Naturality is the visible variation of things created in order; morality, the informing of an honest life; rationality, the desirable attainment of spiritual and divine knowledge.

Indeed, from the consideration of naturality, while it is heeded of how great beauty, of how great utility, how becomingly, how rationally, and how orderly the created things have been disposed, how unchangeably it keeps its appointed turns in place and time, it can be sufficiently weighed of what kind and how great, how much more beautiful, how much more sublime, how much more powerful, and therefore how justly to be worshipped, how worthily to be adored, is God, the founder of this creature. Whence the Apostle: *The invisible things,* he says, *of him from the creation of the world through the things which are made, being understood, are clearly seen, his eternal power also and divinity, [1013C] so that they are inexcusable [n: (Rom. I)]*. Therefore man will not be able to be excused, because by consideration of this kind he is led to the knowledge of the Creator, and to the faith which believes to be true the things which God promises to the just, threatens to the unjust; and from this he is pricked, first by fear, afterwards by love.

For first by fear he rises to the honesty of morality, until, by the progress of conversation, morality itself is taken up through charity, which *casteth out fear [n: (I Joan. IV)]*. For since man is more excellent than every earthly creature, and the creature itself is, as we have said, of such great beauty, surely he will be able to weigh how much more beautiful he will be, if through the comeliness of mortality he cleaves to his Creator. For to the image [1013D] and likeness of God man was made [n: (Gen. II)], who is compounded of two substances, that is, of soul and body: he has the likeness of God in this, that he lives and among the other creatures is called very good, raised up originally by the very bearing of his body for receiving heavenly things and trampling earthly ones. But because, just as God himself neither grows with growing creatures nor decreases, although he yet is in all; so neither is the soul diminished when the members are diminished, nor increased when they are increased, and yet it is in all.

But he has the image of God from the better portion, namely the soul, which thrives in wit, reason, understanding, in which the image of God is indeed shown, by which he is conformed to him through the honesty of morality, so that, *as* he bore *the image of the earthly* by loving earthly things, *he may bear [1014A] also the image of the heavenly [n: (I Cor. XV)]*, by ardently seeking out and exercising heavenly things. For it is not to be thought childishly that God, who is incorporeal and incomprehensible, can be comprehended by any bodily lineaments, so that he should have an image to which man was made; but his image is the reason implanted in the human mind, by which man himself differs from irrational things, and by which he discerns the just from the unjust, the honest from the dishonest, the useful from the useless, by which finally he represses the bestial motions of the mind, lest they corrupt the integrity of the mind; and if, some frailty impelling, they have corrupted it, he may reform it anew, the same reason leading, through the study of holy morality.

By which study, man being morally instructed, the grace of rationality worthily advances him, by which he may receive knowledge of himself [1014B] and of God: of himself indeed, by which he will weigh what he is, and whence the gift which he receives, that he is saved not by his own merit but by the grace of God, [and] has been taken up *in the liberty of the glory of the sons of God [n: (Rom. VIII)]*: but of God, by which he may understand that nothing is better, nothing more powerful, nothing more sublime than God, whom alone he ought to know, fear, venerate, love and worship with all the virtue of his mind. And this is *the wisdom which will not enter into a malicious soul, nor dwell in a body subject to sins, because the Holy Spirit of discipline will flee the deceitful, and will withdraw himself from thoughts that are without understanding, and will be rebuked by iniquity coming upon it [n: (Sap. I)]*.

Therefore the virtue of morality takes up the grace of rationality, because the mind which draws near to God through the honesty of discipline considers the more the subtlety of his knowledge [1014C], according to the voice of James, who exhorts his hearers, saying: *Draw nigh to God and he will draw nigh to you [n: (Jacob. IV)]*.
