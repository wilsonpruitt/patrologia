## CHAPTER ONE. (cont.)

Let the bride say, then: *Therefore the young maidens* *14* *have loved thee exceedingly,* because the minds of believers, still advancing daily in faith through grace, greatly love the sweetness of thy mercy, O beloved, since they can attain it for its sweetness by thy gift: by thy gift, I say, because without thee we can do nothing; and therefore I cry aloud, and invite all who love thee to cry aloud with me: *Draw me: after thee we will run,* etc.

VERS. 3.-- *Draw me: after thee we will run to the odour of thy ointments. The king hath brought me into his storerooms: we will exult and rejoice in thee, mindful of thy breasts [1028D] above wine: the righteous love thee.*

But whence comes such a proclamation, or petition, of the believing soul that sighs toward God? Surely from the consideration of her own infirmity, by which she weighs that without the aid of grace she can in no way suffice for herself; because, according to the Apostle, *it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that showeth mercy [n: (Rom. IX)]*. By which sentence free will is not destroyed, but rather it is shown that the help of divine grace is altogether necessary to free will, without which every will to work well will always lie ineffectual. For indeed, from free will anyone has the willing to come to God; not indeed that the willing itself is from himself, since *not even to think,* which is less than to will, *anything of ourselves, as of ourselves, are we sufficient [n: (II Cor. III)]*. [1029A] These are the words of the Apostle, who attributes everything that can be of good, likewise to think, *to will and to accomplish according to his good will [n: (Philip. II)]*, to God, not to his own choice. If, then, God, as to these three things, that is, to think good, *to will, to accomplish, works in* us, he does the first assuredly without us, the second with us, the third through us. For by sending in a good thought he goes before us; by imitating [cj: *imitando*; read *immutando*, "changing"] also the evil will, he joins it to himself through voluntary consent; by supplying to the consent the faculty or facility, the inward craftsman becomes known outwardly through our covered [cj: *opertum*; read *apertum*, "open"] work. Therefore *it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that showeth mercy [n: (Rom. IX)]*, when by the free gift of grace working within we have the willing; but we cannot be moved to the course without that same assisting grace; and when we shall be in the course, it will again [1029B] be of pitying grace that we may be able to arrive.

This drawing, then, which the bride asks for, is not the compulsion of a soul unwilling to follow God, but rather the leading of a willing one, which the Prophet prays for in the Psalm: *Lead me,* he says, *in the path of thy commandments, for I have willed it [n: (Psal. CXVIII)]*: as if he should say in other words: because by the gift of thy grace I have [sic: *nabeo*] the will to work well, lead me by the help of the same grace into action, that I may be able to arrive at the promise.

Such a drawing, indeed, is made in four ways, that is, through preaching, through infirmity or adversity, through most evident miracles, through persecution or striking; the kind Father intending this, that he may make [1029C] them willing *15* and not save them unwilling; so that, while he changes from evil into good, he may transfer the will, not take away the will. For no one is saved unwilling; for what is read in the Gospel: *No man cometh to me, unless my Father draw him [var: Vulg. Jo 6:44 *nemo potest venire ad me, nisi Pater, qui misit me*, "no man can come to me, unless the Father, who sent me"] [n: (Joan. VI)]*; likewise in another place: *Compel them to come in [n: (Luc. XIV)]*, is no obstacle; because assuredly, however many the loving Father, *who wills all to be saved [n: (I Tim. II)]*, may seem to draw or compel to salvation, yet he declares none worthy of salvation whom he has not first proved willing.

With this prerogative of divine dignity, indeed, the Maker singularly distinguished the rational creature: that, just as he himself was of his own right, and it was of his own will, not of necessity, that he was good, so also she too might exist in a certain manner of her own right in [1029D] this respect, so that only by her own will she might either become evil and be justly damned, or remain good and deservedly be saved; not that her own will could suffice her for salvation, but that she would in no way attain it without her own will.

The soul tending toward God, then, desires and prays to be drawn spiritually, not as someone unwilling is drawn, but as a blind or weary man, when he is drawn, is not grieved, but rather gives thanks to the one drawing him, as to one lifting him up and helping him; so she too, giving thanks to God, asks that he who gave the willing may give also the being able, to arrive by his help at that toward which she tends, saying: *Draw me after thee: we will run to the odour of thy ointments.*

Whether thou pronounce it thus: *Draw me after thee,* and, making the division there, [1030A] bring in *we will run to the odour of thy ointments;* or thus: *draw me,* so that thou make the division there, and then add, *after thee we will run to the odour of thy ointments,* it has the same force of sense, namely that to be drawn by Christ or to run after him is to follow his footsteps by believing and working.

*We will run,* she says. Who, or of what kind, is this course, as it ought to be? Assuredly not of the body, but of the mind; not a progress of the feet, but of conduct; of which progress, namely, the Psalmist was speaking, when he spoke of the blessed dwelling in the house of the Lord, who in this vale of tears dispose ascents in their hearts. *They shall go,* he says, *from virtue to virtue [n: (Psal. LXXXIII)]*. And what is the fruit of this course or progress? *The God of gods shall be seen in Sion [n: (ibid.)]*. For what [1030B] is the reward of this course, save true and eternal beatitude? And what is true and eternal beatitude, save the contemplation of that supreme and eternal and consubstantial Trinity? I will not run alone, then, but with thee, O Lord Jesus, holding my right hand, drawing and leading me, and as it were running with me, we will run.

By what way? Through thyself, assuredly, who hast said: *I am the way, the truth and the life [n: (Joan. XIV)]*; for through thee I will run to thee for thy sake. Through thee, who art the way, I will run ready; to thee, who art the truth, I will run secure; for thy sake, who art the life, I will run glad. For I will run ready because I have thee as helper; secure, because I do not doubt of the reward; glad, because I acknowledge [1030C] and believe that to know thee and to have thee is this reward of mine.

*16* So then I will run *not as at an uncertainty [n: (I Cor. IX)]*, but that I may lay hold. Of inestimable price is the gift, the things which thou hast promised to those who run in the stadium of this life through thee, and to thee, and for thy sake; which, namely, thou criest through thy prophet to be of such a kind that neither *eye hath seen, nor ear heard, nor hath it ascended into the heart of man [n: (Isai. LXIV)]*.

*We will run,* then, she says; but how? *In the odour of thy ointments.* [var: Vulg. Ct 1:3 *in odorem*, "to the odour"] Since the perfumes of the bridegroom's ointments are very many and diverse, and therefore not fragrant in one and the same manner, why is it that she says *in the odour* in the singular number, then *of ointments* in the plural, when she could rather have said, in the odours [1030D] of thy ointments? She attends to something, as I think, because she has considered diligently how and by whom those perfumes are spread. For what is that special and singular odour save charity? And every virtue, if it is without charity, is now no virtue, but rather a damnable vice; because all virtues ought to come forth from the root of charity, as the Lord shows in the Gospel, where he has admonished how the virtue of abstinence is to be held, saying: *But thou, when thou fastest, anoint thy head and wash thy face, that thou appear not to men to fast, but to thy Father who is in secret, and thy Father who seeth in secret will repay thee [n: (Matth. VI)]*. What is it to anoint the head, save to cherish the ruling part of the mind in good work through the sweetness of charity? And what to wash the face, save to show forth the habit of the inner [1031A] man, festive and glad from the joy of good works, through the sweetness of charity? Therefore neither abstinence, nor chastity, nor humility, nor obedience, nor any virtue is anything, or can be, without charity; because, although it may put forward the appearance of virtue, unless it proceeds from love of God or of neighbour, it is not only not a virtue, but even a detestable vice before God; and by this, while anyone takes from it human praise only, he deprives [himself] once for all of the reward of the divine gift. But the manifestation of this doubtful virtue will be made at the last judgment, when, according to the voice of Truth, *in* the *night* of that judgment, that is, in tribulation, *two shall be in one bed, two women grinding together, two in the field,* of which two, *one being taken* to life, *the other shall be left* to death [1031B] [n: (Matth. XXIV; Luc. XVII)].
