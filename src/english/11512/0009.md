## CHAPTER ONE. (cont.)

By these names of things, indeed, that is, of the bed, the mill, the field, three kinds of profession are denoted; because there are some who keep sabbath with the beloved in rest of mind, while many are occupied with the labours of the active life, and some cultivate the hearts of their hearers with the word of life; but in each profession an outcome of differing intention often appears. For in one bed, that is, in the rest of the contemplative life, which belongs chiefly to those who, separated from the cares of the world, give themselves to God alone in the pursuit of prayer or of sacred meditation, this twofold intention is sometimes found, while there are some who show this for human favour alone *17* or fear, but others practise it from the love and fear of God alone. So too in the active life either the favour of the world or the love of God compels certain men to labour [1031C]; and either the vice of boasting or the love of eternal life leads many to apply themselves to the word of preaching. Whence blessed Augustine, when he was speaking of charity *which dealeth not perversely [n: (I Cor. XIII)]*, which is the judge of this twofold intention: « See, he says, how great works pride does, how like it makes them, and as it were equal to charity. Charity feeds the hungry, pride feeds him too; charity that God may be praised, pride that itself may be praised: charity clothes the naked, pride clothes him too; charity fasts, pride fasts too; charity buries the dead, pride buries him too. All the good works which charity wills to do and does, pride drives against them, and as it were leads its horses [1031D]; but charity is more interior, it takes away the place of pride ill driven. » And after a little: « Do not, he says, attend to what flowers outside, but which root is within rooted is cupidity: there can be an appearance of good deeds, truly good works there cannot be. Charity is rooted, be secure, nothing evil can come forth. » In every good work, then, the mind of each one ought to burn with the fire of charity, so that, the virtues being kindled from it as it were like spices, it may send forth a most sweet smoke of fragrance. With such a fire of charity the Psalmist was burning when he said: *My heart grew hot within me, and in my meditation a fire shall flame out [n: (Psal. XVIII)]*. Whence also in the law it is commanded *that fire burn continually on the altar [n: (Lev. VI)]*. For what is the altar save our heart, in which we offer to God by intention whatever [1032A] good we do, or in which we immolate ourselves by the mortification of vices and the exercise of virtues. On this altar, then, fire ought to burn continually, because every good work, unless it proceeds from the ardour of charity, has no vigour. In another place also it is commanded *that oil be always in the house of the Lord for trimming the lamps [n: (Exod. XXXV; Levit. XXIV)]*. For by oil, which excels all liquids, charity is understood, which stands out above all the virtues. And by the lamps good works are understood, as the Lord says in the Gospel: *And burning lamps in our hands [var: Vulg. Lc 12:35 *in manibus vestris*, "in your hands"] [n: (Luc. XII)]*. And the house of the Lord, or temple, is the body or the mind of any saint, of which, the one and the other, the Psalmist: *Holy is,* he says, *thy temple, wonderful in equity [n: (Psal. LXIV)]*. And [1032B] elsewhere: *Holiness becometh thy house [n: (Psal. XCII)]*. The Apostle too, to the believers: *The temple,* he says, *of God is holy, which you are [n: (I Cor. III)]*. And elsewhere: *Know you not that your bodies are the temple of the Holy Spirit? [var: Vulg. 1Cor 6:19 *membra vestra*, "your members"] [n: (I Cor. VI.)]*

In the house of the Lord, then, there ought to be oil for trimming the lamps, because each faithful man who is the house or temple of God ought to have charity as the matter by which he may be kindled to the pursuits of piety.

Fittingly, then, the beloved speaks to her Bridegroom, saying: *We will run to the odour of thy ointments;* because assuredly anyone labours in vain in the course of good works if he is not at all fragrant with the odour of charity.

But in a course of this kind four things are to be considered, [1032C] namely the manner, the quality, the intention, the usefulness. *18* Now this course is nothing other than the life of each saint. And the perfect manner of running, that is, of living, ought to be such for each one that there be nothing above, nothing below, but all things be paved with charity in the midst [n: (Cant. III)], according to the footsteps of the preceding Fathers, as Scripture commands: *pass not beyond,* it says, *the bounds which thy fathers have set [n: (Prov. XXII)]*, of whom the Psalmist says that *the Lord knoweth the way of the just [n: (Psal. I)]*; as if he should say: Since the way of those is known to God and pleases him, who, doing nothing outside discretion, hold their life in the even balance [sic: *liberamine*] of justice. For to those who will or do anything above and beyond what is just the Apostle commands *not to be more wise than it behoveth to be wise, but to be wise [1032D] unto sobriety [n: (Rom. XII)]*. But of those who fall short below or grow lukewarm it is written: *Cursed is everyone that doeth the work of the Lord deceitfully [n: (Jerem. XLVIII)]*. And those have their pursuits paved with charity in the midst who, like those four living creatures full of eyes before and behind, whose feet, straight feet, with two wings [n: (Ezech. I)] of the twofold charity, of God, namely, and of neighbour, are both lifted up and diminish their bodies, that is, their works, so that they neither withdraw from love of neighbour through the contemplative life, nor fall away from the charity of God through the active. On both sides they hold so discreet a manner of living that the perfect rule of justice does not admit whatever may be above or below. Whence a certain wise man of this world says: [1033A] *There is a measure in things; there are, in short, fixed bounds,*
*Beyond and short of which the right cannot stand.*
[n: (HORAT., Satir. I, I, 107.)]

Because, then, every weight greater and less displeases God, each one's life is to be weighed with such evenness that he neither collapse under too great a weight, nor appear negligent and slothful under a very slight one.

And the quality of this course, or of the runner, is to be attended to, namely that it be such as the Apostle describes, *in all things,* he says, *let us show ourselves as the ministers of God, in much patience, in tribulations, in necessities, in distresses, in stripes, in prisons, in seditions, in labours, in watchings, in fastings, in chastity, in knowledge, in long-suffering, in sweetness, in the Holy Spirit, in charity [1033B] unfeigned, in the word of truth, in the power of God, by the armour of justice on the right hand and on the left, by honour and dishonour, by evil report and good report, as deceivers and yet true, as unknown and yet known, as dying and behold we live, as chastised and not killed, as sorrowful always, yet rejoicing [var: Vulg. 2Cor 6:10 *quasi tristes, semper autem gaudentes*, "as sorrowful, yet always rejoicing"], as needy, yet enriching many, as having nothing and possessing all things [n: (II Cor. VI)]*.

Behold with what motions of the soul this course is expressed, which is accomplished by the labour of both men, that is, of body and of spirit, so that after that course is completed, as in both there was labour and suffering, so in both there may at length be rest and consolation, which is the beatitude of the double stole.

Now the wise men of this world say that there are four [1033C] species of quality, *19* of which the first is habit and disposition; which, while they are one species of quality, differ in this, that disposition is a habit easily noble [cj: *nobilis*; read *mobilis*, "movable"], but habit a disposition remaining for a very long time. The second species of quality is that which comes about according to power and impotence: according to power, that it can easily be bent naturally into any accident whatever; but according to impotence, that it cannot not [cj: *non flecti*; read *flecti*, "be bent"] be bent lightly into anything else whatever. The third species is that which they call passible quality and passion, which two they distinguish within one species thus, saying that passion is that which, having arisen for a time, is easily changed; but passive quality, according to which we are said to be of such a kind, perseveres [1033D] perpetually. This species indeed seems similar to the first, and as it were the same, but it differs in this, that passion is a momentary reception of something; while disposition, which they also call affection, is the enlarged passion of that same accident. Further, habit, since it is perfected by very many things and by a certain order, is called augmented affection; but passible quality is a suddenly inborn and permanent passion. The fourth species they placed in figures and forms, calling figure certain outlines of things, but forms the beauties and comelinesses of those outlines. These species of quality are considered in the course of each one's life according to the diversity of will and purpose, in which is contained the quality of applying the mind in whatever manner to some work

[1034A]For through disposition or affection some pass into habit, when some, from occasion or necessity, turn certain good works they have begun into custom by the progress of their way of life, and by a certain delight in the virtues always gain increase, as is said of the saints: *They shall go from virtue to virtue [n: (Psal. LXXXIII)]*. Further, according to power and impotence, there is no one who, in this course of a life to be well ordered, does not have the power through the good of the reason granted him, and the grace that assists free will; to which grace he does not have the inability to assent and to labour together. And according to the third species, that is, passion and passible quality, there are many who, when they hear the word concerning the punishment of the wicked and the glory of the good, are pricked for a time [1034B] and grow warm with love of good work; but because this charity is not rooted in them, they grow cold in the time of temptation. But there are some who, when once they hear the word of God, are kindled to the practice of good work, and because they are *rooted in charity* and made firm [n: (Ephes. III)], they do not fall away from the love once conceived, but always direct the step of good work even to the end of life. Or we may say it is passion when the mind for an hour as it were suffers through humility, patience, obedience, by struggling against its own will, not another's, but this very rarely. But passible quality, *20* when by a permanent passion of voluntary mortification, through the aforesaid or any other virtues, it is worn down daily, consenting to no pleasures of this world. But according to the fourth species, which they place [1034C] in figures and forms, this quality of life can be considered, which is established according to the example and form of some life, as James admonishes his disciples, saying: *Take, brethren, the example of labour and patience through the prophets who spoke to you in the name of the Lord [var: Vulg. Jac 5:10 *exitus mali, laboris, et patientiae, prophetas, qui locuti sunt in nomine Domini*, "of suffering evil, of labour and patience, the prophets who spoke in the name of the Lord"] [n: (Jac. V)]*. And Paul: *Be ye followers,* he says, *of me, as I also am of Christ [n: (Phil. III)]*.

That quality, then, by which affection passes into habit, and by which through the grace of God willing and being able are present, and which in good work is not momentary nor of an hour but perpetual, or which takes the form of the preceding Fathers, is to be attended to with the greatest care by everyone who wishes to run well, so that according to it he may become a kind of mirror of honourable conduct and discipline, by which he may gain both the reward of his own labour and the salvation of those who imitate him, and the profit [1034D] of his neighbours.

And the intention of running in this way, that is, of working rightly, will be this: that no desire of vainglory creep into the mind, but that the love of God or the love of eternal life be the cause of labour and watchfulness of this kind.
