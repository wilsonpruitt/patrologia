## CHAPTER ONE. (cont.)

Now this intention is as it were a certain eye of the mind, of which the Saviour says in the Gospel: *If thy eye be single, thy whole body will be full of light: but if it be evil, thy body also will be dark [n: (Matth. VI)]*. And in this same *Canticle of canticles* the bridegroom speaks to the bride: *Behold, thou art fair, my love, behold, thou art fair, thine eyes are doves' [n: (Cant. V)]*. Sometimes certain works are done which are interpreted in the bad sense by those who [1035A] do not know from what intention they proceed; just as, on the contrary, certain things are regarded in the good sense which are disapproved by the Inspector on high, because they are brought forth with an evil intention; and these are not only not rewarded, but rather are condemned, just as those things too which are judged worthy of condemnation by the ignorant are rather rewarded by the just judge. Whence the Lord also says of the reprobate through the prophet: *This people honoureth me with their lips, but their heart is far from me [n: (Isa. XXIX)]*. All good things, then, are directed according to this eye of intention, so that they are worthy either of reward or of condemnation: of reward, if they are done for God; of condemnation, if for human favour.

But what the profit of this course or labour is, the Apostle sets forth, saying: *The sufferings [1035B] of this time are not worthy to be compared with the glory to come that shall be revealed in us [n: (Rom. V)]*. And the Lord in the Gospel cries out to those who run in such a way, saying: *Come to me, all you that labour and are burdened, and I will* make you to rest [var: Vulg. Mt 11:28 *reficiam vos*, "I will refresh you"] [n: (Matth. XI)]. The Apostle too, understanding that he had not run in vain, after he declares the course of his labour, saying: *I have fought a good fight, I have finished my course, I have kept the faith,* *21* goes on to promise himself confidently the fruit of that very labour: *as for the rest, there is laid up for me a crown of justice which [sic: justitiaequam] the just judge will render to me against that day [n: (II Tim. IV)]*.

The profit, then, great and inestimable, is the very fruit of eternal life, which in the life to come is bestowed eternally, without any failing, on those who labour for God; [1035C] but because it is granted to the Church, or to any faithful soul, to foretaste the fruit of this profit in the present life also, it follows, saying: *The king hath brought me into his storerooms.*

This bringing in has no small dignity, if both the person of the one who brings in and the place and the manner of the bringing in are rightly considered. For it is not just anyone, but a *king*, and not only a king, but the King of kings, who brings in; nor only into the house, but also into the storerooms; nor as it were abruptly, but this bringing in is done in a fitting manner of reason. For there are many who enter the house of this king, that is, the Church, but very few who can have entrance into the storerooms. Some can keep watch before the doors of these storerooms, they are able to look in, but they do not prevail to go in [1035D] unless he brings them in *who hath the key of David, who openeth and no man shutteth; shutteth and no man openeth [n: (Apoc. III)]*.

But it must be considered what these storerooms of so great a king are. All Holy Scripture, which is divided into two Testaments, namely into the law and the Gospel, is the repository, or storehouse, of the riches of this king. And this is divided threefold, that is, into history, allegory and morality. For this King, wise, merciful, and ruling and cherishing with great kindness and with his providence, established such a manner for his riches that there should be in them that which, according to the capacity of diverse understandings, both the strong might seek and the weak might not shun.

For many delight in history, very many [1036A] are fed by allegory, some are instructed by morality. But those whom this king deems worthy of so great a grace of his that he grants them entrance into all these storerooms, so that, namely, from historical knowledge they enter into the allegorical, and from the allegorical into the moral: with what sweetness, thinkest thou, are these fed? with what torrent of the Lord's pleasure are they made drunk? [n: (Psal. XXXV.)] To such indeed the Lord promises through the prophet, saying: *Behold, I will send you corn, wine and oil, and you shall be filled with them [n: (Joel. II)]*.

Desirable are storerooms of this kind, in which the delights of these kinds are contained, so that bread strengthens the heart of man, wine gladdens the heart of man, oil makes the face cheerful [n: (Psal. CIII)]. For we receive corn from the Lord when, in the more obscure sayings, [1036B] the covering of the letter being drawn away, we perceive the inward things through the marrow of the spirit of the law. The Lord supplies us his wine when he inebriates us with the lofty preaching of his Scripture; he bestows his oil on us also when he orders our life with gentle mildness by the plainer precepts.

*22* It is pleasing to attend still more fully, and reverently [sic: *adreverenter*], to why the divine storehouses contain these kinds rather than others. For if thou considerest that one grain of wheat which, having died in the earth, brought forth much fruit [n: (Joan. XII)], and attendest to the glorious greatness of the numerous harvest springing forth from it, thou wilt see how magnificent the examples of distinguished works that shine out from the historical storehouses, how sweet the marrows of the mysteries [1036C] that breathe their fragrance from the allegorical storerooms, how useful the teachings of the precepts that shine forth from the moral repositories.

A most beautiful comparison of Christ in the grain of wheat: for in the grain the marrow is covered by the husk: what then is figured by the husk but the flesh of Christ? what by the marrow but his divinity? In the grain, then, the marrow is covered by the husk; in Christ the divinity is hidden by the flesh: the husk and the marrow make one grain, the flesh and the divinity make one person, Christ, God and man. When therefore thou hast looked into all the things that were done concerning this grain of wheat, that is, Christ, God and man: namely, how this grain, descending from the heavenly granary, fell into the earth and died, then, rising again [1036D] and ascending into heaven, being multiplied in the apostles and the other believers, filled the whole world with the corn of faith and justice; how, with the wine of preaching overflowing, he restrained offenders with the severity of correction, and soothed penitents with the oil of charity and mercy; for in this manner he who had fallen among robbers was healed by that Samaritan of the Gospel [n: (Luc. X)]. When, therefore, thou hast read and understood all these things, some historically, some allegorically, and some morally, now separately, now together, in this same threefold manner, and hast been able by the grace of God to minister the same to others in the same manner, wilt thou not be able, for so great a benefit conferred upon thee, to say gratefully: *The king hath brought me into his storerooms.*

But what ensigns of the virtues have shone more greatly and more perfectly [1037A] than in this grain, that is, in this Lordly man? Surely, whatever the saints have of the bowels of mercy, whatever of meekness of spirit, whatever of the heaven of uprightness, whatever of the keeping of humility, whatever of the fervour of charity, this they have drawn from that very fountain of mercy, from that very root of meekness, from that very power of justice, that is, from the very mediator of God and men, the Lord God.

More, then, and as it were principally, these three kinds are held in the Lord's storehouses; and whatever breathes forth from the abundance of the Old and New Testament is either wheat, which strengthens and confirms those perfect in faith and in the exercise of the virtues; or it is wine, which either soothes the saints with the sweetness of the heavenly promises, or *23* deters sinners by the severity of the divine judgment; [1037B] or it is oil, which is either poured out in the hearts of the saints through charity, or consoles penitents through mercy. Whence holy Church, or each faithful soul beloved of God, considering in mind such treasures of her bridegroom, her king and her Lord, better than all the precious furniture of all kings and princes, and understanding what mysteries or sacraments are contained in them, can rightly chant with the joyful voice of the soul: *the king hath brought me into his storerooms.* And such a bringing in is indeed commendable enough from the person and the place; but the manner of this bringing in is this, that it is not done for an hour, but with the right of possessing, and as it were under the drawing up of a dowry, so that, so great a grace [1037C] having been conferred, there may be the assigning of a spiritual and most holy dowry, whereby the coupling of the divine and the human mind may be confirmed.

This admirable bringing in having therefore been celebrated, there follows the fitting giving of thanks for the gift conferred, which the mind gives to God, joined to him and sharing in the goods designated for her by him, with the motion of both the inner and the outer man, *we will exult,* saying, *and we will rejoice in thee.*

Exultation and gladness seem in some manner to differ from each other, so that the description of each is such that gladness is said to be a motion of the soul only, but exultation of body and soul.

The holy soul, then, perceiving in herself the gifts of divine grace, ought to receive and keep them with gladness and exultation, and to exercise in work what she perceives in mind: [1037D] for because « the proof of love is the showing of work (Gregory); » assuredly the mind joined to God through charity, while it is glad inwardly over the grace of understanding conferred on it, ought also to exult outwardly unto the works of the virtues. Whence also, in the likeness of the children playing in the market place, the apostles cry out to those who would do no good works at their preaching: *We have sung to you, and you have not danced [n: (Luc. VII)]*. For they desired their hearers to have the dance of the spirit, in order to show that faith without works is idle [n: (Jac. II)].

This religious dance, then, David the king prefigured, playing and leaping before the ark of the Lord, because in the spirit he foresaw from afar that through Christ, who was to be born of his flesh, that sacrifice of the Lord, not made with hands but [1038A] heavenly, was at some time to be established by faith and works. For this most beautiful play of holiness is the wonderful Prophet bore before him in his own prelude, fittingly and becomingly; for so great a grace of honour to come, which was to be administered and confirmed through Christ, his son according to the flesh, he rejoiced and exulted before that typical sacrifice of the Lord. And because he was the ruler and leader of the people of God, by such a deed of his he taught beforehand the rulers of the Churches, who ought to be over those subject to them by word and example, that before the ark of the Lord, which is his Church, they ought to dance with a spiritual dance, namely with a motion of soul and of body; so that, according to the Apostle, they may present their *bodies* *a living sacrifice, holy,* *24* *pleasing to God [n: (Rom. XII)]*: being a sacrifice indeed by the mortification of vices, [1038B] but living by the showing of good works. And this before the ark of the Lord, *that they may see their good works and glorify their Father who is in heaven [n: (Matth. V)]*.

Since therefore *with the heart one believes unto justice, but with the mouth confession is made unto salvation [n: (Rom. X)]*, which is to be moved in soul and body, it is certain that the one cannot be without the other; whence the prophet exhorts: *Lift up,* he says, *your hearts with your hands to God [var: Vulg. Lam 3:41 *Levemus corda nostra … ad Dominum in caelos*, "let us lift up our hearts … to the Lord in the heavens"] [n: (Thren. III)]*. For to lift up the hearts with the hands is to believe and to work; for he who believes and does not work lifts up the heart indeed, but does not lift up the hands: but he who works, but does not have faith, lifts up the hands indeed, but does not lift up the heart.
