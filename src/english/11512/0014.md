## CHAPTER ONE. (cont.)

The place, then, where God feeds those whom he feeds is the meadows or green places of the virtues, and also the pastures of spiritual doctrines, where it is found and recognized who are the sheep of the Lord and who are not, because *they that are according to the spirit mind the things that are of the spirit; but the sensual perceiveth not the things that are of the Spirit of God [n: (I Cor. II)]*.

Now these pastures in which God feeds whomsoever he feeds are not uniform, because of a twofold [1049D] manner, namely of living and of teaching, although in each case they are in themselves indeed the same: for the one is called good life, the other good knowledge; yet in the subjects in which they are, and of which they are predicated, they are diverse because of the diverse distributions of graces; because one indeed is predicated humble, another gentle or mild; this one patient, that one obedient; another chaste, and another merciful or generous, or any other thing of this kind that pertains to the manner of living well. But in the other part, which is good knowledge, it is predicated of this subject that he is a good orator or preacher; of that one, that he is a good interpreter or expositor; of another, that he is skilled in the liberal arts, or any other thing that belongs to the manner of teaching well.

[1050A]The things predicated particularly also admit of more and less, while on this side one is more patient than another, one more chaste than another, or anything else that can befall an honest life more and less Further, on the other side, one is more learned than another, one more eloquent or more erudite than another; or anything else that can befall a knowing soul also, more and less.

Although, then, in pastures of this kind, in which God feeds what he feeds, not simply but manifoldly, there is the diversity which we have spoken of, and in that very diversity more and less occurs, yet those who are fed, although by reason of the diversity and measure of gifts they are not fed equally, are all nevertheless the sheep of the Lord and are so called; because, as, though star differs from star in glory, yet all the stars [1050B] are contained under one firmament, so there are indeed many differences of the saints, because of the different virtues that are in them, yet all are known to belong to the one kingdom of God, *37*. Whence, when the Lord said through the prophet: *Behold, I myself will seek my sheep and will visit them, as the shepherd visiteth his flock in the day when he shall be in the midst of his sheep that were scattered,* he adds a little after: *in the rivers and in all the habitations of the land and on the mountains of Israel shall be their pastures [n: (Ezech. XXXIV)]*. The rivers come forth from the springs; but the pastures of the land are lowly, those of the mountains are lofty

What then is meant by the rivers but certain deeds and sayings which flow forth from the great Fathers as from springs? [1050C] And what by the pastures of the land but the lighter teachings of the divine precepts? And what by the pastures of the mountains but the lofty sacraments of the divine mysteries? God has therefore set before his sheep, great and small, weak and strong, pastures befitting both, according to the capacity of both, so that both the small and the weak may have that whereby they may be nourished and strengthened unto eternal life, and the great and strong that whereby they may advance and be filled. In such sheep of his, surely, which he feeds, and in which he feeds, in this manner, God prepares rest for himself, so that by the grace of his indignation [cj: *indignationis*; read *dignationis*, "condescension"] he may lie and rest in them, in whom he is then said to lie and rest when he takes delight in their devout acts. Whence through the prophet: *Upon whom,* he says, *shall I rest but upon the humble and the quiet and him that trembleth at [1050D] my words? [var: Vulg. Is 66:2 *ad quem autem respiciam, nisi ad pauperculum, et contritum spiritu*, "to whom shall I look, but to the poor little one and the contrite of spirit"] [n: (Isa. LXVI.)]* On the contrary, he is said to labour in the minds of the reprobate when he is as it were wearied by their depraved acts. Whence, when the prophet said to certain perverse men: *You have made the Lord to labour with your words;* and they answered: *Wherein have we made him labour; in this* he says, *that you have said: Every one that doeth evil is good in the sight of the Lord, and such please him [n: (Malach. II)]*. The Divinity, then, which is driven away from the hearts of the wicked, exasperated and in a manner wearied by their perverse acts: *For the holy Spirit of discipline,* as it is written, *will flee from the deceitful, and will withdraw himself from thoughts that are without understanding, and he shall be rebuked when iniquity cometh in [n: (Sap. I)]*, whose repulsion is their rejection, it assuredly, in the minds of the saints, by whose devout acts [1051A] it is invited and received, sweetly and as it were delightedly lies and may rest. whose reception is their election.

The Church, then, or the holy soul, asks that it be shown her where the Bridegroom feeds and where he lies, and this not at any time whatever, but in the midday. Since in this place midday ought to be taken only according to anagogy, it is pleasing first to consider what, or in what ways, day is said, and how many and what the parts of the day are, so that, these things being known beforehand, we may leave to another's judgment which more ready sense the diverse signification contains.

Now day seems not to be said simply, because of the diverse forms of its signification, so that, because of the community of the name but the diversity of its reason or definition, it holds the force of equivocation.

[1051B] *38* Let us suppose, then, that there are four forms of day, of which the first indeed may be called the natural day, but the following one, established after the form of the former, may not absurdly be named the active day. The third, which consists by the light of reason alone, may not unfittingly be called the intellectual day; further, the last, which never fails and alone lacks time, and stands fixed in its own immutability, may most fittingly be written the eternal day; and the one indeed is the support of human life, the second the pattern for ordering life, the third the mirror for discerning life, and the fourth and last the reward of a life well consummated.

Let us establish that each of these is divided into three parts, namely into morning, midday and evening, excepting only the last, which always was without time, [1051C] and is and will be: for its being and its incomparable beauty is God himself.

And the first indeed, which is the natural, pertains nothing to the present place, except that the other two only, the last indeed, as we have said, being without time, are established after its form.

In the natural day, then, morning is the first rising of the sun, which, increasing little by little as it ascends, makes midday about the middle of the day, which comes about at that place beyond which the sun, not advancing, is fixed as it were at a centre; and thence again, decreasing as it descends, it makes evening about the end of the day, which is the full setting of the sun.

Now after the form of this day is ordered that one which we have called the active, from the beginning of the world up to its end, whose morning was that time before [1051D] the law in which man used the natural law only; its midday under the law and grace, when he was instructed by both the natural and the written law; further, its evening the time of the last judgment, when he will be judged both for the natural and for the written law, whether unto life or unto death.

For before the law, through the natural law, he had morning, that is, a certain beginning of discernment, with reason as guide, by which he could see and recognize how far he excelled the brute animals, when he could discern good and evil; but above all, to consider and understand that no creature can create itself, but that there is a certain uncreated thing which commanded all things that have been made to be made, and to perceive, from the beauty and usefulness of the creature itself, how much more excellent and glorious that is than all created things, [1052A] and for this reason that he would be inexcusable if he should pay to the creature the honour due to the Creator.

But when that natural law had in some been overwhelmed by the custom of sinning, the written law was given, which should forbid and restrain sinners, from which the merit of each might be established, while either the keeping of it was the cause of reward, or the transgression of it the cause of damnation. But in some *39* that natural law itself so flourished that they had no need of the written law, since they were a law to themselves and showed the work of the law written in their hearts, and, being without the law, did the things that are of the law, their conscience bearing them witness in the day when God shall judge the secrets of men [n: (Num. II)]. But where to the natural law [1052B] the written law was added, after the morning there came as it were midday, because those upon whom the sun of justice had now risen, the commandment having been given them, whether of truth or of the new law, were on both sides kindled with so great a fervour of charity towards God that they would rather choose to die than to transgress the honourable law prescribed to them by God.

This one may see in those most fervent zealots of both laws who had the strongest zeal, whether for defending or for binding themselves by oath to the holy law; of whom, for example, one in the old law was Phinees the son of Eleazar, who vindicated the law by slaying those who had become transgressors of the law: Mathathias also and his sons, who defended the law, and the seven brothers, who chose rather to die by keeping the law than to live by transgressing it. What [1052C] then shall we say of those most glorious and most valiant fighters in the new law, namely the apostles and their followers, in whom there was so great a fervour of divine charity, so great a zeal for rightness, that it did not hesitate for the faith, for justice, for the truth to bear all adversities, to undergo every kind of death, and finally to fall in death itself? Of whom the Apostle says: *The saints,* he says, *had trial of mockeries and stripes, moreover also of bands and prisons, they were stoned, they were put to death by the sword [n: (Hebr. XI)]*. Were not all these, under the law or under grace, in the midday, when they bore so great a heat of temptation in body and heart, the great heats, surely, of that midday burning doubly, because both their mind burned with the fervour of holy charity and their body [1052D] was afflicted with the heat of much tribulation.

The evening, too, which is the time of the last judgment, ought to decree for such veteran recruits refreshment and rest for the heat of their labour, to whom, by the heavenly king for whose love they have toiled, there will be given the bounty of eternal reward, namely that most general, unfailing and unchangeable good which is the God of gods himself. For this is God's rewarding: to give himself to his elect.

Further, that intellectual day consists not unsuitably of the same parts which we set down above, because in the understanding of each one who flourishes in the splendour and heat of the virtues, the beginning of a good work is as it were a certain morning; midday, the perfecting of the work well begun through the increase [1053A] and progress of holiness; and evening, the best completion, even to the end, of the work well begun and better perfected. This day in each wise man is the understanding itself, in that he judges and discerns the things that are about him *40* whether in thought or in work, because he is in the light and can see what he ought to do or what to avoid; *he* moreover *that walketh in darkness knoweth not whither* he goeth [n: (Joan. XII; Isa. XXIX, L)]; because the darkness has blinded his eyes, and therefore his works are works of darkness, who, since he has not this day, that is, understanding, is said to differ little from irrational things, because of certain irrational and as it were bestial motions of depraved acts.
