## CHAPTER ONE. (cont.)

The first beginnings, as it were, of the light of this intellectual day are [1053B] the first works of uprightness, still small and slight. But when they have grown by assiduity, and the soul has conceived a certain heat from them, that heat little by little taking increase, it blazes up into the whole fullness of divine charity, as into the midday of this day, when a clearer light of understanding is opened, so that now nothing in earthly things pleases it, but it glows wholly in the love of God. Whence the Psalmist, to describe the morning and the midday of this day, says: *My heart grew hot within me, and in my meditation a fire blazes out [n: (Psal. XXXVIII)]*; as if he said openly: Long since, through the desire of good work, my mind grew hot; but because by the meditation of this, of the commandments of God, it is daily led by contemplation from the lowest things to the highest, it no [1053C] longer only grows warm, but burns wholly with the fire of charity in the love of its Creator.

But the evening of such a day is the consummation of good work even to the end of life, nor will the preceding parts be able to confer anything. If this last be lacking, *for he that shall persevere unto the end, he shall be saved [n: (Matth. XXIV)]*. Whence the Psalmist: *The lifting up,* he says, *of my hands, an evening sacrifice [n: (Psal. CXL)]*; as if he said in other words: The beginning or the progress of my works is then a sacrifice to God, if I shall have closed my last day in the pursuit of them. And by the precept of the law the tail of the victim is commanded to be offered in sacrifice. For in the tail is the end of the body. And he sacrifices well who brings the sacrifice of good work through to the end of the action that is due. Hence Joseph is described as having had among the rest of his brothers a tunic reaching [1053D] to the ankles; for a tunic reaching to the ankle is good work reaching to consummation.

The morning of the intellectual day, then, is inchoative virtue; midday, perfective virtue; evening, contemplative virtue. And such a day admits more and less according to the threefold quality of temporal increase in man: for in boyhood the understanding is less, in youth greater, in old age greatest. For what remains over fails in many, as in the decrepit, who sometimes seem to become boys again in sense. Now boyhood, or adolescence, is a kind of morning, as it were the rising of the sun; youth a kind of midday, as it were the sun's highest ascent and its hottest time, just as that age too is of the greatest heat and strength; but old age a kind of [1054A] evening, as it were the sun's descent from the high pole and the failing of its heat, just as in that *41* age also a man fails from the heat of youth.

Whether, then, the understanding be distinguished by the stages of the ages, or be distinguished within the understanding itself by the increases of works, it may not absurdly be called the intellectual day, so that this may be what the Psalmist says: *Evening, and morning, and at midday I will tell and declare [n: (Psal. LIV)]*; as if he said: both in boyhood and in youth and in old age, whether of age or of understanding, as in morning, midday, evening, by teaching and by working I will declare the rule of living rightly.

The last form of day, indeed, which we have called the eternal, has of these three parts midday alone. For of itself it has no morning at all, but in those who [1054B] partake of it, some morning: before the resurrection indeed in the soul alone, when by dying happily they are born to it; but in the resurrection both in soul and in body, when by rising most happily they are born to it again; which happiness of body and soul Holy Scripture calls the double robe, but the blessedness of the soul alone, the single.

That, then, which has some beginning of blessedness, even now in the soul, and then in body and soul together, is in those who partake a kind of morning, as has been said; but in its own nature and property, which unchangeably was, and is, and will be, it never had a morning, because blessedness itself is God, in whose unchangeableness those who have been made unchangeable partake.

[1054C]Further, evening neither could nor can be had in any way, whether in itself or in those who partake, because they will stand fixed eternally with him to whose natural eternity they have been joined through grace. But midday it has always had and will have, both in itself and in those who partake: in itself indeed, because of the special excellence of beauty, brightness and rest; but in those who partake, because of the most ardent fervour of divine charity, which in that life is more fervent than that which they can have in this life by so much as it is agreed that that life is far happier than this, and beyond comparison with it. Whence the prophet: *The Lord liveth,* he says, *whose fire is in Sion, and his furnace in Jerusalem [var: Vulg. Is 31:9 *dixit Dominus*, "saith the Lord"] [n: (Isa. XXXVI)]*. It is more to be a furnace than a fire, because a fire can be even small; but in a furnace a vaster flame [1054D] is kindled. Now Sion is called *watching,* but Jerusalem *vision of peace*; for our peace we watch for here meanwhile, that there afterward we may see it fully. From the love of the Lord, then, there is fire in Sion, a furnace in Jerusalem, because here we burn to some extent with the flames of his love, where we contemplate something of him, but there we shall burn fully where we shall see fully him whom we love.

This eternal day, then, has midday alone, far brighter and more fervent than the middays of the formal or natural days described above; which midday nevertheless, according to the merits of those who partake, admits more *42* and less, because, though in itself it is one and the same, yet those who partake partake of it unlike. For *in the house of the Father there are many mansions* [1055A] [n: (Joan. XIV)], because in the one reward the glory of the elect is diverse, as also the misery of the reprobate, according to the merit of each.

In this place, therefore, neither the midday of the first nor of the last day is to be considered, but of those between them, the middle ones, namely the active and the intellectual, because the intention of each one who lives rightly ought to hang upon the pursuit either of the active or of the contemplative life.

Since therefore many seem to be in the fervour of divine charity, whether existing in the active or in the contemplative life, who do not have the single eye, because they do good things not with a right intention, while they make show of these not for God but for empty glory or for earthly gain, whom it is very difficult to distinguish from those who truly [1055B] love God, the Church, or some holy soul, asks her Bridegroom to show her in some manner of the modes set out above, through the spirit of discretion, who they are who love and follow him not feignedly [sic: *simultane*] but truly; in whom he feeds with the sweetness of his commandments, and in whom he lies and rests with the delightful rest of the virtues in the midday, that is, in the fervour of his charity, as he himself says: *If any one love me, he will keep my word, and my Father loves him, and we will come to him and will make our abode with him [n: (ibid.)]*. For he who observes the words of God proves that he loves God; and he who loves God is worthy of God's abode. And why she asks for the gift of this discretion she adds the cause, saying: *Lest I begin to wander [1055C] after the flocks of thy companions.*

Some are companions of Christ, some are thought to be; and because they differ in the quality of their life, they are rightly called not a flock but *flocks*. For those who are are spoken of in one way, those who are thought to be in another: because those who are truly imitate Christ, whether they be prelates teaching and instructing their subjects by word and example, as Christ did his disciples, or whether they be subjects hearing and questioning one another, as it is written of Christ that *they found him in the temple hearing and asking them [n: (Luc. II)]*. But those who are only thought to be, but are not, feigning that they follow Christ, are set over others in name alone, and are set over their subjects neither by word nor by example. And the subjects among themselves, as though they were brothers, are joined by a feigned [1055D] charity, and therefore they are not recognized. Such companions of the Bridegroom, then, both are and are thought to be: they are because true, they are thought to be because feigned.

Wherefore she asks for the discretion needful to her, so that, since she had set forth above that even the sons of her mother had fought against her, and it is therefore doubtful who are faithful, who unfaithful, the grace of discretion being given her, she may be able to recognize to whom she ought to join herself, to whose teaching to give credence, from whom to take the form of living rightly, *43* so that [cj: *ut*; read *ne*, "lest"] it be necessary for her to go round as a wanderer and perhaps ignorantly to go off into the errors of companions [sic: *sed alium*] who are feigned.

To her thus asking, the Lord, as if chiding her why in some temptation she distrusts his help, on whom for her great consolation he had conferred a diverse [1056A] gift of his grace, answered: *If thou know not thyself, O fairest, among women?*

VERSE 7.-- *If thou know not thyself, O fairest among women, go forth, and go away after the steps of the flocks, and feed thy kids beside the tents of the shepherds.*

A little above, where we described the fourfold day, intellectual or active, we set it particularly in some wise man: intellectual indeed because of reason and light among the darkness, that is, rightly dividing virtues and vices; but active because of discretion, bringing forth into works of light the things it has rightly perceived. But now nothing prevents our setting universally also in each man an intellectual day only, because of the liveliness of the reason naturally implanted in him.

[1056B]Let us suppose, then, after the likeness of the natural day, which, whole, is divided into day and night, two portions of this intellectual day also in universal man, according to privation and habit, that is, knowledge of self and ignorance, of which the former is reckoned for the day, the latter for the night.

For this is a certain habit of the soul, of justice, when out of full knowledge of itself it takes on a certain form of uprightness, affected by which, or disposed to which, it makes such progress through the grace that helps it that such an affection or disposition passes into a habit, and from this the whole man is declared just. And the privation of this habit is injustice, when the soul itself either never admitted any [1056C] form of piety, or, if it had one, lost it, divine grace betaking itself away; or drew to itself the vice of some deformity, whose affection or disposition in like manner passes into a habit contrary to the former, so that, as by participation of justice one is called just, so by participation of injustice he is called unjust: which is according to habit; but according to privation, either that he himself lacks justice, or that injustice is the privation of justice, according to the description of evil, by which it is said that evil is nothing other than the privation of good; and justice is a species of good, as on the contrary injustice is of evil.

But because this species of good is born from that part which is knowledge of self, and the species of evil from that part which is ignorance of self, it must be said what it is to know and not to know oneself.
