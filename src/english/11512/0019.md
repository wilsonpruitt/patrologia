## CHAPTER ONE. (cont.)

By a still ampler showing of his grace the bridegroom [1069B] leads the bride to the knowledge of herself, while he now proclaims her beauty, whose fortitude he had proclaimed before, so as to intimate the conjunction of this twin virtue to be such that the one cannot be without the other, since the one is the fortification of life that is to be guarded, the other the ornament of it when guarded: for fortitude of spirit is needed to guard that very integrity of life, beauty to adorn that very integrity with good works. « For chastity is nothing without good work, nor is good work anything without chastity. » [n: (GREGORIUS.)]

The virtue of fortitude, then, having been set first, the necessary virtue of beauty is added after, so that it may be shown that no one has done anything bravely in all the pursuits of a dutiful soul if it be established besides that he has not increased beautifully through cleanness [1069C] of mind and body. But this beauty is stretched out with a twin grace, while in general it is described by the one name of beauty, but in particular the beauty of the cheeks is one, that of the neck another; the difference of which is gathered from the quality of the things to which they are compared.

For although in this statement there are two subjects, one predicate, the subjects namely being the cheeks and the neck, and the predicate that they are beautiful, yet in this place the nature of the accident of one predicate cannot be present equally, in one and the same manner, in two subjects: one predicate can indeed, through the grace of God, be present in several subjects, namely so that the cheeks are beautiful and the neck is beautiful, but, as we have said, the beauty of the cheeks is described in one way, [1069D] the beauty of the neck in another, though beauty is nevertheless predicated of both; and therefore in this place the two subjects indeed admit each other, but not what otherwise is one predicate.

But if we wish to gather the diversities of this beauty, the things whose likeness is brought in must be looked into, namely the turtle-dove and the jewels, so that they may be discerned from their quality, whether natural or material: for the beauty in the turtle-dove is natural, in the jewels material.

*58* Of the turtle-dove the Physiologi hand down three marks of its nature: one, that it is of such chastity that, having lost its mate, it seeks no other thereafter; another, that instead of song it has a moaning; the third, that, seeking secret [1070A] places, it rejoices in a friendly solitude, building its nest there and rearing its young where there is no injury from adversaries, while its refuges can scarcely or never be found. What is expressed by the turtle-dove, which loves chastity, which moans instead of singing, which seeks secret places, but the beauty of chastity, of compunction, of contemplation. For this wonderful beauty of the cheeks is distinguished in a threefold manner of this kind, according to the threefold nature of this animal; and since there is one subject and three predicates, those three both are in the subject and are predicated of the subject, when through the grace of God the cheeks are beautiful both by the virtue of chastity and of compunction and of contemplation. For these several marks of the cheeks have probable signs of their beauty. For [1070B] the cheeks blooming with the rosy blush of modesty, and with bent head fixing their gaze on the earth, denote the comeliness of chastity; but, moist with a wholesome fountain of tears, they speak the grace of compunction; and pale with the zeal and labour of fastings, vigils and holy meditations, they announce the virtue of contemplation too.

But since, as we set down above, the beauty of the cheeks is one, that of the neck another, we have divided the beauty of the cheeks into three species, namely chastity, compunction and contemplation, so that, these being set as it were as subaltern genera, a further subdivision may be made, and it may be said that chastity is one virginal, another widowly, another conjugal; compunction one reconciliatory, another amatory; contemplation one natural, another intelligible, a third intellectible.

[1070C]For virginal chastity is the everlasting integrity of soul and body; but widowly chastity is a devotion of the soul praiseworthy by the comeliness of continence; further, conjugal chastity is a coupling of soul and body commendable by the grace of faith and modesty. But reconciliatory compunction is when someone, visited by heavenly grace, is pricked to sorrow from the remembrance of his sins, and while he is afflicted through the lamentations of penance is reconciled to God whom he had offended: for first the mind is pricked with fear, afterwards with love. Whence the other part may not unfittingly be named amatory compunction, when the mind is kindled with such love towards God that she who before wept lest she be brought back to punishment afterwards begins to weep most bitterly because she is kept back from the kingdom. The grace of which twofold [1070D] compunction is well described by a figurative narration in the sacred and truthful history, where Axa, the daughter of Caleb, sitting upon an ass, is recorded to have asked of her father the lower watered ground and the upper watered ground [n: (Jud. I)]. *59*
