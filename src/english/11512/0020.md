## .

. . . . For Axa sits upon the ass when the soul presides over the irrational motions of its flesh, which, sighing, asks of the Father a watered land, because the grace of tears is to be sought from our Creator with great groaning. From him the soul receives the upper watered ground when it afflicts itself in tears with desire for the heavenly kingdom; but it receives the lower watered ground when, weeping, it is in great dread of the punishments of hell.

But although these are two species of compunction, yet compunction itself is distinguished by four qualities, [1071A] because the soul of a just man is vehemently afflicted in compunction: either when it remembers its own evils, considering where it was; or, fearing the sentence of the judgments of God and seeking secure things, it ponders where it will be; or when, attending skilfully to the evils of the present life, it considers mourning where it is; or when it contemplates by the good of the heavenly fatherland, which, because it does not yet attain, it beholds lamenting where it is not.

Now of the third species, that is, of contemplation or speculation, the first part is that which is called natural, which is occupied about bodies and the knowledge and cognition of them, which the Greeks call physiology, and which declares the natures and passions of bodies. The second part is the intelligible, which of all the heavenly works of the supernal divinity, and [1071B] anything prevails by the sublunar globe with a more blessed mind and a purer substance, and lastly the wonderful disposition of human souls, comprehends by thought and intelligence. Further, the third part is called the intellectible, when the mind is led outside visible things, above itself, to another, invisible thing, which, one and the same through itself, ever abiding in its own divinity, is grasped by no senses ever, but by the mind and the intellect alone: for this is composed, by the searching of true philosophy, for the speculation of God and for the incorporeality and the consideration of the soul; which part the Greeks name theology.

Truly, in these divided parts of the parts it is to be considered that, if they are compared with one another, one is greater in proportion than another according to the more excellent dignity of the altered beauty [1071C]. For virginal chastity is greater in proportion than widowly and conjugal chastity, since to them belongs the merit of this profession alone, *to say the new canticle, and the Lamb whithersoever he shall go* to follow [n: (Apoc. XIV)].

Further, that compunction is more excellent which is generated from love than that which is generated from fear, although by the latter one comes to the former, as the son takes precedence of the servant, because to fear is the part of servants, to love, of sons. And that part of contemplation which is called intellectible is incomparable with the two remaining, since the speculation of these is occupied about the creature, but that of the purified mind, lifted beyond the creature by its eye into the Creator himself, although not perfectly, yet as far as shall be possible [1071D] through his grace, is held suspended; whereby the holy *60* man is separated like the fat from the flesh, seeks or desires nothing outward, but, thirsting for God with burning desire, cries out with the Psalmist: *As the hart longeth for the fountains of waters, so my soul longeth for thee, O God; my soul hath thirsted for the living God, when shall I come and appear before the face of God? [n: (Psal. LXI)]*.

This part the Apostle calls *the third heaven*, into which he says he was *caught up and* heard unspeakable things *which it is not lawful for a man to speak [n: (II Cor. XII)]*.

Now this threefold species of beauty your holy college possesses, as we believe, as familiar things, most beloved sisters, you whom a blessed solitude has set apart [sic: *se posuit*] from all the din of worldly disquiets, so that the further removed you are from the world, [1072A] the more freely you may be able to keep the comeliness of chastity, to seek the grace of compunction, and to stand on the summit of holy contemplation. Indeed your very solitude is a delightful garden of the Lord, where the rose and the lily breathe, the testimony of virginity and of martyrdom; of that martyrdom, namely, which can be without the shedding of blood, and in the mind through the exercises of the virtues; where are the watered grounds of holy contemplation; where the little trees of heavenly contemplation spring up, flower and bear fruit; where all these honest things are so composed that in them are the pleasing, chosen walking-places of the Holy Spirit, who says: *I will dwell in them and walk among them; and they shall be to me for a people and I truly to them for God [n: (Lev. XXVI; II Cor. VI)]* [var: Vulg. 2 Cor 6:16 *et ero illorum Deus, et ipsi erunt mihi populus*, "and I will be their God"]. But with very wary circumspection you must take heed lest [1072B] you lose the privilege of so great a good by any worldliness or empty levity, lest that most sweet and most mighty indweller and walker of yours, provoked by you, flee away, saying *that he hath not where to lay his head [n: (Luc. IX)]*.

But since we said above that the beauty of the cheeks is one thing and that of the neck another, and have already described the beauty of the cheeks according to our small measure, it remains now also to speak of the beauty of the neck, which is also divided into two, this into preaching and incorporation; and first we must speak of preaching, afterwards of incorporation. In the neck is the throat, and through the throat the voice is expressed; food too, when it has been ground by the teeth, is passed through the throat into the belly; by the neck, therefore, the proclaimed saints are not [1072C] absurdly understood, in whom is this twin beauty, namely of preaching and of incorporation. The beauty of preaching, then, is considered in the comeliness of the neck, because in the neck is the voice, and through the voice preaching is done; and as the voice is the mark of the mind and the expression of the silent heart, so the preaching of the saints is a thing to be sought by every man, the honest demonstration of the commonwealth. For I here call that the commonwealth which, for its honesty, can and ought to be taken up publicly, in knowledge, will, action, as is every good whose privation is evil.

*61* For to be chaste, humble, patient, modest, or whatever else is marked by the name of virtue, is manifestly good; and of this good [1072D] the negation, that is, not to be chaste, not humble, not patient, not modest, is consequently evil. But since one or the other of these is consequent, that is, to be or not to be, the simple negation of this simple affirmation which proposes to be chaste, or humble, or patient, the negation which says not to be, is followed by the privative affirmation which says to be unchaste, or proud, or impatient. For it is the same to say not to be patient as it is to say impatient, and that evil which is impatience is the privation of that good which is called patience, and in the same way of the rest. There is, then, a certain commonwealth, the array of the virtues; and the ruler and administrator of this commonwealth is that eternal Emperor from whom all good things proceed, and without [1073A] whom nothing can be called good or be good.

This commonwealth, then, the preaching of the saints teaches and shows, and through it the other part, which we called incorporation, is accomplished; because, as through the throat food is passed into the belly, so through the preaching of the saints some sinner, broken from the grossness of his depravity, is incorporated into the Church by conversion.

But since the beauty of the neck is twin, as we have said, namely preaching and incorporation, each is distinguished by subdivision in a threefold manner; because one preaching is done by word, another by example, another by word and example; and of the incorporated, one by conversion, another by satisfaction, another by conversion and satisfaction together.

[1073B]Preaching is done by word alone, because there are some in the Church who have this by the gift of God, that they are able to edify others by the word of exhortation, but, their infirmity standing in the way, they are not so able to give examples of virtues. It is done also by example alone, because there are very many who do not have the gift of discoursing in the Church, and even if they perceive something, cannot bring it forth openly, yet they shine with such proofs of spiritual works that by wonderful examples of virtues they provoke the minds of those who wonder at them to their imitation; and what those speak with words, these speak with deeds, and these philosophize better by keeping silence than those by declaiming. Of which twin quality of preaching the Apostle says: *For to one is given the word of wisdom, to another the word of knowledge [n: (I Cor XII)]*; because there are some, as we have said, who are wise through the understanding of reason in the word [1073C] of the divine commandments to be observed, but through the slowness of their wit are not eloquent in bringing it forth; and others, again, pour out mightily with an abundance of eloquence the very word which they comprehend, to be sown, of a capacious wit.

Likewise preaching is done in both ways, *62* that is, by word and example, when holy doctors have it also by divine grace, that what they preach with the mouth they show in deeds, and what they teach is to be avoided they also indicate in their own deeds is not to be done; in whose person the Psalmist, speaking to God, when he had first said, *and I spoke of thy testimonies before kings, and I was not ashamed,* added in what follows: *And I lifted up my hands to thy commandments, which I loved, and I was exercised in thy [1073D] justifications [n: (Psal. CXVIII)]*: as if he said openly: The law of thy commandments, which I pronounced by preaching openly and confidently, I proved also by the execution of works, so that my hearers might both hear in my words and see in my deeds the things which they ought to do for their own profit and salvation.

Truly this third predicament of this beauty is necessary for the doctors of the Church, because the preaching of those is frequently despised whose life is known to be alien from the good of their preaching itself. And they sin greatly before the eyes of almighty God who, the grace of preaching having been granted them, acquire no gains of souls because of their silence. Whence in the Old Testament it is commanded that the priest, entering the tabernacle, should have little bells [1074A] set in the hem of his vestments, lest he die [sic: *nemoriatur*] [n: (Exod. XXVIII)], because it is a great peril for the doctor if he enters the Church of God without the sound of preaching; and the vestments of the priest are the works of justice, as the Psalmist says: *Let thy priests be clothed with justice [n: (Psal. CXXXI)]*, to which vestments the little bells cling, so that the very works of the priest also may cry out the way of life together with the sound of the tongue.

The other part of the beauty in the neck, which is incorporation, is done by conversion, because there are some who, converted from heathendom to God, are incorporated into the Church through faith and the grace of baptism, as we read of Cornelius, who, the merits of good works intervening through the grace of God, and the gift of the Holy Spirit running before, was shown worthy to be joined to the body of the Church [1074B] [n: (Act. X)].

For when, as though an undeserving man, they were being repelled by those who had believed from the circumcision, the Holy Spirit fell upon him as a needful advocate, whose condescension it pleased to show that God willed this man too to be incorporated into his Church through faith and baptism.

Others also are incorporated into the Church through satisfaction, who, while at first indeed they are of the body of the Church through the unity of faith, yet, their faults requiring it, are sometimes cut off from the body of the Church by a peremptory sentence; but, recovering their senses through satisfaction, they are joined again by a reconciling sentence to the body from which they had been cut off. Whence the Apostle too, writing to the Corinthians, commands that he who had perpetrated the crime [1074C] be delivered *to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, that* *63* *he may be saved in the day of the Lord [n: (I Cor. V)]* [var: Vulg. 1 Cor 5:5 *ut spiritus salvus sit*, "that the spirit may be saved"]: and afterwards he orders him, amended through satisfaction, to be incorporated again into the Church, lest he be swallowed up by overmuch sorrow.
