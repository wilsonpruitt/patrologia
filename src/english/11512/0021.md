## . (cont.)

Some, again, are incorporated through conversion and satisfaction together, who both receive the faith by believing and, confessing their crimes, are joined to the body of the Church through penance and baptism. Hence it is that Peter, preaching to the slayers of Christ: *Repent,* he says, *and be converted, that your sins may be blotted out [n: (Act. III)]*, showing that through the sending before of conversion and satisfaction the remission of sins follows, which is done in the Catholic Church alone, because [1074D] that very remission is plainly incorporation into the Church. But although in the former modes, which we set down above separately, saying that incorporation is done now through conversion, now through satisfaction, there can in both be both conversion and satisfaction, yet on account of the greater and more evident showing of the one part or the other we have distinguished them. For although any saint is adorned not with one but with many virtues, yet a virtue is predicated of him the more, the greater in proportion it is recognized to be in him. Whence the Apostle too, when he was describing the divisions of graces, said: *For to one is given the word of wisdom, to another the word of knowledge, to another faith, to another the grace of healings: to another prophecy, to another the discerning of spirits, to another kinds of tongues, to another the interpretation of speeches [n: (II Cor. XII)]*; not that in this he who has had one has any of the other graces [1075A] whatsoever, but because he is said to have that grace the more which is known to be stronger and more abundant in him. And in Cornelius we read of no satisfaction for his past life; but because it was said to him by the angel that his prayers and alms had been heard, and he is therefore bidden to send after Peter, so that, formed unto the faith by his doctrine, he might become a member of the Church through conversion [n: (Act. X)]. But he who has been converted to the faith, but for some fault is separated from the Church by excommunication, has need of satisfaction in order to merit being received back, although satisfaction itself also can be called a conversion from the former error.

Now since we have distinguished beauty in a twofold manner, and have subdivided those very manners into others, it remains [1075B] now to say why this beauty is compared to necklaces.

A necklace is a breast ornament of matrons, named from *muniendo*, because it fortifies their breast, lest any adulterer injure in any way the modesty of their chastity. And the necklace is not any sort whatever, but that which the hand of a careful craftsman laboriously fashions: it is composed of gold and gems in such a way that a somewhat larger gem is set in the middle *64*, and other little gems, standing at equal distances from it, encircle it.

And what is expressed by the gold but wisdom, what by the gems but the virtues? The necklace, then, is composed of gold and gems, because out of the understanding of wisdom and the comeliness of the virtues is made a certain spiritual and necessary ornament of the soul, which so [1075C] fortifies the secret places of our breast that the ancient enemy dares not put forth any hand of his malice against it.

And certain openings are made in the crafted ornament itself, in which the gems are to be enclosed. And what are these openings but our outward senses, which are as it were certain instruments of our acts? For the lowering of the eyes and the honest or disciplined composure of the countenance is a most beautiful gem, shining with the virtue of humility; the ear obedient to the precepts of God and of prelates bears the gem of obedience; further, in the nostrils glows the gem of holy discretion; the mouth shows a gem radiant with a threefold virtue, namely of abstinence, of silence, and of speech honestly and usefully uttered [1075D] and the hands conspicuously display a gem sprinkled with a twofold brilliance, that is, of continence and of munificence.

Among these, set in the middle place, the greater and more splendid gem of holy charity shines chiefly in the breast, which so supplies the comeliness of this ornament [sic: *ornament*] that nothing of beauty is proved to be had without it: for without [sic: *sive*] charity no virtue can either be or be called [a virtue], because *on two precepts*, namely of God and of the neighbour, *dependeth the whole law and the prophets [n: (Matth. XXII)]*.

Beautifully, then, the neck of holy Church, or of the faithful soul, is compared to necklaces consisting of the material of gold and gems, because through the preaching and the unanimity of the saints we ought so to compose our senses out of the [1076A] brightness of wisdom and the holiness of the virtues that we may establish for ourselves a certain ornament of the soul in which that heavenly bridegroom may delight. But because the page of both Testaments is necessary to the preachers of the same Church, from which they may take the order and manner of their preaching, there is added, in the person of the bridegroom himself, or of those through whom, by his inspiration, the scripture of the Church was set forth, of whom some were at the same time at which Solomon spoke these things, some were to be afterwards.

VERSE 10-- *We will make thee golden murenulae, vermiculated with silver.*

The *murenula* is a certain kind of ornament, like a little chain woven of gold and picked out with little rods of silver in the manner of little worms; named *murenula* for [1076B] this reason, that like the moray it is pliant and flexible, or coiled. For the moray *65*, when it is caught, bends and coils itself in different directions. By the golden *murenula* vermiculated with silver, then, we understand Holy Scripture, whose inner sense, on account of its eminence and dignity, is compared to gold, while the utterance by which that sense is expressed is signified by silver on account of its clearness and brilliance. For Holy Scripture is golden, because it shines with the brightness of wisdom; silver, because it gleams with the clearness of utterance. But because this same Scripture is divided into two, that is, into the Old and the New Testament, it is therefore said in the plural: *We will make thee golden murenulae, vermiculated with silver,* so that both Testaments may be shown to be both golden with the brightness of the inner sense and [1076C] silver with the lustre of eloquence.

These two Testaments are called *murenulae* because they can be bent into different modes of exposition, coiled about with many meanings, only so that they sound nothing contrary to the faith. And although, as we have said, they are golden by the valuation of surpassing knowledge, yet, being marked out by the clearness of utterance, they are recorded as vermiculated with silver, because by the brightness of that very knowledge they thunder now humbly, now moderately, now sublimely; and now they unfold history, now allegory, now morality, not simply but manifoldly. Whence it is also said in Ezekiel that as the living creatures went, *the wheels also went together, following them, and with those that blew they blew, and with those lifted up they were lifted up together [n: (Ezech. I)]* [var: Vulg. Ez 1:21 *cum stantibus stabant*, "with those that stood they stood"]: because, doubtless, [1076D] the Holy Scriptures stand or are moved according to the sense of him who searches and understands them; they stand, namely, when they are understood simply according to the letter; but they are moved when they are expounded according to the spiritual sense, whether allegorically or morally, and the more subtly they are considered, the higher they are lifted up together with the sense of him who understands.

Since, therefore, they are bent by the preachers of the Church not uniformly but with a beautiful variety in the modes of diverse senses, now allegorically, as we have said, now morally, and these things are brought forth according to the capacity of the hearers, now humbly, now sublimely, rightly are golden *murenulae* vermiculated with silver promised to the Church by the voice of the bridegroom, which hang as it were from her neck when they adorn the knowledge and the utterance of the holy [1077A] preachers.

Now it is not without some reason to recapitulate briefly the things above, and to consider how these lower things are continued on to those; above, when the bride had said: *I am black, but beautiful, O daughters of Jerusalem,* she did not recognize that same beauty of the perfect: whence also she complains that so great a heat of temptations affected her that even her brothers fought against him. But the bridegroom reproves this faintheartedness of hers, teaching that she had not rightly attended to the grace of beauty *66* conferred on her, she who had indeed said that she was beautiful, but had not perfectly recognized the quantity and quality of that beauty; wherefore it could happen that when she began to disagree she would go out from the fellowship of her bridegroom and follow the various errors of the world. Whence the bridegroom, [1077B] with his accustomed kindness bringing her back to herself, strengthens her by enumerating the parts of the beauty granted her, so that she, recognizing herself, may perfectly acknowledge how and how greatly she is beautiful, and from this may cleave more ardently to the bridegroom [sic: *sponsio*].

Now that beauty, that we may so define it, is the habit of a well-constituted mind, whose parts are fortitude, beauty, the knowledge of the holy Scriptures, which the bridegroom himself has already enumerated: fortitude, indeed, in the place where he said: *To my company of horsemen in the chariots of Pharaoh have I likened thee, my love:* beauty, not uniform but as we distinguished above, when he says: *Thy cheeks are beautiful as the turtle-dove's, thy neck as necklaces:* and the knowledge of the Scriptures where he says: *We will make [1077C] thee golden murenulae, vermiculated with silver.*

Now in a maximal and principal proposition, what is in the parts must necessarily be in the whole: taking, then, an argument from the parts, he proves that she is truly beautiful, because if the species of beauty, that is, fortitude, beauty, the knowledge of the holy Scriptures, are the habit of a well-constituted mind, the genus, which is beauty, must necessarily be the same; and accordingly, since she has said that she is beautiful, she ought not to fear adversities, she whom the grace of the bridegroom has made such. But if she does not recognize that she is such through grace, she will not belong to the fellowship of the bridegroom, since the cause of ignorance brings this about, that, going out of doors through sloth and negligence, she is compelled to stray from the companionship of the bridegroom. And it is a topic [1077D] from the efficient cause it may be proved: that every one who has not understood the good of the grace conferred on him is, through ignorance, negligent of that very good in himself, and the negligent is not near to God: the bride therefore, not knowing herself, withdraws from the fellowship of the bridegroom and of necessity seeks outward things. Brought back, then, to herself, she shows by what she adds how she has recognized herself and profitably attended to the grace granted her, saying: *While the king was at his reclining, my nard gave forth its odour.*

VERSE 11.-- *While the king was at his reclining, my nard gave forth its odour.*

The reclining of the king is the dispensation of all the temporal actions of Christ, because he deigned to humble himself from that ineffable sublimity of his divinity, and for human salvation to incline himself to the *67* infirmity of [1078A] humanity: first indeed by being born into the womb of the Virgin, then by dying upon the cross, by lying in the sepulchre and by descending to the lower regions; afterwards, raising himself from that very inclining of his, by rising again and by ascending to the heavens.

In the time of this dispensation the Church, growing up chiefly unto the increase of the virtues, was also taught by the precept and example of Christ to share in his sufferings through patience, when she had advanced into so great a charity towards him that she did not prefer even life itself to his love, saying: *While the king was at his reclining, my nard gave forth its odour.*

Nard is an aromatic shrub, with a heavy, thick, short, black and brittle root, and a small and dense leaf, whose tops spread out into ears; and therefore the makers of perfumes [1078B] celebrate the spikes and the leaves of the nard for their twofold endowment. Whence it is related in the Gospel that a woman brought, for the anointing of the Lord, *an ointment of precious spikenard [n: (Joan. XII)]*.

And what is signified by the nard but the virtue of good work? And the root of good work is charity; because, as from the root is born all the virtue and vigour of plants, so from charity alone is generated the virtue of every good work.
