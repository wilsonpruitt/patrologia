But since above, companions are spoken of by the bride in the place where she says: *Lest I begin to wander after the flocks of thy companions,* while now by his own voice the bride is called his love, it must be considered what difference there is between a friend and a companion. A companion is one who is associated with us for doing something together with us; but a friend is one who, like in the practice of life, is joined to us by a devout and faithful love: the companions of the bridegroom, then, are those who seem as it were to be associated with him through some good works, but he proves of things with what intention they did it, when, being desirous of human praise, they show *76* themselves to be very alien from the love of him whom they seemed to imitate not simply and devoutly but for their own temporal advantage. But his friends are those who for the sole hope of eternal goods [1086B] are faithfully joined to him, and, while they are separated from him by no prosperity and no adversity, show how greatly they burn with the love of him before whom they care to set nothing at all. On account of such a quality of life the Church, or any holy soul which follows the footsteps of Christ, when this beauty is proclaimed of her, deserves to be called by the name of his love; but because that beauty shines forth from the speculation of the mind as from a kind of face, he adds the cause why he called her fair, saying: *thine eyes are doves'.*

The eyes of the Church are the holy doctors, because, as the eyes are leaders of the way for us, so the holy doctors, showing us the way of life, go before us by word and example as with a double eye.

Morally, too, any soul whatever, that is, in the head of the inner [1086C] man, has two eyes, understanding and discretion. For it understands and sees what it must do, then discerns how that action ought to be ordered, so that with the one eye it may look at the quality of its act, with the other at the manner. For he sees well whither he ought to tend who both considers the quality of the life to be held, and, by now tightening and now relaxing that quality according to place and time, forms the strong and the weak; yet so that that very tightening or relaxing is set within a fixed and fitting limit. The cause, then, why he says *fair* is this, namely that her *eyes* are *doves'*; which comparison is rightly made on account of the sevenfold virtue of the dove's nature and simplicity.

For the dove lacks gall, harms no one, picks the better [1086D] grains, nourishes the young of others, nests in the clefts of the rock, has a moan for a song, is accustomed to sit above the waters so that it may foresee the coming of the hawk. On account of these seven marks of its nature the bride is compared to the dove; so that if she refers these things with a dove's eye to the moral form, she may worthily be called fair, while she strives to have by grace what the dove has by nature.

It lacks gall; which the bride of Christ also ought spiritually to lack, so that, according to the Apostle: *Let all bitterness, and anger, and clamour, and blasphemy be put away from* her *with all malice [n: (Ephes. IV)]*. It harms no one; the bride of Christ too ought to hurt nobody, by word nor by deed; but, according to the Apostle, [1087A] to have *peace with all men* [n: (Rom. XII)]. It picks the better grains; so too one who loves Christ ought to gather from Holy Scripture the better sentences, by which he may know how both to order his own life honestly and usefully and to form the life of his neighbours after his own example by teaching and acting. It nourishes the young of others; the Church too gathers into her bosom, as if her own sons, those who are not of her *77* body, such as heretics and perverse Christians, and is busy to join them to her body by admonishing, by reproving, by drawing them with benefits; and this any soul which is truly the bride of Christ ought to do, that she love and cherish strangers and those contrary to her, as her own household, for Christ's sake, so that she may always conquer evil into good [n: (ibid.)]. It nests in the clefts of the rock, that it may escape the bites of serpents; so [1087B] too the Church, or the soul joined to God, ought to seek refuge in the passion of Christ, *for Christ is the rock [n: (I Cor. X)]*, whose passion's clefts are the wounds, the strongest defences against the snares of the malignant spirits. It has a moan for a song; and in this temporal life, to one who loves God, moaning ought to be for a song, that is, in place of worldly place and gladness, *that sadness which is according to God, which worketh penance steadfast unto salvation [n: (II Cor. VII)]*: for it is written that *blessed are they that mourn, for they shall be comforted [n: (Matth. V)]*, and on the contrary: *Woe to you that laugh now, for you shall weep [n: (Luc. VI)]*. It is accustomed to sit above the waters so that it may foresee the coming of the hawk; so too the faithful soul ought to dwell in mind above the streams of the Holy Scriptures, [1087C] that from them it may understand and learn how it may be able to beware of the snares of the ancient enemy.

The bride, then, is proclaimed fair on account of these seven parts of dovelike simplicity; which, because she exercises them with the simple eye of the mind as with a dove's eye, she so pleases the eyes of the bridegroom that he declares her fair not once but twice, as though confirming it, and intimates in these parts the twin love, namely of God and of the neighbour. For not to be angry, to hope in the sacraments of the passion of Christ, to groan [sic: *genere*] for one's sins or for the delay of the heavenly kingdom, to bend the mind to sacred Scripture, is the sign of divine love: further, to harm no one, to receive and cherish strangers as if one's own, is the proof of brotherly charity. But because [1087D] *the Holy Spirit, through whom the charity of God* is poured forth *in our hearts*, is the same charity [n: (Rom. V)], consisting in the twin part of love, for John says: *God is charity [n: (I Joan. IV)]*; let us consider in these seven parts of innocence the sevenfold grace of the Holy Spirit himself, namely the spirit of wisdom and of understanding, the spirit of counsel and of fortitude, the spirit of knowledge and of piety, the spirit of the fear of the Lord [n: (Isa. XI)]. For it is of wisdom to repress the motions of the mind while it wisely considers that *the anger of man worketh not the justice of God [n: (Jac. I)]*; hence Solomon: *The fool,* he says, *immediately sheweth his anger, but he that dissembleth an injury is shrewd [n: (Prov. XII)]*; and again: *A stone is heavy, and sand weighty, but the anger of a man is heavier than both; Anger hath no mercy, nor fury when it breaketh forth* [var: Vulg. Prov 27:3 *ira stulti*, "the anger of a fool"] [1088A] [n: (Prov. XXVII)]. Whence a certain one of the Gentiles says: *He who will not moderate his anger*
*Will wish undone what his resentment and his mind have urged,*
*While he hastens by force the penalties of his unavenged hatred.*
[n: (HORATIUS, Epist. I, II, 59.)] *78* And to show that it is of wisdom to know how to rule the mind, he adds: *Anger is a brief madness, rule the mind: which, unless it obeys,*
*Commands: check it with reins, check thou it with a chain.*
[n: (HORATIUS, Epist. I, II, 62.)]

By the spirit of wisdom, then, the disordered motion of the mind is to be repressed, because through this evils are wisely avoided, which for the most part are born from it so grievous that they are scarcely or never laid to rest.

*The spirit of understanding also is [n: (Isa. IX)]*, to harm neither oneself [1088B] nor others, and through the discretion of reason to understand that it is just *that what one would not have done to oneself one should not do to another [n: (Matth. VII)]* But because every injury of hurting is done in two ways by him who inflicts it, namely by word and by action, and is received in two ways by him who suffers it, that is, in mind and in body, a twofold remedy of understanding is also to be applied, that he may weigh that both the integrity of brotherly charity is corrupted and the severity of divine goodness is provoked, which is to harm both oneself and others. For that is true innocence by which one harms neither oneself nor others, because *he that loveth iniquity hateth his own soul [n: (Psal. X)]*, and no one sins against another without first sinning in himself. Great indeed is the gift of this understanding, by which one establishes for oneself such a manner of living that one pleases both God and men, and that this is [1088C] the sign of true innocence, to love God and the neighbour, which is the perfecting of all holiness.

Further, by the spirit of counsel, anyone having zeal for a holy intention gathers the better and more useful sentences of the holy Scriptures, from whose sense, as from a certain help, taking counsel for himself, he may find a certain state of conversation, or one befitting quality; and this, collected out of diverse sentences, may become the counsel of a life to be well ordered.

But the spirit of fortitude greatly strengthens the mind that trusts in the power of the passion of Christ, when by the seal of that passion it is both fortified outwardly in the body and made firm inwardly in the mind against every onset of diabolical assault. For by this spirit of fortitude Moses warred down the Egyptian enemies *(Exod. [1088D] VII-XIV)*, when he laid them low by manifold plague through the rod and delivered the people of God from their bondage, intimating that he himself, *the Lord strong and mighty in battle [n: (Psal. XXV)]*, was to succour the whole world by the wood of his cross, the universal enemy, that is, the devil, being laid low by the power of his passion. By this spirit of fortitude also Gideon conquered the enemy with three hundred [n: (Jos. VIII)] bearing before them the form of the cross in the character of this number, which is marked by the letter TAU: and in Ezekiel [n: (cap. IX)] no one is struck who is found sealed with this letter, as with the form of the cross.

But the spirit of knowledge is, to commit to tenacious memory the series of the holy Scriptures, the things by which he may pour forth the streams of sacred doctrine, which [1089A] he may know how to divide with such discretion that both the simple may have that by which they are instructed, and the learned that by which they are exercised.

*79* But to offer equal charity to all, so as to love enemies equally with friends for God's sake, and, according to the Apostle: To become all things to all, that we may gain all [n: (I Cor. IX)], is assuredly the spirit of piety; by which spirit certainly he is led, whoever strives not slothfully to succour his neighbour in every need, whether of body or of soul, according to his power and beyond his power.

Further, to groan in the twofold manner of compunction, that is, either by the remembrance of sins or by the inmost desire of the soul thirsting for God, is from the spirit of the fear of the Lord, by which one fears either to be damned for sins or to be deprived of the inheritance of the loving Father.
