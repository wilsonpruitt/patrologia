## . (cont.)

But since there are four fears, one servile, the other [1089B] worldly; the third initial, the fourth filial, the two last are referred to the spirit of the fear of the Lord, of which the one indeed, that is, initial fear, is not without charity, I do not say perfect, but inchoate. Whence John in his Epistle, when he said: *Perfect charity casteth out fear [n: (I Joan. IV)]* showed that, although not yet perfect, charity nevertheless is in initial fear. But in the other, which is filial, consisting in love alone, there is perfect charity, of which the Psalmist says: *The fear of the Lord is holy, enduring for ever and ever [n: (Psal. XVIII)]*. Initial fear is that of which it is said: *The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom [n: (Eccle. I)]*, when a man begins to be wise and to taste God, where fear and the spirit accompany one another, [1089C] as it is read, fear is without hope. And the more good will grows, that is, charity, so much the more does fear almost decrease, so that from this initial fear, by which one fears to be punished, which is of the imperfect, one may at some time advance to filial fear, by which one fears not to be punished but to be separated, which is of the perfect. But the two fears which we set down in the preceding, that is, the servile and the worldly: they do not pertain to the spirit of the fear of the Lord, because they are separated from chaste fear; for servile fear is to cease from evil for the punishment threatening from man, the evil will nevertheless being retained. Worldly fear is to cease from good on account of the punishment which is feared from man: and these fears, namely the servile and the worldly, Christ forbids, saying: *Fear ye not them that kill the body, but have nothing that they may do to the soul* [1089D] [n: (Matth. XX)]. Afterwards he adds concerning initial fear, saying: *But rather fear him who can destroy both body and soul in hell [n: (ibid.)]*, which he would not command unless charity were in it, even if not yet perfect.

And it is to be noted that sometimes in Holy Scripture this fear is called servile, because it is akin to the servile, since both there and here one ceases from evil by fear of punishment. But that fear makes one cease from evil in deed and not in will; this one both in deed and in will. In the spirit, then, of the twofold fear of the Lord, that is, initial or filial, every holy soul has groaning for song, because in the joy of the present life she groans forth a mournful mind, either because she fears to be damned for sin, or because she fears *80* to be separated from God. Therefore, as we have said, on account of the sevenfold [1090A] quality of virtue and of dovelike innocence, the bride is recorded by the Bridegroom to have the eyes of doves, because she shows forth in action what, by the grace of the Holy Spirit, judging it useful, honest, she sees with right intention. But because she understands that every good, if she has any, she has not from herself but from God, she ascribes the whole grace of the beauty conferred on her to her Creator and Redeemer himself, saying: *Behold, thou art fair,* etc.

VERSE 15.-- *Behold, thou art fair, my beloved, and comely. Our bed is flowery.*

*Thou,* she says, *O beloved,* essentially *art fair and comely,* I, whatever of beauty I have, have accidentally, by participation in thee, according to the measure of grace bestowed on me by thee, from thee; because thou, as Creator, art capable of being received through indwelling grace, I, as [1090B] creature, am capable of receiving, participating in that grace as thou thyself hast granted. And this indeed according to the power of thy divinity; but according to the humanity, according to the outward man, thou wast *beautiful in form above the sons of men [n: (Psal. XLIV)]*; but according to the inward man, to thee alone, the only-begotten, *God giveth not the spirit by measure [n: (Joan. III)]*, while those who partake of thee receive him by measure, one indeed thus, another thus.

But all beauty and comeliness they have from thee, who truly and naturally *art fair and comely,* because whatever of meekness, whatever of piety, whatever of justice, whatever of patience, of humility, whatever finally of virtue or holiness ever could be or can be, they look upon and receive in thee, the singularly [1090C] holy man, as in the highest exemplar.

Having set forth this saying, then, *behold, thou art fair and comely,* she pursues some parts of that very beauty and comeliness, while she adds: *Our bed is flowery,* etc.

Of which parts indeed the first, that is, *our bed is flowery,* can be referred both to the head and to the members of the head, but the two remaining to the members alone, which nevertheless depend on the head itself by participation in its beauty and comeliness. But first, concerning the first part, it must be said how it will be sought both as the head and toward the members of the head: toward the head indeed, either according to the good pleasure of the divinity ruling all things, or according to the sacrament of the humanity [1090D] redeeming the world; but toward the members according to the participation, as we have said, of divine grace dwelling in the mind of each holy man and working in it the gifts of its condescension.

By the name of the bed rest is designated in two ways, that is, either bodily or spiritual. For as after labour the body seeks rest, so the mind too, wearied by certain labours of perturbations, desires rest; but in this place by the bed spiritual rest ought *81* to be understood, because we have said it is to be referred both to the head and to the members of piety.

Nevertheless, we must first see concerning the rest of the head, which in no way ought to be believed necessary or advantageous to the substance of him who is subject to no accident, and *with whom there is not any change, nor [1091A] shadow of vicissitude [n: (Jacob. I)]*, but the rest of the head is the salvation of the body. For just as God says that he labours when he bears with the depravities of the wicked, so too he says that he rests when he delights in the virtues of the good. Of his labour indeed, by which he bears with those still living perversely, he says through the prophet: *I have laboured, sustaining [n: (Jerem. VI)]*: for the Lord does not labour in sustaining, whose power of dignity no fatigue touches, but, speaking in the words of humanity, he calls his very patience towards us labour. Further, of the rest by which he delights in the good he says: *Upon whom shall I rest, but upon the humble and the quiet and him that trembleth at my words? [var: Vulg. Is 66:2 *ad quem autem respiciam, nisi ad pauperculum, et contritum spiritu*, "to whom shall I look, but to the poor little one and the contrite of spirit"] [n: (Isa. LXVI)]*; as if he should say in other words: He prepares rest for me in himself who in humility and quiet keeps the rule [1091B] of my precepts. The rest of God, then, is our advantage, just as, on the contrary, his labour is our loss.

To a member of this head, then, that is, to some faithful and holy man, the signification of this bed, which we have called rest, is fitted; because just as, by the opposite, for the reprobate the rest and delight of this world is in the future a perpetual labour and fatigue of the soul, so for the elect the labour and fatigue of this world is in the future the eternal rest and consolation of the soul. For the bed of a holy man is the silence and the cultivation of the contemplative life, which the more remote it is from the world, the nearer it is to God; and the more he labours in the observance of the divine precepts, the greater rest he has in the contemplation of the divinity as it were they are in one bed. God through grace inspiring, governing [1091C] and preserving, man through grace inspiring, governing and preserving, willing, acting and perfecting; God participable by grace, man participating in grace.

The members of this head therefore say, *our bed is flowery,* because, since with the aforesaid they are as it were in one rest, participable and participating, that is, head, member, unfading buds of virtues sprout, so that deservedly that bed is called flowery, that is, composed with the varied comeliness of good works. Thus the bride speaks to the Bridegroom: the *bed* is not thine, but *ours, flowery,* because to adhere to God through honesty of life flowering with holiness, in thought, will, action, is as it were to rest with him in one bridal chamber; otherwise the soul profits nothing without grace resting [1091D] with it.

Further, according to the sacrament of the humanity, the bed of the beloved is the womb of the blessed Virgin Mary, because it is wonderfully flowery *82* both with the beauty of virginity and with the inestimable and ineffable operation, through the Holy Spirit, of a singular nativity. But *our,* she says, *bed,* because thine indeed it is by the election of the incarnation, mine by faith in that same incarnation; thine on account of the manifold gifts of the proportion of thy grace dwelling in the mind of the Virgin and Mother, mine on account of the example of her magnificent holiness, to be imitated according to my ability, and the excellence of the novelty of novelties, to be adored without example.

The bed of the beloved is also the sepulchre, in which after the passion he was laid away, which is therefore flowery because from [1092A] this burial the ensigns of the glorious resurrection flowered forth. This *bed* too is not only thine, she says, but *ours,* because thou didst rise from this bed, that is, from the sepulchre, and gavest me the hope of rising again, the certainty of which hope is to me as it were a bed in which I rest secure, wholly, through the zeal of thy grace working with me, because I do not doubt of the future joy of the resurrection, and of the everlasting reward of transitory labour.

VERSE 16.-- *The beams of our houses are of cedar our panelled ceilings of cypress.*

But the beauty and comeliness of the following parts, by which the beloved is proclaimed fair and comely by his beloved, which are: *The beams of our houses of cedar, the panelled ceilings of cypress,* look specially to his members, whose virtues nevertheless [1092B] and graces are ascribed to his beauty and comeliness, whose is all that is called good and honest.

First, however, we must look into whose houses these beams and panelled ceilings are: according to universality, the great house of this beloved, that is, of our Lord Jesus Christ, is the holy universal Church; but according to particularity, the single churches which are contained within this universality are also called his houses. And the beams and panelled ceilings of these houses are the angels, that is, the rulers and doctors of the several Churches, who have been in time and place for protection and adornment by words and examples, who also, spreading far and wide the odour of their holiness and doctrine, have protected and adorned the universal Church itself as well, [1092C] protect and adorn; will protect and adorn, whose memory is in benediction for ever and ever.

But these *beams* are said to be *of cedar,* the *panelled ceilings,* however, *of cypress.* Now the cedar has three memorable things in its nature: height, odour, incorruptibility; which indeed rightly befit the holy doctors, because they are raised up by the contemplation of the Divinity, are spread round about by the odour of doctrine and of good action, by no rottenness of unbelief or depravity *83* are corrupted. There is also a fourth distinction of the cedar's nature, that by its odour it is said to put serpents to flight, showing indeed this virtue of the doctors whom it designates, that by their doctrine they are able to repel evil thoughts creeping secretly in hearts [1092D] and to put to flight, together with the thoughts themselves, the ancient serpent who suggests them, that is, the devil.

The cypress too has in its nature two of the graces written above, namely of odour and of incorruption; which also befit the doctors on account of the odour of life and doctrine and the incorruption of faith.

The beams and panelled ceilings may, however, not unfittingly be understood differently, so that we take the beams in the Church as the prelates, and the panelled ceilings as their subjects, because the beams support the panelled ceilings, as the prelates their subjects, while they provide them with necessaries, so that, as by the conjunction of beams and panelled ceilings the house is clothed with beautiful comeliness, so by the religious unanimity of prelates and subjects the Church of God may be ordered with fitting and reasonable disposition.
