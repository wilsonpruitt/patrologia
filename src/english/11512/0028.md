## CHAPTER TWO. (cont.)

*Under whose shade* or the Church, because, the branches of that tree, whether of sacraments, or of commandments, or of gifts, being spread through the whole breadth of the world, she is wonderfully protected and consoled from all heat of sadness and perturbation, remaining secure and immovable through the hope of future retribution, *whose fruit is sweet to the throat* of her, because all the mystical things of his utterances, and the venerable institutions of the sacraments, which [1097C] he brought forth from the hidden wisdom of his divinity, are sweet and pleasing to the mind that devoutly embraces and venerates them, *89* on account of the salvation which is conferred on it from thence, quickening and refreshing without end.

But what effect the sweetness and suavity of this fruit has, what more sublime and more perfect thing it brings forth, she shows by what she adds.

VERSE 4.-- *The king hath brought me into the wine-cellar, he hath set in order charity in me.* [var: Vulg. Ct 2:4 *Introduxit me in cellam vinariam*, without *rex*, "the king"]

The effect of this introduction proceeded from the cause of the fruit, well pleasing and therefore taken up with great zeal of mind, because the more the precepts of God are desired, so much the more fully are they admitted by divine love to ever greater things to be desired. That fruit indeed [1097D] which flowed forth, and still flows forth, from that glorious and most excellent tree, the Lord Jesus Christ, not all receive equally: because one receives this, another that, according to what he savours and loves.

For to this one the virtue of patience savours and pleases more, to that one, of obedience; to this one, of humility, to that one, of continence; this one the assiduity of prayer delights more, that one, of reading, of fasts; and in the whole they procure by divine gift the species of the virtues, of the graces.

But there is a certain as it were singular and as it were separate storehouse, standing in a certain secret place, which is called a cell because of a certain narrowness of its own, in that it admits few into its recesses, where is laid up the sweetness and suavity of that fruit which *maketh glad the heart of man [n: (Psal. CIII)]*, and boils out every humour of vices, [1098A] namely the evangelical precept concerning the utility of charity. Which is rightly called by the name of wine, because as wine, by purging itself, rejects from itself everything pestiferous, and by its natural colour boils out every noxious humour; so the precept of charity puts to flight from the heart every pest of vices, and expels all the passions of the mind, because no one can both have charity and be evil; and therefore the full understanding of this is deservedly called the wine-cellar.

Those, then, who delight to rest under the branches of that saving tree and to gather without loathing the sweetest fruit of holiness, will at some time arrive at this perfection, that they enter the wine-cellar without obstacle, because this way is not narrow for them, for whom the way of worldly pleasure, [1098B] broad and spacious, is utterly closed. For the way of this precept is narrow, because it seems difficult to many and laborious when it is said: *Love your enemies, do good to them that hate you [n: (Matth. V)]*.

But because we read that it has been observed by many saints, this precept is not difficult except to those who have not yet perfectly tasted the sweetness of the fruits, that is, of the divine commandments. This, then, is the wine-cellar, perfection, and, to those freely entering the narrow way, sweetly savouring and on this account perfectible, of the understanding of the divine precept concerning the excellence of charity.

But because charity itself looks in diverse directions, there is necessary in it *90* an order to be attended to, so that one may know in what place each affection is to be put, and which is to be taken as prior, which as posterior. Whence, when she had set before: [1098C] *The king hath brought me into the wine-cellar,* she adds, *and he hath set in order charity in me.* For this is the order of holy love, that first we love God, then our neighbour, lastly even the enemy himself. This order is indeed necessary, because it is impossible that anyone love his neighbour, or, what is more difficult, his enemy, unless he love God, since the cause of the love of these is the love of God, and in this way what was impossible becomes possible. For what is necessary to be, it is impossible not to be, and what is necessary not to be, it is impossible to be. But it is necessary that the love of God be where the love of neighbour is, because no one loves his neighbour for God's sake unless he love God: but it is necessary that the love of God not be where [1098D] the love of neighbour is not, according to the word of John: *He that loveth not his brother whom he saw, God whom he seeth not, how can he love? [n: (I Joan. IV.)]* But it is not always necessary that the love of God not be where the love of the enemy is not, because it can happen that one who loves God has no enemy whom he may love for God's sake: but if he has one, then it is necessary that the love of God not be where the love of the enemy is not, because he proves that he does not love God who is unwilling to love his enemy for God's sake.

This order, then, as has been said, seems to be necessary, when it is determined that love of neighbour is impossible unless the love of God precede, according, however, to that love which is according to God, not that which is according to the world. For the love [1099A] of the world is without the love of God, because that is according to the flesh, this according to the spirit.

But the mode of love according to the flesh is twofold: one which is altogether against God, as is that of the wicked who love one another for the fellowship of iniquity; the other which is not against God, as is that of parents, kinsmen, and friends, to which love, however, the love of God is always to be preferred, and, for God's sake, the love of each neighbour. But the first mode according to the flesh is altogether foreign to the present place, because it is wholly disordered and contrary to God; but the other, which is not against God, is not abhorrent to the present place, if only it be ordered, because if a man is loved more in God than God, then it is disordered.

[1099B]It may, however, not absurdly be taken so that we say that the order of charity is twofold, one in God, the other in the neighbour: for it is written: *God is charity [n: (I Joan. IV)]*: and in this charity which is God, the Church, or any holy soul whatever, ought to learn, know and understand the order, that in the first place she should name the Father as the voice, in the second the Son as the word from the voice, in the third the Holy Spirit as the reason proceeding from the voice and the word. But let her not say it in that sense, as though there were any degree in the Trinity, *91* which it is impious to say, but that she may distinguish in persons, by this order of terms, that charity, that is, God, whom she ought to love with the whole heart, the whole soul, the whole strength, one and the same in substance.

[1099C]But love itself is distinguished by this order, that God be loved first with the whole heart, then with the whole soul, lastly with the whole strength, by believing, with the whole soul by confessing, with the whole strength by working.

But in the love of neighbour charity is ordered through the several precepts of the Law which follow: for not to commit adultery, not to do murder, not to speak false witness, to honour father and mother, etc., following the order of the Decalogue, is charity ordered, each in its own place as well. How also, or with what affection, the diverse persons in the Church ought to be loved, charity is ordered; because in one order ought the faithful friend to be loved, in another order the dearest son, in another order parents, in another order the full brother, in another order [1099D] the spouse, in another order the servant by the master, in another order the master by the servant, in another the stranger, in another the acquaintance, in another the priest, in another the kinsman and neighbour. For to bestow necessaries on the needy, to visit the sick or those committed to prison, to console the needy and those oppressed by unjust judgment, to bury the dead, to correct those who act wickedly, to help brothers in some labour, to instruct the less knowing, or to expend whatever other things are necessary to the utility of neighbours, all these assuredly are orderable offices of holy charity.

Deservedly, then, the holy mind is brought in by her king and Bridegroom into the wine-cellar, that is, into the understanding of the divine precepts, when, inebriated by the sweetness of his grace, she understands the diligence of charity to be expended, so manifoldly ordered. Whence, [1100A] when she is affected by so great a strength of this inebriating wine that she remains as it were insensible to every worldly delight, and for the immense desire of charity so ordered is not only stupefied but even languishes, demanding for herself some solace of this languor, she goes on to say: *Stay me up with flowers,* etc.

VERSE 5.-- *Stay me up with flowers, compass me about with apples, because I languish with love.*

What are the flowers but souls bringing forth the beginnings of good work and beginning to be fragrant with heavenly desire? And what are the apples but souls already holding perfection from the beginnings of good works through the nourishment of divine grace, as it were advancing from flowers to fruit? She, then, who languishes with divine love [1100B] seeks to be stayed up with flowers, compassed about with apples, because, since she is not yet able to see as he is him whom she loves so orderly with the whole heart, the whole soul, the whole strength, that is, with faith, confession, operation, yet it is a great consolation to her that she rejoices in the pious advancements of her neighbours, now beginning, now perfected.

*92* For holy Church has in herself certain lesser ones whom she may instruct by word and example, that they may be as flowers catching the first-fruits of holiness. She has also certain greater ones in whom she may be instructed, and by whose doctrines and examples she may be daily edified, who are as apples, pleasing already by the maturity and perfection of good works. Such, then, namely both the lesser and the greater, growing up in this manner with zeal for piety, [1100C] greatly console holy Church, which surely with great gladness of heart sees her sons, small and great, rude and perfect, love this which she herself vehemently loves; and this is a great consolation of her languor, in the exile of this pilgrimage, that they tend with her by desire towards that to which she believes she will at some time arrive by reward.

In this way too any saint whatever who languishes with the love of God can say: *Stay me up with flowers [sic: loribus], compass me about with apples,* when he esteems as a great remedy of such languor the progress of his neighbours, which he sees in the good work of his own, whom he loves in God, these indeed sprouting towards pious pursuits, those bearing fruit in those very pursuits. Did not he who [1100D] languished with such love that he said: *I desire to be dissolved and to be with Christ,* nevertheless desire the utility of his neighbours, saying, *but to remain in the flesh is necessary for your sakes? [n: (Philipp. I.)]* Whose progress to see he meanwhile reckons as a great consolation, when he is perfectly able [cj: *praevalet*; read *non praevalet*, "is not able"] to comprehend the face of God which he so greatly desires to see. Although he greatly desires to be dissolved and to be with Christ, did he who languished with divine love fear that he could not at some time attain what he loved? By no means; but this came about by the dispensation of God, that the delay of this reward might become a greater exercise of virtues, and also an ampler remuneration of labour for the utility of those whom he loved; for he who desired to be dissolved was certainly sure of the reward, as he himself says elsewhere: *We know [1101A] that if our earthly house of this habitation be dissolved, we have a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens [n: (II Cor. V)]*.

Wherefore he bore the pain of this delay more lightly, because he rejoiced that those whom he loved were tending with him to receive the same reward.
