## CHAPTER II FOLLOWS. (cont.)

Hence he is said to be *like a roe and to a young hart:* for it is natural to the roe always to seek ascents; and while to the saints the distribution of graces is allotted in diverse manner: *Some indeed thus, and others thus,* to the only-begotten alone [sic: *sol*] *God giveth not the spirit by measure, because in him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead corporeally [n: (I Cor. VII; Joan. III; Coloss. II)]*. For the saints [1108D] in the pursuit of the virtues now ascend, now do not ascend, because in their merits that grace which they have now works, now rests, so that this may serve them for the keeping of humility, that they do not always have ready to hand the grace of the virtue conferred. But in this beloved the grace of the virtues abides not for a time, nor by intervals, but without intermission, as it is written of him that *the Holy Spirit descended in the form of a dove and remained upon him [n: (Luc. III; Joan. I)]*.

He is likened also to a young hart for swiftness, because, as the young hart, through the lightness of its youthful body, is much swifter than the hart, so this beloved, as it were the young one of the harts, that is, the son of the patriarchs, from whose stock he came forth, is far [1109A] more excellent and more agile in the efficacy of the virtues and the wisdom of his words than even the pursuits and teachings of the ancient Fathers themselves.

Since, then, this beloved by exhorting and by leaping on before calls all to the pursuits of the virtues, and some follow according to their power, some, their frailty standing in the way, despair of being able to follow, some, weighed down by the mass of their sins, distrust that they can rise again to them, [she] takes away from them every occasion of distrusting, holding out to the beloved the ineffable kindness of this beloved, mercifully inviting all to his grace, and adds: *Lo, he himself standeth behind our wall,* etc. There is one general wall, namely that of original sin; but this wall is spread out into many special walls [1109B] of actual sin. For from original sin, as from a certain mass, have come forth many and diverse actual sins, because after they fell through the original sin of the first parent, the kind of this infirmity is poured over into their posterity, namely to be always prone to sins. But this general wall began to be destroyed when the Son of God came through the flesh, by whose blood it was at length removed and scattered, when, *dissolving the enmities between God and man, he reconciled us to God [n: (Eph. II)]*; and lest it be able to be built again, it is destroyed daily in the Church through the grace of baptism.

But the special wall is set up in diverse men in diverse manner, because in one it is the vice of pride, in [1109C] another that of avarice, *102* in this one that of lust, in that one that of unchastity, in some one that of obedience [cj: *obedientiae*; read *inobedientiae*, "of disobedience"], in some other that of impatience, or whatever other vice, which according to the quality, and the manner and the number, of the vice itself makes a wall of whatever kind between man and God also [cj: *quoque*; read *quo*, "by as much as"] the vice is greater or more frequent or more numerous and in man, by so much the heavier and more immovable is the weight in the wall.

But behind this wall, whether the general one of original sin or the special one of actual sin, this beloved stands, because he is ready mercifully to receive whoever, some such wall being removed, have been zealous to draw near to him again by destroying it through penance. For to stand is the part of one who helps, to sit of one who judges. *He standeth behind the wall,* because while he disposes through his grace to come to us [1109D], the wall of sin standing in the way, he is as it were compelled to stand. For at first he stood long so as not to come to remove that wall of original sin, on account of many things, by hindering his coming, by many afflictions and adversities by which those were pursued who desired and awaited his coming, at whose coming they knew that this wall was to be destroyed through his blood and that they were to be reconciled to God.

He stood, then, for this cause and put off coming, *looking* meanwhile *through the windows, looking forth through the lattices.* For what were the windows in the wall but the prophets and other teachers of the divine law, by whose doctrine and preaching God might find as it were certain outlets for looking, through which he might pour out some [1110A] light of his grace, by which the human race, enlightened and admonished, might cease to delay the coming of this beloved, which was to profit its salvation, by the interposing of so great malice as of a certain wall

But the lattices, that is, the front part of the building, were the ancient fathers who went before, namely the patriarchs before the law announcing Christ, through whom he who was to come long after looked forth in part by signs and portents and certain teachings of reasons; admonishing that they should not hinder by the wall of their evils the future times of his so great salvation.

So long, then, did he stand behind our wall, looking forth mercifully and taking counsel by word, law, signs and prodigies, until at last, moved [sic: *per motus*] [1110B] by the affliction of his own, who cried out often: *Come, O Lord, and do not delay, loose the crimes of thy people,* the commotions of all lands being quelled under Caesar Augustus, and a most firm and most true peace being disposed by the ordinance of God, as though the wall of the resisting malice were now shaken, this beloved began to show by being born the face long desired, *103* and thereafter by preaching, dying, rising again, ascending into heaven, and so spreading the law of his Gospel through the whole world, to destroy daily through the grace of baptism the wall of original sin long set against him.

Further, the windows and lattices in the wall of actual sin, through which this beloved is daily looked out upon, [1110C] are the examples and teachings of the Gospels and of the apostles and of the other holy fathers; windows indeed there, where they pour the light of knowledge and understanding into our hearts, that we may be able to see how we may remove the wall of each sin set between us and God; but lattices in that place where, from their writings, through the power of contemplation, *we know in part and we prophesy in part [n: (I Cor. XIII)]* God looking upon us, so that while we attend to the glory or the punishment of the future life, we may labour daily to destroy the wall of sin that separates us from God.

Moreover. The Son of God, coming in the flesh, stood behind the wall of our mortality, looking forth upon us through the windows and lattices of his divinity; who [1110D] also shone out as God from the power of the miracles and yet lay hidden from the nature of human passibility. For he who looks forth through windows and lattices is neither wholly seen nor wholly not seen: so, surely, our Redeemer too, coming in the flesh, showed himself God by the miracles, but hid it by the sufferings. He stood, then, behind the wall of human passibility, because, hidden among men by his humanity, he did not come as God; but he looked forth through the windows and lattices, because by divine compassion he dictated to the whole human race the everlasting gifts of salvation. And because the beloved whom he drew inwardly by the regard of his mercy he instructed outwardly too by the word of preaching, he shows it by adding:

VERSE 10-- *Behold my beloved speaketh to me: Arise, make haste, my love, my dove, my beautiful [sic: formo sa] one, and come.* [1111A]

This speech of the beloved is distinguished in a threefold manner, by the beauty of the address, of the exhortation, and of the cause: of the address indeed, when he says: *my love, my dove, my beautiful one,* but of the exhortation when he says: *Arise, make haste, come;* of the cause when he says: *For winter is now past, the rain is over and gone, the flowers have appeared, the time of pruning is come.* For the beauty of the address is to say: Love, dove, beautiful one; the beauty of the exhortation, *arise, make haste, come;* the beauty of the cause, that the winter and the rain have gone back, that the flowers appear, that the time of pruning is here, that the voice of the turtle has been heard, that the fig tree has put forth her green figs, that the flowering vines have given their fragrance.

[1111B] *104* But the order in this address, exhortation and cause is not disposed without consideration. In the address indeed, when first he says love, then dove, lastly beautiful one; but in the exhortation first *arise,* then *make haste,* lastly *come.* Further, in the cause, in the first place *winter is past,* in the second *the rain is gone,* in the third *the flowers have appeared,* in the fourth *the time of pruning is come,* in the fifth *the voice of the turtle is heard in our land,* in the sixth *the fig tree hath put forth her green figs,* in the seventh *the flowering vines have given their fragrance.* The cause of this exhortation, by which the beloved is admonished to arise, to make haste, to come, is the going back of the winter and the rain, the appearing of the flowers, and the time of pruning; the voice of the turtle and the fig trees putting forth their green figs, and the flowering vines.

[1111C]But it must be seen whence this speech proceeds: she had said above, *the voice of my beloved,* which voice we said is Holy Scripture, for the whole divine page is the voice of God, through which by reading the beloved the leaps of him clothed with the wall of our mortality, and speaking to her through the windows and lattices of miracles and doctrines; for as often as we see or read the Holy Scriptures, God speaks to us or with us. In this manner, then, the beloved, that is, the Church or any faithful soul, perceives the beloved speaking to her, when she hears, reads and understands the order of the divine dispensation, the religion of the sacraments, the profit of the divine precepts.

But the beloved himself, as it were solicitous out of the salvation of his beloved, [1111D] in a speech of this kind takes on [sic: *assumunt*] himself the office of a good orator, so as to draw the hearer in three ways, that is, so as to make her attentive, teachable, well-disposed: for while he calls out not once but three times, saying, *arise, make haste, come,* as if he said, Offer thyself not sluggishly but swiftly and zealously, he makes her attentive, as one about to hear something settled and unwonted; but when he often addresses her with a word of love and of grace, saying: *My love, my dove, my beautiful one,* he renders her well-disposed, so that she may bring forth a greater will to prepare herself and to hear.

Further, he teaches him the cause of this calling distinctly by number, saying: *For winter is now past, the rain is over and gone, the flowers have appeared, the time of pruning is come, the voice of the turtle is heard, [1112A] the flowering vines have given their fragrance,* he makes her teachable, so that she may recognize the time of great salvation and grace, which she ought to receive with great veneration and joy.

And this is the sign of great kindness, by which this beloved often looks toward us through the *windows* and *lattices* of his infinite clemency, *standing behind the wall* of our evils, *set against us as a cloud, that prayer may not pass through [n: (Thren. III)]*, speaking daily and admonishing us to scatter this wall or this cloud with the battering-ram of the virtues and the breath of the Holy Spirit.

What, then, does the beloved speak to the beloved? *Arise,* he says, *make haste, my love* *105* *my dove, my beautiful one, and come:* arise through faith, make haste through hope, [1112B] come through charity: or, arise through intention, make haste through working, come through contemplation: for this is the perfection of thy whole worship and religion, to believe, to hope, to love: for the other three, namely intention, working, contemplation, are constituted from these. For if thou believest rightly, thou also aimest rightly at doing good things; but thou workest for the hope of obtaining the reward, and thou contemplatest the more subtly, the more thou art raised up to God through charity.

And this thou wilt be able to do if thou be a love, that is, if, the error of unbelief being cast away, to be reconciled to me through faith; if thou be a dove, that is, if thou live simply, harm no one and be content with thine own alone, in the hope of the future reward; or if, some frailty [1112C] compelling thee, thou go out of the ark, that is, out of the Church, by doing ill, thou remain not in the world, but return quickly bearing an olive branch in thy mouth, that is, having hope of mercy through confession and the measure of penance.

If thou be beautiful, that is, keeping by the love of God alone, thou set behind thee all the cares of the world, or whatever things can infect the beauty of thy mind, so that, having not the bleared eyes of Lia but the beautiful and fair face of Rachel, thou mayest be able with the eye of the mind purged to gaze into that majesty of heavenly beatitude which is God.
