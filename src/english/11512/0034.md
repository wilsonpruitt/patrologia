## CHAPTER II FOLLOWS. (cont.)

Whence the beloved too, admonishing her, adds: *Show me thy face.* For what is the face of the beloved but a certain image of holiness shining out from fellowship with the beloved? For the more sedulously each one [1116D] cleaves to divine contemplation, the more beautiful the face of the mind shines out from the radiance of holiness. Hence it is that Moses, having conversed forty days with the Lord on the mountain, *100* is written to have brought forth a shining face from fellowship with the speech of the Lord [n: (Exod. XXXIV)]. For he who has tarried on the mountain of heavenly contemplation brings forth a certain face of holiness from the very Spirit of God, with whom he is one; whence the Apostle: *With open,* he says, *face beholding the glory of the Lord, we are transformed into the same image, from glory to glory, as by the Spirit of the Lord [n: (II Cor. III)]*. Those, then, who are led by the Spirit of God are transformed by the Spirit himself into the image of Christ according to the inner man, from glory to glory, so that those who have drawn from him something of glory into the face of the inner man [1117A], by which they may know him more clearly, the nearer they draw to him by contemplative glory, the more glory they may gather to themselves from him, through which they may know him ever more clearly and more truly. Whence also Ezechiel, when he described the vision of the holy living creatures, said: *The likeness of a man in them [n: (Ezech. I)]*. For whatever of justice and meekness, whatever of modesty and humility, whatever of patience and piety, whatever finally of holiness the elect of God ever could have or can have, they drew from that singular man, the Lord Jesus Christ; and, illumined by that principal face of holiness, they too conceived in their own manner a certain face of holiness.

Such, says the beloved, *thy face*, drawn out spiritually from my grace, *O beloved, show me,* that [1117B] is, go forth in public to the praise of my name with a fair appearance of action and speech, so that thy neighbours, seeing *thy works, may glorify* thy *Father who is in heaven [n: (Matth. V)]*, and themselves, from the rightly composed countenance of thy action and speech, may compose the countenance of their own mind and intention too.

*Let thy voice sound in my ears*: the voice of one preaching sounds in the ears of the Lord then, when it accords with my voice, so that what sounds outwardly to the ears of man through the voice, this may speak in the ears of God through faith. And, having set first, *show me thy face,* he fittingly adds, *let thy voice sound in my ears,* to show that *with the heart it is believed unto justice, but with the mouth confession is unto salvation [n: (Rom. X)]*; for by the face [1117C] the knowledge of faith, but by the voice the sound of preaching [can] be expressed.

Further: *Show,* he says, *me thy face, let thy voice sound in my ears,* that is, go forth in public in action and preaching from that angelic dormitory of thy holy solitude with such an intention that thou admit me alone as witness of thy conscience, not so as to expose thyself to human favours, but so as to please my sight alone. *For thy voice is sweet, and thy face comely.* For this reason thou oughtest to go forth, because thy neighbours can attend to this in the beauty of thy manners and the sweetness of thy speeches, whence they may both correct the depravity of their own manners and take hold of the integrity of right faith.

*111* But often also the very showing of the countenance and bearing [1117D] is a certain voice of the mind, and as a certain one says: *Often the countenance, though silent, has a voice and words.*


Whence of her who showed the sadness of her mind by the change of her countenance, after the reproach of barrenness was taken away, it is written that: *Her countenance was no more changed to divers ways [n: (I Reg. I)]*. For the diversity of the countenance showed the diversity of the soul; but the cause of this diversity being taken away, a right and composed countenance too spoke a right and composed soul. But this voice of honesty, set in the countenance and bearing of the outward man, sounds in the ears of God then, when it keeps it unchangeably for meriting the glory of eternal life alone. *Thy voice,* he says, *is sweet and thy face comely: thy voice,* he says, which is the interpreter of the soul and speaks either by manners or by tongue, [1118A] for it preaches and teaches, whether by doing well or by speaking well; this *voice,* he says, which *is sweet, let it sound in my ears,* because that life pleases me greatly which both does and teaches good things, the love of God and of neighbour so acting in it that it both pleases God and edifies the neighbour. *And thy face comely,* because it brings this to pass from thy intention and faith, both of which are known to me alone, that what thou doest or sayest may be worthy of the praise of my approval. With this comeliness, then, and thy beauty set out, proceed prosperously and reign, so that, since thou art joined to me by such a dowry of virtues, thou mayest not unfittingly be called Queen of thy Bridegroom who is King, since, with me ruling and instructing, thou knowest both to rule thyself reasonably and to bestow on others the rule of souls.

[1118B]But because heretics often deprave this sweetness and beauty of right faith and right preaching by perverse doctrine, so as to pervert the hearts of the simple, the voice of the beloved makes an apostrophe to the masters of this very faith and preaching when it adds, *catch us the little [sic: purvulas] foxes.*

VERSE 15.-- *Catch us the little foxes that destroy the vines; for our vineyard hath flourished.*

The foxes are the heretics, because of the craftiness and cunning with which they deceive the unwary by the arguments of words; who always seek hiding places in which to teach, and think those things perversely, making conventicles apart, lest the holy and Catholic Church detect their depravity. And they are called little foxes, [1118C] whether because of the feigned humility of the heretics themselves, or because of the subtlety of their most keenly sought-out sentences, by which they force chiefly the minds of those who marvel but understand less to favour their depraved doctrines. *Catch,* he says, *us;* for the heretics are then caught for God when by Catholic doctors, through the assertion of right reason, which they can destroy by no arguments, divine grace going before, they are led back to the bosom of their mother, holy Church, from *112* which they had departed by erring, and confirm by words and examples the faith which before they attacked. *Catch,* he says, *us the little foxes,* as if he said in other words: Convict the subtle and crafty corrupters of right faith by their own sentences, and by probable reason, which they cannot refute at all, compel them to pass over into [1118D] the fellowship [sic: *conturbernium*] of our party: *For I will catch the wise in their own craftiness, and I will destroy the wisdom of the wise, and the prudence of the prudent I will reject [n: (I Cor. I, III)]*; so that when they have recognized the reason of living or teaching rightly, their own wisdom may displease them, and what they strove before to build up by it they may destroy when the truth is recognized, and may become one with my faithful.

*For our vineyard hath flourished,* that is, either the universal Church or a particular one, or some faithful individual and single person, has taken hold of the beginning of living or believing rightly; and therefore they are to be removed or caught, if any have arisen among them not living or believing rightly, lest they suffocate the tender faith, the works of piety, both by depraved dogma and by depraved examples.

[1119A]Nor is it absurd, as we believe, if we call each faithful soul the vineyard of the Lord, as we do the universal or particular Church, because of the like propagation of the virtues, and the spiritual *wine* which *maketh glad the heart of man [n: (Psal. CIII)]*, which can abound in each faithful individual through the grace of the Holy Spirit, as also in the universal or particular Church. For each one ought with the knife of spiritual exercise to cut back the sacraments of the vices, to turn over the earth of his heart with the hoe of inmost consideration, and if he has admitted anything earthly or sordid in thought or in deed, to lay it about the root of the heart by keeping it in memory, and to water it by repenting with frequent fountains of tears; to bind the whole intention of his good purpose to Christ, who is the tree of life, [1119B] lest it flow down again to earthly things, and to hope ever in his help and support; or to cleave to the tree of the cross of Christ, bearing about his mortification in his body; and in such a manner, the mind, assiduously tilled and tended by the grace of the Holy Spirit, becomes ever more fruitful, and is called, according to the prophet, the vineyard of Sorec, which sounds "chosen and fair." Whence the Lord's sentence in the Gospel concerning the barren vine and the vine bearing fruit, where he calls himself the vine, and the disciples the branch, but the Father the husbandman: *Every branch in me,* he says, *that beareth not fruit, he will take it away,* the Father, *and every one that beareth fruit he will purge it, that it may bring forth more fruit [n: (Joan. XV)]*. If, then, the Saviour is the vine and we the branches, and God the Father the husbandman, so long as we keep the precepts of God we always [1119C] cultivate, and our tillage is a purging, that we may bring forth more fruit: *For it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that showeth mercy [n: (Rom. IX)]*, whose help we always need, that we may arrive at the end of our good purpose.

* 113* VERSE 16, 17.-- *My beloved to me, and I to him, who feedeth among the lilies, till the day break, and the shadows retire.*

Divine love and human love are not constituted in a like manner nor on an equal ground; and though it is said equivocally, yet by the ground of definition it is very diverse, since between affections of this kind, of the flesh and of the spirit, there is no comparison at all. For divine love always has a certain relative, but human [1119D] love not always, because often one who loves is not loved, nor is the consequence the same on both sides. For it follows that a man loving God is loved by God, since to love God is nothing other than to live justly and piously, and it is consequent that God, just and pious, loves the just and pious, when such an affection comes from the gift of his grace: but it does not follow that a man loving is always loved by a man, because, though often, yet not always does carnal affection work this relative; but the affection of the Divinity always works it, according to the sentence of the Saviour, who says: *he that loveth me shall be loved by my Father, and I will love him and will manifest myself to him [n: (Joan. XIV)]*. For the relative of this love is converted, so that because he loves he is loved, [1120A] and because he is loved he loves, the prevenient grace working this in the hearts of the elect, so that they may see by divine love, which also loves its own gifts in each one. Whence here the beloved speaks of her beloved: *my beloved to me and I to him.* For it is proper to relatives to be said reciprocally; for what is predicated of him who is beloved is conversely predicated of her who is beloved: for a *dilectus* is someone's *dilectus*, and a *dilecta* someone's *dilecta*. This relation, then, comes about in this manner, that it is said: *dilectus dilectae*, *dilectus et dilecta*, *dilecti dilecta*: let her say, then, *my beloved to me and I to him;* as if she said: Because the beloved has conferred his grace on me to this end, that I may please him by right faith and working, therefore he is beloved to me; and because, since he has given me free will, I obey the calling grace by keeping his precepts, therefore I am beloved to him: [1120B] for unless he loved me out of mercy, I could not love him out of grace, he would not love me out of mercy. For whatever good lies near me, to will, to be able to do, to perfect, I have wholly from his gift [n: (Rom. IV)].

*Who feedeth among the lilies.* Behold the cause why I am beloved to him: for he feeds, that is, he delights in whiteness, that is, in the cleanness and chastity of mind and body, and in the most sweet fragrance of the virtues; which, since through his grace they breathe out from my love, therefore he has made me worthy of his love.
