## CHAPTER THREE.

*[1123B] 117* VERSE 1.-- *In my bed by nights I sought him whom my soul loveth; I sought him, and found him not.*

By the name of the bed the rest and tranquillity of the mind can be designated; because while through thought we rest from worldly din, we can investigate more subtly the quality and manner of the good to be sought, the end of which good to understand is to find God: but we call it the end, not that by which it ceases to be, but that by which it perfectly begins to be. *In* her *bed,* then, the beloved seeks the beloved, when in the rest of her mind she desires to understand the right rule of goodness and of discipline and of knowledge.

But she seeks by nights, because in the consideration of subtle things the eye of the mind is darkened, so that it does not at once prevail by [1123C] itself to see what it seeks. Whence she by no means finds the beloved whom she seeks, because the very difficulty of understanding makes a certain delay of finding.

VERSE 2.-- *I will rise, and will go about the city: in the streets and the broad ways I will seek him whom my soul loveth: I sought him, and found him not.*

*118* What then is to be done? assuredly, that from that quiet of mind in which she lies alone with her own sense, she rise to study, searching out also the senses, actions and writings of others, so that what she does not prevail [sic: *praevale*] to understand by herself, she may perceive from their sayings and writings.

For this is to go about the city, and to seek the beloved through the streets and the broad ways: to run through the Church and [1123D] to search out the sentences of the holy doctors, to investigate those who walk by the broad and by the narrow, and to consider whether there be in them any traces of holiness, because sometimes secular men have something of good work which is to be imitated, and also to be imitated according to one's power. For the broad way is that of secular men, the narrow that of the saints, because many are they who walk by the broad, but few who walk [sic: *gradiuntu*] by the narrow way [n: (Matth. VII)].

VERSE 3.-- *The watchmen who keep the city found me: Have you seen him whom my soul loveth?*

But the watchmen find her going about the city and the streets and the broad ways, because to the studious mind searching into the documents of the Church, looking into history also, which is as it were the broad way, because it is open and easy to understand, and allegory also, which is as it were the narrow way, because it is [1124A] subtle and difficult to understand, and searching out whether there be in them any things useful and necessary to faith and to working, the holy doctors come to meet her with their sentences and authorities, and show the beloved whom she was seeking to her as she understands and desires to imitate. For they are called watchmen of whom the Lord says in the Gospel: *Blessed are those servants whom, when the Lord shall come, he shall find watching [n: (Luc. XXII)]*. For the holy doctors, set in the body, were watching, and still watch by doctrine and working for their own profit and that of their neighbours; but stripped of the body they watch and will watch by writings and examples, for their own reward indeed, but for the salvation of their neighbours. These watchmen, namely, keep the city, because the holy doctors confirm and protect the holy universal Church by the reasons and authorities of their testimonies and [1124B] examples, and make it impregnable to the enemies of the holy faith.

Such watchmen, then, the beloved, hesitating, questions [sic: *suscitatur*], saying: *Have you seen him whom my soul loveth?* Can you, she says, show and prove in your writings and deeds that you so loved God that assuredly to imitate your life and doctrine is to find the beloved?

VERSE 4.-- *When I had a little passed by them, I found him whom my soul loveth; I held him, and I will not let him go, till I bring him into my mother's house, and into the chamber of her that bore me.*

*119* But because perseverance of the search does not put off the effect of the finding, she adds: *When I had a little [1124C] passed by them, I found him whom my soul loveth:* for when for some while she has passed through the deeds and writings of the holy Fathers by reading or hearing, at length she finds the beloved by understanding and by desiring to imitate.

But because what she has understood here she resolves to observe in faith and work, as the beloved found, she goes on, saying: *I held him, and I will not let him go, till I bring him into my mother's house, and into the chamber of her that bore me.* But what is sought studiously with desire and eagerness is held dearer when found and is held more strongly. Whence the beloved, that is, the holy soul, after, by going about the Church and frequently looking into the sayings and deeds of the holy Fathers, understanding the norm of living or of teaching rightly as God himself, [1124D] found the beloved. Since indeed to live and to teach rightly is to have God, she holds him firmly in memory, and does not let him go, until she insinuates him to others also by teaching and doing, and brings him into their sense through faith and love; not that after this bringing in she ceases to hold him, but that she desires that to others also the participation of this grace, and, by virtue, of it diffused in herself through the Holy Spirit of charity, be conferred everlastingly together with herself. For this word *until* must be understood to have been set for a limit [cj: *pro determinatione*; read *non pro determinatione*, "not for a limit"], but for what is everlasting, as if she said: So long will I commend him to their tenacious memory, pitying him to my sense and to that of others, until he be so made firm in us by faith and action that he cannot fall away any more from our mind.

[1125A]But it is not to be thought that the places of this bringing in are set down so distinctly and differently without reason, that is, *the house of the Mother and the chamber of her that bore.* For since the whole body of the Church, on account of the diversity of senses and of pursuits, is divided into three, that is, into the little, the middling and the great; the house indeed receives all, but the chamber not all, but the great only, and those who attain something of speciality more differently from the others: for every house is a more evident and ample place, into which not only the Lord but also the whole household enters: it is the public place of the universal Church; but the chamber is a certain singular and as it were private place in the Church, to which access and entrance lies open to none but those raised up by ecclesiastical dignity and order, whose profession it is specially to cleave to God. But relative [1125B] names, like father and mother, which are always said to be that which they are of others, are taken in three ways, that is, *120* by nature, by affection, by adoption, of which, the first being passed over, the two remaining ways can be fitted to the mother or her that bore of this place. For holy Church is the mother of every faithful one by affection and adoption, because to him whom she has adopted to herself through faith for a son, living holily and justly, she always lays open her affection. And that we may distinguish something here, let us say that she is also she who bore him, according as she begets him, by forming him to the honesty and utility of a holy life, or by instructing him with the pages of sacred Scripture, and so by bringing him up into a perfect man in virtue and knowledge according to the measure which is pleasing to helping grace.

[1125C]The beloved, therefore, brings the beloved into the house of her mother, when she shows God, whom she acknowledges by faith and work, to the sons of her holy mother the Church, that is, to the little, the middling and the great, both by the examples of the acts of the saints and by the teachings of the Holy Scriptures, which she now brings forth for the letter alone, now interprets for the mystical sense, so that all may attain them, that is, the little, the middling and the great, each according to his capacity; the little indeed to the letter only; the middling both to the letter and to the mystical sense, but in part and not perfectly; the great both to the letter and to the mystical sense, not in part but perfectly, and who can investigate and understand things yet other, and things as it were unsearchable to others. Whence to them [1125D] only is the beloved shown in the chamber of her that bore, because to them the subtlety and discernment of the precepts and a certain secret discussion of the divine sacraments is covered [cj: *operitur*; read *aperitur*, "is opened"] from the secret place of the Church that instructs them.

But after these things the seeking, the finding and the bringing in by the beloved, to be taken in this manner also, so that with respect to the preceding sense they are said in some things otherwise, in some things uniformly. For otherwise by the name of the bed can be understood the secure dissoluteness of carnal pleasure, and dissolute security, where, because it is the night of sin, Christ is not found; wherefore from this useless and wretched quiet let the sinner rise with the eagerness of a ready mind, and, that we may speak uniformly according to the sense above, let him run through holy Church with devout intention, passing through whose watchmen, that is, the doctors, let him learn by reading [1126A] and hearing how he may correct his morals. And let him find in the city of virtues God whom he could not find in the bed of evil security, and let him grow by advancing so long, until he brings him into the house of his mother, which is otherwise to say this world, which is the place and habitation of human offspring, as of a mother procreating universal man. For he openly proclaims to all how great things God has done for him, and, provoking them to his example, exhorts that, carnal pleasure being despised, they forget the house of this their mother by leaving the world.

And when in this way he has brought *121* the beloved into the house of his mother through the active life, at last he advances so far that he brings him through the contemplative life into the chamber, that is, into the secret places of solitude, [1126B] whether of the hermitage or of the monastery, which is uniformly to say of her that bore, that is, of the Church, which begets, nourishes and forms him to the honesty of the monastic life, which he himself afterwards so surpasses by so great an excellence of virtues, that by his example and doctrine the Church herself advances more than he had before advanced by her instruction; and let this be to bring the beloved into the chamber of her that bore him.

Loving and praising whose study and purpose, the beloved addresses the daughters of Jerusalem, that is, the simple souls in the Church, who are therefore called daughters, not sons, because for infirmity of mind they cannot yet climb up to those things to which all the perfect have attained by manliness of soul. *I adjure you,* he says, *O daughters of Jerusalem,* etc.

[1126C]VERSE 5.-- *I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem, by the roes and the harts of the fields, that you stir not up nor make the beloved to awake, till she herself please.*

For to those who rest in mind and body in the chamber of monastic conversation, such a cause often arises that it is necessary to lay aside for a while that contemplative eye and to provide in some things for the profit or necessity of neighbours; but because there are some infirm minds which wish this to be done by their superiors too frequently, and demand rather that they be stirred up and awake to exterior things, they are as it were forbidden by certain adjurations, by the sentences and authorities of the Fathers running through the breadths of the holy Scriptures, but rather that they leave it to the judgment of those [1126D] who understand through the spirit of discretion in what place and time they ought to awake to this office.

For the sentences of the holy Scriptures, or the authors themselves of the sentences, are as it were certain roes and harts, because while they propose that earthly things are to be left and heavenly things sought, they show as it were certain leaps from earth to heaven, and while they command men to be fervent and solicitous in good work, they hold out a certain agility of mind to be had by them; for it is written: *Mind the things that are above, not the things that are upon the earth; seek the things that are above, where Christ is sitting at the right hand of God;* and elsewhere: *But our conversation is in the heavens;* and in another place: *Hasten to enter into that rest;* and again: *Hastening and [1127A] making speed unto the meeting of the Lord;* and elsewhere: *So run that you may comprehend;* and the Lord in the Gospel: *Run while you have the light, lest the darkness comprehend you;* [var: Vulg. Jo 12:35 *Ambulate dum lucem habetis*, "Walk whilst you have the light"] and elsewhere: *Lift up your heads, for your redemption is at hand;* and in the Psalm: *Blessed is the man whose help is from thee, he hath disposed ascents in his heart in the vale of tears [n: (Col. III.--Phil. III.--Heb. IV.--Amos. IV.--I Cor. IX.--Joan. XII.--Luc. XXI.--Psal. LXXXIII)]*.
