## CHAP. III.-- *Continuation.*

[1127C]

We read in the history of the Kings that, when Eliseus the prophet had been questioned by the king of Israel, saying: *Why hath he gathered these three kings, to deliver them into the hands of Moab?* after some things: *Bring me,* he says, *a minstrel; and when the minstrel sang, the hand of the Lord came upon him, and he said: Make the channel of this torrent ditches and ditches [n: (IV Reg. III)]*. It is plain in this matter that the spirit of the prophet is not always present to the prophets, and therefore the prophet, the spirit resting in him, caused a minstrel to be brought, so that, while the minstrel sang, the spirit which was in a certain manner resting in him, granted [n: ( an. concussus?)], might be roused up and might see for him that about which he was being questioned.

But to what end the bringing forward of this example? assuredly [1127D] it looks to your intention, O beloved sisters, and to mine. For because it seemed to your humility that the Spirit of holy love was resting in you, and was as it were idle, you indicate that it is useful and necessary to bring you some minstrel, at whose singing that spirit which had as it were fallen asleep in your mind might be roused, for a double cause, as I think: either that, as David by singing softened the fury of Saul, so, if anything of unevenness should suddenly steal upon your mind, it might be composed by the sweetness of the singer, [1128C] or, if your soul had anything less of fervour in divine love, it might be kindled by a certain inmost force of song.

Since, then, this was your intention, by I know not what spirit you brought my littleness, which knows how to sing nothing of the kind, and enjoined upon me the office of this minstrel. What was I to do? on the one side your affection was urging me on, on the other side the danger to my wit was driving me back. At length, presuming on the help of God and the suffrage of your prayers, I did not refuse to be your minstrel, however unlearned, since I reckoned that with your love my excess would be easy, and because, according to the sentence of the great orator, virtue which charity follows is by no means to be rejected; but what is my virtue? Small, assuredly, and such as [1128D] is of almost no power in the arduous work of so great an office. Yet if charity can do anything in any way, it administers this.

But this indeed was your intention; my intention, however, was to obey those who asked, and to sing something by which your spirit might be roused. But behold, unskilfulness stands in the way of good will, since this ought to be the part of a skilled singer, that he know both how to profit and how to delight, according to that of Flaccus: *He has carried every vote who has mixed the useful with the sweet.*


[1129A] *124* Secular letters report that a certain Orpheus by his song soothed tigers and lions, by way of likeness, namely, because he tamed the bestial and wild manners of men by the reasonable sentence of his speech. Amphion also is said to have moved stones with the sound of the lyre [sic: *testitudinis*] and with bland entreaty to have led them whither he would, because he composed the hard and uncomposed manners of men rightly and by the sweet measure of reason. Great, then, is the skill of a singer, to change the minds of the hearers by the measure of his song. But I do not reckon that the measure of this my begun song has availed to this with your sanctity, not on account of the slowness of your wit, but on account of the density of my ignorance. For he is an unskilled singer who does not know the property of voices and of modes, [1129B] *And the harper*
*Is laughed at, who always strays on the same string,*
[n: (HORAT., De arte poetica, V. 356.)] Whence, after some part of this most excellent canticle had been sung through, I had decided rather to be silent than to uncover further such great follies of mine.

But because sometimes a skilled craftsman, laboriously reshaping a form made from the same material by an unskilled craftsman, makes it as it were another, and a skilled singer restores to a dissonant and as it were discoloured song its proper, very pure and entire colour: I too, encouraged by this, for the sake of your love, will gird myself again with the help of God for those things which I had thought to leave, so that if I [1129C] shall have made some twisted and uncomposed work, some learned and skilled man may find material whereby he may exercise his wit, by acquiring for it a better and more useful, or more lively, form and colour, if yet the work itself shall have escaped the erasure or the deserved flame.

VERSE 6.-- *Who is she that ascendeth through the desert, as a little rod of smoke from the aromatics of myrrh and of frankincense, and of the whole powder of the perfumer?*

Now the beloved has entered the chamber of the beloved, now she has begun to draw a little near to his kiss, because she has flown away from the allurements and cares of secular life, fleeing from which she has removed herself far, and has remained in the solitude of her body and soul. But the solitude of the body is to be quiet in the place assigned to oneself, and not to wander through the exterior senses about the business of this world, either [1129D] in speech or in action. But the solitude of the soul is to be occupied with no exterior things, either in thought or in will.

Which solitude, assuredly, is a great ascent of the mind. And the daughters of Jerusalem marvel at the wonderful ascent of the beloved through the desert, because infirm minds are greatly amazed at and praise the singular life of those who are free for God alone and have no care of worldly honour and advantage. It says, therefore: *Who is she that ascendeth?* But before we describe an ascent of this kind, let us consider what and of what sort is the desert through which this ascent is made. And first let us understand that both ascent and desert are each named equivocally, because by one name they can signify diverse things. For ascent is the ascent of the body and of the heart

[1130A] *125* The desert is the Church of the gentiles; the desert the Synagogue of the Jews, the desert the world, the desert some separated place, the desert the soul taken out from all the cares of the world.

But desert is so called from *deserting,* because it deserts and is deserted; because it both deserts the continent one and is deserted by the continent one: but by grace the world will desert the one who contains himself, because in no manner can it come by affection for itself to the holy soul, which reckons all things of this world as nothing; it is deserted by the continent one, because by no reckoning does the holy soul consent to share in the pleasures or actions of the world, lest, held back by it perhaps, she be able to escape with difficulty or never.

But this continence we name both according to [1130B] the general situation of the body, which belongs to the good and the bad, and according to the situation of the soul, which belongs specially to the good, and specially to the bad, according to the affection of good and of evil.

The situation of the body is the exterior place: the situation of the soul the interior place: and sometimes the exterior place profits or harms the interior place. But we said a little above that the desert is the world, or some place set apart, or the soul exempted from the cares of the world. But to the soul exempted from the cares of the world the world does harm, not according to its substance, but according to the affection of its malice; but the place set apart profits, not according to its nature, but according to the quiet and the clean removal of din from it. According to this, therefore, the exterior both profits and harms the [1130C] interior place. Let us say, then, why each of those things which we set down above is named by the name of desert.

For the Church of the gentiles, so long as she remained in gentility, was a certain desert, because not yet was there in her the worship of any religion and the frequency of sacred laws or sanctions, but as it were the lairs of beasts, on account of the bestial manners of men, in whom reason itself and the natural law were so lulled to sleep that they could not discern what of honesty they owed to their own life, or what of reverence and subjection to the Creator. For of this desert the Psalmist says: *the beautiful places of the desert shall grow fat [n: (Psal. LXIV)]*. For what are the beautiful things of this desert, except the most wholesome and most seemly [1130D] institutions of the sacraments and of the venerable sanctions, which then began to grow fat when, by the teachings and authorities of the holy Fathers, they advanced to greater increments of honesty and reverence? Of which desert the Lord says through Isaiah: *I will set the desert into a pool of water, and the impassable land into streams of waters [n: (Isa. XLI)]*: for he set the desert into a pool of water and the impassable land into streams of waters, when to gentility, which for dryness of mind bore no fruits of good works, he gave the flowings of preaching, and to which, for the roughness of its dryness, the way did not lie open to preachers, he afterwards made streams of doctrine to flow forth. To which also in another place, through the same prophet, he promises again, saying: *The desert shall rejoice, and the impassable and the solitude shall exult, and shall flourish like the lily [n: (Isa. XXXV)]*: *126* [1131A] it shall rejoice in faith, exult in action, flourish in the beauty of virtues, so that like the lily it may be fragrant with the odour of good life, shine with the whiteness of cleanness, avail with the medicine of inmost salvation.

Judaea also is called a desert, from whom the testament and the giving of the law and every right of sacrifices and ceremonies departed, so that, the temple being destroyed and the city made desolate, the whole royal and priestly dignity among them, and every solemn rite, has ceased, but they themselves, dispersed through diverse regions, bear the sign of this desolation. Which the Lord foretold to them in the Gospel as to come, saying: *Your house shall be left to you deserted [n: (Matth. XXIII)]*; and in Jeremiah it is written: *The Lord hath cast down, and hath not spared; all the beautiful things of Jacob he hath destroyed in his fury* [1131B] [n: (Thren. II)].

The world also can be called a desert, not bodily but spiritually, because the more it is heaped with the filth of sins, the more it is made desolate of the presence of divine grace; for it is written: *The land shall be brought down in solitude [n: (Jer. L)]*; the land indeed in solitude, because the mind occupied with earthly cares and pleasures is made desolate of the assembly of the virtues.

A place set apart also is called a desert, because where there is no access of secular business or of forensic actions, that is not unfittingly named a certain solitude. Whence when the Psalmist had set forth beforehand the tribulations of enemies and the troubles of iniquities, and such disturbances of heart that he even [1131C] feared death from them, at length turning back from there: *I said,* he says, *who will give me wings like a dove, and I will fly and be at rest [n: (Psal. LIV)]*. And at last, a wholesome counsel having been found: *Lo,* he says, *I have gone far off flying away, and I abode in the solitude; I waited for him that hath saved me from pusillanimity of spirit and from the storm [n: (ibid.)]* And the Lord, after baptism, being about to fast, was led into the desert, where, being tempted by the devil [n: (Matth. IV)], he triumphed in himself over our temptations, showing that those who love a quiet life are indeed to be wearied by temptations, but to be delivered by the help of God through fastings and prayers.

The soul also removed from all the cares of the world is a desert, because the less it thinks of the things that are of the world, the more it carries a heart solitary from carnal [1131D] desires. Whence the Lord in the Gospel: *When,* he says, *thou shalt pray, enter into the chamber, and having shut the door pray to thy Father [n: (Matth. VI)]*; for praying we enter the chamber when in the secret of our heart we stand alone and without voice lay open to God alone what we ask; we shut the door also when we stop up the entrance of our exterior senses, lest through them anything should be brought into the heart which could hinder our prayers. And this is the solitude of the soul very necessary to each one who seeks God and desires to find him.

But we said above that ascent is both of the body and of the mind, but this is to be considered only of the ascent of the mind, which is not made except through the desert.

*127* For so long as the mind is entangled in the cares and [1132A] tumults of this world, it is by no means able to ascend to the height of the virtues, but first the solitude of body and of mind is to be sought, so that, while the mind is drawn away by no exterior or interior disquiet, it may be lifted up more freely to the study of good work.
