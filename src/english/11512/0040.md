## CHAP. III.-- *Continuation.* (cont.)

The soul, too, of each saint is the bed of this our true peaceable one, because, since *the soul of the just is said to be the seat of wisdom [n: (Sap. VII)]*, there surely God rests sweetly where, wisely, reasonably and honourably, with wisdom itself governing, the place of that same resting is arranged; where the mind is by grace so coupled to God that it is made one spirit with him, the sacrament of which unity confers the whole effect of salvation, [1136A] as the Saviour himself says: *He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same beareth much fruit, for without me you can do nothing [n: (Joan. XV)]*. For since the mind is powerless for any fruit of goodness and discipline without grace working with it, so great a power of this conjunction is conferred upon it that, since he is almighty to whom it is coupled through unity of spirit, it too is itself made almighty through grace, as he himself says, *all things are possible to him that believeth [n: (Marc. IX)]*. For if the saints joined to God are called gods, he indeed essentially, they by appellation, it does not seem inconsequent that, since they are made one body with the Almighty through the sacrament of unity, they should also be made partakers of his omnipotence in a certain manner of their own, who can say with the Apostle, *he hath given us all things with him [1136B] [n: (Rom. VIII)]*. For to those to whom he has given all things with him, when they do not have the Almighty, there is nothing which in him and through him they cannot do. But such a mind is called determinately the bed of the Saviour, that is, of the peaceable one, because wherever this peaceable one has rested and made his dwelling by the condescension of his grace, all the adversity of carnal affection that fights against the spirit is lulled to sleep.

Further, this bed of this Solomon is also, not absurdly, taken to be Holy Scripture, because in it Christ rests through the teachings of his precepts and the examples of the virtues, where the testament of this peaceable one is preached, which he confirmed in his death, namely the precept of peace and love, when [1136C] he says: *A new commandment I give unto you, that you love one another as I have loved you [n: (Joan. XIII)]*; the fulness of which love consists in a twofold relation, namely of God and of neighbour, on which the whole *law hangeth, and the prophets [n: (Matth. XXII)]*; and because the whole page of each sacrament *132* contains it, and discerns nothing useful or honourable without it, not undeservedly may sacred Scripture be named the bed of this peaceable one. For there Christ is found by one who reads and meditates in it; there he can understand what he ought to believe, what to hope, what to love; and to have this threefold virtue is to have God, to the eternal having of whom he is led and confirmed by faith, hope, charity. There Christ, lying hidden and as it were resting, is recognized and awakened, because in the minds of the reader, [1136D] after he is understood, if the mind is moved by the steps of faith and of good works, Christ is as it were awakened from rest. For in the Gospel it is written that Christ was known in the breaking of bread, he who lay hidden in the form of a stranger [n: (Luc. XXIV)], because Holy Scripture, which is that heavenly and living bread, Christ, is strange to those who do not understand; but when it has been distributed through the breaking of exposition, Christ, who lay hidden in it, is recognized by the mind of one who understands.

But we must see what it is that threescore valiant ones, of the most valiant of Israel, all holding swords and most expert in war, are said to surround the bed of this peaceable one. For many have tried to scatter this bed and to carry off this peaceable one: some by vain [1137A] and irreligious worships, some by perverse morals, not a few by the errors of depraved dogmas; whom these valiant ones most valiantly withstood by deeds, words and writings, holding the swords of the word of God, and most expert in warring down enemies of this kind: first indeed the holy apostles and apostolic men, then the holy martyrs, finally the priests, who were all of the most valiant of those who see God with the mind and draw from him the strength of unconquered fortitude, surrounding and guarding this bed, each in his own time and place; not that they were necessary to this peaceable one, but that he was exceedingly necessary to them, whom they feared to lose through the perversity of the wicked, who laboured in every way to overthrow the Catholic faith.

[1137B]But why is the number of these valiant, or most valiant, ones fixed at the number sixty: for it is not without some reason that, although the multitude of these valiant ones was innumerable, it is comprehended in this finite number: it could indeed be that that real Solomon had more guards of his body, or appointed and designated keepers, each at his own office, of his possessions stored in diverse places; but since God granted him so great a peace that no wars were stirred in his time, it seems a wonder, if it is to be taken according to the letter, why so many valiant ones were deputed to guard his body alone. But since reason demands rather that these things be referred to the spiritual sense, let us see what this number *133* sixty signifies; for sixty is composed of [1137C] six and ten, the one multiplied by the other. Now six, which, when its parts are gathered together, is neither diminished nor extended, is said to signify perfection. But ten, which is composed of five taken twice, suggests the Decalogue of the law, for the keeping or contempt of which, on account of the five senses of the body, by whose office the mind is led either to good or to evil, the whole of mankind, in the order of the ten virgins, the five wise and the foolish, is either rewarded or condemned. So six, which signifies perfection, and ten, which shows the Decalogue, multiplying one another and making up sixty, show that these sixty valiant ones so perfectly fitted their outward senses to keeping the divine precepts [1137D] that they are deservedly reckoned in the lot of the five wise.

Now those who have the science of the art of Arithmetic are wont to say to me that such a number as sixty is superfluous; for, since some numbers are so equalled by the collection of their parts that they take back neither less nor more than the whole body, and are therefore called perfect, while some, because by the composition of their parts they return less than the whole body, are named diminished, those which, when their own parts are gathered together, surpass the whole body by some quantity are called superfluous. But we, who refer this number of these valiant ones to the good part, ought to take this surpassing not as superfluity, but as the increase of spiritual gain, which advances from virtue to virtue, [1138A] so that we may say that these valiant ones, in the talents of the five senses entrusted to them, wrought so magnificently for their own profit and that of their neighbours that, their zeal in good works always taking increase, from valiant they were made most valiant.

Let us see, however, what the parts of this number sixty bring, so that we may be able to know whether they pertain to the perfection of these valiant ones of Christ. Of the number sixty, then, the half part is thirty, the third twenty, the fourth fifteen, the fifth twelve, the sixth ten, the tenth six, the twelfth five, the fifteenth four, the twentieth three, the thirtieth two, the sixtieth one. These parts, gathered together, make up a hundred and eight, surpassing the whole body of sixty [1138B] by forty-eight. And what is the reward of all the perfect, but a recompense of all their labour a hundredfold better, in the future regeneration, when they shall be born again into the immortality of their bodies, to that life of the eighth age which shall never be ended.

Well, then, is the company of these valiant, or most valiant, ones comprehended in the number sixty, whose parts, brought into one, grow *134* to a hundred and eight, so that we may refer this not to superfluity but to the increase of virtues, since by doing and teaching rightly they have gained advancement for themselves and for others, for which they may obtain perfect blessedness, which is designated by the number a hundred, and which will be fulfilled in the eighth age, as we have said, to abide without end.

[1138C]VERSE 8.-- *All holding swords, and most expert in war: every man's sword upon his thigh, because of fears in the night.*

And these sixty valiant ones of the most valiant of Israel, surrounding the bed of Solomon, what do they do? *All,* he says, *holding swords, and most expert in war:* he does not say having, but *holding,* because they could indeed have them in a certain secret store-room of a soul that knows and understands, but not hold them in the public carrying, as it were, of the voice and work of one who preaches. They hold swords, therefore, because they always carry ready the teachings of knowledge and of action, by which they may train those who see and hear them for spiritual contests; but what are [1138D] these swords but the words of God: *For the word of God is true, and effectual, and more piercing than any two-edged sword, and reaching unto the division of the soul, of the joints also and the marrow, and a discerner of thoughts [n: (Hebr. IV)]* [var: Vulg. *Vivus*, "living"]: for such a sword, dividing and separating, God sent into the earth when he came *to set a man at variance against his father, and the daughter against her mother, and the daughter in law against her mother in law, and a man's enemies they of his own household [n: (Matth. X)]*. This sword, surely, of the word of God, by which separation and every kind of discernment is commanded to be made between good and evil, between virtues and vices, the renowned warriors of God ought to use, whose purpose it is to surround the bed of this peaceable one with doctrine and working, whether we refer this bed to the Church or to the proper mind of each saint, [1139A] in which the Godhead is believed to rest by the condescension of his grace; so that in both places they may both defend the Church from the depravity of unbelievers and guard themselves from the suggestion or deception of malignant spirits.

And what are the wars in which such men ought to fight, and in which they are recorded as most expert? Assuredly the great strife and discord of virtues and vices, where at issue is the conflict of two adversaries to one another, namely of the flesh and the spirit, *for the flesh lusteth against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh [n: (Gal. V)]*. And rightly they are proclaimed most expert not in war but, in the plural number, in wars, because one is the war of humility against pride, another of chastity against lust, another of patience against anger, another of obedience against contumacy, another of sobriety [1139B] against luxury, another of avarice against liberality, or of whatever virtue struggles against the vice opposed to it; for carrying through which wars happily these sixty *135* valiant ones are recorded as most expert, because in their deeds and writings are the method and counsel for the equipping of these wars, to be brought to a good and honourable end.

Whence also is added: *Every man's sword upon his thigh, because of fears in the night.* For the method of repressing diverse vices is diverse, because pride is repressed in one way, luxury in another, and avarice in another, and single vices demand single contrary remedies, so that against each one it is fought with its contrary as with a different sword. Now the sword is the watchful guard of the mind, of which Scripture commands: [1139C] *With all watchfulness keep thy heart [n: (Prov. IV)]*: *which sword upon the thigh* is set when the suggestion of the delighting flesh is repressed by the watchful guard of the resisting mind.
