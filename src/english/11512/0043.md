## CHAP. III.-- *Continuation.* (cont.)

But since this whole epithalamium of the Bridegroom and the bride, which in this place is called, for its excellence, the *Canticle of Canticles;* looks to the wonderful spectacle of this espousal, let us see how beautifully and pleasantly the Bridegroom and the bride, alternating with one another, sing the proclamations of holy love. For above the bride set forth the dignity and excellence of the Bridegroom who is to be coupled to her spiritually, in the reverence of the bed, in the abundance of the litters, in the splendour and glory of the ornaments, and, what is greater than all these, in the singular prerogative of charity; and she was exhorting the daughter of Sion to go forth to behold and admire the beauty of these nuptials, so that [1147C] they too, striving according to their power to be joined to these nuptials, might, him whom they now saw through a glass in a dark manner, afterwards, the eye of the mind being now wholly purged, see face to face, the king in his beauty.

To whom the Bridegroom presently responds with a songful testimony of beauty, and enumerates very many parts of that beauty, offered to him as it were for the espousal gifts of the bride; which nevertheless are of the grace of that same Bridegroom who gives them, so that he may show in turn that she who receives is deservedly to be coupled to him, whom he has made glorious by his own gift with so many proclamations of beauties, to whom he has fitted the name also of the bed and of the litter signified before, and whom he has made the cause of the ornaments laid upon her. He says, therefore, *how fair thou art, my love.*
