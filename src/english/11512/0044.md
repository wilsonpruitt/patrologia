## *138* CHAPTER IV.

[1147D]VERSE 1.-- *How fair thou art, my love! how fair thou art! thy eyes are doves' eyes, besides that which lieth hid within; thy hair is as flocks of goats which have come up from mount Galaad.*

Since by the grace of the Holy Spirit every blessing of the virtues flows forth in the Church's field, fittingly now the Bridegroom extols her with sevenfold proclamations of beauties, which he regards in the comeliness and grace of the eyes, the hair, the teeth, the lips, the cheeks, the neck and the breasts; because surely *139* Christ judges the Church worthy of his fellowship, whom, for the abundance of her graces, he commends by the elegance of all the members in her face.

For the face of the Church is her public assembly, in which are considered, ordered and prescribed [1148A] the matters always necessary and useful, by which her members may be informed and set in the comeliness of unity, so that no spot of deformity may appear in them, whether in faith, or in action, or in any constitution of the canonical sanctions. For this public assembly of the Church is distinguished by the varied quality of professions, of dignities and of orders, because one is the profession of monks, another of clerics, another of laymen; and among these one profession is of virgins, another of the continent, another of the married; and of dignities one is imperial, another pontifical, another prefectural, or whatever else can be called by the name of dignity. Further, of orders, one is priestly, another levitical, another sublevitical, or any other ecclesiastical order whatever; all which things are signified by [1148B] those parts described above, laudably placed in the body of the bride by the testimony of the Bridegroom. For the eyes of the Church are the watchfulness and prudence of those set over her; the hair, the multitude of subjects; the teeth, the expositors of sacred Scripture; the lips, the elegance of preachers, or of readers reciting divine Scripture; the cheeks, the modesty of those under discipline; the neck, the sublime eloquence of doctors; the breasts, the diligence of masters instructing the lesser; the distinct variety of all whom in the body of the Church the Psalmist praising together says: *There stood,* he says, *the queen on thy right hand in gilded clothing, surrounded with variety [n: (Psal. XLIV)]*. But of these one by one we must speak more fully in their place.

*How fair thou art,* he says, *my love, how fair thou art!* She whom he calls fair he also names his love, [1148C] to suggest that the one is productive of the other; for she who is adorned with so many kinds of beauties, which he adds below, is deservedly made his love, and she who is proved his love is assuredly shown to be fair: for he would not have made her, as his love, privy to his secret, unless he had coupled her to himself through grace, pleasing him by the beauty of the virtues. And by the repeated praise of beauty he approves the twin virtue of faith and of working, which are so inseparably joined to one another that the one without the other is always proved useless.

And adding the first member of the sevenfold beauty, *thy eyes,* he says, *are doves' eyes, besides that which lieth hid within [sic: intrisecus].* As was noted before above, the eyes of the Church are the watchfulness and providence of those set over her [1148D] towards the utility of their subjects; because, as the eyes are certain guides and guardians to the body beneath them ( *for the blind man walketh in darkness and knoweth not whither [sic: ea] he goeth [n: (Joan. XII)]* [var: Vulg. Jo 12:35 *qui ambulant in tenebris, nescit quo vadat*; the wording is that of 1 Jo 2:11, *in tenebris ambulat, et nescit quo eat*]; *140* so prelates by their care and vigilance guard those subject to them, and lead them into the chosen way by preaching and action, set as watchmen in both respects, both for the necessity and utility of souls and of bodies. But these *eyes* are *doves', besides that which lieth hid within,* because innocently and simply they provide for carnal things, and prudently, by administering spiritual things, they foresee and beware the peril of their subjects: for when at times they are occupied in earthly things for the utility of their subjects, they look upon those earthly things not with a raven's eye but with a dove's, because without cupidity and carnal desire they seek and have them simply for sole [1149A] necessity; of whom the prophet says, *and as doves to their windows [n: (Isa. LX)]*; for what are our windows but the outward senses, through which we look out upon these visible things, either by desiring them or by setting little store by them? But the saints are *as doves to their windows,* because whatever they taste in outward things through the bodily senses they set out simply and innocently for their uses; they seize nothing in them with avidity and delight, but regard in them only the common necessity of themselves and of their own.

It is praiseworthy, then, to hold in this way the pursuit of the active life, but far more praiseworthy is that which lieth hid within, to have, namely, the virtue of the contemplative life; for outwardly they dispense outward things honourably, but [1149B] with what intention they do it lies open only to divine knowledge, while very often certain religious men, handling outward things at times, are judged by human eyes, which do not see hearts.

There follows the second part of the sevenfold beauty, in which it is said: *Thy hair is as flocks of goats, which come up from mount Galaad.*

By the hair, as was noted before, the multitude of subjects is designated, who, although they are not able to attain the perfection of their elders, yet adorn the assembly of the Church as hair adorns the head, adhering to their heads, that is, to their elders, by obeying according to their own manner and ability and by keeping the law appointed for them. For each one, in his own place and order, adorns and graces each profession according to the rule fixed for it by the fathers to be [1149C] kept in common, though in diverse manner. For although in the Church universal or particular not all live equally, not all, each set in his own private office, keep the rule equally, and very often exceed it, yet they are covered by the public and common ornament of religion and order; just as, on the contrary, the devotion of one man confers nothing of honour or honourableness on the whole body of the Church, if its whole institution be deformed by irreligiousness. In short, its religious community is its beauty and nobility, as also irreligious unanimity is its baseness and ignobility.

*141* Whence also it is said that these *hairs* are *as flocks of goats,* because in the several professions [1149D] there are certain flocks, multitudes gathered together; flocks indeed as regards the diversities of orders, or as regards particular Churches, but one flock as regards the unity of the faith and the Church universal. And they are flocks of goats, because all the ecclesiastical professions, though differing among themselves on account of the various qualities of orders, yet always look with equal intention to one end, while all, desiring heavenly things, adhere to God each in his own manner.

And goats of what kind? *Which have come up,* he says, *from mount Galaad.* Galaad is said to be *a heap of testimony;* and who is this heap of testimony, but a certain collection and sum of the divine Scriptures, or of the institutions written down by the holy Fathers for the utility and honourableness of the Church? For what is [1150A] sacred Scripture but a certain heap of testimony, everywhere preaching and testifying of Christ? Of which testimony it is sung in the Psalm: *The testimony of the Lord is faithful, giving wisdom to little ones [n: (Psal. XVIII)]*. For the little ones, that is, the humble, understand the testimony of holy Scripture, and while they obey it by believing and living well they are wise; for the highest philosophy is the best manner of living. The institution of the holy Fathers also is a certain heap of testimony, because, while it is lawfully observed, it is certified to its observers for honourableness of life.

The *hair* of holy Church, therefore, is *as flocks of goats which have come up from mount Galaad,* because the multitudes of subjects advance daily by the testimonies of the holy [1150B] Scriptures and by the institutions of those set over them, fixed for them for a common order of living.

VERSE 2.-- *Thy teeth are as flocks of shorn sheep, which have come up from the washing, all with twin offspring, and there is none barren among them.*

In the third place is set, *thy teeth are as flocks of shorn sheep, which have come up from the washing, all with twin offspring, and there is none barren in them.*

The teeth, as was written above, can not absurdly be taken as the expositors of sacred Scripture; because, as the teeth grind the food, so the holy expositors in the divine Scriptures reduce the grossness of the letter to the subtlety of the spiritual sense, so that what could not be understood on the surface may, ground as it were [1150C] by these teeth, pass more easily into the belly of understanding.

But why are these *teeth as flocks of shorn sheep which have come up from the washing?* Namely on account of a mind freed from the business of the world, and washed from the dust of earthly action in the fountain of the divine Spirit. For they cannot advance to the *142* subtlety of so great an understanding unless such cares have been laid aside as certain burdens, and with the whole body of the inner man in the whole of that fountain of the Godhead, of which the Psalmist says to God: *Thou shalt make them drink of the torrent of thy pleasure, for with thee is the fountain of life [n: (Psal. XXXV)]*. And what of utility this washing or drinking confers he suggests when he adds, *and in thy light we shall see him [n: (ibid.)]* [var: Vulg. *lumen*, "light"], as if he said openly: thou pouring into us the light of knowledge, [1150D] we shall understand the obscure things of divine Scripture.

The teeth, then, of holy Church are *as flocks of shorn sheep which have come up from the washing,* because the handlers of sacred Scripture, the more they have been freed from earthly actions and purged in the washing of divine inspiration, the more strongly are they able to break, and the more finely to grind, the whole literal solidity of the Old and New Testament.

For the allegorical or moral sense, flowing forth fittingly and usefully from the literal surface of the holy Scriptures, is the becoming whiteness and as it were the alluring beauty of these teeth.

Whence also is added: *All with twin offspring, and there is none barren among them.* For what are the twin [1151A] offspring of these, but either the double sense of the holy Scriptures, which are as it were broken by exposition, namely the allegorical and the moral, or the knowledge of the two Testaments, or the showing forth of the twofold charity? For since the sense in the whole body of divine Scripture is threefold, namely historical, allegorical and moral, wherever the historical is expounded it is resolved into one of these two parts, namely allegory or morality, and very often the understanding of one and the same history is brought back into the senses of both parts, so that sometimes what the history sounds ought to be understood only mystically. Of which threefold sense of divine Scripture it is said: *Write them threefold in thy heart [n: (Prov. XXII)]*. And they are enriched as it were with the twin offspring of the two testaments, because whatever [1151B] they expound they take up either from the Old or from the New Testament. Further, they are multiplied with the offspring of the twofold charity, because the love of God, whom they savour delightfully in Scripture itself, draws them to the labour of expounding sacred Scripture; the love of neighbour also, for whose utility they willingly take up this occupation for themselves.

In whom there is no barrenness of these twin offspring, because all whom the weight of earthly action does not burden, and whom the most healthful fountain of the divine Spirit washes, can delightfully give themselves to the business of expounding the books with this twin intention, with maternal love; looking by intention to allegory and morality, by matter to the Old and New Testament, by love to God and neighbour.
