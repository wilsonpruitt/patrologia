## *138* CHAPTER IV. (cont.)

Of which sevenfold beauty the proclamation is: *thy lips are as a scarlet fillet, and thy speech sweet:* the lips can be called the preachers of the divine utterance, or those who pronounce the utterances themselves elegantly by reading or by singing, and kindle the people of God, by sweetness of sense or of voice, to divine fear and love. Whence also they are compared to a scarlet fillet, because, as a fillet binds the hair together and orders it lest it be scattered, so the holy preachers, or those singing or reading sweetly in the Church, join the listening people by the bond of peace and unanimity, and make them sound together with one another in the harmony of faith [1151D] and love. For the fillet of the subjects is as it were dissonant and discordant from the fillet of the holy preachers, when they neither believe rightly nor work rightly; but when by their preaching they are led through to truth and rightness, they are as it were joined to them in faith and love; while they believe and love the same thing as they themselves, and agree with them in the law of God. And when they hear and attend to those pronouncing the same truth, whether with fair harmony or with an assertion probable to reason, through the grace of compunction they bind themselves the more strongly in the love of God.

Whence also the fillet to which they are compared is said to be *scarlet.* For scarlet is of a fiery colour, and what is charity but fire? For when some, hearing of that [1152A] highest good which God is, are pricked to well-doing for the sake of receiving that good, assuredly they are kindled with the fire of divine charity.

The lips of the Church, then, that is, the holy preachers, or those who dutifully compose the preaching itself, are *as a scarlet fillet,* because by the dignity and sweetness of the very things they preach they set the minds of the hearers alight to the love of eternal life

Whence is added, *thy speech sweet:* for the weak are drawn by gentler precepts and repelled by harsher ones; but under the law sinners were commanded to be punished, and the guilty received retaliation, when they were bidden to give *eye for eye, tooth for tooth [n: (Exod. XXI)]*; but in the time of grace the speech of the Church is sweet, because to sinners confessing their faults [1152B] not punishment but mercy is promised, and the guilty, if they look back, are not fined with retaliation but are absolved by penance. Or else the holy preachers are a scarlet fillet for this reason, that by their preaching they bind in the superfluities of thoughts and works, so that through zeal for holy working they may burn with the fire of divine charity, and through contempt of the world may redden with a certain *144* martyrdom of the mind; for scarlet is both fiery and red, and those who glow with divine charity do not fear to suffer all adverse things for God.

Further, how great a kind of the sevenfold beauty proclaims the modesty of the disciplined in the Church, when it is said, *as a piece of a pomegranate, so are thy cheeks, besides that which lieth hid within.* It is called the "malum punicum" because its kind is from the Punic region, [1152C] carried over from there; and it is also called the "malum granatum", because it contains within itself a multitude of grains; and a piece of pomegranate reddens without and is white within, and gives off more fragrance broken than whole. The cheeks of the Church, then, are said to be *as a piece of a pomegranate,* because the discipline of the religious, who in the house of discipline hold forth the habit of honourableness, displays modesty outwardly in the face with a rosy beauty, which proceeds from the inward diligence of a mind graciously shining white. For the whiteness of chastity which shines in the mind begets the redness of modesty in the face, when the religious soul fears to commit any such thing as would detract from its honourableness or its discipline. Whence, by contrast, to the reprobate mind, which boldly [1152D] does not blush to sin, it is said through the prophet: *The forehead of a harlot woman is made thine, and thou wouldst not blush [n: (Jer. III)]*.

The lowering of the countenance, then, and a glorious composure of the eyes, is the sign of a religious and chaste mind, and therefore those composed honourably in their conduct are fittingly said to be *as a piece of a pomegranate,* who reverently convey outwardly, by habit and conduct, the comeliness of holiness which they bear inwardly in intention and thought. And though the outward bearing, religiously composed, is praiseworthy, yet far more praiseworthy is that inward habit of the mind which is known to God alone, since the holy soul hardly or never suffices outwardly [sic: *est*] to show how greatly it burns within with divine charity.

Whence, when the bridegroom had first said of the bride, *as a [1153A] piece of a pomegranate, so are thy cheeks,* he added, *besides that which lieth hid within.*

For what is it that lieth hid within, but the force of charity which is manifest to God alone, and works outwardly whatever there is of honourableness or holiness.

The disciplined hearts of holy Church, then, are as it were *a piece of a pomegranate,* *besides that which lieth hid within,* because the whiteness of the virtues which they have inwardly through the grace of God, this they display outwardly through the form of patience and modesty; but the very force which contains in itself, by the working of the Holy Spirit, the whole quickening and strengthening efficacy, they leave to the judgment of God alone, while they stand indeed in great hope. But in their very hope they are not without fear. The force of divine charity they indeed [1153B] feel in themselves, but by what scrutiny the things which, out of that charity, they proclaim by conduct and life are weighed before God, they do not know.

* 145* VERSE 4.-- *Thy neck is as the tower of David, which is built with bulwarks; a thousand bucklers hang from it, all the armour of valiant men.*

But in the sixth place of the beauty enumerated this proclamation is set, *thy neck is as the tower of David, which is built with bulwarks, a thousand bucklers hang from it, all the armour of valiant men.* We noted above that by the neck is signified the sublime eloquence of the doctors, which is compared to a tower for this reason, that it is raised aloft by the depth of its words and senses. But this is called *the tower of David,* that is, *strong of hand,* because, namely, whatever of comeliness or [1153C] sublimity the utterances of the doctors contain in themselves, whether by word or by writing, is assuredly of his grace who is spiritually called strong of hand, because it is his *To spare the subject, and to war down the proud.*
[n: (VIRG., Aeneid., IV, 654.)]

As it is written of him, that *he resisteth the proud, but giveth grace to the humble [n: (Jac. IV)]*. For he rests in the heart of the humble, and distributes to them fittingly the gifts of his knowledge, as it shall please him, to one thus, to another thus.

And what are the bulwarks with which this tower is built, but the fortifications of strong and sublime sentences? For by the authorities of the sentences with which the doctors thunder, the Church is fortified and strengthened [1153D] against the dogmas and deeds of the perverse, lest, founded upon the rock of faith, she should be able to fear any contrivances of the contrary and adverse father. For in this are beheld the counsels and precepts of the virtues, and the wonderful deeds and miracles of the holy Fathers.

Whence also is added, *a thousand bucklers hang from it;* for what is designated by the thousandfold number, which is set for perfection, but universality? For the thousand bucklers are all the instructions of the virtues, which fortify us against the assailing engines of the vices: For if *The breast [sic: petus] seethes with avarice and with wretched desire?*
*There are words and sounds by which thou mayest soothe this pain*
*And lay aside a great part of the disease.*
*Dost thou swell with love of praise? There are sure expiations which,*
*The little book thrice purely read, will be able to restore thee.*
[1154A] *The envious, the wrathful, the sluggard, the drunkard, the lover,*
*No one is so wild that he may not grow gentle,*
*If only he lend a patient ear to cultivation.*
[n: (HORAT., Ep. I, I, 39.)]

For if the glory of this world delights thee, hear the apostle John saying: *Love not the world, nor the things which are in the world; if any man shall have loved the world, the charity of the Father is not in him [n: (I Joan. II)]*: if thou art tempted by the vice of vainglory, hear the Apostle saying: *Let us not be made desirous of vainglory [n: (Gal. VI)]*: if thou art tempted by avarice, hear the same saying: *That God loveth a cheerful giver [n: (II Cor. IX)]*: if thou art tempted by lust, hear him again saying: *Know you not that your bodies are the temple of the Holy Spirit? If any man violate this temple, he shall destroy [1154B] him; for the temple of God is holy, which you are [n: (I Cor. VI)]*. *146* If anger strikes the mind, hear what is said: *Be angry, and sin not;* and that *the anger of man worketh not the justice of God [n: (Ps. IV)]*. If the sin of disobedience corrupts the mind, hear it written *that obedience is better than victims, and to hearken rather than to offer the fat of rams, because it is as the sin of witchcraft to resist, and as the crime of idolatry to refuse to acquiesce [n: (I Reg. XV)]*: if impatience weighs down the mind, attend to what is written, that *the patient man is better than the stormer of cities [n: (Prov. XVI)]*. If pride presses upon the mind, hear what is written, that *God resisteth the proud, but giveth grace to the humble [n: (I Pet. V)]*.

Behold, these are the bucklers which hang from the tower and the bulwarks [1154C] of the most eloquent doctors, whose sentences and authorities are the strongest fortification of the Church, while their precepts and counsels are to every soul, against vices and sins, both instruction and fortification. *A thousand,* then, *bucklers hang from it,* because there is no vice against which a fortification is not found out of all the sayings and writings of the holy Catholic Fathers. But because in the same writings are recited both the valiant deeds of the saints and the precepts of the divine law most constantly kept by them, it is well added, *all the armour of valiant men.* For there thou wilt find how valiantly they fought against vices and sins with the arms of the spiritual warfare, whose armour was the observance of the divine commandments and [1154D] the imitation of the Fathers who went before. *A thousand,* then, *bucklers and all the armour of valiant men hang from it,* because whatever of strength one is pleased to seek against the enemies of the soul, one may find in the most learned sentences and the magnificent deeds of the fathers.

But though the bulwarks and the bucklers have the same office of fortifying, yet they differ in this, that the bulwarks stand immovably, while the bucklers can be held and moved by the hand. And what can the bulwarks be called, but the miracles of the saints? And what the bucklers, but the excellent cultivations of the holy virtues. In Holy Scripture, then, which the utterances of the doctors bring forth and commend, are narrated the wonderful miracles of the saints, which we indeed cannot do and as it were move; but the works of the virtues each of us can exercise [1155A] and, as though holding them in the hand, move by diverse pursuits according to our small measure, if we burn with the love of God.

From the neck of holy Church, then, *a thousand bucklers hang, and all the armour of valiant men,* because in Holy Scripture, which the eloquence of the doctors uncovers and adorns, she finds, in general, against the darts of the vices both protecting safeguards and repelling armaments.
