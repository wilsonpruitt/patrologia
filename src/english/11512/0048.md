## *138* CHAPTER IV. (cont.)

In another way also there can be understood *155* the one of the eyes and the one hair of the neck, namely sameness and unchangeableness, [1162C] as regards the state of the mind in its purpose of good; so that in the one of the eyes there is always the same and unfailing intention of spiritual actions, and in the one hair of the neck always the same and persevering habit of humility and lowliness of mind; whence it is written: *A holy man continueth in wisdom as the sun, but a fool is changed as the moon [n: (Eccli. XXVII)]*, and of the kine carrying the ark of God it is written that *they went straight on, and walked by one way, and turned not aside to the right hand nor to the left. [n: (I Reg. VI.)]* For what is the ark of God, but the mind of the saint, in which divinity is contained through the gifts of diverse graces; but he who carries it must not turn aside from the way of justice, lest, walking by a perverse way, when divine grace withdraws [1162D] from the mind, he be no longer said to bear the ark of God at all. The one of the eyes, then, is the one way of those who walk rightly before themselves, as is written of the holy living creatures, that *every one went before his face:* the one of the eyes [sic: *soculorum*] is the same countenance of the mind, always *forgetting the things that are behind, and stretching forth to those that are before it [n: (Philip. III)]*. The one hair of the neck is always the same littleness of humility, and a thinness so great that, though the holy man be of great merit with God, he so despises himself among men that he scarcely permits what he is to be seen in him, as one hair, though it be something, is yet scarcely seen and held. The one hair of the neck is the sameness of a mind humbling itself to God through obedience, in the state of whose purpose it so perseveres that it is neither lifted up by prosperity nor takes delight in adversities on the contrary it is said of the reprobate [1163A] mind that *it walked with neck stretched out and nape upraised [n: (Isa. III)]*; and of the Jews who did not humble themselves to God through obedience, *with stiff neck and untameable heart. [n: (Act. VII.)] [var: Vulg. Act 7:51 incircumcisis cordibus et auribus, "uncircumcised in heart and ears"] With one*, then, *of the eyes and with one hair of the neck* the beloved is wounded, because there greatly pleases him the one and the same persevering intention and discernment of spiritual actions, and also the one and ever stable humility of a mind bowed to God through obedience.

VERSE 10.-- *How fair are thy paps, my sister, bride! Thy breasts are fairer than wine, and the odour of thy ointments above all spices.*

It must be diligently considered what this praiseworthy beauty of the paps, or of the breasts, of the bride is, that is, of the Church, or of any holy soul whatever; [1163B] for as in paps or breasts is the nourishment of little ones and of bodies newly begotten, so there can be certain spiritual nourishments by which souls still tender and weak may receive some increases of holiness, either themselves from themselves or others from them: themselves from themselves *156* through exercise, others from them through instruction.

But first it must be seen what these paps, or these breasts, are, and then what their beauty is, thus praised by the bridegroom: I do not think it contrary to reason if we say that these paps or these breasts are the knowledge of truth and the love of the virtues. But since there are two creatures, one spiritual and the other corporal, we believe it is not beside the matter if for each we appoint two qualities of increase, as it were two paps, or two [1163C] breasts. For as there are two creatures, as we have already said, one spiritual and the other corporal, so the good of the creature also is twofold, one of the spiritual, the other of the corporal; for the good of the spiritual creature consists chiefly in the two things which we set down above in the signification of the paps or breasts, that is, in the knowledge of truth and the love of virtue. But the good of the bodily creature is either in itself or outside itself: the good in itself is the integrity of the senses and affections according to the five senses; the good outside itself is the ordered distribution among the creatures: for whatever is corrupted or amended is for man's sake, and whatever is good or evil is good or evil to man. And since all goods, [1163D] whether inward or outward, are the gifts of almighty God, the privation of either is the anger of God and the punishment of man, as, on the contrary, the bestowal of them is the grace of God and the salvation of man: but the knowledge of truth and the love of virtue are to be considered in particular; in each soul they always receive increases, as though from certain paps and breasts of exercises, because the exercises themselves admit of more and less, more if they persist, less if they cease, namely so that they know the truth and love virtue more or less. For these two are so bound together with one another, as two breasts naturally in one body, that he cannot love virtue who has not known the truth, and he who knows the truth cannot but love virtue

[1164A]But if we look at the whole body of the Church universally, as regards itself it does not admit of more and less in knowing truth or loving virtue, she who has an imperfect knowledge of truth and love of virtue. But as regards others whom she believes in Christ, increases of holiness accrue to her daily from the knowledge of truth and the love of virtue.

On both sides, moreover, there are first paps, then breasts; because, as paps are those of girls, who cannot yet have the nourishment of milk but hold the hope of having it, while breasts are those of women, who have abundance of such nourishment, so the Church in those whom she nourishes, or each soul in itself, first has indeed a certain hope through the *157* aptitude of a mind applying itself to good things and always advancing to [1164B] better, and then, now grown up as it were in faith and love, begins to have so full a knowledge of truth and love of virtue that it can nourish all its senses, both inward and outward, and bring them to perfection through the grace of God: and we say here that the outward senses are to be nourished, not that they may be able to do better bodily, but spiritually, lest, badly guarded, they become instruments of sin, as the prophet reproves sinners concerning them, saying: *Death is entered through your windows [n: (Jer. IX)]* [var: Vulg. Jr 9:21 *ascendit mors per fenestras nostras*, "death is come up through our windows"].

And the Church holds out the nourishments of these her breasts in three ways, namely by things, by deeds and by words: by things, as in the sacraments, without which no salvation can come to the soul; by deeds, as in the acts of the saints, by whose examples she is provoked and invited; [1164C] by words, as in the precepts and admonitions of Holy Scripture, by which she is nourished daily to eternal life

We have said, then, what the paps or breasts of the bride are; now it must be said what their beauty is, thus praised by the bridegroom: this beauty is not accidental, such as can be present and absent without the corruption of the subject, but essential, consisting in the property of its own nature: for that the knowledge of truth and the love of virtue are beautiful, what else is it than that they are? For it is beautiful to know the truth and to love virtue, as it is beautiful to be chaste, or just, or humble, or patient, or obedient; and chastity and justice, humility, patience and obedience are a certain beauty, and [1164D] these, as we have said, are not beautiful accidentally in the subjects which partake of them, but essentially in themselves; for if they are, they are beautiful; if they are not beautiful, neither are they, because while they are corrupted they are not beautiful either; because they cease to be what they were. For chastity, or any other virtue, while it is corrupted, how is it beautiful, since that no longer is whose corruption is evil?

Since, then, the good of the soul consists chiefly in two things, by which it is nourished to eternal life as though from breasts, which are the knowledge of truth and the love of virtue, the corruption of knowledge can be called ignorance, of which the Apostle says: *He that is ignorant, shall be ignored [n: (I Cor. XIV)]*, and the corruption of love *covetousness, which is the root of all evils [n: (I Tim. VI)]*. And the more [1165A] this ignorance and covetousness grows, the more the good of the soul is corrupted: for the soul, since it is an incorporeal substance, cannot be corrupted according to its substance, but according to the good in which it partakes, which is the more *158* worn away and diminished in it the more it is itself deformed by sins.

Fair, then, are the paps or breasts of holy Church in general, or of each holy soul in particular, because both the one for her sons and the other in itself shine with so copious an abundance of good senses in the knowledge of truth and love of virtue, that from them flow forth the nourishments of all spiritual goods, and natural goods also, such as reason, wit and memory, flourish the more, and so the whole [1165B] beauty of the soul is formed by partaking of these.

But what is it, that these *breasts* are called *fairer than wine,* when in wine there is riotousness [n: (Ephes. V)], while in these breasts there is the holy and sober delight of the soul, and on this account there can be no right comparison of these at all? Truly it is through the figure tapinosis, which is the lowering of a great thing, that these are called fairer than wine; not that there is any praiseworthy beauty in wine, but because the things aforesaid, namely the knowledge of truth and the love of virtue, are better and fairer in all things to the rational soul, for their utility and sweetness, than all the delights of the world which are signified by wine; since these corrupt the soul by partaking of them, those refresh it. In the same way, finally, [1165C] what follows: *And the odour of thy ointments above all spices:* if we take the odour of the ointments to be the good fame of the virtues, what comparison is there of spiritual and corruptible things? Since indeed there can be both a corruption of the good which is in the spiritual creature, and a corruption of the good which is in the corporal creature; because, as there are two creatures, spiritual and corporal, as we have also already said above, there are also two goods, one in the spiritual, the other in the corporal creature. The good of the spiritual creature is virtue; the good of the corporal creature is the ordered distribution among the creatures: the corruption, then, of the good which comes about in the spiritual creature is, as in the soul, sin and fault, but either punishment or towards punishment. If, then, by the incorruption [1165D] of the spiritual good they unite to God, but by the incorruption of the corporal good they not only do not unite to God, but very often are separated, when through the very delight of it we very often forget ourselves and are led away from the good of our purpose, while by its corruption, when we are afflicted, we are sometimes led back to good things, what comparison can there be in so great an unlikeness.

But in this comparison the argument is from the lesser to the greater, in this manner: If corporal goods, that is, the odour of spices, are of such sweetness, how much more spiritual goods, that is, the odour of the virtues: an incorruptible good incomparable to a corruptible one, as the soul, which is incorruptible, to the corruptible body.

*159* But I call the soul incorruptible according to [1166A] its own substance, which is simple and incorporeal, as the incorruptible good which consists in the soul is so called according to substance, not according to addition. For good can be spoken of in two ways, substantively and adjectivally: for good in itself is not corrupted, but it is said to be corrupted by privation of good, when it withdraws from the soul and is changed into evil, as the soul itself also is not corrupted in itself, but is said to be corrupted by privation of good, because the privation of good is evil.

It is said, then: *The odour of thy ointments above all spices,* as if it were said: The fame and repute of thy virtues is better and more useful to thee than all the chosen things of this world, because these kill, those give life.
