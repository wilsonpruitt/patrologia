## *138* CHAPTER IV. (cont.)

[1166B]But if in another way we wish to refer both parts, namely *the odour of the ointments* and *all spices,* to the spiritual understanding, it must be seen what this special odour of the ointments of the beloved is, and what are all the spices to which that odour is so greatly preferred. For if we take *all spices* to be all the virtues, what will those singular virtues be which are preferred to the whole body of the virtues? It seems to us not absurd if we say that these virtues are faith, hope, charity; for without these virtues all the other virtues are as it were idle; for it is written that *without faith it is impossible to please God [n: (Hebr. XI)]*. But if no one can please God without faith, it is clear that no good works can confer salvation without faith; and he who, believing, works well [sic: *bone*], assuredly does so [1166C] in hope of future goods; but each one loves the things which he hopes to obtain, and whatever good he works, he works through love; because unless he loved, he would not labour to attain them.

Since, then, all good works cannot exist without these, that is, faith, hope, charity, deservedly are these preferred to all the virtues; and so it is said: *The odour of thy ointments above all spices,* as if it were said: Above all virtues there are fragrant in thee faith, hope, charity, which make thee perfectly into a bride and beloved.

But why are these called ointments? Because, as many kinds of herbs, when they have been gathered into one so as to make a certain composition of ointment, [1166D] take the name of the ointment itself from that from which they have the greater virtue, so whatever good works have been compounded with the seasoning of faith, hope and charity are principally called the ointments of the Church, or of each holy soul, because they are not otherwise efficacious unless they have been named by the addition of these, so that it may be said: This ointment is compositive of faith, hope and charity; for example, someone has humility, chastity, patience, obedience, and some other virtues, whose composition can be called a certain spiritual *160* ointment. But it will be altogether inefficacious unless faith, hope and charity be there. Again, there is someone who does not have all the aforesaid virtues, but has others; but neither will he be able to have an approved ointment of these without [1167A] the addition of these three virtues. Whatever virtues, then, or however great, anyone may have, he must never lack these three; if he desires to have sufficiency for his salvation. Since, therefore, there are in the Church some endowed with these virtues, others with those, some with more, some with fewer, yet always cleaving to those, faith, hope and charity, it can rightly be said, not in the singular number the odour of the ointment, but in the plural number *the odour of thy ointments;* but this *odour above all spices,* because the virtues intermingled with these are fragrant and avail; those lacking them, even if they be fragrant, remain inefficacious. There follows: *Thy lips are a dropping honeycomb.*

VERSE 11.-- *Thy lips are a dropping honeycomb, bride; honey and milk are under thy tongue: and the odour of thy garments as the odour of frankincense.*

[1167B]What ought we to understand by the lips of the bride, but the preaching of each Testament? For as in the body the lips are so set that one is above, the other below, so the Old Testament is prior, the New is posterior; and because speech is expressed through the lips, the preaching of these two Testaments is not unfittingly understood by the lips. But what is it that these lips are called *a dropping honeycomb?* for a honeycomb is honey in wax. And what is honey in wax, but Divinity in humanity? And what else does the preaching of each Testament sound but God made man for the redemption of the human race, the one signifying through figure, the other representing in truth the thing signified? And whatever the Church preaches of Christ is both of his divinity and of his humanity [1167C]. But who does not know that honey is sweet, that wax is of honey? And Christ was sweet from his divinity through his miracles and his preaching, and soft and tractable from his humility through the passion of the body: whence also he could be seized and slain, because God, immense, incomprehensible and invisible, through humility showed himself *161* visible and humble and as it were soft and flexible wax: for he was as it were honey in wax, because, bending himself from heaven to earth and ministering the light of his truth, he distilled the sweetness of his mercy and compassion through the whole world. The *lips* of holy Church, then, are called *a honeycomb* that is *dropping,* because the sweetness and grace of all the divine dispensation which was wrought concerning the human race, [1167D] and is now daily wrought in the sacraments and the realities of the sacraments, is known by her preaching. Of which it is further added, *honey and milk under thy tongue,* that is, both in voice and in heart, because, namely, honey is the food of the strong, milk that of infants; and these things in preaching, or the Church, pronounces by speaking or writing, by which both the strong may be strengthened and the weak nourished; and because she confirms her preaching by good works, instructing her sons by word and example, it is added:

*And the odour of thy garments as the odour of frankincense;* that by the garments good works ought to be understood, the authority of Holy Scripture teaches; for it is written: *Blessed is he that keepeth his garments, lest he walk naked, and they see his shame [n: (Apoc. XVI)]*. [1168A] And in the Proverbs of Solomon: *At all times let thy garments be white [n: (Eccle. IX)]*; for to keep one's garments is to have good works, lest one suffer a shameful repulse in the judgment, if one appear naked of the covering of the virtues with which to cover the deformity of one's sins. And to have white garments is not to defile one's works with the filth of vices, because before the eyes of God those works of the virtues are foul which are fouled by the admixture of vices.

And what is the odour of the garments, but the fame and repute of good actions? But this *odour of the garments* is *as the odour of frankincense,* because no good work is of any efficacy unless it be fragrant from the fire of charity. For as frankincense, when kindled, smokes and gives off its fragrance, so the mind of anyone, kindled with the fire of the Holy Spirit, [1168B] smokes from the grace of compunction, either by bewailing its sins or by sighing for the supernal fatherland. And when through the grace of compunction it has recognized its past fault, or in hope of future goods has wished for a short stay in this life, it pursues the more ardently the gains of piety, when it both burns more inwardly through charity and is fragrant outwardly through good repute. For while his neighbours profit by his example, he makes their gains his own also, because in his actions they learn whence they may acquire for themselves the merits of good works.

The bridegroom says, then, to the bride, that is, to the Church or the faithful soul, *162* *The odour of thy garments as the odour of frankincense,* as if he said: The good odour of the virtues pleases me for this reason, that, kindled from the fire of my charity, it [1168C] is fragrant, and so that odour is directed to me as it were a certain prayer, just as frankincense is kindled and offered to me alone for the devotion of prayers.

VERSE 12.-- *A garden enclosed, my sister, bride, a garden enclosed, a fountain sealed.*

Through the figure metaphor, which is made in four ways, that is, from animate to animate, from inanimate to inanimate, the bridegroom goes on to proclaim the beauty of the bride, two ways of the aforesaid manner of speech being intermingled, that is, from animate to animate and from inanimate to inanimate: for whereas above he had declared the varied [1168D] beauty of the bride *with the eye and the hair, the paps or breasts and the lips,* which are parts of an animate body, *with the odour* also *of the garments,* which is part of an inanimate body, now through that one mode alone which is from inanimate to animate he enumerates other proclamations also of her beauty, of her very dignity, through the likenesses *of the garden and the fountain and the sendings-forth of the garden itself,* which are described through seven kinds; namely *of Cyprus, spikenard and saffron, sweet cane and cinnamon, myrrh and aloes,* of each of which we must speak.

And why is the Church called *a garden?* Surely because of the shoots of the virtues sprouting in her continually; and as a garden is called "hortus" for this reason, that something always arises ("oriatur") there, so the Church is called a garden because the works of the virtues always arise in her, through the progress of her sons, whom she daily acquires in baptism, and nourishes in faith, and advances in the works of faith. [1169A] But why is *this garden enclosed?* Either because of the invisible virtue and singular dignity of the sacraments, or because of the wonderful depth of the Holy Scriptures, or because of the impregnable firmness of the divine defence. For although from the invisible virtue of the sacraments the salvation of believers daily sprouts, yet it is not open to all: how does the Holy Spirit inwardly work in each sacrament the virtue and the reality of the sacrament? why were the sacraments themselves instituted? how and by whom are the same sacraments to be given and received? what faith, what merit of faith, what order, what cause of order, what finally the reverence of the sacraments themselves, what and how great the fault of irreverence; all which things, because they are so hidden and closed that they are open to no one [1169B] except to those who can say with the Apostle: *But to us* *163* *God hath revealed them by his Spirit [n: (I Cor. II)]*, rightly is the Church, in which these sacraments are contained, called *a garden enclosed;* whence also the officers of these sacraments are not admitted into a garden of this kind [sic: *husjusmodi*] unless they enter through him who says: *By me, if any man enter in, he shall be saved [n: (Joan. X)]*.

But because many obtain the offices of those sacraments through ecclesiastical dignities for a price, although before human eyes they seem to enter this garden, yet before the divine eyes it is altogether closed to them; and though they be called officers of Christ, yet they are not, because they have not entered through the door, [1169C] but have climbed up another way. And what they are, the Saviour teaches, who, when he said that he was the door, adds: *He that entereth not by the door, but climbeth up another way, the same is a thief and a robber [n: (ibid.)]*.

Behold how *enclosed* this *garden* is, that access to it is granted to no one except through the *door,* that is, what is written [cj: *scriptum*; read *Christum*, "Christ"]; and he who has not come to it through him is proclaimed a thief and a robber. Whence Gregory: *Why is it not weighed that the blessing is turned into a curse for him who is promoted to this end, that he become a heretic? I do not think that the priesthood subsists there.* To such men, then, this *garden* is truly *enclosed.* Who, although they have the sacraments, yet have not the reality of the sacraments, because they are proved to have these not to salvation but to their own damnation; and therefore what before [1169D] human eyes they seem to be, before the divine eyes they lose.
