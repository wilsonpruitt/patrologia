## *138* CHAPTER IV. (cont.)

Further, on account of the depth of the Holy Scriptures the Church of God is called *a garden enclosed,* because full entry into their sense lies open to no one, except as he leads him in *who hath the key of David, who openeth and no man shutteth, shutteth and no man openeth [n: (Apoc. III)]*. But they who by the grace of God have merited entry into this garden, how many, and how great, and how wholesome kinds of spiritual herbs thinkest thou he will find in it, by which he may be able to drive off the diseases of the soul and to revive the wounded mind? This *garden* is enclosed to Jews and heretics, because they do not understand the sacraments hidden in it, but attend only to the letter; to whom that saying of Isaiah applies: *And the book shall be given to one that knoweth [1170A] letters, and it shall be said to him: Read, and he shall answer [sic: etrespondebit]: I cannot, for it is sealed [n: (Isa. XXIX)]* [var: Vulg. Is 29:12 *nescienti litteras*, "to one that knoweth not letters"; *scienti litteras* and *Non possum, signatus est enim* are the wording of Is 29:11]. This *book,* however, that is, the whole series of Holy Scripture, is contained in this enclosed garden, that is, the Church, *written within and without,* that is, in the literal and the mystical sense, *sealed with seven seals [n: (Apoc. V)]*, that is, with seven sacraments, which the faith of the Church holds, namely the nativity of the Lord, the circumcision, the baptism, the passion, the resurrection, the ascension, the judgment to come.

*Enclosed* therefore is this *garden,* because of the sealed depth of this book, *164* which no one can open, *save the Lamb that was slain, who alone was found worthy to loose this book, and to open the seven seals thereof [n: (Apoc. V)]*, because it belongs to his grace alone to open to human sense the mysteries of Holy Scripture, which [1170B] proclaim the power of those seven sacraments.

But on account of the impregnable firmness of the divine protection the Church can be called *a garden enclosed,* because she who is always fortified by divine defence can by no means be shaken by the onset of malignant spirits or of men; for although they make many and frequent assaults upon her, yet in no wise do they enter into her with any power of their evil will, because she is so enclosed by the hedge of divine protection that they find no means at all of entering into her from the opposing side. Whence, having received great security, she cries out confidently in the Psalm, saying: *The Lord is my light and my salvation, whom [1170C] shall I fear? The Lord is the protector of my life, of whom shall I be afraid? [n: (Psal. XXVI.)] The Lord is my helper, I will not fear what man can do to me; the Lord is my helper, and I will look down upon my enemies [n: (Psal. CXVII)]*.

*A garden* therefore *enclosed* is the Church under divine protection, because God has so strengthened her with his grace that the perverse can neither corrupt her faith, nor take away the institutions of the sacraments, nor cast her down from the state of inmost peace. And if it is granted to them for a time to tempt her by afflicting her outwardly, this temptation turns out for her not to damnation, but to a crown.

Whence also she is called *a garden enclosed* not once, but a second time, because God has enclosed her not only outwardly but also inwardly, while he so sanctifies and guards her [1170D] in all things, that she is kept whole in body and soul unto the day of our Lord Jesus Christ [n: (I Thess. V)]; here indeed temporally, but afterwards eternally.

Not only, however, is the Church called *a garden enclosed,* but also *a fountain sealed,* both on account of the uniformity of baptism and on account of the purity of holy preaching: for as by a fountain the filth of the body is washed away, so by baptism the filth of the soul, that is, sins, are washed; and as by a fountain the weary body is refreshed, so by holy preaching the soul that is failing from God is relieved. Now this *fountain* is said to be *sealed;* but we must see whence, by whom, and why it is sealed.

Whence, then, is it *sealed?* In baptism, in the form, the thing and the intention of the sacrament; in preaching, [1171A] however, in truth, sweetness and utility; for from this the Church is rightly called, in these two, that is, baptism and preaching, *a fountain sealed,* because baptism avails nothing unless there be present both the form of the sacrament, that is, the invocation of the holy Trinity, and the thing of the sacrament, that is, the power and efficacy of the sacrament; the intention also, that is, the will to baptize in the catholic manner. Further, preaching, *165* confers nothing, unless it be true, that it may stir up faith, sweet, that it may draw, useful, that it may inform. For by these parts of the sacrament, as by certain seals, this fountain is shut up, so that its special power and purity and wholesomeness may be intimated.

By whom, then, is it *sealed?* Surely by the Holy Spirit, who has sealed it with the seal of his sevenfold grace, because from the institution and power of the sacraments, and [1171B] from the utility of holy preachings, there flow forth in the Church the watering streams of all spiritual charisms: for there the holy mind receives what, and how, it should savour and understand, by what counsel, by what fortitude it is strengthened, by what knowledge and piety it is adorned, or with what fear of the Lord it is filled. Surely with these spiritual gifts the *fountain* of the Church is so *sealed,* that no one can be a partaker of that fountain who has none of these seven graces.

But why is it *sealed?* Because the sacraments of the Church are to be committed to no one unless he have faith and works; faith, that he may understand and believe that the things which he sees done outwardly and visibly in the sacraments work salvation inwardly and invisibly; works, that in partaking of those sacraments he may show himself worthy. To no one [1171C] also is the office of preaching committed, except to him who is proved to have the grace of this knowledge, as the Apostle says: *How shall they preach unless they be sent [n: (Rom. X)]*? he obtains from God the grace of the office.

VERSE 13.-- *Thy sendings forth are a paradise of pomegranates with the fruits of apples.*

But what do this *garden,* *enclosed and fountain sealed,* kept by so great a divination providence, send forth? *Thy sendings forth,* he says, *are a paradise of pomegranates with the fruits of apples;* for the sendings forth of this garden must needs be wonderful, which is made fruitful by the watering of a fountain so sweetly and wholesomely flowing, and so carefully sealed. For it is established as a paradise by the glorious sendings forth, spiritually coming forth, of diverse [1171D] virtues, or of flowers and herbs, which, although they are enumerated below as very manifold kinds of prayers setting forth the whole range of the virtues, are yet divided into two final causes on account of the twofold quality of the times, namely of persecution and of peace. For every consummation of the saints has either been celebrated in persecution by martyrdom, or accomplished in peace by confession.

Whence well are the *sendings forth, a paradise* of this garden, that is, of holy Church, said to be *of pomegranates with the fruits of apples,* because by the glory either of martyrdom or of confession her holy praises everywhere flourish and give forth fragrance. For what is meant by *pomegranates* but the glorious *166* purple of the martyrs, what by *the fruits of apples,* but the life of the confessors shining white with good deeds? [1172A] O how great are the delights of this paradise, how great the sendings forth of virtues, flowing forth abundantly and happily, where both the strong are nourished and the weak are refreshed! because there those who desire to imitate admire, proclaim and venerate the constancy of the martyrs; there those who cannot be martyrs attend to, approve and emulate the praiseworthy life of the saints living gloriously in peace.

VERSE 14.-- *Cyprus with spikenard, spikenard and saffron, sweet cane and cinnamon with all the trees of Libanus, myrrh and aloes with all the chief ointments.*

Whence there also follows the designated beauty, wholesomeness and utility of this *garden enclosed,* or *fountain sealed,* by seven kinds of aromatic herbs, not only because the grace of the sevenfold [1172B] Spirit effects these sendings forth of her holiness, but also because by this sevenfold number of kinds the totality of the virtues is expressed. For in the whole body of the Church no virtue can be lacking, because, since the head of the Church is Christ, and the body of Christ the Church, whatever of holiness, of honourableness, and of utility ever could be, or can be, it will be permitted to consider in the whole Christ, that is, in head and members.

Let us look, then, at the kinds themselves, by which the totality of the virtues, as we have said, is denoted, and let us know that what we understand in the parts is to be understood in the whole. *Cyprus,* he says, *with spikenard, spikenard and saffron, sweet cane and cinnamon with all the trees of Libanus, myrrh and aloes with all the chief ointments.*

*Cyprus* is an aromatic tree, signifying the blessing of spiritual [1172C] grace, and it is interpreted *mourning* or *sadness. Cyprus* therefore in the Church is any holy soul, like an aromatic tree, which has it from the grace of God that it will despise the passing joys of this world, having that sadness *which is according to God, which worketh penance unto a stable salvation [n: (II Cor. VII)]*; mindful of that saying of the Gospel: *Blessed are they that mourn, for they shall be comforted [n: (Matth. V)]*; and of that apostolic one: *Be wretched and mourn, let your laughter be turned into mourning, and your joy into sorrow [n: (Jac. IV)]*.

*Spikenard* is said to drive off cold, *saffron*, however, to bring coolness to those who burn: what, then, is meant by spikenard but charity, what by saffron but chastity [1172D] is figured? For charity rouses the lukewarm in good work, and makes them burning; chastity tempers the mind and restrains it from the heat of evil concupiscence.

*Sweet cane* is an aromatic tree, short and small, but of hard bark, which, when crushed, is drunk and heals the entrails: further, cinnamon has an ashen colour, and keeps its strength a long time. By the sweet cane, which however, as we have said, is short and small, humility is not unfittingly understood; but *167* by its hard bark, crushed and drunk, the entrails are healed, because while by the virtue [sic: *virtu e*] of humility the elation of the hard and proud flesh is broken, the wound of the soul is healed by the medicine of a contrite and humbled heart. By *cinnamon*, however, which is of the colour of ashes, and keeps its strength a long time, the abject habit of penitents is designated, who can say [1173A] with the Prophet: *I did eat ashes like bread [n: (Psal. CI)]*, and with blessed Job: *Therefore I reprehend myself and do penance in dust and ashes [n: (Job XLII)]*. But this abjection keeps its strength a long time, because that is true penance which so keeps the rectitude of a converted heart that it does not afterwards commit things to be repented of.

*With all the trees of Libanus,* that is, with all the virtues of a life made white by baptism, made solid by right faith, and raised on high by good works. For when he had set forth particularly those virtues which we placed above, by the forenamed kinds of trees, he adds universally, by all the trees of Libanus, all the virtues, that we may understand that the garden of the Lord, that is, the Church, sends forth indeed every virtue from herself, through the varied life of the saints, but that those forenamed are the heads [1173B] of all the virtues, so that the other members of the virtues lie weak without them.

*Myrrh and aloes with all the chief ointments.* He adds yet two kinds of the sevenfold virtues, namely the mortification of the flesh and patience; by myrrh, then, by which dead bodies are kept unharmed from the corruption of worms and from putrefaction, the mortification of the flesh ought to be understood, by which the putrefaction of vices and of carnal pleasures is restrained; by aloes, which has indeed a sweet odour, but a bitter taste, and purges the stomach, we can take patience, which, because on account of the future goods of perennial life it endures the evils of the world, is indeed fragrant from the cause for which it suffers, but bitter from the cause by which [1173C] it suffers: and if any disease of sin weighs down the soul, the bitterness of the very evil which it suffers purges it.
