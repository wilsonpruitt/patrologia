## *138* CHAPTER IV. (cont.)

*With all,* he says, *the chief ointments,* that is, with the precious and approved gifts of spiritual charisms, which the Apostle enumerates, saying: *To one indeed by the Spirit is given the word of wisdom, to another the word of knowledge according to the same Spirit, to another faith in the same Spirit, to another the working of virtues, to another prophecy, to another the discerning of spirits, to another kinds of tongues, to another the interpretation of speeches [n: (I Cor. XII)]*. Behold, these can be called the chief ointments, which are compounded by divine grace, before which, not without reason, are set the two kinds of virtue foreshown above by myrrh and aloes, namely mortification and patience, that we may understand that those only are to be imbued with these spiritual ointments, [1173D] *who have crucified their flesh with the vices and concupiscences [n: (Gal. V)]*, who can say with the Apostle: *And we glory in tribulations [n: (Rom. V)]*.

It is pleasing, then, here to attend and to wonder *168* that the garden of the Lord through these its sendings forth is truly as a most beautiful paradise, that is, a garden of delights, where the eternal salvation of the soul, and all sweetness, flourish, where all beauty and spiritual delight bloom, where perennial life and everlasting rest abide; not that these are conferred here in the present life, but that through those sendings forth of virtues and graces which we spoke of above one attains to these, and that the exercise of those is temporal, the showing forth of these eternal. Whence most rightly this *garden,* or paradise, is recorded to be [1174A] *enclosed,* because by no adversity of malignant spirits is holy Church overcome, she who is strengthened by the grace of the spiritual sendings forth of this garden.

But since, as the Church in general is called the garden of the Lord, so also in particular each holy soul, which flourishes with the bud of the virtues and the beauty of manifold graces, can be called the same, it must yet be considered, since the Church is a certain whole, while one soul is a part of the whole, how much the part differs from the whole. For what is in the part must be in the whole, but it is not converted, so that what is in the whole must also be equally in the part. For the universal Church in its parts, that is, in the singular and individual men of whose collection into one it is constituted, is a certain whole, because while [1174B] many flourish with diverse virtues, and in this diversity all kinds of virtues are contained, the whole indeed contains all in its parts, but the part not all, but some.

Therefore the whole and the part differ in this, that the whole is far more comprehensive than the part, since all things flow together at once in the whole, but in the part not all, but some. Since, then, the holy universal Church in general is called the garden of the Lord, because within her the buds of all the virtues are celebrated; any holy soul also which brings forth some buds of the virtues can be called the garden of the Lord, according to some manner of its own which divine grace has conferred upon it.

VERSE 15.-- *The fountain of gardens; the well of living [1174C] waters, which flow with a rush from Libanus.*

But since whoever is instructed is instructed either by word or by example; and the part has what it has from the whole, while words and examples are universally in the whole, not without reason does it follow that that principal garden of the Lord, that is, the universal Church, should be called *the fountain of gardens* that are particular, that is, of the single souls. For whence is it that they say, that they do, that they teach, except from the doctrines and institutions of the holy universal Church? And whence those doctrines themselves, except from the words, writings and examples of the holy apostles and of apostolic men? Wherefore rightly the Church is called the *fountain* of these *gardens,* that is, of holy souls. *169* Because whatever of holiness or honourableness [1174D] they have, they conceive from the watering of ecclesiastical preaching, and they bring forth all the things which their progress is, the rule and state of ecclesiastical discipline. For a fountain [n: (Gen. II)] goes forth from the paradise of the Lord, that is, from the Church of God, which is divided into four rivers, that is, into the four Gospels, which water the whole face of the earth, that is, every intention of the dry mind, so that, inebriated by their doctrine through the grace of God, it may say with the Psalmist: *The Lord will give goodness, and our earth shall yield her fruit [n: (Psal. LXXXIV)]*: for unless the goodness of God has visited the human heart, it will be able to bear no fruit of good works

But though the same Church, for all these gardens diversely [1175A] bearing fruit on account of the diverse qualities of knowledge and of works, is in common called a *fountain,* because she waters all with the infusion either of knowledge or of pious zeal, yet to some she is singularly called *a well of living waters,* on account of the depth of divine knowledge specially hidden in them, which flows forth from the fountain of the Church itself [sic: *exipsius*]. The fountain indeed flows openly upon the earth, but the water of the well is hidden in the earth, because one is the knowledge which the simple and weak attain for its easiness, another that which the learned and strong scarcely search out for its subtlety. The Church, therefore, is a *fountain* to all the elect, but not to all is she a *well,* because all indeed read or hear and understand the literal surface, but very few look into the depth of the spiritual sense hidden within [1175B].

But this well has living waters, because the Church, while she opens the depth of the divine utterances to those who read or hear, both corrects them from depravity, and kindles them to good works, and thus refreshes, by a vital doctrine as by living waters, those who ought to have died in their sins.

Of what waters, then? *Which flow,* he says, *with a rush from Libanus:* for what is the rush of these waters, but the vehement course of the Holy Scriptures flowing forth with great authority? And what is expressed by Libanus, but the knowledge and virtue of sublime doctors? The waters therefore flow with a rush from Libanus, because the Holy Scriptures, from the knowledge and virtue of the holy [1175C] Fathers, show the great force of their authority. This rush was indeed necessary to the Church, because the course of faith and of sound doctrine would have been hindered by heretics and perverse men, had not the torrent of the eloquence of the holy doctors driven back the opposing machinations of falsehood by the rush of true reason and eloquence.

But because against holy Church, or any holy soul, the adversary has always stood with a twofold battle line, for he tempts her either by threats or by blandishments, that he may cast her down from the rectitude of her state, the bridegroom, wishing to show how much she profits from this temptation, adds in a permissive manner of speech, saying: *Arise, north wind,* etc.

*[1175D] 170* VERSE 16.-- *Arise, north wind; and come, south wind, blow through my garden, and let the spices thereof flow.*

By the north wind, which is a harsh and cold wind, the harshness of threats, which descends from the coldness of a perverse mind, is understood; by the south wind, which is warm, the smoothness of blandishments, which proceeds as it were with a certain sweetness from a deceitful heart, is taken. When therefore the Church or some holy soul is wearied in these two ways, she is trained for greater exercises of the virtues, so that the more strongly she struggles against the enemy, the more gloriously after the victory she is joined to the embraces of her bridegroom. For by this twofold war the constancy of the martyrs was proved, by this also the conversation of the faithful is daily proved and crowned. For what mind is not tempted by the prosperities and adversities of this world? but that mind is crowned as proved, [1176A] which is neither lifted up by prosperity nor cast down by adversity. Whence John is commended by the Lord in the Gospel, because he is recorded not to be a reed shaken by the wind [n: (Matth. XI)]: for a reed, when it is driven by the wind, is bent to whatever side; but he is not a reed shaken by the wind who is bent back to neither side, namely to the right or to the left; because he is neither shaken by terror nor seduced by blandishment. *Arises,* then, the *north wind,* when one is driven by detraction, or by some harshness; *the south wind comes* when one is tempted by favour or by some prosperity.

But these blowing through the garden of the Lord, *the spices thereof flow,* because the more the Church, or a holy soul, is touched by adversities or prosperities, the more, through progress, [1176B] is she filled with the good odour of the virtues. For this quality of life is probative of holiness and justice, so that he may deservedly be called the *garden* of the Lord who is adorned with such virtue of patience, humility and longanimity: but where there has been privation of this good, there cannot be the appellation of spiritual good.

Moreover: By the north wind the devil is often designated, by the south wind the Holy Spirit, because the one binds up in cold, and the other loosens in heat. For on the testimony of the Prophet the devil is related to have said: *I will sit in the mountain of the Testament, in the sides of the north [n: (Isa. XIV)]*. And at the suggestion and breath of the devil iniquity and malice abounded in the world. For on the testimony of the Truth: *Where iniquity hath abounded, the charity [1176C] of many shall grow cold [n: (Matth. XXIV)]* [var: Vulg. *quoniam abundavit*, "because iniquity hath abounded"]. And the prophet asserts of Judaea, saying: *As a cistern maketh its water cold, so hath she made her malice cold [n: (Jer. VI)]*. But by the name of the south wind the Holy Spirit is taken, because by his grace the torpor of the mind is loosened, as the Psalmist testifies: *Turn,* he says, *O Lord, our captivity, as a torrent in the south [n: (Psal. CXXV)]*: *171* for our [sic: *nostrae*] captivity, which through the cold of torpor had remained sluggish for following God, is loosened by the heat of the Holy Spirit, so that it may run into the love of God.

The heavenly Bridegroom, therefore, deigning to visit his bride, that is, the Church or a holy soul, bids the north wind withdraw, and the south wind come, because it is a just progress and a fitting order that, first [1176D] the cold of unbelief and malice having been driven off, by the coming of the Holy Spirit, the mind should receive the heat of faith and of good working. *Arise,* he says, *north wind, and come, south wind, blow through my garden, and the spices thereof shall flow:* as if he should say in other words: Because into the minds designated for me the grace of the Holy Spirit cannot come unless the cold of unbelief has been put to flight, whatever torpor, whatever sloth, thou hast settled in those same minds, whether through ignorance, or through negligence, arise and withdraw and come, O Holy Spirit, who mayest loosen by the heat of thy breath those same minds bound with torpor, that, the vigour of good will and of pious intention having been conceived, they may flow forth with the fruits of holiness and with odours of fruits, spreading far, and wide, drawing many round about to their fellowship.

[1177A]Further, at this voice of the bridegroom, the bride, now as it were animated and in a manner raised up again, rises with a ready mind to the inspiring grace, and because it follows that, the Holy Spirit blowing, she should bring forth the fruit of good working, she shows it, saying: *Let my beloved come into his garden,* etc.
