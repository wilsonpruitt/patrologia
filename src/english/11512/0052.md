## *172* CHAPTER V.

VERSE 1.-- *Let my beloved come into his garden, and eat the fruit of his apples. Come into my garden, my sister, bride, I have reaped my myrrh with my spices: I have eaten the honeycomb with my honey, I have drunk the wine with my milk: eat and drink, friends, and be inebriated, dearest.*

Well does she say that the beloved comes into his garden, because the cause of his coming is that he is beloved, that his [1177B] is the garden; for unless he were loved he would not come. *If any one love me, he will keep my commandments, and my Father will love him, and we will come to him, and will make our abode with him [n: (Joan. XIV)]* [var: Vulg. *sermonem meum servabit*, "he will keep my word"]. But he who says that he loves God ought to prove it by work, for this is to be his garden: for where diverse flowers of the virtues bloom, and fruits abound, it is not undeservedly called spiritually the garden of the Lord, and to be such a garden is to love God. *Comes,* then, *the beloved into his garden,* when he illumines with the grace of his visitation a mind given to good actions. In which garden *he eats the fruit of his apples,* because he is delighted and fed by the odour and sweetness of the virtues in the mind which his grace has filled. Whence the beloved himself, as if congratulating, answers his beloved: *Come into my [1177C] garden, my sister, bride, I have reaped my myrrh with my spices.* What is taken by myrrh but the mortification of the flesh? But because, as by the delight of the flesh vices are nourished, so by mortification virtues are generated, rightly he puts myrrh first specially, and afterwards subjoins the spices generally: for unless the flesh be subjected to the spirit through the mortification of the vices, the mind does not rise to the virtues, because in its suggestion the spirit does not contradict the flesh; but when the spirit always resists the flesh, *173* to the summits of the virtues, the flesh being now at length subjected, the mind advances: and when through the daily progress of good work it has come to a certain ripeness of perfection, it is as if the time of harvest has now come to it.

[1177D]Her good works, therefore, the beloved reaps, when he gathers her, cut off by the sickle of death from the field of earthly habitation, into the barns of the heavenly kingdom, to be rewarded: this harvest indeed is either the separation of the soul from the flesh, or of the elect from the reprobate: now he who reaps takes up a part, and leaves a part; and in the separation of the soul from the body the soul is taken up and the body is left. Further, the elect, when they are separated from the reprobate, are themselves taken up to life as good corn, as the reprobate, as remaining stubble, are left to the eternal fire. But the beloved reaps myrrh when he says: *Come to me all you that labour and are burdened, and I will make you to rest [n: (Matth. XI)]* [var: Vulg. *reficiam vos*, "I will refresh you"]; he reaps spices when he says: *Come, you blessed of my Father, [1178A] receive the kingdom which is prepared for you from the beginning of the world [n: (Matth. XXV)]* [var: Vulg. *possidete paratum vobis regnum a constitutione mundi*, "possess the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world"]. He reaps myrrh, then, with spices, when he divides from the company of the wicked those who through contempt of the world and affliction of the flesh have advanced to the works of the virtues, and lays them up in the storehouse of his kingdom.

Whom he well calls his myrrh and his spices, because whatever good they have they possess from the gift of his grace: and this harvest is made, as has been said, by the sickle of judicial sentence in this time, both through dissolution and through predestination. Through dissolution indeed, when the soul, as the superior and worthier part, is taken up to heaven, but the body, as the inferior and viler part, is left in the earth. Through predestination, however, because those whom in his foreknowledge he holds as going to persevere [1178B] in good he chooses as corn by predestining them to eternal life; but those going to remain in evil, as stubble fixed in earthly acts, he leaves by predestining them to eternal death. Let the beloved therefore say: *Come into my garden, I have reaped my myrrh with my spices,* because after, by my grace, visiting the Church or the faithful soul, I made it bud with virtues, I fitted specially to myself those dead to the world and given to pious actions, whom, because I separated them from the reprobate by election, I numbered into the fellowship of my kingdom. Whence he presently subjoins: *I have eaten the honeycomb with my honey, I have drunk my wine with my milk.* Yet further the beloved rejoices at the progress of his budding garden, in the pursuits and virtues of the elect wonderfully adorning that same garden. In [1178C] the honeycomb there is honey and wax, the honey indeed sweet, the wax soft and pliant, and a minister of light: further, in wine there is austerity, but in milk whiteness and smoothness. What, then, can be signified by these kinds of things, but the diverse quality of speech in holy preachers, on account of the diverse qualities of minds in hearers? For as the Apostle says to one of them [sic: *corum*], whom he was instructing for preaching: *Reprove, entreat, rebuke [n: (II Tim. IV)]*, the holy doctors *174* temper the force of their preaching, when they consider person, place, time, and profitably weigh where and when, or whom they ought to reprove, entreat, rebuke.

For according to the diversities of minds, there are some who [1178D] willingly receive the word of holy preaching, as soon as they have heard it; and there are some who will neither hear nor receive it; further, there are many who what they will not or cannot receive and hear in this place or time, hear and receive in another place and time. Again, there are some minds which are amended by soft and gentle preaching. But some which are restrained by nothing but hard rebuke, while some are exercised to greater works of the virtues by pious entreaty. On account of these diverse qualities of souls, therefore, the doctors of souls ought to be very discreet in their preaching, because they ought subtly to weigh what, and when, and where, or to whom they preach, lest they seem to throw words away in vain. When, therefore, they correct delinquents whom they see soft and pliant, they ought to reprove them with gentle and [1179A] sweet admonition, according to the saying of the wise man: *A softer answer breaketh wrath [n: (Prov. XV)]* [var: Vulg. *Responsio mollis*, "a mild answer"]: but those whom they see hard and intractable they ought to strike with harsh rebuke, and with the terror of divine punishment; while those given to good acts they ought to entreat, that they may always advance to better.

Since, therefore, prelates ought to instruct their subjects in these three ways, surely when they cherish the good with the sweetness of pious exhortation, and console them by preaching the sweetness of the heavenly kingdom, they are *the honeycomb with honey;* but when they now restrain delinquents with modest speech, now reprove them with hard rebuke, announcing the pains of hell, they are *wine with milk.*

The beloved, therefore, *eats the honeycomb with his honey, drinks wine [1179B] with his milk;* because the rulers of holy Church, whom by his grace he composes for preaching in the manner said above, he makes his members by incorporating them into himself, in whose pursuits and progress he is delighted, and as it were refreshed. And because he has proved them to be of his body, by the conformation and unity by which he has incorporated them into himself, he makes an apostrophe to those who can be instructed by their doctrines and examples, saying:

*Eat and drink, friends, and be inebriated, dearest:* as if he should say openly: Because I have prepared for you the refreshment of souls in the deeds and words of the doctors, whom I have filled with my Spirit, you who ought to be instructed by their doctrines, what you hear and see from them lay up in the belly of memory, and fill your mind [1179C] with such sweetness of the virtues, that you may remain insensible to every motion of the vices: *friends* indeed to me through faith and working, *dearest* through intimate love. For this eating and drinking, the *175* full perception of the divine precepts: whence the Psalmist says of those who are inebriated with this fullness: *We are inebriated with the plenty of thy house [n: (Psal. XXXV)]* [var: Vulg. *Inebriabuntur*, "they shall be inebriated"]. For what is the house of God, but the Church, or the mind of any just man? And what is the plenty of this house, but a certain fullness of the divine precepts and sacraments, with which holy Church, or the soul of some saint, is filled and grows rich? But with this plenty they are filled and inebriated, whoever, from the institution of the Church or the teaching of any saint, [1179D] receive full knowledge in the necessary utility and honourableness of those precepts and sacraments. With this plenty was he filled who said: *Though rude in speech, yet not in knowledge [n: (II Cor. II)]*: the fullness of this plenty the Psalmist desired when he said: *Let my soul be filled as with marrow and fatness [n: (Psal. LXII)]*.

But because the bride had heard the voice of the exhorting bridegroom, and had tasted the sweetness of his precepts with the palate of the heart, and had been inebriated with the fullness of this good by hearing, reading, believing and working. what effect this plenary outpouring had, or what of utility she obtained from it, she declares, saying: *I sleep,* etc.

VERSE 2.-- *I sleep and my heart watcheth. The voice [1180A] of my beloved knocking: Open to me, my sister, my love, my dove, my undefiled; for my head is full of dew and my locks of the drops of the nights.*

The act of this sleeping and watching, if thou look to outward things, can by no means be at once in one and the same, at one and the same time, because no one can at once both sleep and think something by watching, at one and the same moment or time: but if thou look to inward things, and attend to the manner of this sleeping and watching, these two so inseparably cleave to each other that at one and the same time they must either not be, or be together.

For what is this sleep, but a disengagement from all the cares and actions of the world, and as it were a certain [1180B] rest and pause of the mind? And what is the watching of the heart, but a contemplative love tending continually to God with sincerity, or an amatory contemplation? But since it is certain that no one can have the virtue of holy contemplation unless he has striven to be free and disengaged from all the business of the world; and conversely that no one can disengage himself from the business of the world unless he begin to love and desire God with pure and simple intention, it is established that these two, as has been said, so cleave to each other, that at one and the same time, in one and the same, *176* they must either [sic: *ab*] altogether not be, or be together. In this manner, therefore, it is possible to sleep and to watch at once, nor is there any contrariety there, where there is this identity of habit in the affected mind, for to watch in this manner is the same [1180C] as to sleep, and to sleep is the same as to watch; because to cleave to contemplation and to sleep to the world, and to watch to God, and unless he sleeps to the world he does not watch, and unless he watches to God, he does not sleep to the world.

But it can be asked why the bride says: *I sleep, and my heart watcheth,* as if the whole man were said to sleep, and a part of the man, that is, the heart, to watch. To which it must be said that the whole man is said to be body and soul, while a certain part of the whole is the heart; and this part looks to the inner man alone, that is, the soul; but the whole man tends to the outer and at the same time the inner man. Now the whole man ought to sleep when this part, namely the heart, is zealous to watch in contemplation, that is, in body and mind, [1180D] which is, in will and in action, to cease from all exterior things, and as it were to rest, that he may be able more freely to be at leisure for interior things; because, therefore, the bride considered that she could not perfectly cleave to the vision of the heavenly bridegroom unless she had all her senses, interior and exterior, free and disengaged, rightly does she say: *I sleep, and my heart watcheth:* as if she should say powerfully in other words: Because I rest utterly from the din of earthly things, in thought and in work, therefore I watch in mind more subtly in divine contemplation.
