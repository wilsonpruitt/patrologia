## *172* CHAPTER V. (cont.)

But to what precedes it must be joined thus, that because the bridegroom had said of his preachings that he had filled them with such sweetness and authority of sound doctrine that no one ought to doubt that they were incorporated into him, and that therefore to be inebriated in their preaching [1181A] was both very useful and honourable; the bride, as if roused by this admonition, and having greatly profited by the preaching, words and examples of the saints, answers: Since, she says, O beloved, I have obeyed thy admonitions, thou preventing me by inspiring and following me by helping, and the doctrines of thy preachers; I have so applied my mind through faith, hope and charity, that I have not only as it were eaten and drunk, but am wholly inebriated, so my carnal senses have been lulled by this inebriation that behold I now sleep utterly from all the cares and desires of the present life, and in the sole contemplation of the Godhead I watch in heart through intimate love.

I indeed, she says, although taught by the sayings, writings and deeds of the holy Fathers how I ought to advance both in the active [1181B] and in the contemplative life, have yet chosen the better part; since I determined to be at leisure for God alone in the contemplative life with whole heart and body; but because the beloved through his grace *hath fed me with the bread of life and understanding, and given me the water of wholesome wisdom to drink [n: (Eccli. XV)]*, that I may be fit *177* for the office of preaching, he knocks and rouses, and commands that I take care not only of myself alone by resting in contemplation, but also of my neighbours by labouring in preaching. *Open,* he says, *to me,* for *the foxes have holes and the birds of the air nests, but the Son of man hath not where to lay his head [n: (Matth. VIII; Luc. IX)]*: for save a few, by whose faith I have been received, almost the whole world repels me either by unbelief or by depravity of faith, the Jews [1181C] and pagans not believing, the heretics depraving the orthodox faith. Whence my head, placed under the open sky, is drenched with dew, because my Godhead is dishonoured by the perverse opinions of heretics, falling as it were from heaven on account of the elegance of their speech, but warm [cj: *calentibus*; read *carentibus*, "lacking"] with the heat of truth and of right faith. My *locks* also *are full of the drops of the nights,* because the faithful, who ought to cleave to me as to their head through faith and working, are infected by their perverse dogmas, falling in the nights of depraved senses, and are made cold from the love of truth.

Or: *My head is full of dew and my locks of the drops of the nights,* because from the love of God and of neighbour cold [1181D] are the hearts of unbelievers: for by the head God, by the locks neighbours can be understood. *Open* therefore *to me,* because unless thou preach and teach I shall not be able to enter into their hearts: wherefore rise at times from that sweet dormitory of thy inner solitude and take up the office of preaching, that after they, taught by thy preaching, have renounced their former errors, and have prepared for me the lodging of their mind by right faith and working; I may gladly enter and bestow on them the saving gift of my grace.

*Sister,* he says, *mine, my love, my dove, my undefiled:* what means the proving of these four appellations? Does the Holy Spirit attend to nothing in so distinct a placing of these four words? by no means. For if thou apply the eyes of the inner man, [1182A] thou wilt perceive in this number of appellations a reason not to be despised, and the utility of the reason: for the form of spiritual squareness is necessary to every soul wishing to stand on the summit of perfection; for a square, whichever way it is turned, will stand. And this ought to be the constancy of the perfect, that they be neither lifted up by prosperity nor cast down by adversity, but persist always in one manner in the order and place of their holy purpose

But what is this spiritual squareness? Surely faith, hope, charity and working. For *faith,* as the Apostle says, *worketh by love [n: (Gal. V)]*, and, *Faith, if it have not works, is dead in itself [n: (Jac. II)]*; and the proof of charity is working: but he who does not believe does not hope either; and he who does not hope, [1182B] has not what he may love: further, he who does not believe neither hopes nor loves what there is *178* for which he should work. This is the intention of all good work in each one, that what he believes, hopes, and loves, he may at some time merit to obtain. In the measure of a square, however, not one side is greater and another less, but all four sides together are stretched out with equal extent, because also in these same virtues which we have mentioned, namely faith, hope, charity and working, as long as we live in this life, we shall find that they are equal with ourselves. For each one who is engaged in the exercise of the active life believes as much as he hopes; as much as he believes, he works, and loves; he loves as much as he believes, hopes and works; he works as much as he believes, hopes and loves.

[1182C]But let us say that this squareness of the soul also consists in four principal virtues, namely prudence, fortitude, justice, temperance; for these virtues are so in a square that one does not exceed another. For prudence is great; but if it is less brave in dangers, less just in operations, less temperate from pleasures, surely it is less prudent.

Fortitude is great, but if it understands less what goods it should guard, what evils it should resist, if it holds less to the works of justice, and at times is overcome by the domination of injustice; if it tempers the appetite less from pleasures, but is conquered by delight, it is less brave. Justice is great, but if it discerns less than it desires [1182D] between just and unjust works, if it strengthens itself less against adversities, if it tempers the flesh less in delight, it is less just. Temperance is great, but if it understands less whence it should temper itself, if it is less able to endure adversities through fortitude, and casts down the mind in fear, if by its precipitation it sometimes breaks out into works of injustice, it is less temperate.

If, then, the life of the perfect faithful is measured by the square, one side has as much as the single sides; because each one is as much prudent as he is brave, just and temperate; as much brave as just, prudent and temperate; as much just as prudent, brave, temperate; as much temperate as he shall have been prudent, brave and just. [1183A] But because the Doctor of the Gentiles says: *I chastise my body, and bring it into subjection, lest perhaps, when I have preached to others, I myself should become reprobate [n: (I Cor. IX)]*, the preacher ought always to study this, that the right things which he preaches with the mouth he himself also shows in his acts.

There is necessary, therefore, to every preacher the squareness of these virtues which we have set down, namely that he himself have in himself faith, hope, charity and working, and preach them to others to be had; that he also shine with prudence, fortitude, justice, temperance, and train others also to these by teaching.

*179* Since, then, this beloved rouses his beloved, resting securely in contemplation, to the office of preaching, he calls her by the name of fourfold grace, so that by this he may intimate to her that he who is sent to [1183B] preach ought always to be squared by these four virtues. For he calls her *sister* on account of faith, *love* on account of hope, *dove* on account of charity, *undefiled* on account of working. For holy Church, or any holy soul, which has obtained the name of bride and beloved by believing in the Almighty, attains the eternal inheritance, that she may become an heir of God, and a joint heir with Christ, because she merits by grace the sonship of God which he has by nature.

This heavenly Bridegroom, therefore, calls the soul beloved by him, redeemed by the price of his blood, *sister,* because what he is essentially, this she can be called by name; for John says in his Gospel that *as many as received him, he gave them [1183C] power to be made the sons of God, to them that believe in his name, who are born not of bloods, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God [n: (Joan. I)]*: of whom the Lord himself also says: *Whosoever shall do the will of my Father who is in heaven, he is my brother and sister and mother [n: (Matth. XII)]*.

He calls her *love,* because while he manifests to her certain secrets of his knowledge, and the immensity of his ineffable goodness, he raises her up into great hope of future beatitude; for it is a sign of true friendship to make someone privy to one's secret; whence the Lord to the disciples, to whom he had revealed the mysteries of the divine will: *Now I will not call you,* he says, *servants, but friends, because all things which I have heard from my Father [1183D] I have made known to you [n: (Joan. XV)]*.

He names her *dove,* because he approves her as simple, innocent and peaceable, endowed also with the love of God and of neighbour, as the dove is a simple and innocent animal, abstaining from all tearing, and sometimes cherishing others' young as her own.

For he who is truly innocent is known to have the charity of God and of neighbour, because true innocence is that which is thus defined: True innocence is that which harms neither itself nor others: for it harms itself when it offends God by acting ill; it harms others when it offers examples of evil action; he, then, who avoids working ill proves that he loves God, because thereby he also keeps himself, whereby he labours lest he lose God; he proves that he loves his neighbour, because thereby he invites him to act well, [1184A] whereby he guards himself lest he offend God. Right, therefore, is the true definition of innocence, which harms neither itself nor others, because through it the love of God and neighbour is expressed, in which the whole fullness of the law consists.

*180* For what the fullness of the law is, is beautifully declared by the fifth kind of definition, which in Greek is called κατα αντιλεχιν, in Latin "ad verbum", word for word, when that which is asked is shown by one word, in this manner: *The fullness of the law, love [n: (Rom. XIII)]*. If, then, the fullness of the law is charity, *but charity dealeth not perversely [n: (I Cor. XIII)]*, surely he who is known to have charity is proved to keep the whole law: and since that charity is poured forth in the hearts of the elect through the Holy Spirit [n: (Rom. V)], it follows that he who possesses the gift of charity confirms himself illumined with the gifts of the Holy [1184B] Spirit. For if no virtue avails without charity, and charity is conferred through the Holy Spirit, surely he who has charity is proved to have the Holy Spirit, in whom the gifts of all the virtues are bestowed. Whence now rightly the bridegroom calls the bride whom he invites to preaching a dove, that he intimates to her that she ought to have charity, which is given through the Holy Spirit, who is asserted to have been seen in the form of a dove.

He calls her *undefiled,* that whatever she shows in working she may remember to do it with pure intention, not that she may receive human praise from it, but that she may please the Godhead alone, because before God those works are foul which have been displayed for the favour of the world.

Or he calls her *undefiled* for this, that he may intimate that cleanness [1184C] of mind and of body is to be loved, or that he may forewarn her to avoid diligently in her preaching every stain of heretical depravity.

Further, on account of the four principal virtues in which the most beautiful squareness of the mind consists, the beloved calls his beloved by these four names, that he may show that she whom he exhorts to rise to preaching ought to be prudent, brave, just and temperate, as being *sister, love, dove and undefiled.*
