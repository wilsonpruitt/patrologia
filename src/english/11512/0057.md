## *172* CHAPTER V. (cont.)

*His lips are as lilies dropping choice myrrh.* Behold the perfumers by whose industry the grace of modesty shines forth in the cheeks, namely the holy preachers, who are therefore designated by the lips, because by their preaching they *194* compound the diverse perfumes of the virtues. They are as *lilies,* because they both give a [sic: *secundam*] precept for integrity and purity of life, and give counsel for the wounds of souls, and they can say with the Apostle: *We are the good odour of Christ unto God in them that are saved [n: (II Cor. II)]*. For the nature of the lily shows forth whiteness [1196B] and odour together, and yields very much healing for the curing of wounds.

They are *lilies dropping choice myrrh,* that is, the best, because, while for the keeping of integrity and purity of heart and body they mortify vices and sins in themselves, and unceasingly preach to others by word and example that they should do the same, they assuredly show how great a charity they have toward God and their neighbour.

For charity can be called choice myrrh, that is, the best and proved, because, where it is true and perfect, it extinguishes in man all the incentives of vices; for as the bodies of the dead are embalmed with myrrh, lest they putrefy, so souls are strengthened by divine charity, lest they incur the putrefaction of vices.

[1196C]And in this member of his body also the beloved is white and ruddy, because in his holy preachers he is called white for the lily of chastity, but through the myrrh of the passion, *For all that will live godly in Christ shall suffer persecution, [n: (II Tim. III)]*, he is proclaimed ruddy.

VERSE 14.-- *His hands are turned and of gold, full of hyacinths. His belly is of ivory, set with sapphires.*

If we look to the head itself, his *hands* are assuredly *turned* and *of gold,* because of him it is written: *How great are thy works, O Lord, thou hast made all things in wisdom [n: (Psal. CIII)]*: for he who works with the lathe works more quickly and more perfectly and more smoothly. But [1196D] who ever worked more quickly and more perfectly or more smoothly than God himself? For very quickly he made what he disposed to make, because he only *spoke, and they were made [n: (Psal. XXXII)]*, and at one and the same time, though in a diverse manner, in a diverse form also, his having spoken was his having made. No one has worked more perfectly than he, because he made not things diminished, or by parts, and such as were yet to be bettered, but made all things perfect, and his having made once was his having made the whole and entire. Further, nothing was smoother than his work, because the nature of things could be retarded by no hindrance, but the course and measure which it once received it kept as a perpetual law: nothing in his works is distorted and unequal, diminished or superfluous, because *he appointed all things in measure, and number, and [1197A] weight [n: (Sap. II)]* [var: Vulg. Sap 11:21 *omnia in mensura, et numero et pondere disposuisti*, "thou hast ordered all things"]. Well, therefore, were *his hands turned,* because he wrought all things with such, as has been said, swiftness, *195* perfection and beauty, that from the works themselves it becomes clear to one who considers them of how great beauty, or of how great majesty, the founder himself is, not that this consideration is a full comprehension of his deity, but that it is a certain beholding of the Creator, setting out from the dignity of the creature and shining through as far as is possible to the human mind.

Whence also the *hands* themselves are called *of gold,* because this, that they are, that they live, that they thrive and flourish, stands by the disposition of the divinity: for by gold, which is more precious than all metals, the divinity is designated, than which there is nothing above.

[1197B]But what is it that they are called *full of hyacinths,* when there are also other stones very suitable too for the adornment of the hands or fingers? All visible things indeed are certain wonderful and beautiful adornments of the divine works, but in these they are more to be wondered at, because they are also the subtler secrets of heavenly wonders; the consideration of which is, in the first place, in the incorporeal creation, namely of the supernal spirits or of holy minds always attending to heavenly things alone, whose conversation is the working of the divine goodness; in the second place, in the incorporeal creation of the superior things, which are certain nourishments of the inferior, whose rational disposition is also the wonderful working of the divine wisdom. For the hyacinth [1197C] is of the colour of the air, and therefore the *hands* of the beloved are called *full of hyacinths,* because the works of the Creator are proclaimed rather in the comeliness and adornment of heavenly things.

Further, if you look to the members of the head, *his hands are turned and of gold and full of hyacinths,* because, intent on good works and always hastening to enter into the rest to come, not slowly nor sluggishly, but swiftly and zealously, they run the way of the commandments; whatever they discern crooked and unequal in their manners they drive out with the turning of right working, and make it smooth, and render it perfect in the eyes of their Maker, yet with him as the craftsman; the cause of which effectiveness is the understanding in them, answering to the gold of wisdom divinely conferred, and always attending with the sweetness of the contemplative life to the felicities of heavenly goods.

[1197D]And they are therefore called his *hands,* because whatever good they do is not of their own virtue but of the grace of God, whose also is this, that they are *turned* by the perfection of manners, that they are *of gold* by the clearness of the understanding, that they are *full of hyacinths* by the sublimity of the angelic conversation.

Truly, in the consideration of this member also a twofold colour, proclaimed with special praise, appears in the body of the beloved, because in this, that the *hands* are *turned,* there is the sign of labour or of passion, by which they have been turned from all superfluity and inequality of manners into a vessel *196* of honour and worthy to be looked upon by the divine eyes. But in that they are *of gold and full of hyacinths,* the shining virtue of the celibate life is designated, by which they glow with the clearness of chastity and modesty; and in this manner in this part also the beloved is sung *white and ruddy.*

[1198A] *His belly is of ivory, set with sapphires:* If we look distinctly upon the person of the beloved himself, we accept not absurdly that his humanity is designated by the name of the belly, because the belly is the frailer part of the body, and that humanity experienced all the frailty of the human body except sin. But what is it that this *belly* is called *of ivory*? For ivory is the bone of the elephant, which animal is held to be most chaste: and what is more chaste than the humanity of Christ, which was assumed from the most chaste Virgin without any work of man, through the sole operation of the Holy Spirit? The *belly,* then, of the beloved is *of ivory,* because, whereas all men are conceived in iniquities and sins, he alone in his humanity was conceived singularly without sin of the most chaste Virgin, as has been said [1198B].

Further, what is it that this belly is recorded to be *set with sapphires*? For the sapphire is of the colour of the sky; and the belly of the beloved is said to be set with sapphires because, although from his earthly humanity he bore hunger, thirst, weariness and the other things which he could suffer according as he was man, yet while suffering he showed heavenly miracles from his divinity by working them.

And if we consider the multitude of the faithful who cleave to that person as members to a head through faith, which for its greater frailty can be compared to a belly: for there are in the Church some given to the bond of marriage, there are some wonderfully continent in the frail sex: but *they that have wives, as though they had none [n: (I Cor. VII)]*, can rightly be called a *belly of ivory* [1198C], because, although outwardly they seem frail, yet they are strong through the comeliness of a holy and chaste life. Whence also of holy married persons it is written that *they were walking in all the commandments and justifications of the Lord without blame [n: (Luc. III)]*: for this is for the belly to be set with sapphires, because amid the cares of worldly things, which the frailty of marriage requires, they shine forth by the devout observance of the heavenly precepts. Further, those wonderfully continent in the frail sex show in this a more praiseworthy keeping of their purpose, because, though they are weak and frail in body, yet they rise bravely by virtue of soul to the merits of holiness. Whence in these also a *belly of ivory* is spoken of, because the virtue [1198D] of chastity is more proclaimed in the frail sex.

And it is recorded *set with sapphires,* because the virtue of chastity is adorned also with the ornaments of the other virtues: for otherwise chastity of body cannot be a virtue, unless *197* it be adorned with chastity of mind, which consists in humility, patience, sobriety and contempt of the worldly life, and the observance of the other heavenly precepts. For, that it may be of the body of this beloved, the *belly of ivory* ought to be *set with sapphires,* because chastity of body profits nothing without good work, nor good work without chastity (GREGORIUS). » Because, then, the frail sex holds the virtue of chastity with a strong mind, the *belly* of the beloved is *of ivory*; but because it also strives after other virtues for heavenly glory, it is *set with sapphires,* because in [1199A] the ivory belly the colour of chastity gleams, in the sapphire, although sky-coloured, yet for the labour of the virtues the colour of the passion is ruddy. Chastity itself also, which outwardly is white in a pure body, inwardly is ruddy through the martyrdom of the mind: for to fight against the natural motion of the flesh is assuredly a certain suffering of the mind.

Truly the singing of the present portion has regard in no small measure to the rule of your profession, most beloved sisters, because, since you are in truth of the body of this beloved, you can be a most comely member of his of this kind. For when, in so frail a little vessel of your body, you shine with the white garment of chastity and modesty, assuredly you can be called and be the *belly* of this beloved *of ivory*; but because together with that chastity you shine also with other adornments of virtues, you assuredly [1199B] gleam *set with sapphires.* But you must take very great heed lest you think you can please God by chastity alone, if you have not other good works, and therefore you ought always to join both chastity to good work and good work to chastity, that you may be able to please God perfectly.

VERSE 15.-- *His legs are pillars of marble, that are set upon bases of gold. His form is as of Libanus, chosen as the cedars.*

In the eighth place, as it were, the attentive beholder of the beloved touches the eighth string of the ten-stringed psaltery, as though rendering that symphony διαπασον, whose property it is to have the same voice in the eighth as in the first. For, whereas above, on the first string, it sounded that the head of the beloved is the finest gold, now she says that the *legs* are set *upon bases [1199C] of gold,* so that the same voice may be low and high, sounding the highest and the lowest golden adornment of the beloved. But it is to be considered what are the legs of this beloved, or why they are compared to pillars of marble built upon bases of gold.

Since God in his nature is immovable and unchangeable, inasmuch as he is not in a place, for he fills and contains all things, while he himself is contained by nothing, who is always wholly everywhere; and since he is in a place who is moved from place to place, but God is not moved, and therefore is not in a place, because immovable, yet he is said to be moved, *198* not in himself, but in those whom he moves by his nod and will through the power that disposes all things, whether according to justice or according to mercy, *for he hath mercy on whom he will, and whom he will he hardeneth* [1199D] [n: (Rom. IX)], of which the one is of mercy, the other of justice. Since, therefore, he goes before all his creation as it were by this twin way of his disposition, namely justice and mercy, justice and mercy can rightly be called his legs: *for all his ways,* as the Psalmist says, *are mercy and truth [n: (Psal. XXIV)]*. But truth and justice are the same: for what is true is just; and what is just is true.
