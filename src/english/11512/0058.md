## *172* CHAPTER V. (cont.)

But why are these *legs* called *pillars of marble,* unless because they are straight and strong? For in a pillar there is straightness, in marble hardness; for the justice of God is hidden, yet never unjust. For who can know why he chooses this man mercifully, and [1200A] repels that man judicially? Therefore the Apostle cries: *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and of the knowledge of God: how incomprehensible are his judgments, and unsearchable his ways! [n: (Rom. II.)]*

Further, what are the bases of gold upon which these pillars, which are understood as those legs, are set? Assuredly, if you weigh it rightly, they are his wisdom and goodness, because all things that he makes he orders and disposes wisely and well: they are *bases,* namely, because they contain and sustain all things, *of gold,* because they shine forth wonderfully in the very effecting of things. The *legs,* then, of the beloved, if you attend to the head itself, are, as has been said, mercy and truth, which, set *upon bases,* that is, the feet of his wisdom and goodness, either stand or walk, according to the measure and quality [1200B] of the things that have been made: for all things that have been made are either eternal or temporal. And they stand in those things which God himself has foreordained for his own to abide eternally according to grace and mercy; they walk in those things which he either confers or takes away temporally, whether mercifully or judicially.

Further, if you look to the members of that head, what do you think are to be called the legs of this beloved? In that ancient people of God, in which alone God was then known, God set chiefly two dignities, by which that people might be fortified and adorned, namely the kingship and the priesthood [sic: *secerdotium*]; outwardly indeed it was armed with royal strength against those who, at the devil's prompting, laboured to destroy the purpose of God, which he held for the salvation of the whole world, and which he showed beforehand to that same people [1200C] in type, so that in their whole commonwealth, with peace and honour, they might run beforehand, in figure, through the business of the divine promise that was faithfully to be fulfilled. Further, inwardly it was instructed by the priestly office, to understand prudently through figure and to handle reverently that same business, so that it might labour toward that purpose of God, one day to be fulfilled, by faith and love, in hope of the salvation to come.

*199* In which dignities, namely, lay so much of the strength of that same people, that afterwards, when God, angered at that same people sinning, threatened many punishments, at last, as it did not correct itself at all, he set forth this final punishment, that, the kingship and the priesthood being taken away and destroyed, all their honour and religion should depart utterly. For it is said thus through Jeremiah [1200D] the prophet: *The Lord hath delivered up to oblivion in Sion the festival and the sabbath; and to reproach, into the indignation of his wrath, the king and the priest [n: (Thren. II)]*. For there to be no king and priest, this was for all the rite of the ceremonies to have failed.

Does not holy Church also in this time, in which that promise is now fulfilled, stand by two dignities of this kind? For the power of royal judgment fights outwardly with the material sword against the unbelieving and incredulous, and the majesty of the priestly office fights inwardly also against the same with the spiritual sword. See, when the Lord, for defending the universal salvation of this new commonwealth which now is, permitted the sword, and heard that two were ready, he answered that it was enough [1201A] [n: (Luc II)], because he established in the Church two peremptory sentences, one by the material sword, the other by the spiritual sword. For outwardly this is brought about by the fear of the kingdom, that the Church may serve God with peace and honour; inwardly she is instructed by the priesthood, that she may know how to serve God with reverence and love. What then? Since these two dignities are to the Church of God, which is to be propagated with all religion, as it were a certain strength and, if I may rightly say so, the strongest support of the whole body of this beloved, can not the kingship and the priesthood rightly be called the legs of that same beloved?

And these, for their strength and eminence, are called *pillars of marble,* since they too are raised up by virtue and power, and in them the whole house of holy Church is sustained. And what are the bases [1201B] of gold upon which these pillars are built? Plainly it is fitting that in that kingship or priesthood, because their cause ought to be that of common utility and comeliness, a great force of reason and virtue of discretion should shine forth, so that in all their dispensation and judgment there may always be reason and discretion. But since these ought to proceed from a great clearness of understanding and wisdom, can they not rightly be called the golden bases of these pillars?

These legs, then, of this beloved are pillars of marble placed upon bases of gold, because in holy Church the kingship and the priesthood flourish by the reason and discretion of dispensation and of judgment.

To the song of this eighth string also that prelude answers, set beside the entry of the ten-stringed song itself, by which the beloved is sung white and [1201C] ruddy, when this part of the kingship so acts, with labour and peril of life, that in it this beloved may be proclaimed ruddy: further, *200* the other part, of the priesthood, may so act in peace and tranquillity [sic: *tranquil itate*] that in it the beloved may be pronounced white, in holiness and chastity, in conversation, in the observance and preaching of the commandments of God. *His form is as of Libanus, chosen as the cedars.* As, she says, mount Libanus is more excellent than the other mountains, in comeliness and beauty, and in the wonderful order of the trees set there, so my beloved is more eminent than all the saints, and far more beautiful, in power of virtues, in wisdom also and knowledge, and in all comeliness of worldly actions, and in all fulness of divine graces. *For God doth not give him the Spirit by [1201D] measure [n: (Joan. III)]*. But *in him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead bodily [n: (Coloss. II)]*; and of this his fulness the saints receive *grace for grace [n: (Joan. I)]*; and what he himself has universally, these obtain particularly, and this not always according to each one's will, because that same particle of grace in them is at times ready, and at times lies sluggishly as though asleep, which must be stirred up not without some labour Whence Paul says to Timothy: *For which cause,* he says, *I admonish thee that thou stir up the grace which is in thee by the imposition of my hands [n: (II Tim. I)]*. And it is stirred up in many ways, but most of all by praying and by singing psalms; for example, when the king of Israel said to Elisha: *Why hath God gathered these three kings together, to deliver them into the hands [1202A] of Moab?* he answered: *As the Lord liveth, in whose sight I stand, that unless I reverenced the face of Josaphat king of Juda, I would not indeed have hearkened, nor looked; but now bring me a minstrel: and it came to pass, while the minstrel was playing, the hand of the Lord came upon him,* and the rest. [n: (IV Reg. III.)]

Except, then, this beloved, of whom it is written: *He upon whom thou shalt see the Spirit descending and remaining upon him, he it is that baptizeth in the Spirit [n: (Joan. I)]*: all the other saints receive a divided or particular grace of the Holy Spirit as it pleases the Holy Spirit himself, who breatheth *where* and when *he will [n: (Joan. III)]*. Rightly, therefore, is the *form* of this beloved said to be *as of Libanus,* because he is *beautiful in form above the sons of men [n: (Psal. XLIV)]*, and no form can be compared to his form, no [1202B] virtue to his virtue, no grace to his grace.

*Chosen as the cedars:* why are the cedars chosen before the other trees of Libanus, unless because they have certain special virtues of their nature? For the cedar is incorruptible, it is fragrant, it is hostile to serpents; and who could be without corruption, save only this beloved himself, *who doeth not sin, neither was guile found in his mouth? [n: (I Petr. II.)]* No one was ever able to be so holy, so innocent, that he did not contract something of corruption, since it is written that God found iniquity even in his angels [n: (Job IV)]. For sin is a certain rottenness, a certain corruption, and though in one it is greater, in another less, yet in no one, however very holy, very just, [1202C] can it not be, though it be even the very least; but in this beloved alone no stain at all of rottenness or corruption of this kind, *201* not even the slightest, could exist.

Well, therefore, is he called *chosen as the cedars,* because this one has specially by nature what has been granted to none of the saints by grace. These things indeed are to be said of the head alone.

But if you look to the body, which, joined to the head, is one according to incorporation, because it is incorporated to that head through his grace, fittingly is his form compared to Libanus, when this whole beloved is attended to distinctly in himself and in his body. For what can be considered in the face or form of the Church [1202D] but the presence of the divinity in the performing of the sacraments, in the utterance of the divine oracles, in the disposing and ordering of fitting and reasonable causes? And who is this Libanus, to which the form of this beloved in his whole body is likened? If you attend to the thing itself as it is, Libanus is a mountain of Phoenicia, abounding in cedars both rooted in it and stretched up on high: and as this mountain is adorned with a multitude of cedars, and thereby offers great beauty of itself to those who behold it, so this beloved, adorned in his body with the multitude of his elect who are rooted in him by faith and cleave to him in charity, assuredly offers to the whole world a wonderful spectacle of holiness and comeliness.

[1203A]But if you understand it metaphorically, this Libanus is that of which the prophetic word cries: *Open,* it says, *Libanus, thy gates, and let fire devour thy cedars [n: (Zach. II)]*. For the temple of God, which had been built in that earthly Jerusalem and had boards within round about of the woods of Libanus, of cedar woods, was therefore called by the name of Libanus, because in it was the Holy of holies, which was a certain glory and an eminent beauty not only of that city, but also of the whole people itself, spread through many borders. The form, then, of the beloved is as the form of Libanus, because, as this temple was formerly the head and singular monument of religion to that ancient people of God, so now the assembly of the Church, where Christ is honoured in his body, is a certain head [1203B] and a certain eminence of holiness to all the Christian people, because in it is regarded a certain way and arrival at the sublimity of that highest good and at the eternity of the salvation to come. Still now, to the assembly, as it were to a certain mountain of God, the faithful people runs daily, to look forth in hope upon that land of promise which God will give to his elect, in which is the temple of that supernal Jerusalem, which the Holy of holies himself inhabits, in which the saints are multiplied like the cedars of Libanus, made incorruptible by participation in that eternal beatitude and in the highest good, which is God.

*Chosen as the cedars:* how is this beloved, in his body, that is, the *202* Church, said to be chosen [1203C] as the cedars? If you attend to the natures of the cedar which we recounted above, namely that it is incorruptible, that it is fragrant, that it puts serpents to flight, assuredly you can weigh how great is the utility of the unity of holy Church, and how much it is to be chosen by every soul, and how much it is to be preferred to all other things. For there incorruption of soul is attained, the odour of virtues is perceived, vices and sins, as venomous serpents, are put to flight. What is more excellent than this thing? Rightly, therefore, is Christ in his body said to be *chosen as the cedars,* because in all its action or disposition, canonical or dispensatory, he is the cause of all spiritual effecting.
