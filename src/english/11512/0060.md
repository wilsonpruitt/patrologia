## CHAPTER VI.

* 206* VERSE 1.-- *My beloved is gone down into his garden, to the little bed of spices, that he may feed in the gardens, and gather lilies.*

What indeed was it to seek the beloved, but to ascend to him through the power of contemplation? for since in the hearts of the saints *faith worketh by love [n: (Gal. V)]*, and love always draws the mind to the things above, to work good is therefore to descend to God in mind. But since all good is from God, without [1207A] whom nothing of good exists, for anyone to be helped by him in good is surely his descending to him; for it is written in Exodus that Moses ascended into the mountain, and the Lord descended to him [n: (Exod. XIX)]. For as much as each one ascends to God in thought, and in action by seeking him, so much does God descend to him, whether by opening his mysteries to him through the understanding, or by conferring the help of his grace through strength of soul. For where anyone ascends to God by free will, in will and in work, there God descends to him through helping grace, by his mercy and condescension

Whence also here the beloved fittingly answered those desiring to seek the beloved, that *my beloved is gone down into his [1207B] garden:* as if she said: Since you desire to know whither my beloved is gone, or whither he has turned aside, behold, I tell thee that *my beloved is gone down into his garden.* What is the garden of the beloved, but either holy Church or any holy soul sprouting with the diverse beauty of the virtues? into which the beloved descends, because to it as it sprouts with good acts he bestows the help of his grace. But after he has gone down into the garden, whither has he turned himself? *to the little bed,* she says, *of spices.* What is the *little bed of spices,* *207* but a mind planted with various kinds of sweetly fragrant virtues, or sprinkled with the repute of diverse graces as with a good odour? for those who can say with the Apostle: *We are the good odour of Christ unto God in every place [n: (II Cor. II)]*, these surely [1207C] in the garden of the Lord, that is, in holy Church, each one in his own manner can be called a *little bed of spices,* because they spread on the minds of their neighbours the odour of good repute through the examples of good works.

But why does she say not "bed" but, by diminution, *little bed,* unless because the holy mind which shines with the sublimity of the virtues ought to be little in its own eyes and greatly to show that *the Lord is high and looketh on the low things and will save the humble of spirit? [n: (Psal. CXII, XXXIII.)]* To this *little bed,* then, the beloved, going down into his garden, turns aside, because to the holy mind, through increases of holiness and knowledge, he shows himself easy and as it were tractable. For every pursuit seems difficult to the beginner, easy to the one grown used to it: and to one entering the way of the commandments of God, or the knowledge of Holy Scripture, [1207D] the gate seems narrow and the entry difficult, but to one who wears it by long exercise, and as it were thrusts back frequent obstacles, the way of holiness and understanding is opened broad and spacious, and therefore easy ingress and egress thereafter lie open.

She adds, however, the cause why he descended: *That he may feed,* she says, *in the gardens, and gather lilies:* he feeds in the gardens because he delights in that odour which is spread from the little bed of spiritual spices, that is, of good works. For he himself says in the Gospel: *My meat is to do the will of my Father [n: (Joan. IV)]*; for the will of the Father is that all men be saved [n: (I Tim. II)]. Further, that men desire salvation and attain to it is his work. And what things the Father works, these same the Son also works, because what [1208A] the Father wills, the Son also wills, and one is the will and operation of both. And he gathers lilies, because he chooses rather those who are white with integrity and cleanness of heart and body, or takes up those perfect in faith and work to be rewarded in eternal beatitude.

VERSE 2.-- *I to my beloved, and my beloved to me, who feedeth among the lilies.*

My beloved, she says, had above commanded me, saying: *Arise, open to me, my sister, my love, my dove, my undefiled,* and I, obeying him, *opened the bolt of my door [n: (Cant. V)]*, by preaching him to all and showing reasonably how great and of what kind he is. But what did my beloved to these things? truly he repays the return of my obedience, chanting to me, rejoicing, the praise of his condescension, beatifying not my grace but [1208B] his own in me. For his was it that I willed, that I was able, that I accomplished, and therefore he himself proclaims his gifts in me, that to me and to all obedient to his grace *208* he may show of how great merit before God is a life obedient to holiness and uprightness.

But of what kind is the beloved? *Who feedeth,* she says, *among the lilies,* that is, he delights in clean thought and working, not that he grows into anything by accident, who never receives any increase or loss. Since he remains always unchangeable in one and the same essence and property, but that, by some manner of speech transferred from the creature, the affection of his goodness is expressed toward those [1208C] whom he has deigned to count worthy of his love and grace. For he is said to feed when, in the mind of anyone which he inhabits by his grace, the progress of the virtues is increased.

With what song, then, or in what mode of song, did the voice of this beloved sound to me? with a sweet song surely, a song to be attended to not moderately, in an eight-stringed mode indeed, but on the several strings, because of the force of each sound, notably disposed with its own theorem in this manner.

VERSE 3.-- *Thou art beautiful, my love, sweet and comely as Jerusalem: terrible as an army of a camp set in array.*

Here it is pleasing to behold, as a spectacle, how the modulation of the present song runs through eight parts as through eight voices, so that the property of that symphony which [1208D] musicians call διαπασον may here be attended to, whose last voice is the same as the first, as can be seen also in the disposition of these parts, where, while the first part is set down in this manner, *terrible as an army of a camp set in array,* the last part is nonetheless ended in the same manner, namely *terrible as an army of a camp set in array.*

But the several parts demand several reasons, which are distinguished in this manner. The first is, *thou art beautiful, my love, sweet and comely as Jerusalem, terrible as an army of a camp set in array.*

*Thou art beautiful,* he says, *my love, sweet and comely as Jerusalem: beautiful,* in intention, *sweet* in speech, *comely* in working, and therefore to be joined to me in the inviolable covenant of holy friendship. And how do this [1209A] beauty, or sweetness, or comeliness appear? *As Jerusalem.* And what is Jerusalem interpreted, but *vision of peace?* Now three qualities of this vision are denoted under the name of Jerusalem: one earthly, another spiritual, the third heavenly [sic: *coelestus*]; and the middle one indeed is constituted after the likeness of the extremes, while yet it is itself the surpassing of the first and the preparation of the last. And how is the middle one constituted after the likeness of the extremes? in the mind of each one are the virtues, as the citizens and household of the soul, to which are opposed vices and sins, as *209* the most bitter enemies of that soul. And a likeness is given to it from the vision of earthly peace, that as a city, its enemies conquered and driven out, flourishes in peace, rejoices and prospers, so the mind, vices and sins overcome, rests sweetly in the delight [1209B] of the virtues and grows peaceably in the grace of God, the flesh subject to the spirit, and is raised the more to the contemplation of the Godhead, the less it is assailed by earthly desires.

Further, it receives the other likeness from the vision of peace above, but not wholly comparable, because this is in a riddle, but that in truth, so that as there the soul, free from all misery and labour, beholds God continually with contemplative charity, so here the mind, freed from every assault of the flesh, advances by daily increase to the sweetness of that same contemplative life. Whence this middle one rightly, as has been said, can be the preparation of that last and eternal one, because through this one that one is reached: further, it is the surpassing of the first, because although there is some likeness in them in reason, [1209C] yet there is no comparison in dignity, since that is the momentary reception of worldly peace, this the eternal beholding of the peace above, which here indeed begins but there is perfected.

To which, then, of these three visions of peace is the beauty of the beloved compared? If we say to the first, what comparison will there be of flesh to spirit, of light to darkness, of time to eternity? But if we say to the last, what comparison is there of that which is still held with fear, and of that which, free and secure from all fear, is granted without end? To the middle one, then, it can be compared, so that this beauty, this sweetness, this comeliness are gathered from the vision of inward peace, and these things arise there, advance and abide, where no delights [1209D] of temporal things weary the soul, but, the fight of flesh and Spirit ceasing, according to the Apostle: *The peace of Christ which passeth all understanding keep the hearts and understandings [n: (Philip. IV)]* [var: Vulg. *pax Dei*, "the peace of God"], of one resting in the love of the contemplative life. *Thou art beautiful,* then, *beloved, sweet and comely as Jerusalem,* like the vision of a pacified mind, which no injury of disquiet, no trouble of adversity disturbs in the joyfulness of holy contemplation.

And what effect does the joyfulness of this contemplation impart? surely a great one, because it kindles the whole mind in the charity of God and neighbour, and to the malignant spirits, that is, the enemies of its soul, for she adds, *terrible as an army of a camp set in array;* for the army of a camp, when it is so set in array *210* that, [1210A] from the unanimity and concord of the host disposed together, no access of breaking in can open to the enemy, then surely shows itself terrible to that enemy. But where access of breaking in is granted to the enemy through the disorder of the host, there certainly it seems in no way terrible.

Holy Church, therefore, or the soul beloved of God, when it sets the battle line of spiritual combat against the malignant spirits, must of utmost necessity be found always united and bound close through charity, and never broken through by discord, because, even if it should have whatever goods, if charity be lacking, a place is opened in the line through the evil of discord, whence the enemy may be able to enter to strike. For the ancient enemy fears neither chastity, nor abstinence, nor [1210B] the distribution of earthly things, if they should be without charity, because he himself is not pressed by flesh, so that either he should be undone by its lust, or be urged by its necessity in taking food, or in acquiring the supports of riches.

But he greatly fears true charity, that is, humble love, which the faithful bestow on one another in turn: and he envies their concord exceedingly, because they hold this on earth which he, being unwilling to hold it, lost in heaven.

Well, therefore, is it said *terrible as an army of a camp set in array,* because the malignant spirits fear the multitude of the elect the more, the more they see them united and massed together against themselves through the concord of charity.
