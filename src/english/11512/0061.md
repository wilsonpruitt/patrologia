## CHAPTER VI. (cont.)

The second and third parts are contained in this verse. He says, therefore, *turn away thy eyes from me, for they have made me flee away;* because, he says, the building of thy mind rises honourably and profitably from the foundation of fraternal charity within Jerusalem, that is, *the vision of peace,* showing its beauty far off, in thought, speech and work; this truly is the summit and the top of that same building, and as it were a certain conclusion, that it be finished in one cubit, namely by setting thy whole intention on the love of God alone: whence thou hast this also, O love, still more abundantly [1210D] from my grace, that thou canst penetrate with the subtlety of a purged mind something of my power and brightness. Yet however much thou strivest, however much thou puttest forward the sharpness of thy soul, thy labour is frustrated, because it is impossible by any wit or reason to define the incomprehensible and uncircumscribed.

Wherefore *turn away thy eyes from me,* that is, do not wish to know perfectly the substance of my Godhead, which is granted to no one in this life, but rather turn back to thyself, and be mindful of human frailty: *211* for thy eyes, that is, the senses of thy mind, fixed too much on me, have themselves made me flee away, because the more thou stretchest toward me, the more thou wilt prove me incomprehensible, according to what is written: *Man shall approach to a deep heart and God shall be exalted* [1211A] [n: (Psal. LXIII)]: for the higher and more subtly thou approachest me by examining the property of my substance, the more sublimely do I fly away, by exceeding all the consideration and subtlety of thy sense. Wherefore *turn away thy eyes from me,* because thy senses are indeed very keen in the contemplation of the mind, by the largess of my grace, but they fail utterly in the consideration of me, as I flee away for the depth of the matter. But since so great is the grace of thy eyes, what too is the grace of the hair?

*Thy hair,* he says, *is as a flock of goats which have appeared from Galaad.* A little above, the praise of the beloved was sung in the same modes, which he therefore now repeats, either to show that she ought to have the same comeliness and beauty of bearing and of soul in the preaching of the word [1211B] of God, for the instruction of the hearers, which she had in the solitude of contemplation for her own profit; or because holy Church, whether universal or particular, is more commendable in her twin order from which she is constituted, namely of those set over and of subjects, in whom all her beauty, grace and honour is considered, from the life and doctrine of the former, and from the obedience and reverence for discipline of the latter.

For as the hairs, springing from the head, cleave to the head, so subjects cleave to the prelates who begot them by the word of life, in faith and morals; as it were going up from Galaad, because, while at their teaching they bear the heap of testimony, that is, of Holy Scripture, they lift their life up to heaven by living well.

[1211C]VERSE 5.-- *Thy teeth are as a flock of sheep which have come up from the washing, all with twin offspring, and there is none barren among them.*

Here the fourth part is touched. What indeed is understood by the *teeth* but the doctors, carrying the grossness of the letter over into the subtlety of the spiritual sense, and white with simplicity, as washed in the washing of holy compunction, whence they themselves also are fruitful with twin charity, or instructed in the double testament, and render those subject to them fruitful with the same charity and the same testament, so instructing them that none among them, that is, those set over or subjects, grows sluggish with barrenness of the virtues.

But the hairs of the particular soul, born from the principal part of the mind as from the *212* head, are the [1211D] subtleties of the thoughts, attending to the heap of divine sentences: further, the *teeth* of that same mind, as it handles and handles again, are the vehement applications with zeal, breaking the hard and strong propositions or oppositions of the Scriptures, and unfolding very many of their wrappings. In these there is no barrenness, because the more zealously and the longer they apply the soul to the Scriptures, the more vehemently are they pricked to the love of God and neighbour, and the more fully do they attain the knowledge of each Testament, or, keeping the precept, they add the counsel over it: for the precept is not to seize what is another's, the counsel to give away one's own.

These surely are the *twin offspring,* by which the soul is made fruitful, namely the love of God and of neighbour, the knowledge [1212A] of the Old and New Testament, the observance of the precept with the adding of the counsel. By the intent and zeal of these, surely, all inward barrenness is driven off, so that deservedly in thought, working, preaching the beloved is pronounced *beautiful, sweet and comely.*

VERSE 6.-- *As the rind of a pomegranate, so are thy cheeks, besides thy hidden things.*

If we refer it universally to the whole Church, her *cheeks* can be called those who live spiritually in her, who, while shining in memorable and wonderful words and deeds, gleam also with stupendous miracles, appear venerable as it were in the face of the Church; for the beauty of cheeks is considered in their redness. But if so great is the glory of her comeliness in those things which appear, what pure glory of hers will there be in those things which do not yet appear? of which it is written: that [1212B] *neither eye hath seen, nor ear heard, what things God hath prepared for those who love him [n: (I Cor. II)]*.

The *cheeks,* then, of the Church are compared *to the rind of a pomegranate,* but her hidden things, because they are ineffable, are therefore also incomparable.

But if we refer it specially to the honour of the holy soul, very often its inward beauty is declared by certain outward signs; for while the modesty of virginal chastity blushes in the face, that is shown outwardly which is held inwardly in the soul. Her *cheeks,* then, are *as the rind of a pomegranate besides her hidden things,* because outwardly indeed she puts forth from bashfulness a certain sign of holiness, but how much that holiness itself flourishes in the hidden place of her mind lies open to him alone [1212C] from whom nothing can be hidden. And this of the cheeks.

* 213* VERSE 7.-- *There are threescore queens and fourscore concubines, and of young maidens there is no number.*

The sixth and seventh parts are described in the present verse. For since in the foregoing he had proclaimed the beauty of the Church by parts, in the prelates and subjects and those living spiritually, who are designated by the hair, teeth and cheeks, now he follows up with renowned praise her comeliness, perfect as a whole, in the unity of faith: for although holy Church in some manner seem diverse by the diversity of merits, yet because of one and the same faith she is not found diverse; for three qualities of those living in her can be considered, because there are in her both the perfect and the less perfect and the imperfect: the perfect indeed [1212D] are those who have left all their things, and have cleaved to God alone with their whole mind; the less perfect, those who have not indeed left all their things, but out of those things which they can have are busy to minister to God in his members; the imperfect, those who in faith indeed cleave to the higher ones, but, as it were in a still tender manner of life, have not yet attained the good of perfect discretion.

Whence here not unfittingly the sum of three numbers is brought in? namely of six, of eight and of ten. For ten, multiplied by six, rises to sixty, while eight, led through ten, makes eighty. Six, moreover, is called perfect by arithmetic law, because when with all its parts, which are three, two, and one, [1213A] for its half part is three, its third two, its sixth one, it is gathered together, it is restored into the prior sum without any increase or decrease. Further, eight is named diminished, because since its half part is four, its fourth two, its eighth one, when these same parts are gathered together seven is made, less, that is, in quantity than the prior sum. And ten, which intimates the Decalogue of the law, because it consists of seven and three, the sevenfold grace of the Holy Spirit and the faith of the holy Trinity confession, which numbers show who are perfect, who imperfect. For the Holy Spirit himself shows in the effects of his gifts who love God perfectly, who less, who as yet have neither the fear nor the love of God.

[1213B]For there are two things which make us wholly subject to God, namely fear and love: his power indeed and his wisdom chiefly *214* strike fear, when we know that he whom we know to exist is both able to punish all things he will, and that nothing is hidden from him; but his kindness [sic: *begninitas*] pertains specially to love, that we may love most him whom we hold most kind. From which also it is certain that he wills to punish impiety, because the more equity pleases him, the more iniquity displeases him, as it is written: *Thou hast loved justice and hated iniquity [n: (Psal. XLIV)]*.

By the number sixty, therefore, which is made by six, perfect, multiplied by ten, the life of the perfect is expressed, who would not perfectly observe the divine precepts [1213C] unless they had first the initial fear, afterwards the filial love that shuts out that very fear. And such souls are well named *queens,* because, divine grace working with them, they know how and are able to rule themselves by reason and understanding, when they discern profit, glory, and honour in the performing of good, and on the other hand loss, misery, and horror in the committing of evil, and therefore they strongly strive after good and strongly turn aside from evil.

But by the number eighty, which is completed by ten led through eight, which is called diminished, the life of the less perfect is denoted, who observe indeed the divine precepts, but do not love God so perfectly that they are able to leave all earthly things. And these souls are designated by the name of *concubines,* because, [1213D] although they are joined to God through good works, yet they have not yet obtained that divine right of which it is said, *He who cleaveth to God is made one Spirit with him [n: (I Cor. VI)]*: for this is the full writing of the spiritual dowry, that those who renounce the world utterly are never separated from the divine fellowship. But of the *young maidens* the *number* is not defined, because infinite is the number of the imperfect, who, set as yet under the first-fruits of faith, need the instruction of their elders for conceiving the fear of God, through which they may at some time advance to his love.

Among whom there are some concerning whom very often the estimate is doubtful, since not all belong to the lot of the elect, [1214A] according to the voice of the Lord which says: *Many are called but few chosen [n: (Matth. XX)]*.

VERSE 8.-- *One is my dove, my perfect one: she is the only one to her mother, the chosen of her that bore her. The daughters saw her and declared her most blessed: the queens and concubines praised her.* [var: Vulg. *matris suae*, "of her mother"]

But since the qualities of merits in the Church are so diverse, is she therefore said to be diverse? by no means; but as follows, *one is my dove, my perfect one, she is the only one to her mother, the chosen of her that bore her.* Whence *one?* namely *215* because of the one and the same receiving of faith and of the sacraments, and because of the worship of one God, and a like rite of religion: *for one is the faith,* as the Apostle says, *one baptism, one God and Father of all, who is above all [1214B] in us all [n: (Ephes. IV)]*.
