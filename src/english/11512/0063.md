## CHAPTER VI. (cont.)

But what, someone will say, does this fourth member, as it were the fourth voice of the δια τεσσερον symphony described above, pertain to the sacraments: *terrible as an army of a camp set in array?* Plainly, I say, this too pertains well enough, and this is established from the effects of the sacraments themselves, for what [1218B] is more terrible to the enemies of the soul, that is, to the malignant spirits, than the powers of the sacraments, by which their power is weakened, and all their might and daring put to flight?

For where any sacrament is so performed that the thing of the sacrament also can be present, that is, in the peace and unity of the Church, outside which there is no place for the saving sacraments, there surely the effect of that same sacrament is very *terrible* and unbearable to the evil spirits themselves, whether this be in baptism, or in the Lord's body, or in the distribution of orders, or in the ordering of whatever things are performed by ecclesiastical institutions; for this [1218C] comparison seems to intimate this, by which it is said *terrible as an army of a camp set in array:* because as a well-bound battle line, and packed close in order, suffers no breaking in upon itself, so the Church, bound together by the sacrament of unity, is scattered by no incursion of the malignant spirits. But the heretics who have cut themselves off from this unity, because they are not contained in this battle line, being set outside, are able to repel none of the assaults of those spirits, since indeed the sacraments of the Church which they seem to have, they have not in reality, but only in appearance. Yet those same sacraments, rightly celebrated by them, if the faithful receive them, they receive both *220* the sacrament and the thing of the sacrament, that is, the power working within: while the heretics themselves are proved to take the sacrament indeed, but not the thing of the sacrament, [1218D] and therefore their gathering is not terrible as an army of a camp set in array, because as far as they are concerned the sacraments do not work this, which confer no remedies on them.

VERSE 10.-- *I went down into the garden of nuts to see the fruits of the valleys, and to look if the vineyards had flourished, and the pomegranates budded.* [var: Vulg. *floruisset vinea*, "the vineyard had flourished"]

After the beloved has followed up with gracious praise the beauty of the holy beloved, namely the Church, whose praise however is from him who by his grace presented her to himself *not having spot or wrinkle [n: (Ephes. V)]*, he shows in what follows that, because of the unbelief of the Jewish people, she earned the gift and merit of faith, as the Apostle says: *By their offence salvation to the Gentiles, and their offence the delight [1219A] of the world, and their diminution the riches of the Gentiles [n: (Rom. XI)]* [var: Vulg. *divitiae mundi*, "the riches of the world"]. For he who extolled the one in the manner of admiration, in the same manner presses down the other, because while the one believed and received the word of God, the other would neither believe nor receive that word: whence it is written of her in the Gospel: *And he wondered,* no doubt Jesus, *at their unbelief [n: (Marc. VI)]*. He says therefore, *I went down into the garden of nuts.* What was his going down, but to come down from the height of his Godhead into this world by being born, then to endure all the weak things of humanity? Were not all these things, fulfilled in their order, a certain descent? And where were these descents celebrated? Was it not in Judaea? In short, the Lord himself says: *I was not sent but to the sheep that are lost of the house of Israel [n: (Matth. XV)]*, for this [1219B] is what he says here, *I went down into the garden of nuts;* for the nation of the Jews can, not unfittingly, be called the garden of nuts: for as in a nut the kernel is covered under the rind and shell, so among the Jews the sweetness of spiritual understanding is hidden under the hardness of the literal surface. That nation, then, is called *the garden of nuts,* because although it is called a *garden* because of the profit of the legal precepts and observances, which flourished in their time and profited their observers up to the time of grace, yet all the future sacraments of the Church lay hidden in it under a figure, as under the rind of a nut. Into this garden, therefore, the Lord went down, because first of all he showed the presence of his flesh to the Jewish people by preaching and doing miracles.

[1219C]But why did he go down into this garden? *To see,* he says, *the fruits of the valleys,* that is, to know whether any had made fruit of faith and of injustice [cj: *injustitiae*; read *justitiae*, "of justice"], and, reason showing it, might understand that they cannot be justified by the *221* works of the law, but by the faith which I have set before those who believe through the grace of the New Testament. But I say "to know," not that I have not had this or anything else present to me from eternity, who am everywhere, and to whom all things past and future are present, but, speaking in human fashion, that I might show their works to the world, whether they were to be followed or not. But why do I say *of the valleys?* Because of the lowliness of the letter which they follow carnally, or because of the shadow of the law by which it is surrounded, [1219D] lest they be enlightened by the light of its truth.

*And to look if the vineyards had flourished,* that is, whether they had conceived at least some savour of spiritual understanding, whether they had applied themselves to the faith by some beginning of good work: for the houses of Israel are called vineyards, which are distinguished by twelve tribes; which, although they are called vineyards because of the distinction of the tribes, yet are called one vineyard, because of the one observance of the law; as, although there are now said to be many Churches because of the diversity of places, yet there is said to be one Church because of the unity of faith. And that the whole of that people is called the vineyard of the Lord the Prophet testifies. *The vineyard,* he says, *of the Lord is the house of Israel [n: (Isa. V)]*; of which the Psalmist also says: *Thou hast brought a vineyard out of Egypt [n: (Psal. LXIX)]*; but *when I looked that this vineyard [1220A] should flourish and make grapes, it made wild grapes [n: (Isa. V)]*. for after the Gentiles believed, they remained in unbelief.

*I went down* also *to look if the pomegranates had budded,* that is, that they might not only believe in me, but also have the will of suffering for me: but they would neither believe, nor suffer anything for me, but rather they killed me as I preached to them and showed the way of salvation.

In another way also it does not seem burdensome to expound this particle of the present song by the likeness of the nut, so that the reader may choose what in these he weighs better and more rightly. It does not seem absurd to take the garden of nuts as the whole of the human race, for in the nut three things are considered, rind, shell and kernel; [1220B] and in the human race man consists of flesh, bone and soul; for the matter of the flesh is as the rind, the bony strength is as the shell, further the sweetness of the soul is as the kernel: and as the kernel is covered by rind and shell, so the soul is clothed with flesh and bones. Whence Job, considering that man consists of these three, says: *Thou hast clothed me with skin and flesh, thou hast put me together with bones and sinews [n: (Job X)]*. And to show the third, which quickens and enlivens those two, that is, the soul, he adds: *Thou hast granted me life and mercy [n: (ibid.)]*. Into the garden of nuts, then, Christ went down when, by coming into this world, he took up human nature.

And why? *To see the fruits of the valleys* *222,* that is, to visit the humble having the fruit of justice, [1220C] and to choose them for himself, for his seeing is choosing, so that, coming humble, he might intimate that he takes up the humble and obedient, as it is written: *And he will save the humble of spirit [n: (Psal. XXXIII)]*. He went down also to look *if the vineyards had flourished, and the pomegranates budded,* that is, whether the faithful of the Church had begun the pursuit of living or teaching rightly, and were striving to persevere in his love even unto death, so that by bestowing his help on them he himself might be both inspirer and fellow worker, as he says, promising them: *Behold, I am with you all days even to the consummation of the world [n: (Matth. XXVIII)]*.

[1220D]VERSE 11.-- *I knew not: my soul troubled me, because of the chariots of Aminadab.*

The people of the Jews puts forward the cause of its ignorance and blindness, that since the law had been given which should justify and save them, and God had confirmed it by many signs and by the great authorities of the holy Fathers, it would be impossible for it to be made void by any novelty, since God is unchangeable, nor does he, in human fashion, say, command and institute anything at one time which at another time he forbids and destroys. For if, they say, that displeases in the future which now pleases, he is changeable, which is most absurd to say, nor can it stand by any reason that God is changeable. But if he himself is unchangeable, his words also and precepts are unchangeable: but because he is wholly unchangeable, whatever [1221A] he has said or established must be fixed and unchangeable, therefore he has laid down no second and new law to destroy the first and old one.

To whom it is answered that what they have proposed must be granted, namely that God is unchangeable, whence in the new law he has established nothing which derogated from the former, but rather that the new law and the old are the same, but that one in the letter, this in the spirit; that one in figure, this in truth. That this is so was shown beforehand of old in the vision of Ezechiel the prophet, where *a wheel in the midst of a wheel [n: (Ezech. X)]* is recorded to have been seen. For the wheel in the midst of the wheel is the New Testament within the Old Testament, because what the Old Testament designated, this the New displayed: God, then, being unchangeable, changed nothing in this, who [1221B] so placed the one under the other that the one should be the signifying, the other the signified.

This, then, that people at first knew not, because it read the signifying, but did not understand the signified: but after, visited by divine grace, it began to know and believe that the letter killeth, but the spirit *223* quickeneth, repenting of its error, it was sore afraid, saying: *I knew not: my soul troubled me because of the chariots of Aminadab.* As if it said: Therefore I could not believe, because I did not yet at all understand the grace of the Gospel [sic: *Evangeiii*], for *my soul troubled me,* because I wondered what it could be, that I should now have to receive the four books of the Gospels who before had received the five books of Moses, as if this change could befall God, that in this way that should not please him which had pleased him before, [1221C] who should found a new law, the old one transferred. But after I understood the wonderful providence of God, who instructed me through carnal things toward spiritual things, so that I might comprehend more easily and more truly not first the spiritual, but that which is animal, afterwards that which is spiritual, having laid aside all doubt I afterwards defended the truth which I had before assailed, not only by believing and teaching that truth, but also by dying for it.

But why do I say *the chariots of Aminadab?* truly because of the four books of the Gospel, in which are preached the four sacraments of that same Aminabad, who is interpreted *the willing one of my people.* And who is this *Aminabad,* but Christ who willingly laid down [1221D] his life, that he might make me for himself a willing people and acceptable to God? And what are his four sacraments but his incarnation, passion, resurrection and ascension? The *chariot,* then, of this *Aminabad* is the book of the four Gospels, containing and conferring the knowledge of the four sacraments aforesaid, the Holy Spirit driving, by whose inspiration chariots of this kind were composed and ordered.

And when the kind Father received that same people which had believed out of the circumcision, returning to him, the black darkness of ignorance shaken off, running to meet it as it were with a consoling and exhorting voice, he calls it to penitence, saying: *Return, return, Sunamitis,* etc.
