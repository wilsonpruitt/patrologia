## CHAPTER VI. (cont.)

Behold, he says, there runs to meet it the voice of my chariot, which sounds forth the cause and order and necessary profit of my sacraments, namely of my nativity, passion, resurrection and ascension, which voice indeed thou, formerly ignorant, wouldst neither hear nor receive, but now, O *Sunamitis,* that is, captive, who art still held captive because thou hast not yet laid down the bond of original or actual sin through the washing of baptism and the participation of my body and blood, *return,* because thou hast hope of pardon. For thou didst withdraw from me through unbelief and ingratitude, forgetful of the benefits which I wrought toward thee, when I freed thee from the [1222B] bondage of Egypt, when I *224* fed thee with heavenly bread, when I gave thee my law, and chose thee before all nations for myself as a peculiar people. But now *return* to me through the voice of my chariot, through the four Gospels, [the chariot] crying out to thee as through four wheels and four times exhorting thee to penitence, *return, return, return, return.* Return through faith, return through working, return through right intention, return through love: for first thou must believe, then work, because *faith without works is dead [n: (Jac. II)]*; further thy working must proceed from good intention, namely that faith may work through love; for thy working, if it is not from divine love, lies subject to vain [1222C] glory, and therefore lacks reward, because it has not the merit of holiness.

*Return,* moreover, *that we may behold thee,* that is, that we may approve the comeliness of thy soul which thou hast received through faith, and set thee forth as an example for others to follow: for this is thy praiseworthy beauty which pleases our eyes, that, error laid aside, thou follow the truth, and that thou who through error and the shadow of the law didst seem misshapen and dark, through thy return, through the truth and light of the Gospel, be seen beautiful and bright. For as long as thou didst lie hid under the shadow thou wast despicable, but after thou camest forth to the light of truth thou didst appear worthy to be seen, and now *the king hath desired thy comeliness [n: (Psal. XLIV)]* [var: Vulg. *concupiscet*, "shall desire"], that heavenly King namely [1222D] who made thee his bride through the grace of the Holy Spirit, after thou didst return to him by faith, working, right intention, love; these things indeed have been said specially with regard to that ancient people of God, which, the less it at first understood the chariot of our Aminadab, the more it received it with veneration after it understood.

But to return to the moral sense, the signification of this chariot can, not unprofitably, be referred to the whole of the human race, so that we make two parts of the whole, to one of which we give that chariot whose charioteer is the devil. For man, consisting of four elements, and four humours, and also of the increases of four ages, seems to have as it were a certain material chariot [1223A] on the side of the body: further on the side of the soul, since it consists of four affections, and also four virtues by which it is led to good, the force and progress of that soul seem to have a spiritual chariot.

Let us see therefore first the complexions arranged in man by fours according to the body, such few as have occurred to the mind, then the qualities of the affections, designated also in fourfold manner according to the soul, so that from these, as we have said, *225* we may make two chariots, the material namely and the affectual, which both, looking to two chariots [cj: *quadrigas*; read *aurigas*, "charioteers"], namely Christ and the devil, are made subject to one of them, since they cannot *serve two masters [n: (Matth. VI)]*, and either, running rightly and happily through the grace of God, the spiritual one is called the chariot of Christ, [1223B] or, running perversely and unhappily through the privation of that same grace, the carnal one is called the chariot of the devil.

There are, then, as we have said, four elements of which man consists, fire, air, water, earth; which are intermingled with four qualities, namely heat, cold, moisture, dryness. For fire is hot and dry, air hot and moist, water cold and moist, earth cold and dry: and man himself, consisting of these four elements and of the qualities of the elements, while he has heat from fire, breath from air, blood from water, flesh from earth, in four seasons is changed by four humours through the increases of four ages.

[1223C]For spring, which is as it were the first flower and beginning of the year: for then the earth begins to flower and bud, and it is hot and moist; it shows forth childhood, which is hot and moist, because in the child the blood is hot and moist; summer, which is hot and dry, shows youth, which is hot and dry, having red choler which abound in the young, dry and hot. Further autumn, which is cold and dry, announces manhood, which is cold and dry, having melancholy, that is, black choler, which are in men in their prime cold and dry. And winter, which is cold and moist, intimates old age, which is cold and moist, slipping phlegm, which abounds in the old, cold and moist.

For complexions of this kind, when they flourish well and in order in man, [1223D] show the man healthy, but when they wander out of order, they disturb the man and make him sick: but because this fourfold constitution is materially in the body of man, by which man himself is drawn to the love of the flesh and the desires of this world, which he contracted from the propagation of original sin, there are born also from this material constitution four affectual qualities, which draw their force and nature chiefly from four inward members, namely the gall, the spleen, the liver and the heart. In short, man has from the gall that he is irascible, from the spleen that he is risible, from the liver that he is concupiscible, from the heart that he is rational: further from that heart, or mind, or soul, there proceed as it were four conceptions, which [1224A] precede every action, namely thought, intention, will, deliberation. *226* For being about to do something we first think, then we intend why and how we should do it, afterwards we dispose in the will what is to be done, lastly we deliberate to accomplish it. But the very force of reason is spread fourfold: he who has wit, memory, understanding, discretion; for he has through wit to find things unknown and hidden by seeking and investigating, through memory to retain things found, through understanding to dispose things retained prudently, manfully and honourably, through discretion to assign things well disposed modestly to the places fitting them.

The rational soul itself also has four affections implanted in it: that is, fear, hope, joy [1224B] and sorrow, of which the poet says: . . . *These fear, desire, and rejoice, and grieve.*
And a philosopher, to a certain pupil of his whom he desired to relieve from the affections of these passions, says: Drive out joys,
Drive out fear,
And put hope to flight,
Nor let sorrow be present.
And these affections, by those who treat of physics, are said, according to the complexions of the material qualities which we have set down above, to rule more in some, less in others: but [1224C] over all these presides the soul strong in reason, which, when they thrust themselves in out of order, is to compose them and bend them to some measure of honour or profit, the divine Spirit ruling. And for a pursuit or discipline of this kind there must of necessity be applied, and implored from God, the four principal virtues, that is, prudence, fortitude, justice, temperance, which, because in every structure of the spiritual building they are laid as it were as certain corner stones by which the other virtues are enclosed and held, greater gifts of divine grace are considered in them.

Since, then, we have made two chariots, the material namely and the affectual, let us set also as it were two presiding over each, spirit and flesh, and as charioteers God and the devil: for that affectual one [1224D] is moved more or less toward reason and honour just so far as it is more or less driven by that material one, through care of the flesh, to the love of temporal life; and so far as the one presiding over each, namely spirit and flesh, which two are always opposed to one another, shall have been superior or inferior; because *the flesh lusteth against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh [n: (Gal. V)]*. For therefore we say that spirit and flesh preside over each, because there are in them certain parts which tend from the flesh to the spirit, or slip from the spirit to the flesh.

For in that material chariot very often some who are stirred by the natural *227* heat and moisture of the flesh contend with such strength of spirit through the grace of God that they break all that natural impulse of the flesh [1225A] through the fire of divine love and the impulse of the spirit, and at times utterly burn it out, and so grow hot toward God that they remain wholly cold toward the world. On the other hand some of cold nature, as they are cold inwardly toward every action in the love of God, therefore are zealous to labour at no good work. In that affectual chariot also there are those who are angry for temporal inconvenience, and there are those who are angry for justice; there are also those who desire good things, there are those who desire evil things; there are those who laugh and rejoice because of the wretched prosperity of this life, there are also those who rejoice and laugh in adversities because of the hope and security of future beatitude; there are also those who use the good of reason well, and act and savour rightly; there are those who [1225B] use it ill and savour and act perversely. There are nonetheless some who use natural wit for good, there are those who use it for evil; there are those who understand good rightly, there are those who understand it ill; there are those who offer rightly, but do not divide rightly, that is, discern; and there are those who both offer rightly and divide rightly, that is, discern; as on the other hand some neither offer rightly nor divide rightly, that is, discern.

Whence to both these chariots there cleave four species which lead both into the full motions of their own pursuit, namely suggestion, delight, consent, custom. For the soul of each one is led either to good or to evil in these four ways, to good indeed by the suggestion of the Holy Spirit, as it is said of him: *For he himself shall suggest to you [1225C] all things whatsoever I shall have said to you [n: (Joan. XIV)]*; by the delight of the soul, by the consent of the spirit, and by the custom of good itself: but to evil by the suggestion of the devil, by the delight of the flesh the consent of the spirit, by the custom of evil itself.

In the midst, then, of these chariots, which are formed from the matter of the flesh and the affection of the mind, the four principal virtues which we have set down above, namely prudence, fortitude, justice, temperance, must needs be set, so that by a certain setting of them as it were round about, the whole concretion of the body placed within may be ruled and composed to some measure of rational form. For as a certain one says: *Virtue is the mean of vices, and drawn back from both,* [1225D] [n: (HOR., Ep. I, XVIII, 9.)]
that is, removed from either part: for example, to be dull or to be evilly cunning is vicious, but in the midst of these vices, namely dullness and evil cunning, the virtue of prudence is set, by whose governing this inequality may be corrected: fortitude also is set between timidity and daring, so that this may truly be to be brave, *228* to be found neither timid in good nor daring in evil. Likewise it can be seen also in the other virtues, from the consideration of which, set beside themselves, the quality of the vices is understood.

From the whole matter, then, of this kind, carnal namely and spiritual, distinguished fourfold, there is made one certain thing formed of matter, which shows in itself as it were a twin chariot of flesh and spirit, over which presides [1226A] as charioteer either God or the devil: but God ruling, saving and quickening; the devil overturning, damning and killing.
