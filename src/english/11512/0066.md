## *229* CHAPTER VII.

VERSE 1.-- *What shalt thou see in the Sulamite, but the choirs of camps? How beautiful are thy steps in shoes, O daughter of the prince! the joints of thy thighs are as necklaces which are made by the hand of a craftsman.*

[1226D]So long as Judaea was held captive by the chain of her error, or any soul whatever by the yoke of sin, no gladness could be seen in her, but misery, because both the former, in the fight between the letter and the spirit, and the latter, in the fight between the flesh and the spirit, was ever inferior in her better part: for the former followed the letter alone, the latter the flesh; but when through penance the former acknowledged and received the spiritual sense, and the latter, the love of the flesh trodden down, began to live according to the spirit, the kindly Father, receiving each mercifully, showed that he would bestow his help upon her, so that, having now obtained true and full liberty, and having as it were won the victory over the part adverse to her, she might, rejoicing in flesh and spirit, pay him devout praises. For this he himself promises through the prophet, saying: [1227A] *Return ye to me, and I will return to you. [n: (Malach. V)]*. Whence also, rejoicing together at their conversion, he says to the Church: *What shalt thou see in the Sulamite, but the choirs of camps?* For in choirs is shown the alternation of melody sounding in concord; but in camps a fellowship of warriors is gathered for defence. What then are *the choirs of camps,* but the praises of warriors? and the sense is: Because this Sulamite has turned to me from the captivity under which she was held through *230* guilt, I will confer on her so great a grace of liberty and security that thou, O beloved, oughtest to see and wonder at nothing else in her than joyful thanksgivings and daily proclamations of praise for the motions of the vices overcome and cast down, and the advances in the virtues granted.

[1227B]Now, the two walls coming from opposite sides being as it were joined into one, namely the Jewish and the gentile people, of which he shows the whole universal Church to consist, he is directed again to her praise, that he may proclaim the so diverse graces of his condescension notably set in one body, and that for this reason thanksgivings are justly and worthily to be paid to him. He says therefore: *How beautiful are thy steps in shoes, O daughter of the prince!*

What is expressed by *steps,* but the courses of actions? what is signified by *shoes,* which are made from the skins of dead animals, but the examples of the fathers who went before and have been withdrawn from this life? for as the feet are protected by shoes, so by the examples of the holy fathers the manners of those who follow them [1227C] are defended.

But who is the *daughter of the prince,* but either the Church, or any holy soul whatever, given over through the grace of adoption to him who is the prince of the kings of the earth? The Church therefore, or any holy soul whatever, has beautiful steps in shoes, because she has beautiful and reverend acts from the deeds and teachings of the holy fathers. Wherefore not unfittingly he adds: *The joining of thy thighs as necklaces which are made by the hand of a craftsman.* [var: Vulg. *Juncturae femorum tuorum*, "the joints of thy thighs"]

By the *joining of the thighs* the concord of the two Testaments is understood, because the Old and the New Testament are so evenly joined that what the one announces in figure, this the other represents in truth. [1227D] But what is it that these testaments are compared to *necklaces*? For a necklace is composed of gold and silver and precious stones, and is placed on the breast of matrons, that it may be to them both for a defence and for an ornament. But each Testament is built from the brightness of the divine sense and the lustre of the utterance, as it were from gold and silver, and also from the predications of the diverse virtues, as it were from certain precious gems. And as a necklace defends and adorns the breast of matrons, so each Testament spiritually defends and adorns the breast of holy Church, or of each holy soul: defends it indeed from the hostility of the vices, adorns it with the beautiful variety of the virtues.

*The joining* therefore *of her thighs, as necklaces,* because [1228A] the concord of the two Testaments, by which holy Church, or any holy soul, is daily instructed, confers on her a great protection and ornament; a protection *231* indeed against the perversities of heretics, an ornament on account of the comeliness and beauty of the senses and utterances, or of the power of the sacraments which are celebrated in her, and the honourableness of the ecclesiastical orders.

But who is this craftsman, by whose hand these necklaces, that is, the Testaments, have been made, but almighty God, by whose will, power, working these things have been honourably composed, profitably set forth, happily received? Wherefore the dignity of these Testaments is greatly to be commended, since they have been established by such an author, whose workmanship is not [1228B] human, but divine, not devised, but willed and of his power, for *all things,* indeed, *whatsoever he would he hath done [n: (Psal. XIII)]*, and ever his willing was also his having done.

Holy Church therefore, or any holy soul whatever, gathers all the comeliness and beauty of her inner man from the consideration of these Testaments, [as to] what her needful profit sets before her to be believed, what to be hoped, what to be loved. But why is her *joining of the thighs* praised by the bridegroom? because the concord of the two Testaments, or of the two peoples, or of the twofold charity, constitutes the greatest and whole rule of living well, and lays up the most certain glory of reward for the rule kept; and well did he say the joining [1228C] not femorum, but *feminum*, because from these is conceived, born and advanced all the honourableness and profit of holy Church, or of any faithful soul.

VERSE 2.-- *Thy navel is like a turned bowl; never wanting cups. Thy belly is like a heap of wheat set about with lilies.*

The navel is the middle point of the belly: what then is the *navel* of the Church, but the holy preachers and teachers, who move in the midst of the people, gathering that same people to themselves by teaching and working? For as all the entrails adhere to the navel, so the people adheres to its preachers, ever looking to their words and examples as to a certain middle point [1228D] set about it, that it may establish for itself in their manners and discourses a certain firm foundation of living well.

But this navel is said to be *like a turned bowl,* because, that they may cut away the superfluous and uneven things of manners, the holy teachers ever turn about the same thing, when for the attaining of that which is ever unchangeable and persists the same in its quality they run, labour, and hasten to draw others with them. Which bowl is said *never to want cups,* because these same preachers ever abound in the *232* sentences of the divine utterances, with which they themselves too are made drunk through the grace of the Holy Spirit, and are zealous sufficiently to refresh the thirsting breasts of the peoples.

In another way also, if we look to the soul of each faithful man, [1229A] holy charity can spiritually be called the navel of the mind, which, set as it were in a certain centre, touches all the virtues as it were through the circumference, because, since no virtue can be without charity, all the virtues, looking toward it, assuredly seem to be as it were appendages of charity.

But how is this called a *turned bowl?* The Apostle says that *the end of the commandment is charity unto justice to every one that believeth [n: (I Tim. I)]*; therefore all perfection consists in charity, because it is turned, retaining in itself nothing of superfluity and of obstructing vice, and therefore perfect and smooth; *for perfect charity casteth out fear, because fear is not in charity [n: (I Joan. IV)]*. For this perfection is the end of the commandment, that is, the consummation, as the prophet says, the word [1229B] consummated which the Lord hath made upon the earth [n: (Isa. X; Rom. IX)].

But how *never wanting cups?* the apostles were said to be full of new wine when they burned with the Holy Spirit, that is, with the fire of divine charity: for they were drunk with that cup of the spiritual chalice of which the Psalmist says: *And my chalice which inebriateth me, how goodly is it! [n: (Psal. XXII.)]* They then who are filled with divine charity, how are they not ever moist and warm with that unfailing cup of the Holy Spirit? On the other hand, that land is said to be parched with thirst which is not sprinkled with the infusion of divine charity: whence the Lord cries to those parched with this thirst, saying: *He that thirsteth, let him come to me and drink, and out of his belly shall flow living waters [n: (Joan. VII)]*. Therefore *this turned bowl never wanteth a cup,* because perfect [1229C] charity never lacks the heavenly dew, and the desire of that highest good. But because there are in the Church prelates and subjects, strong and weak, prudent and simple, after Christ has praised his Church in the virtues of the prelates, he adds also a praiseworthy testimony from the obedience and discipline of the subjects, *thy belly,* saying, *is like a heap of wheat.* For because the belly is a frail and soft part of the body, *by the belly* is designated the frailty of the subjects, who cannot yet attain the perfection of the masters; but because they too belong to the Church, and are of the lot of the elect, whom that highest Householder calls *his wheat,* and commands to be gathered into his barn, it is not unfittingly compared to *a heap of wheat.* Now a heap is broadened below, [1229D] sharpened above, and the whole pile is a gathering of grains as it were embracing and bearing one another.

*233* What then is expressed by *the heap of wheat,* but the multitude of the faithful obeying their prelates, who, although through the infirmity of the flesh and the cares of worldly things they are somewhat pressed down about the broad and spacious way, yet through obedience and humility, and brotherly charity also, are raised up by faith and hope to the things above? for embracing and helping one another, and bearing with the infirmities of manners and of bodies, they fulfil that saying of the Apostle: *Bear ye one another's burdens, and so you shall fulfil the law of Christ [n: (Gal. VI)]*.

[1230A]But this is said to be a *heap of wheat set about with lilies,* because through the odour of right faith and the confession of sins it advances much toward the whiteness of eternal life; for in lilies there is both odour and whiteness and medicine: the odour is in faith and working, the medicine in the confession of sins, the whiteness in the recompense of eternal life.

VERSE 3.-- *Thy two breasts are like two young roes that are twins of the she-goat.*

By the two breasts may not absurdly be understood the two precepts of charity, through which the soul of each faithful man is nourished by the tenderness of inward milk and is brought to the strength of spiritual exercise, so that it can say with the Apostle: *I can do all things in him who strengtheneth me [n: (Philipp. IV)]*; for *love is strong as death [n: (Cant. VIII)]*; and no tribulation separates the saints from charity [1230B] [n: (Rom. VIII)]. For through this they go *from virtue to virtue [n: (Psal. LXXXIII)]*: whence also rightly the precepts of charity themselves are compared *to two twin young roes of the she-goat,* which by nature ever seeks the heights.

But why *twins?* surely on account of the concord and unity of that twofold charity, namely of God and of the neighbour, because they are so conceived and grow together in the mind of the saints that neither can be prior to the other, but when one loves God, one loves the neighbour also, and when one loves the neighbour, one loves God. For each is also a proof of the other, because, as John says: *He that saith he loveth God and loveth not his brother is a liar [n: (I Joan IV)]*: and therefore these two precepts of charity are as it were two twins of the she-goat, because they are both carried on in one place and time, [1230C] and ever advance to higher things by the daily increase of the virtues.

Or, the *two breasts* are the two Testaments by which holy Church is daily nourished to works of piety, which also are *as two young roes that are twins of the she-goat,* because they are so one, the signifying and the signified, that although they were not of one time in their showing forth, yet they are of one time in their signification, and so great is the majesty of these Testaments that to those disputing of them an ever greater and loftier subtlety presents itself.
