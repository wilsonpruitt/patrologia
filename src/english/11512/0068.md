## *229* CHAPTER VII. (cont.)

Therefore, because *the head of man is God [n: (I Cor. II)]* [var: Vulg. *omnis viri caput Christus est*, "the head of every man is Christ"], if any faithful man desires by virtue of mind to be a *lock* of this his head, it is needful that as a searcher he should cleave more diligently to the sacred *channels* of the Scriptures, because there he finds wherewith he may be dyed, that he may be made the *purple* of his heavenly King through inward love by the mortification of the carnal life. But it is to be noted that the beloved beautifully answers his beloved with a proclamation of praise of this kind in ten parts as it were on ten strings, sounding spiritually on one ten-stringed instrument, which is so fittingly set over against the ten-stringed instrument above, which the bride had sung in praise of the bridegroom, that the one from the higher parts, the other from the lower, impose the measure of a most pleasing melody. For because the bride ought always to ascend to the bridegroom with right intention and perfect [1234D] love, it is just that she should rise from lesser things to greater, and therefore the proclamation of her advancing and as it were growing toward the virtues of the mind is sung by the bridegroom condescendingly; not that this advance, virtue and power belongs to the one receiving, but rather because it is the praise of the bridegroom who gives and bestows on her this virtue and power by his gift.

But the bride, reverently attending to the immense kindness of the bridegroom descending from heaven, and humbling himself to our infirmity for our sake, begins the proclamations of his praise from the highest proclamation of the body, that is, from the head, as it were from the higher string, that she may show that she, set lower, always ought to look back toward the life and teaching of her bridegroom set over against her above, and to ascend [1235A] by imitation to him whom she knows to have descended to her through the tenderness of compassion.

* 239* VERSE 6.-- *How beautiful art thou, and how comely, dearest, in delights!*

After the bridegroom has proclaimed in the bride, part by part, the variety of the members composed from the habit of a mind well [sic: *bone*] and honourably ordered, showing that one certain whole had grown up thence, he calls the whole of the virtues by the name of stature, but declares the abundance of doctrine by the signification of breasts. For what beauty of manners or of utterances is lacking in the whole body of holy Church? for since this one shines with this virtue, that one with that, another with another, some possess more kinds of virtues, some fewer, what of form, of beauty and of comeliness can there be which [1235B] holy Church does not have in her body? And since diverse men are renowned for diverse learning in holy doctrines, what knowledge of truth or of holiness can there be in which holy Church does not abound? For since according to the Apostle *to one may be given the word of wisdom, to another the word of knowledge, to another faith, to another the grace of healings, to another the working of miracles, to another prophecy, to another the discerning of spirits; to another kinds of tongues, to another interpretation of speeches [n: (I Cor. XII)]*, assuredly all the beauty and adornment of works or of speeches shines forth spiritually in the inner stature of holy Church.

Whence she is deservedly pronounced *beautiful and comely,* and *dearest in delights;* because assuredly the abundance of the virtues and the saving teaching of wisdom are great [1235C] delights of the soul; but the more the soul is delighted by these, the more fully is it filled with the sweetness of divine love.

VERSE 7.-- *Thy stature is likened to a palm tree, and thy breasts to clusters of grapes.*

But why is a stature of this kind likened to a palm tree? For the palm is a sign of victory; and what victory is greater and more glorious than to triumph over vices and sins? And whence has a stature of such a kind, wonderful and glorious, grown up, but from the victory of the virtues and the destruction of the vices? Not unfittingly therefore the *stature* of the Church *is likened to a palm,* because all the honourableness and probity of her manners tells of notable victories.

[1235D]Further, her breasts are likened to clusters of grapes, because her doctrine and preaching abounds in the sweet and pleasant savour of wisdom and of spiritual understanding, and so inebriates her hearers that it makes them as it were insensible to the pleasures of this world. And that the bridegroom himself may show that the beauty of this stature advances and grows chiefly and principally from the mortification of the carnal life, he holds out to her the example of his passion and the gain of that same passion: *I said,* saying, *I will go up into the palm tree.*

* 240* VERSE 8.-- *I said: I will go up into the palm tree and take hold of its fruit: and thy breasts shall be as the clusters of the vine, and the odour of thy mouth like the odour of apples.*

As if he should say openly to that same bride of his: Whence [1236A] hast thou to conquer, whence hast thou to advance? Is it of thy own strength and industry that thou canst procure for thyself the glory of so great beauty? If thou considerest rightly, thou wilt by no means ascribe the merit of any good to thy fortitude or prudence. For I *said,* that is, before the times of the world I decreed and determinately disposed, since through the transgression of the sign thou didst fall and didst begin to be powerless, and by sinning didst find an enemy to thyself, whom afterwards thou couldst not prevail against, but as a captive didst lie subject to his dominion, that I would again through the opposing of the tree recover for thee the good that was lost, and snatch thee from the power of the enemy, and from the captivity in which thou wast held, and, liberty being restored, would lay the enemy beneath thee, and would give thee foresight against his snares by guarding thee beforehand, and the strength [1236B] of resisting: therefore *I said: I will go up into the palm tree,* that is, into the cross, upon which, after I have triumphed over the enemy, and have taken hold of the fruit of this my passion, and in sign of victory by rising again have brought it forth, I will bring forth to all who obey me and who have hope in my strength this oracle of consolation: *Have confidence, I have overcome the world [n: (Joan. XVI)]*. Thou therefore oughtest to fight, and because thou art secure of the victory, I having already obtained the victory, ascend the cross with me by mortifying the flesh, that the whole *stature* of thy soul may be likened *to the palm,* and by the title of perseverance may be made over to eternal victory.

But to be venerated and adored by every soul is the ascent of this palm. Why? because great assuredly and [1236C] incomparable is its fruit: for what? has not the whole human race been refreshed and repaired through this fruit? And who or what sort was and is this fruit? Surely the redemption of the human race, the reconciliation of enmity toward God, the daily remission of sins, a firm defence against the snares of the enemy, the going out of worldly misery, and the entering in of the heavenly kingdom. For all the advance of the Church began from the time of the passion and resurrection of Christ, and from then the proclamations of her holiness and the good things of the Holy Spirit flowed forth most abundantly. Whence also it follows: *And thy breasts shall be as the clusters of the vine, and the odour of thy mouth like the odour of apples.* For her preaching and doctrine, after the removal of the shadow through the showing forth of the truth, flowed out sweeter and richer, and [1236D] after the passion and resurrection of Christ it made many so *241* drunk with divine charity that it boiled out of them all the moisture of worldly pleasure by the heat of the Holy Spirit, and kindled them to the love of the heavenly life.

But from the throat the voice goes out, and therefore by the throat the memory of the divine precepts, by the voice the preaching of those same precepts, is not unfittingly understood; for unless they are first held in the mind through memory, they will by no means be brought forth abroad through speech; but the voice of speech of this kind is compared *to the odour of apples,* because as through odour a thing not only present but also absent is perceived, so from the time of the passion and resurrection of Christ, through the preaching of the Church, not only the present [1237A] deeds and sayings of the Lord himself or of his saints, but also the past, have become known and are daily known by reading and hearing. The good or evil things to come also are gathered from these, when both the rewards of the just and the punishments of sinners are weighed from afar as it were from the odour of a certain inward touch. Now this voice is referred not to the odour of flowers but of *apples,* because the preaching of the Church, which now sounds through the whole world, not rude or tender, but ripe and perfect, shows one faith to believers.

VERSE 9.-- *Thy throat like the best wine, worthy for my beloved to drink, and for his lips and his teeth to ruminate.*

The *throat,* moreover, is said to be *like the best wine,* because the memory of the divine precepts inebriates the mind with its sweetness, [1237B] and what it drinks sweetly by meditating within, it pours out profitably by speaking without. *Worthy for my beloved to drink.* Christ in his body, that is, in the Church, which is made one with him through the Spirit, just as he is said to be refreshed with justice, faith and holiness, so also to be given drink; as on the contrary in the unbelieving he is proclaimed to hunger and thirst for justice, faith and holiness. For hanging on the cross when he said: *I thirst [n: (Joan. XIX)]*, he thirsted for the faith and holiness of the unbelieving people, because for its sake he had been wearied as it were by a certain heat of labour, which he had led out of Egypt with many signs and portents, before whose face he had crushed the nations, to whom he had confirmed the covenant of his testament and law, to whom he had assigned the land of promise, the city and the temple, [1237C] for whose salvation at the last he had not disdained to undergo death itself.

When therefore holy Church, or any holy soul whatever, meditates on the law of the Lord day and night, Christ is given drink in its mind with a certain sweet wine of meditation, and its holy meditation is as it were a sweet drinking and worthy of God. Whence when the bridegroom was proclaiming *the throat of the beloved,* that is, the meditation of the commandments *242* of God, as *the best wine,* she, interrupting the voice of him who praised her, and rejoicing at co-operating grace, *worthy,* she says, *for my beloved to drink,* that is, worthy that he himself should further it by his grace, and bring it to effect.

How? *By ruminating with lips and teeth:* for [1237D] what one understands by often meditating with oneself, he ought to spend on others by teaching, to handle by expounding, and to handle again by discussing, which is as it were the property of a clean animal, to ruminate. For in the one body of the Church, by which the whole Christ is understood, the *throat* is the meditation of Holy Scripture, the *lips* the pronouncing of that same Scripture, the *teeth* its explanation, as it were the breaking up of the coarse letter, and its grinding down to the subtlety of spiritual understanding. Wherefore it is said to be *worthy* for the bridegroom himself *to drink* or *to ruminate,* because all the advance of the Church or of any holy soul whatever is of his gift and grace.

VERSE 10.-- *I to my beloved, and his turning is toward me.*

Since, she says, the grace of my beloved [1238A] himself going before, I please him by meditation of the holy Scriptures, therefore I will, and by right I ought, to draw nearer to him by obeying, by applying myself to good acts, and by announcing his precepts to others, so that that may come to pass which is written: *Draw nigh to God and he will draw nigh to you [n: (Jac. IV)]*. For if I shall turn myself to him, then *his turning is toward me,* as he himself said: *Turn ye to me, and I will turn to you [n: (Zach. I)]*: but there is no changeableness in God, so that he may be said to be turned away or turned toward after the manner of men, but the motion of grace coming or withdrawing in man is called a certain turning or turning away of God. *His turning* to us is the mercy of grace calling, justifying and glorifying, through which free will is helped, and the intention of good will is brought [1238B] to the effect of a good purpose. Whence well he says first: *I to my beloved,* and then, *and his turning is toward me,* because it is ours first to take up the intention of good will toward the pursuit of good work, his to bring the work conceived by good intention through co-operating grace even to a good end. Wherefore, because, feeling a ready will in herself, she takes up confidence concerning the help co-operating with her, she forthwith girds herself for the pursuit of working, saying: *Come, my beloved.*
