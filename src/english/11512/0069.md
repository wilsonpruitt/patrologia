## *229* CHAPTER VII. (cont.)

*Let us go forth,* she says, because after thy turning to me shall have been made through co-operating grace, I can freely and securely go out, both to the [1238C] office of preaching and to the pursuit of good action, so that all my working may both always begin from thee and, begun, be finished through thee: for without thee we can do nothing of good.

But whither shall we go forth first? *into the field,* namely, that is, the nation of the Jews, which is now fit to receive the seed of the word of God, because it has its hearts already ploughed through the written and also the natural law, and therefore can the better understand from the law and the prophets the purpose of the divine goodness by which he decreed to restore the human race. For to them first it behoves *to speak the word of God [n: (Act. XIII)]*, *whose adoption of sons is glory, and the testament and the giving of the law, and the service, and the promises; whose are the fathers, from whom [1238D] Christ according to the flesh, who is God blessed for ever [n: (Rom. IX)]*. *Into* this *field* therefore, to be sprinkled with the seed of the divine word, *let us go forth* through the office of preaching, and *because* first *the words of God were committed to them [n: (Rom. III)]*, let us cast among them also the first seedlings of faith and of the divine sacraments.

Then, going further, *let us abide in the villages,* that is, among the gentiles, who are far removed from the city of God, that is, the Church, among whom, because they are slower to believe on account of the unaccustomed and unheard-of preaching of the faith, it will be necessary to make a stay, and by announcing the word of life more frequently, to bring them back to the elegance of city custom, that is, to the observance of the ecclesiastical law. *Let us abide* therefore among them, thou, O beloved, inspiring through grace, I by preaching [1239A] helping, that, wearing away by frequent instruction their inborn ignorance and the simplicity of a rude mind, we may pour in our civil honourableness and true wisdom, through which they may merit to be enrolled as happy dwellers of that city above.

But when we shall have joined these two walls coming from different sides, namely the Jews and the gentiles, in one faith, there will be not two, but one people, one vineyard of the Lord of Sabaoth, which, cultivated by the work of the Holy Spirit, will bring forth no longer wild grapes, but sweet grapes.

VERSE 12.-- *Let us rise early to the vineyards, let us see if the vineyard hath flourished, if the flowers be bringing forth fruits, if the pomegranates have flourished: there will I give thee my breasts.*

*244* *To* these *vineyards,* moreover, planted from the circumcision and [1239B] the uncircumcision, *let us rise early,* namely in the rising of faith, when the true sun shall have begun to rise in the hearts of believers and to irradiate them with the charity of his wisdom and to kindle them with the heat of his power: *let us see if the vineyard hath flourished,* that is, whether one Christian people, now made, has brought forth the beginnings of good works; *if the flowers be bringing forth fruits,* that is, whether those beginnings are striving to rise to some integrity and perfection; *if the pomegranates have flourished,* that is, whether they burn with so great a love of God that they desire to suffer for him not only in mind but also in body.

*There will I give thee my breasts:* for after we shall have made these two peoples one through the unity of faith, and shall have moved them forward to the pursuit of good work, then to the praise of thy name, O beloved, the sacraments which [1239C] by thy gift we have come to know from the subtlety of the two Testaments we will commit to them also, so that by them they may always be nourished to greater works of the virtues, and be strengthened against the temptations of the world, and be the more kindled to seek the future goods of blessedness [sic: *beatudinis*]. For I will preach confidently, and will unlock the precepts of thy precepts ( *sic* ), since I have hope of bearing fruit, and of attaining the effect of good work. Whence? because *the mandrakes have given their odour.*

VERSE 12.-- *The mandrakes have given their odour in our gates. All apples new and old, my beloved, I have kept for thee.*

That very odour brings me hope of preaching, because I believe that from that odour there consequently comes [1239D] the profit of gathering fruit. But it is to be diligently considered what this *mandrake* is, or what the *odour of this mandrake.* If thou look to the letter, the *mandrake* is an aromatic herb which, formed to the likeness of human members, lacks the head alone, whose power is that, given in drink to those whom wakefulness oppresses, it brings on sleep and rest; further, if barren women use it, it makes them conceive.

But if thou attend to the spiritual sense, the *mandrake* can, not absurdly, be understood as the conversion of the gentiles, which, so long as it was driven on in the depravity of its error, was wearied by a labour not only vain but also destructive; but after, converted, it began to know God, it learned both to be less wakeful to the cares and pleasures of worldly things, nay, more than to sleep, and to rest [1240A] from the depraved labour of this world; and she who before was barren of good work afterwards became fruitful in virtues, the prophecy being fulfilled which says: *Rejoice, thou barren, that bearest not: break forth and cry, thou that travailest not, for many are the children of the desolate, more than of her that hath a husband [n: (Isa. LIV; Gal. IV)]*.

*245* The *mandrake,* however, as we have said, though it has the likeness of a human body, lacks a head, because the gentile people, though it was by nature vigorous in reason for the discerning of good and evil, yet that very force of reason had in it been blunted in this part, by which it was not able to know its Creator, whom, because it did not have in its knowledge, it lacked as it were a head. The Jewish people also can be signified by the *mandrake,* which, while it used reason through the observance of [1240B] the law, yet did not have a head, because it refused to receive Christ; but this *mandrake* afterwards *gave its odour,* because she who before through unbelief had been barren, afterwards, believing, begot many sons to God. *The mandrakes* therefore *have given their odour in our gates,* because the Jewish and the gentile people, thou, O beloved, ascending into heaven, received the sacraments of the faith through thy grace and my preaching. But which are these gates, in which the beloved says that the believing people gave its odour? Surely if thou consider diligently, thou wilt be able to recognize through what gates the soul desiring to find God advances little by little even to the very secret places of the bedchamber: for first there is in man the conceiving of the mind, by which it conceives that whose sum [1240C] it desires to attain, which being conceived, it investigates what or of what sort it is, until it has knowledge of it. And when it is known it embraces it, and so uses it to its own profit that it strives at some time to turn all its wholeness to itself. There is therefore in each one who is to believe a certain gate and as it were a first entrance to God, the first conceiving of faith in the mind, by which he receives the will to believe, then the investigating of that faith, that he may know what or how he ought to believe; after which second gate of investigation there is opened to him a third gate, namely the knowledge of faith; after knowledge follows working, without which faith is judged to be idle; and the contemplation of a clean heart joins to God the one who works well.

[1240D]Those therefore who strive to come to God through these *gates,* at the very entrance of the gates *scatter the odour* of good will; but this odour, passing through the second gate of faith investigated, reaching also the third gate of faith now known, and then running through the fourth gate of working, through the fifth gate at last, of contemplative sweetness, glides in to the very inner secrets of the heavenly bridegroom. When therefore *the mandrakes give their odour in* these *gates,* it is evident that the souls seeking God are now ripening to the fruit of good work. With worthy rejoicing therefore the bride *246* plays with the beloved, saying: *The mandrakes have given their odour in our gates,* because while the Church attends to the advance of her sons, she pays due thanksgivings to God. Whence she girds herself the more for [1241A] preaching, because she hopes that she is making progress in the hearts of those whom she considers to have been turned to God with the whole heart. But this her advance she imputes not to her own labour and industry, but to divine grace, saying: *All apples new and old,* etc., *my beloved, I have kept for thee.* For thy honour, she says, I have kept, O beloved, all the fruit of holiness which I have come to know from the institution of the Old or of the New Testament, or which I have learned from the instruction of the precepts, or which I have gathered from the life and flowers of the Fathers by informing myself. For this is of thy gift, not of my labour, whether that I myself have advanced by living well and teaching, or that others have been able to advance by my example or teaching. Or, to say it in another way, *All apples new and old I have kept for thee,* because all things which in [1241B] the Old Testament are read as done and said mystically, but in the New as shown forth in reality, concerning thy nativity, passion, resurrection, and ascension, I have taught and expounded as written, and to all who read and hear I have shown in them the fruit of faith and of good working.
