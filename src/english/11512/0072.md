## BOOK FOUR. (cont.)

Wherefore he at once adds: *Under the apple tree I raised thee up: there thy mother was corrupted, there she that bore thee was violated.*

After the beloved has approved his beloved, that is, the faithful soul, for the state of virtue and the bearing conferred by his own gift, he suddenly makes an apostrophe to her herself, bringing back to memory the victory of the good state, so that she may grow together into a greater [1248D] grace of divine charity. *Under the apple tree,* he says, *I raised thee up:* as if he said: If thou considerest [sic: *anidmavertis*] what thou wast in the first parent, whence together with the whole human race thou didst contract guilt as it were by descent, not in vain wilt thou cleave to me more zealously, when thou attendest to what manner of one I have now made thee, or how great a grace I have conferred. *Under the apple tree* indeed *I raised thee up,* because, that thou mightest persevere in my grace, I held out before thee the form of thy condition, and that thou mightest learn to be under my power, I bound thee with the law of obedience. For when I said to Adam: *Of every tree of paradise eat, but of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil do not eat [n: (Gen. II)]*; I raised him up as one still lukewarm and ignorant, and admonished him to the duty of using reason well, that he might learn to fit himself to disciplines [sic: *di ciplinas*] and to the pursuit of obedience; but what? [1249A] he transgressed the precept and despised the good of discipline and obedience.

*There* then *thy mother was corrupted, there she that bore thee was violated.* How? Eve was seduced and violated, she persuaded her husband, and both presumed upon the forbidden tree. *Corrupted was* then *and violated under the apple tree thy mother* or *she that bore thee: corrupted* in the incontinence of the outward senses, violated through the consent of spirit and soul, in the guilt of disobedience; for she saw and desired [sic: *concapivit*], she obeyed the persuasion of the serpent and, touching the forbidden fruit, ate, and offered it to her husband to eat [n: (Gen. III)]. *256* This sin therefore, after it was consummated, begot death [n: (Jac. I)], and the penalty of this death, as if proceeding from this root of thy former parents, has been spread into the [1249B] posterity of the whole human race.

Therefore in thy first parents I raised thee up, and called thee forth to the pursuits of the virtues, but thou [sic: *tui*], in thy parents themselves, didst decline rather to vices, and didst not fear to become liable to death.

But when thou hadst been lost in such a way, who restored thee to salvation? who made thee such as thou art now seen to be? Did not I undertake death for thee and redeem thee from that death? Did I not again, *thee under the apple tree,* that is, under the wood of the cross, *raise up,* and lift thee to so great an honourableness and beauty of life?

But that thou mayest keep this honourableness and beauty of life inviolate, thou must needs persist with a strong mind in my charity.

[1249C]VERSE 6.-- *Set me as a seal upon thy heart, as a seal upon thy arm: for love is strong as death, emulation hard as hell; its lamps are lamps of fire and of flames.*

Two seals the beloved here proposes to his beloved, one namely to be set upon the heart, the other upon the arm. For what are these two seals, namely of the heart and of the arm, but the keeping of the heart and the keeping of work? For of the *seal,* that is, of the keeping of the heart, it is written: *With all keeping keep thy heart, for from it life proceedeth [n: (Prov. IV)]*: but of the *seal,* that is, of the keeping of work, it is written: *In all works remember thy last things, and thou shalt never sin [n: (Eccle. VII)]*.

[1249D]And well are these two necessary to man in all his life, and if the first keeping has been neglected, the other perishes; for as *a good man out of the good treasure of his heart bringeth forth good,* so *an evil man, out of the evil treasure of his heart bringeth forth evil [n: (Luc. VI)]*. For whatever proceeds outward into work was first conceived in the heart, and therefore if the keeping of the heart is neglected, so that it is not ruled by reason, whatever is shown outwardly in work is judged irrational. If the heart is ordered by reason, what is brought forth from it into work is honourable, because according to the state or quality of the mind, every action of the agent is understood to be either good or evil.

What is it then that the bridegroom says to the bride: *Set me as a seal upon thy heart, as a seal upon [1250A] thy arm?* What keeping can be safe and secure without God? *Unless* indeed *the Lord keep the city, he watcheth in vain that keepeth it [n: (Psal. CXXVI)]*: and therefore the Psalmist prays: *Keep me, O Lord, as the apple of the eye; under the shadow of thy wings protect me [n: (Psal. XVI)]*. But because, just as nothing of good work can be begun without him, *257* so neither can it be completed, the same Psalmist says: *Unless the Lord build the house, they labour in vain that build it [n: (Psal. CXXVI)]*: Therefore also it is necessary that *the seal of God upon the arm* be set, lest the building of good work be destroyed.

Rightly then is it said to the bride: *Set me as a seal upon thy heart, as a seal upon thy arm,* because she cannot think or do right things [1250B] unless divine grace inspires and governs; nor bring these to the end by persevering, unless the same grace keeps her. For when the works of holiness are shut and as it were sealed, lest they be corrupted by any depravity, then truly *the seal of God upon the heart and arm* of each soul is not unfittingly understood to be *set*.

Or let us say that *the seal of God* is the impression of holiness which is to be set upon the heart and arm of each soul desiring to live holily and justly. For when the life and conversation or the teaching of the Lord Jesus Christ is set before the holy soul, to be imitated or to be observed, rightly *his seal upon the heart and arm* of her is fixed, so that as she has borne *the image of the earthly* she may bear *also the image of the heavenly [n: (I Cor. XV)]*. [1250C] Did not the Lord, when he said to his disciples: *Learn of me, because I am meek and humble of heart [n: (Matth. XI)]*, seem as it were to say: Set *me as a seal upon your heart?* For there he wished to leave as it were a figure of meekness and humility impressed upon their hearts; but when he said: *I have given you an example, that as I have done, so you also may do [n: (Joan. III)]*, did he not seem to say: Set me as *a seal upon your arm?* For there he wished to leave them a certain figure and image of good working and serviceable charity, which they themselves also afterwards might impress upon their hearers by teaching and working. Whence also the Apostle, writing to the Philippians, and wishing to impress the seal or signet of holiness from his own mind [1250D] upon their minds: *Brethren,* he says, *whatsoever things are true, whatsoever modest, whatsoever just, whatsoever holy, whatsoever lovely, whatsoever of good fame, if any virtue, if any praise of discipline, think on these things, which you have both learned, and received, and heard, and seen in me, these things do, and the God of peace shall be with you [n: (Philip. IV)]*.

The bridegroom therefore, that is, Christ, says to his bride, that is, to the faithful soul: *Set me as a seal upon thy heart, as a seal upon thy arm;* as if he said to her in other words: Whatever thou readest in my teaching, whatever in my works, lay up in thy mind, and fulfil in acts, that from them thou mayest receive the form and image of holiness, through which *258* thou mayest lay aside every figure of the old man, which he assumed by sinning *under the apple tree [1251A]*. And why? *for love is strong as death.*

Well and fittingly, when the beloved had first said: *Set me as a seal upon thy heart, as a seal upon thy arm,* he straightway adds, *for strong as death* is *love, emulation hard as hell,* because since *God* himself is *charity,* as John says [n: (I Joan. IV)], to set God upon the heart is to have his charity, but to set him upon the *arm* is to work good through the same charity, because, as it is written: *Faith worketh through love [n: (Gal. V)]*. Now charity is the root of all the virtues, because as from one root many branches come forth, so from one charity many virtues are begotten. If then charity alone begets many virtues, and no one nourishes virtues [1251B] in himself but he who loves God, assuredly to have charity is to have God, and therefore, his seal being set upon heart and arm, the working through love is good and honourable.

But what is it that made God be incarnate and die, but charity? *For God so loved the world, as to give his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him may not perish, but may have life everlasting [n: (Joan. III)]*: and so each faithful soul sets God *as a seal upon its heart and arm,* when, in imitation of him, as he laid down his life for it, so it does not hesitate to labour for him, and, if the cause demands, to undergo death also. Whence well is that very *love strong as death* so called, because it kindles the mind that truly loves God with so great a force [1251C] that it does not hesitate to die for him whom it fears to offend by any love of a worldly thing. For whomever charity itself has perfectly possessed, it kills utterly to the love of worldly things. *Strong* then is *love as death,* because as death destroys temporal life, so the love of God in the mind of the just man slays the love of this world, and whomever charity has perfectly imbued within, it renders as it were insensible to all outward things to be carnally desired.

*Hard* also *as hell is emulation,* because the imitation of the works of Christ is as mighty to absolve and justify man from sins as hell is mighty to torment sinners: which *emulation,* however, proceeds from the ardour of charity, which brings so great a force of pain [1251D] from the desire of the supernal [felicity], as hell brings from the remembrance of their evils to the sinners whom it torments.

Whence it is added that *its lamps are lamps of fire and of flames,* that in the hearts of the elect charity itself may be understood to be not simply heat but fire and flame.

VERSE 7.-- *Many waters could not quench charity, nor shall the floods overwhelm it; if a man shall give all the substance of his house for love, he shall despise it as nothing.*

Because so great is its force in the mind of each saint that, as *259* waters cannot quench an immense fire, so the many tribulations of the world cannot take away the fervour of charity from the mind of the saint. [1252A] Whence a certain one who was burning with this fire of charity says: *Who shall separate us from the charity of Christ, tribulation or distress, persecution or famine, or nakedness, or peril, or the sword? But in all these things we overcome because of him that hath loved us. For I am sure that neither life, nor death, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor virtues, nor dominions, nor any creature shall be able to separate us from the charity which is in Christ Jesus our Lord [n: (Rom. VII)]* [var: Vulg. Rom 8:35–39 sets *persecutio* after *periculum*, reads *neque mors, neque vita*, has no *potestates* or *dominationes* but *instantia, futura, fortitudo, altitudo, profundum*, and reads *a caritate Dei*, "from the love of God"].

Whence rightly it is added that *if a man shall give all the substance of his house for love, he shall despise it as nothing.*
