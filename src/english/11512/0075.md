## BOOK FOUR. (cont.)

But the divine word commanded in the sacred law concerning the stubborn and wanton and disobedient son, that his parents, having seized him, [1259D] should bring him *to the elders of the city and to the gate of judgment [n: (Deut. XXI)]*, namely lest they hasten their own sentence, but that they bring in wise men as witness; for they ought to know that the first fault of our race, for which we all pay, is disobedience, and that the justice of God lays up no pardon for the stubborn and wanton, but, as it is written: *God resisteth the proud, but giveth grace to the humble [n: (I Petr. V)]*.

For such are always to be examined at the gate of judgment, so that, if they remain in their stubbornness or rashness and will not obey, they be cast out from the fellowship of the brethren; but if they repent and become humble and quiet, *268* they be received again. This is also the gate of judgment in the city of God, that is, holy Church: that the stubborn and rebellious, if they will not be corrected, be cut off with the sword [1260A] of excommunication, like putrid members, from the body of the Church, of whom it is written: *They went out from us, but they were not of us [n: (I Joan. II)]*; but that the penitent, and those putting an end to their malice, be restored like healed and holy members into the body of the Church. Therefore the other sins are indeed to be tolerated in the house of God, but the severity of wise fathers overwhelms stubborn and wanton sons, that it may take away the evil from its midst, with the authority of the law of the Lord which we have mentioned; nay, by the very example of God, who expelled the disobedient first men from paradise [n: (Gen. III)].

The word *delivering*, therefore, which is pronounced of the lord of the vineyard, is to be thought on and weighed not negligently but diligently and with fear, because on either side [1260B] neglect or contempt, whether by prelates or by subjects, looks to the judgment of him who delivers.

*A man bringeth for the fruit thereof a thousand pieces of silver.* The diligent and studious inspector of this chapter ought to attend who, or of what kind, or how great is the *fruit* of this vineyard, which is valued at so great a price that for the obtaining of it not ten, or twenty, or thirty, but determinately a *thousand* are the *pieces of silver* to be brought; and that he who, lacking these, could purchase this fruit, is not any powerful and rich man, whether official or private, but ought absolutely to be called, and to be, a *man*.

What, then, is this *fruit*? Surely that which is painted as it were with three colours of beauty, namely power, wisdom, goodness: power [1260C] indeed, than which nothing is more efficacious; wisdom, than which nothing is more reasonable; goodness, than which nothing is sweeter. For power is constrained by no law of impossibility or infirmity, wisdom admits nothing which lacks the virtue of reason, goodness abounds with so great a multitude of sweetness that there is nothing above it. Moreover, the touch and use of such a fruit affords an efficacious and most wholesome experience of health to the whole man. But where is this found among creatures and visible things? Is it in angels? But it is written that *in his angels also* he *found* iniquity [n: (Job IV)]. For a creature can be subject to corruption and mutability; but that which cannot must be immutable and incorruptible.

Further, we have said that the three beauties of this fruit [1260D] are such that they can receive nothing above themselves; yet something can be below, which from affection for them or participation in them may be said in some manner to be powerful, wise and good, *269* but not substantially power, wisdom, goodness. For that which is receivable and participable is not the same as that which receives and participates; for the receiving and participating thing is such when it neither receives nor participates, and therefore it is subject to mutability and can be corrupted: but the receivable and participable, abiding ever immutably in its own essence, can never be corrupted; but no creature is receivable and participable; it is therefore God. When therefore we ask what the *fruit* of this vineyard is, and attend to all the circumstances of its virtue and beauty. [1261A] beyond doubt we conclude at the last that it is God. But by reception and participation the rational creature attains this, that at some time it may pass into incorruption and immutability, not by nature but by grace; and thus, from man working and God co-operating, this fruit is as it were born and grows, which is the reward of all who pertain to the kingdom of God, and is called true beatitude. But true beatitude is God himself, whom to have and whom to enjoy eternally is truly to be blessed. This *fruit*, therefore, is God.

Further, what shall we say of the quality of such a fruit, since it is written of it that *eye hath not seen, nor ear heard, neither hath it entered into the heart of man? [n: (I Cor. II.)]* Nevertheless above we distinguished the same fruit by three colours, which pertains rather to quality, [1261B] not that the difference of the colours makes a different fruit, but that this diversity so inseparably coheres in one substance that it in no way takes away any virtue and efficacy of its property. For just as, for example, three colours come together in some one subject, and one has the name so that it is called red, another white, the third green, yet one and the same is the substance which contains them; so in this fruit, though power is not one thing, wisdom another, and goodness another, yet the fruit is one and the same, and has everywhere one and the same virtue and efficacy, namely to be able, to be wise, to quicken, which nevertheless bring it about that there is nothing good which is lacking to its fullness.

[1261C]But what is to be said of the quantity of this fruit, since it is of such immensity that it is circumscribed by no place, enclosed by no boundary? Yet it seems to have certain extents, not according to itself, but according to the participant: but nothing can be a participant of this thing save the rational creature. Further, the rational *270* creature is in one kind spiritual, as the angel and the soul, in the other corporeal, as the elements and the things which are composed of them. But since this is not the place to dispute about the dimensions of a corporeal thing, which are constituted by line, surface, or solid body, while this fruit, of whose quantity we began to speak, is not corporeal but spiritual; *for God is a spirit [n: (Joan. IV)]*, of whose quantity, because he is incomprehensible and uncircumscribed, [1261D] no one can either think or speak; let us nevertheless now see what are the extents which it can have, not according to itself, but according to the participant. For whatever of goodness, honourableness, holiness, discipline or knowledge the participating soul can have, it takes from the participable God, as it were the vigour of grace flowing forth into it; and through this the participant seems to have something longer or shorter, broader or narrower, higher or lower, according as it pleases him to distribute who gives grace *to every one* according to *the measure of faith [n: (Rom. XII)]*. Whence the Apostle, speaking to those whom he was exhorting to receive such dimensions: *In charity,* he says, *rooted and founded, that you may be able to comprehend with all the saints what is the length, [1262A] breadth, height and depth [n: (Ephes. II)]* [var: Vulg. *latitudo, et longitudo, et sublimitas*, "breadth, and length, and height"].

But since this fruit of which we speak is born and grows from the root of charity, and comes to maturity as it were to perfection, not as regards God who is to be grasped, who is always perfect, but as regards the capable mind of man, which is subject to increase and loss, so that by advancing in this manner it may at some time comprehend God, with whom in its own manner it may become perfect and unchangeable, let us first see concerning our Lord Jesus Christ himself how, according to his humanity, he received in himself from the root of charity all these dimensions, namely length, breadth, height and depth: for who had greater charity than he, who laid down his life for his sheep? [n: (Joan. XV.)]

[1262B]But if we set the eyes of the mind on the kind and quality of his passion, we shall see all these dimensions venerably contained, and every Christian soul manfully promoted by this example. For in the figure of the cross are to be noted these four dimensions, namely length, height, breadth and depth, which from the root of Christ's charity are stretched out into the whole world, signifying and admonishing every soul which has been redeemed by his passion that, if it wishes to attain the aforesaid fruit, that is, eternal beatitude, it must reach it by the dimensions of the same charity, according to the measure and nature granted to it.

*271* For charity ought to be broad, because it ought to be extended even [1262C] to the love of enemies, as Christ hanging on the cross prayed for his enemies, saying: *Father, forgive them, because they know not what they do [n: (Luc. XXIII)]*. It ought to be long, because not for an hour but to the end of life, as it is written of Christ: *Having loved his own, he loved them unto the end [n: (Joan. XIII)]*; for charity ought to have such great perseverance that it fails for no tribulation or distress. It ought to be high, because of the final cause, namely that it attend for what it acts or suffers, that is, for eternal beatitude, which is this fruit of which we speak, for the sake of which it is admonished always to tend upward and to have its conversation in heaven; it ought to have depth, because it ought never to be without fear, but always to attend [1262D] that whatever it attains from breadth, length and height, it has not from its own merits but from the mercy of God; and therefore, because it knows not the hidden and deep judgment of God, why he should choose one and reject another, and the end is always uncertain while *many* are called, *few* are chosen [sic: *cliguntur*] [n: (Matth. XX)], it ought never to be without fear.

Since therefore *in Christ dwelleth all the fullness of the Godhead corporeally [n: (Coloss. II)]*, and to him *God doth not give the spirit by measure [n: (Joan. III)]*, while *all* the saints receive *of his fullness*, *grace for grace [n: (Joan. I)]*, they seem to reach to the extents of these dimensions, each in his own manner or measure, according as he gives to them who has in full and can give of his fullness as much as he will.

[1263A]Further, since charity, set like a centre in the middle, touches all the virtues as it were through the circumference, for every virtue descends from the root of charity; and since every motion has six species, for there is before and behind, up and down, to the right, to the left; to whatever part the mind of man directs its intention for attaining such a fruit, it always receives the origin of every good motion from the vigour of charity. And since man himself, called the lesser world, consists after the likeness of the greater world of four elements, he is also measured by four parts, by which he is wholly contained, if he has been established for preserving the likeness of God, namely prudence, justice, temperance, fortitude.

These four parts being stretched out from the centre of the mind, that is, [1263B] from charity, at an equal distance, whatever of virtue can be in the soul, thou, as with a circle, both touchest by carrying round and enclosest.

This, therefore, can in some manner be the measure or dimension which this fruit, participating, can have, not according to itself, as has been said, but according to the participant, so that when at some time it has been attained by the participant as it is, *272* it may take away from then on all passibility or mutability of dimension in growing, and confer perfectly eternal impassibility, immutability, immortality.
