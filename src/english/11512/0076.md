## BOOK FOUR. (cont.)

This fruit, therefore, is true beatitude, and true beatitude is God, whose quality and immensity is incomprehensible. But since we now know what is the fruit of the aforesaid vineyard, let us now see concerning the [1263C] one who offers, who he is, since he is said to be a *man*, and could not offer unless he were a *man*. For *man* is so called from virtue, and he who ought to offer for so great a fruit must shine with the beauty and grace of the virtues, because an oblation will not be accepted which no brightness of the virtues commends. For in the matter of what is given and received the form ought to be this: that there be in the giver such composure of bearing, and such grace of honourableness and discipline, that it bring forth in the receiver good pleasure and cheerfulness of countenance, and render the gift more acceptable. And if mortals affect this good of discipline and honourableness among themselves, and men approve it, what do we think the omnipotent and immortal God wills and seeks from us, whose nature is goodness, holiness, singular [1263D] honourableness and discipline? Therefore he who offers to such a Lord ought to be *clothed* with *strength* and girded with virtue [n: (Psal. XCII)]: with strength of mind, that is, for restraining and repelling vices and sins, with virtue of spirit for doing and exercising good works. For that oblation is not right which the darkening of guilt repels, since the Lord said even to Cain: *If thou offer rightly, but divide not rightly, thou hast sinned [n: (Gen. IV)]* [var: Vulg. *nonne si bene egeris, recipies*, "if thou do well, shalt thou not receive?"]. He offered rightly indeed, who gave the fruits of the earth to God, but he did not divide rightly, who kept his heart in evil and stained his oblation with the plague of envy: for he ought to have separated the good from the evil, because there is no participation of light with darkness, nor can there be together in the sight of God [1264A] truth and falsehood, equity and malice. He offered rightly indeed, because he offered to God, to whom he ought; but he did not divide rightly, because what was better he rightly denied to God, what was viler he gave: for he is not dear enough to God, nay, he is unworthy of God, who offers his goods to him indeed, but keeps himself for himself.

Such a man, therefore, the Prophet describes in the first psalm, where he represents the bearing and purpose of such a man; and since that psalm is known to those who understand, whoever desires to take on this man, let him meditate on it continually and exercise it in deed: in another place also he mentions this same man briefly, saying: *Blessed is the man who feareth the Lord, he desireth exceedingly in his commandments [n: (Psal. CXI)]* [var: Vulg. *volet nimis*, "he shall delight exceedingly"], and the rest; for this is what makes this man, and blessed, since it belongs to virtue [1264B] to fear God, and to consider and keep his commandments; but to beatitude, that for this, *his seed shall be mighty upon earth, and his justice remaineth for ever and ever (ibid)*.

*273* The offerer having been described, whom it befits to be a man, it is now to be seen what the man brings, and ought to bring, for such a fruit. *A thousand,* he says, *pieces of silver.* The number thousand is put for perfection, and silver designates the pure and bright sense of chaste words, as it is written: *The words of the Lord are chaste words, silver tried by the fire [n: (Psal. XI)]*. But what can we call a *piece of silver* formed and inscribed from this silver? If we look diligently, what is it that forms or inscribes in the mind of man the image of God, the image of holiness and justice, [1264C] save *the commandment of the Lord, lightsome, enlightening the eyes [n: (Psal. XVIII)]*, the eyes, namely, of the inner man? And where do we hear the commandment of the Lord, and whence do we know it, save from Holy Scripture, which is the speech of the Lord? This Scripture, therefore, well understood, forms, and makes as it were a kind of inscription in the mind of man, so that when from the reading of Holy Scripture he has perfectly taken on charity *from a pure heart and a good conscience, and an unfeigned faith [n: (II Tim. II)]*, he receives the coin of a certain piece of silver, inscribed in this manner: *to the image and likeness of God,* for this form the Maker of things himself appointed to be impressed on man in creating him, when he said: *Let us make man to our image and likeness [n: (Gen. I)]*, [1264D] which is to say: Let us make man living, rational, like to God: for the condition of man excels all living things by a twofold honour, when it is said *to our image and likeness; to the image,* namely, that he may be rational, to *our likeness,* that he may be upright by following the justice of God. The one of these, namely rationality, he cannot lack; the other, that is, the likeness of the divine uprightness or justice, he cannot attain save through grace: but the faculty of rationality, which man cannot lack, is the instrument, or as it were a kind of eye, for seeking the likeness of God. He is therefore inexcusable when he has not come to the likeness of God, [1265A] because, namely, he would not use a good instrument for that for which it was given him.

But when God first made man, he made him not only rational, but also endowed with the honour of his likeness, *For God,* says Scripture, *made man upright, but he entangled himself with many questions [n: (Eccle. VII)]*. But now, seduced, he has lost indeed the honour of the likeness of God, but the faculty of reason has remained in him, through which he may be able to hear him who calls him back and to follow him as leader and teacher to his restoration. Such silver, therefore, that divine and highest money-changer inscribed in the soul of man, when he made it rational and a partaker of his holiness.

*274* Wherefore man is a debtor to God, so that, since God [1265B] marked him with this twofold honour, he should understand his debt, namely that, being capable of reason, with reason itself as leader, he should always advance to the likeness of God through holiness, lest, if he understand not, he should be compared *to senseless beasts, and* become *like to them [n: (Psal. XLVIII)]*. Finally, that man owes this debt to God the Lord shows in the Gospel, since, when he was tempted by the Scribes and Pharisees saying: *Whether it be lawful to give tribute to Caesar or not,* he answered: *Show me the coin of the tribute;* and when they had shown it, he said: *Whose is the image and inscription?* And when they answered: *Caesar's: Render,* he says, *the things that are Caesar's to Caesar, and the things that are God's to God [n: (Matth. XXII)]*.

By this sentence of his, therefore, the Lord shows, and by showing teaches, that just as it is just that to a [1265C] mortal man the things which are his, and which have been given by him, be rendered in tributary subjection, so it is just, nay most just, that to the omnipotent and immortal God the things which are his, and which have been granted by him to man, be brought with the highest devotion and humble subjection. Now reason and understanding have been granted to man by God, which he ought so to use that by living well he may advance to the likeness of God, and receive in his soul and mind, from the brightness of holiness, this inscription of the piece of silver: *to the image and likeness of God.*

Such a piece of silver, therefore, one or two or three or more, according to the measure of God's gift, some faithful man offers, that he may purchase that fruit, [1265D] that is, eternal life, which works through love, tends and advances to the likeness of God. But he *brings a thousand pieces of silver,* who by the brightness of perfect holiness has attained the image and likeness of God.

Or else the thousandfold unity signifies one faith, one baptism, one God who worketh all in all; and he who desires that *fruit*, that is, eternal life, must hold and teach the true and right faith, must have and worship one God and one man, must adorn with faith and works the name of Christianity which he received in baptism, must read, teach, embrace and follow the true, catholic, and one Holy Scripture. When, therefore, the beloved says these things of herself to the grace and glory [1266A] of the beloved, he at once answers and says: *The vineyard,* etc.

VERSE 12.-- *My vineyard is before me. A thousand are thy peaceable ones, and two hundred for them that keep the fruit thereof.* [var: Vulg. *fructus*, "the fruits"]

*My vineyard,* he says, *is before me,* that is, I know and approve my *275* vineyard, from this, that I find in it the grace of my planting; if I did not find it, it could not be before me, nor could it be called my vineyard. For of you who can be called and be my vineyard I spoke by the prophet, that *I will look upon you and make you grow; you shall be multiplied, and I will establish my covenant with you [n: (Levit. XXVI)]*: for those who are so before me that they are worthy of my presence and sight, shining with the brightness of good works, I acknowledge and keep; because *the eyes of the Lord are upon the just [n: (Psal. XXXIII)]*, just as on the contrary, [1266B] those who set me behind them by despising, them too I set behind me by reprobating, and therefore by not bestowing the help of my grace.

*A thousand are thy peaceable ones, and two hundred for them that keep the fruits thereof;* from the opposite of that vineyard which was turned into perversity, of which it is said that *I looked that it should bring forth the grape, but it brought forth wild grapes [n: (Isa. V)]*, the sense of this chapter will better appear. For the Lord says by Osee of her, under the figure of a harlot: *And I bought her to me for fifteen pieces of silver, and for a core of barley, and for half a core of barley [n: (Ose. III)]*: now a most beautiful sacrament is hidden in these numbers; here *in a thousand pieces of silver,* there *in fifteen pieces of silver, and a core of barley and half a core of barley;* the sum of which is this, that this one is both a virgin and joined to a husband, that one [1266C] neither a virgin nor joined to a husband; for the thousandfold unity signifies the virginity of this one, but her conjunction or nuptials the hundredfold two. Arithmeticians or abacists know better what is said, and by what reason unity is pronounced a virgin they have in use in their treatises, namely that multiplied into itself it creates nothing, because once one is one, which befalls no other number: for the other numbers, led into themselves, create others, as twice two four, and the third, nine: further, *the hundredfold two designates her nuptials,* and this is most happy, because this beloved who is of faith is both always joined to a husband and always remains a virgin, always brings forth, yet always remains a virgin.
