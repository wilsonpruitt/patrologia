## *282* IN PLACE OF AN EPILOGUE, THE AUTHOR'S PARAENESIS To the sacred virgins for whom he wrote and to whom he inscribed these commentaries.

[1271]

[1271D]Behold, beloved sisters, as we were able we have sung to you, and as much as that highest master of ours has granted, under whose discipline are all who seek the knowledge of singing rightly and well. But since the knowledge of singing well and rightly consists of a threefold instrument, namely *heart, mouth* and *work,* I am convicted of not being a skilled singer. With the mouth indeed I can sing, but if the other two are lacking, every form of my singing will be empty before the Lord: for to sing with the heart and with work can please God, even if one sing not with the mouth; but to sing with the mouth only, and not [1272D] with the heart and with work, it is impossible to be able to please God. Further, he who wishes to have the perfect knowledge of singing, and to know its highest dignity, must always have the faculty of these three. The Apostle, showing that he knew and had it: *I will sing,* he says, *with the spirit, I will sing also with the mind [n: (I Cor. XIV)]*. For to sing with the spirit is both to utter spiritual words from memory, which is to sing with the mouth, and to proceed to the works conceived in the spirit, which is to sing with work; to sing with the mind is to perceive with the mind the living sense of the words, which is to sing with the heart.

[1273A]The instrument, then, having been given for attaining the knowledge of singing rightly and well, there is need that we should also know what is first to be known in singing, and what canticle, that we may come to the canticle of canticles. For first we must learn and sing that new canticle to the singing of which the Holy Spirit exhorts us in the Psalm: *Sing ye,* he says, *to the Lord a new canticle, his praise in the Church of the saints [n: (Psal. CXLIX)]*. If thou consider the quality of relatives, where *new* is said, it follows that there is also an old. But let us first see what the old canticle is, that from the knowledge of it we may better understand the *new*.

That old canticle the old man Adam sang and taught, but it is greatly opposed and contrary to the new canticle which was heard afterwards, and distant from it by that distance [1273B] which is between the mortifying and the vivifying. And what is that canticle? Surely that by which that old man cried out against his Maker, and clamoured against justice and piety, namely pride, disobedience and the excusing of his sin. For it was just that he should be subject to his maker and ruler, and obey; but it was wretched that after his fault he did not acknowledge himself, *283* nor presume upon the piety of God, but, a despiser of his fault, an exile from paradise, he transmitted its punishment to all his posterity. The more many sing the doleful tragedy of this canticle, by imitating his presumption and negligence, the greater the vengeance of the imposed death they pay.

But opposed to this canticle is that *new [1273C] canticle,* the canticle of peace and gladness, the canticle of life and justice, the canticle of virtue and salvation, which that new man sang before us, and not simply man, but God and man; for by such a precentor, who said: *Learn of me, because I am meek and humble of heart, and you shall find rest to your souls [n: (Matth. XI)]*, we are admonished to sing the new canticle of usefulness [cj: *utilitatis*; read *humilitatis*, "of humility"]. Further, by the example of the Lord himself, of whom the Apostle says: Christ *became obedient* to God *the Father unto death [n: (Philip. II)]*, we are admonished to sing the canticle of sweet obedience; at the voice also of the same new precentor, who says: *Do penance, for the kingdom of heaven will draw near [n: (Matth. IV)]*, we are admonished not to incline *our heart to evil words, to make excuses [1273D] in sins [n: (Psal. CXLVI)]*; because this is the most grievous, nay the most wretched voice of the old impenitent man, the voice, I say, of the old canticle full of sorrow; but rather, the manner of such a canticle being converted and changed into the new, let us sing together to God a melody of the contrite spirit, laborious indeed as regards the body but wholesome as regards the soul, in the genus of four modes, that is, compunction, confession, satisfaction, perseverance; and on the four finals of these modes, namely thought, speech, action and custom, let us daily make our cadence.

For of both canticles, that is, of the old and of the new, these are the finals, thought, speech, action, and custom, and according to the form of the preceding mode [1274A] they take their cadence; either the elegiac as regards the old, or the sweet and musical as regards the new. For the musical cadence is that which is made by a reasonable conjunction, disposition, and tenor of voices or tones, and this the new canticle takes more, nay always, on account of the reasonable motion of the mind singing well and beautifully, and always tending to a good end by thinking, speaking, acting, being accustomed. On the contrary, the elegiac, this is a sad and wretched cadence, is that which is made by an irrational and unequal motion of the mind; and because the form of such singing is confused, it generates an unpleasant [sic: *in suavem*] and harsh quality of cadence.

There is therefore between such two canticles that distance which is between life and death; because the old [1274B] kills, but the new gives life, and this by a likeness; for certain sea beasts are spoken of, which are called sirens, which, while they allure and draw sailors by the sweetness of their song, *284* suddenly sink the incautious and negligent: and in this manner, while all carnal men in the sea of this world, sailing as it were, are held by the sweetness of the carnal life, by the delight of sin, suddenly, neglecting the salvation of their soul, and unwilling to be converted through penance, they are snatched by unclean spirits to eternal death. And this is the mode of the old canticle, since, beginning from the spirit of pride and disobedience, it makes its last cadence in the abyss of eternal damnation with the most grievous and most wretched sound of an impenitent heart.

Further, on the other side, there is that likeness which is [1274C] shown us from divine and human Scripture. From the divine, where it is written that David with his harp calmed the fury of Saul [n: (I Reg. XVI)]: from the human, where it is written that Orpheus by his song soothed tigers and lions; and that Amphion by the sound of the lyre [sic: *testitudinis*] moved stones; which signifies that this is the power of the new canticle, that it calms the bestial minds of men raging against their own salvation, and composes the hard hearts of mortals, by a reasonable motion of words and senses, to a good manner of living and a reasonable honourableness of morals. And this is the mode of the new canticle, which, beginning from the spirit of humility and obedience, makes its last cadence in the joy of eternal beatitude with the highest and most joyful sound of an innocent or penitent heart.

[1274D]As therefore life and death are contrary to each other, so the old and the new canticle are contrary to each other, according to the qualities of the persons by whose authority each canticle takes its beginning and end. For the author of the old canticle is the devil, but the author of the new canticle is Christ. Wherefore, despising the author of death, let us follow the author of life, and, continually attending to his delightful and most sweet harmony of holiness, let us pass by with deaf ear the foolish voices of the sirens, that is, the foolish persuasions of malignant spirits.

But the workshops and dwellings in which the instruments of this new and wholesome canticle are prepared and kept, and in which we may hear and learn the canticle itself, and by the custom and exercise of singing [1275A] may come to that Canticle of canticles whose sweetness and delightfulness will never fail, are the beloved tabernacles of the Lord, of which once that diviner, who said that he was a hearer of the words of God, and one beholding the vision of God, who falleth, and so hath his eyes open, was about to inquire:

*How* beloved, he says, *are thy tabernacles, O Jacob, and thy tents, O Israel; as woody valleys, as watered gardens beside the rivers; as tabernacles which the Lord hath pitched, as cedars by many waters [n: (Num. XXIV)]*. Now the tabernacles of the true Israel and spiritual Jacob, sojourning or serving as a soldier in the present age, are the religious, and those well ordered according to the *285* discipline of Christ, namely the orders of the married, the continent, widows and virgins, according to [1275B] either sex; these tabernacles plainly are each beautiful in its own purpose.

*How beautiful the tabernacles, how beautiful the tents? as woody valleys, as watered gardens beside the rivers.* This likeness is clear from other places of Scripture: for in Isaias the Lord, promising to the gentiles, once forsaken, the merits of spiritual virtues like trees to a desert: *I will give,* he says, *in the wilderness the cedar and the thorn, the myrtle and the olive tree: I will set in the desert the fir tree, the elm and the box tree together, that they may see, and know, and consider, and understand together [n: (Isa. XLI)]*. Therefore *the tabernacles are beautiful as woody valleys. Valleys* on account of humility, *woody* on account of the beauty of diverse virtues.

[1275C]For in these tabernacles of God, that is, in holy professions, there are cedars, that is, saints incorrupt and incorruptible in faith and work, and fragrant with the odour of the virtues: there are *thorns,* pricking others by word and example and provoking them to good works: there is the *myrtle,* tempering the tribulations of neighbours through compassion by the word of consolation: there are *olives,* overflowing with acts of mercy and piety; there are *firs,* rising ever on high by virtue for contemplation: there are *elms,* who, though secular and less fruitful, yet sustain and help others who live well with their means: there is the *box,* who, though not rising to the heights of the virtues, nor bearing fruit, yet live always by the faith of their parents: [1275D] these tabernacles of the Lord are therefore *as woody valleys,* because they draw this from a humble heart, and from lowly action also and bearing, that they grow green and flower with the fullness of such great virtues.

They are also *as watered gardens beside the rivers,* as it is said elsewhere: *And thou shalt be like a watered garden, and like a fountain of waters whose waters shall not fail [n: (Isa. LVIII)]*. And in the same canticles: *A garden enclosed, a fountain sealed, the fountain of gardens, the well of living waters, which run with a strong stream from Libanus [n: (Cant. IV)]*. Behold *the tabernacles beautiful as watered gardens beside the rivers.* Because from the moisture of the Holy Spirit they are always green and flowering in speech and action, and in the assiduous meditation of the divine precepts.

They are also *cedars* by the waters; which the Psalmist [1276A] shows, who, when he had said: *Watering the hills from his upper rooms,* etc., continued at once: *The plains,* he says, *shall be filled, and the cedars of Libanus which he hath planted [n: (Psal CIII)]* [var: Vulg. *Saturabuntur ligna campi*, "the trees of the field shall be filled"]. Therefore *the tabernacles are beautiful as cedars by the waters,* that is, beside the waters, namely, in which the sparrows make their nests [n: (Psal. CIII)], by whose abundance, when they are rich men, the need of the poor is supplied.

Therefore these tabernacles are *286* as *tabernacles which the Lord hath pitched,* because all this their beauty, adornment and ordering is the work of the omnipotent God, who by his grace places, advances, adorns and sustains them.

*Water shall flow out of his bucket, and his seed shall be in many waters [n: (Num. XXIV)]*. What is *his bucket*? Nay, what is *his water*? Surely *of the true Jacob,* that is, *the supplanter,* [1276B] or of the true Israel, that is, *the mind seeing the Lord.* Some of whom dwell in these tabernacles, that is, professions. Whose *bucket* is Christ, from whom water is drawn, that is, Holy Scripture. For through him Holy Scripture is opened, nay by him thirst is aroused, that this water may be desired; by him the sense is opened, that the Scriptures may be understood. *Water* therefore *shall flow out of his bucket,* that is, the true understanding of the Scriptures shall stream from his Christ. What next? *And his seed shall be in many waters.* What is *water out of his bucket,* this is the multitude of waters from his seed. For *his seed, the seed of his father Abraham [n: (Gen. XVII)]*, namely of him who truly is *the lofty Father,* or *the Father of many nations,* Christ.
