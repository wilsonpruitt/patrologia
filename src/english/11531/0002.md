## CHAPTER V.

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Certainly, when anything is presumed against the divine commandment, there is action taken through contumacy against majesty. ⟦s2⟧ But when we wish to propitiate the majesty we have injured, we have recourse to its mercy. ⟦s3⟧ And so, to propitiate majesty, we have recourse to its goodness — and not to that alone, but rather to its truth as well. ⟦s4⟧ For pardon has beyond doubt been promised [0999C] to those who truly repent, and that by him, by him, I say, who is utterly unable to lie. ⟦s5⟧ And so we have God's mercy and God's truth as a twofold anchor of hope amid the storms of temptations and the shipwrecks of transgressions, lest, falling into the Charybdis of despair, we flow utterly away. ⟦s6⟧ These are they that lead us, after many shipwrecks, to the harbor of salvation. ⟦s7⟧ These are they that bring us out of the greatest perils into the citadel of true security: *God hath sent,* says the Prophet, *his mercy and his truth, and hath delivered my soul [n: (Psal., LVI)]*. ⟦s8⟧ And so, after many crimes committed, returning often to the laments of penitence, we set God's truth and God's mercy over against God, that we may be able to appease the divine wrath. ⟦s9⟧ You know, then, that [0999D] this name God, is common to the three. ⟦s10⟧ You know likewise that it is the usage of Holy Scripture to attribute power to the Father, wisdom to the Son, goodness to the Holy Spirit, as it were in a special way. ⟦s11⟧ But why the several are attributed to the several, we have already written to you elsewhere, my Bernard, and we ought not to repeat it here. ⟦s12⟧ By the consideration, therefore, of certain properties, majesty belongs specially to the Father, truth to the Son, kindness to the Holy Spirit. ⟦s13⟧ When, therefore, we seek to appease the majesty we have injured, what else do we strive for than to soften the offense given to the Father? ⟦s14⟧ But when we demand the mercy promised to the penitent, and the truth of the promise, what else do we attempt than, with the Holy Spirit mediating together with the Son, to soothe the Father's wrath? ⟦s15⟧ When we importune the highest truth, the highest wisdom, [1000A] we invoke the Son. ⟦s16⟧ To implore the highest clemency, the highest goodness — what else is it than to entreat the Holy Spirit? ⟦s17⟧ It is wisdom that drives out the darkness of error, goodness that drives out the cold of iniquity. ⟦s18⟧ It is wisdom that keeps us amid the horrors of the night, goodness that keeps us amid the chills of the night. ⟦s19⟧ The Son of God, therefore, is a keeper; the Holy Spirit is a keeper. ⟦s20⟧ For the one frees us from error, the other from iniquity. ⟦s21⟧ Since, therefore, in these two we have access to the principal light, it is rightly doubled now, when it is said: Keeper, what of the night? keeper, what of the night? ⟦s22⟧ And there is added, and the keeper said. ⟦s23⟧ Behold, a third time the name of keeper is repeated, because the Trinity of persons is found in one Godhead. ⟦s24⟧ For rightly can the Father himself also [1000B] be called our keeper. ⟦s25⟧ For as it belongs to truth to protect from error, and as it belongs to goodness to protect from iniquity, so it belongs to majesty to protect from any adversity whatever.

## CHAPTER VI.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But let us discuss the letter itself yet more subtly, and from the things that are said let us diligently investigate what ought to be applied to which person: To me he cries out of Seir: Keeper, what of the night? ⟦s2⟧ Unless he acknowledged himself to be the keeper, he would surely not rightly say, he cries to me, in that it is said: Keeper, what of the night? keeper, what of the night? ⟦s3⟧ What is it, then, that he adds afterward? ⟦s4⟧ And the keeper said. ⟦s5⟧ He does not say I said, but, the keeper said, as if he himself were not the keeper. ⟦s6⟧ Or is he perhaps both a keeper, he who is invoked, and a keeper likewise, he by whom the answer is given — and yet not one and another, but one and [1000C] the same Lord and God? ⟦s7⟧ But that first there is set down me, and afterward there is set down said, shows a plurality of persons in one Godhead. ⟦s8⟧ Again, in that he does not say, he cries to us, but, he cries to me, we understand that only one person is invoked, in that it is doubled: Keeper, what of the night? keeper, what of the night? ⟦s9⟧ But perhaps this too is asked, which is that person who is here specially invoked. ⟦s10⟧ This we surely know, that whatever the Son has, he has from the Father; and therefore both that he is able to keep and that he is wont to keep. ⟦s11⟧ What I say of the Son, this I say of the Holy Spirit. ⟦s12⟧ The Father, therefore, keeps through the Son, keeps through the Holy Spirit, keeps through himself. ⟦s13⟧ If, therefore, in that invocation it were repeated a third time, keeper, [1000D] what of the night, it could not fittingly be understood except of the Father. ⟦s14⟧ For the Son indeed keeps through himself, keeps through the Holy Spirit, but not through his Father. ⟦s15⟧ But the Holy Spirit alone in the Trinity does not keep through himself: for no person in the Trinity receives from him that he should do anything or be able to do anything. ⟦s16⟧ Since, therefore, there is a doubling of keeping in the invocation, we cannot rightly understand this of the Holy Spirit. ⟦s17⟧ For the doubling of the invocation compels the saying to be understood of the Son. ⟦s18⟧ The Son, therefore, is invoked when it is said: Keeper, what of the night? keeper, what of the night? ⟦s19⟧ But behold, when one person is invoked, another is bent. ⟦s20⟧ Bent, I say, not only to peace, but also to the promise of what is desired. ⟦s21⟧ For that is promised which is desired by [1001A] the suppliant, when it is said: Morning comes. ⟦s22⟧ We have seen which person it is that is invoked; let us see also which it is that is appeased. ⟦s23⟧ But, to say it briefly, the principal person is understood, for the appeasing of whom another is interposed. ⟦s24⟧ Now the Father is the principle of the Only-begotten, and the Only-begotten is the principle also of the Holy Spirit. ⟦s25⟧ And so, just as in the mediator and propitiator the begotten is understood, so in the one propitiated the unbegotten is understood, and not the Holy Spirit. ⟦s26⟧ For it does not befit the Holy Spirit to demand satisfaction for that which the Son pardons, from whom the same Spirit has all that he has. ⟦s27⟧ You now see, as I believe, more plainly what we have already said above, how in this, that keeper is found a third time, the Trinity of persons is understood. ⟦s28⟧ For the Son is first called keeper [1001B] because he keeps through himself; a second time he is named the same because he keeps through the Holy Spirit. ⟦s29⟧ And so in the first placing the Son alone is understood; in the second the Holy Spirit also is understood together with him; in the third, the unbegotten alone. ⟦s30⟧ The unbegotten alone is understood in that which is written: The keeper said: Morning comes, and night. ⟦s31⟧ What is it to say, unless to bring forth a word? ⟦s32⟧ For the Father to say is to utter forth truth. ⟦s33⟧ For the Father to say is to beget wisdom. ⟦s34⟧ But he says to us then, when he makes his good pleasure known to us by the revelation of his wisdom. ⟦s35⟧ Then something is promised to us according to our desire by the inward speech of the Father, when a firm certainty of our hope and expectation is poured into our heart. ⟦s36⟧ Now, I believe, it is plain to understand to which person that ought to belong which is said: [1001C] The keeper said. ⟦s37⟧ Morning comes, and night. ⟦s38⟧ We have seen what it is for God to say or to promise.

## CHAPTER VII.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Let us now see of what sort is that which he promises: Morning comes, and night. ⟦s2⟧ Under the night, as was said above, of great tribulation they groaned, and panted after the day of a long-desired prosperity. ⟦s3⟧ What is it, then, that in the promise there is set before them not only the morning which they wanted, but also the night which they did not want? ⟦s4⟧ But the one is promised for consolation, the other foretold for instruction, and both for profit: Morning comes, and night. ⟦s5⟧ What is morning but the end of night, and what is night but the end of light? ⟦s6⟧ In one morning we have the end of night and the beginning [1001D] of day. ⟦s7⟧ Morning comes, therefore, because the night at which you shudder will now, behold, be ended, and the day for which you so greatly sigh will be begun in an instant. ⟦s8⟧ But when the long-hoped-for day has come upon you, it will not abide, because morning comes, and night. ⟦s9⟧ As if he were saying more plainly: The night shall so pass away as to return again; the day shall so return as to withdraw again. ⟦s10⟧ Morning comes, and night. ⟦s11⟧ If you are in the night, do not despair, because morning will come; if you are in the day, do not be confident, because night will come. ⟦s12⟧ Morning, therefore, is promised lest those who labor fail; the night to come is foretold that they may become wary. ⟦s13⟧ The one, surely, for a remedy; the other for a caution. ⟦s14⟧ Morning comes, and night. ⟦s15⟧ But what else is it to say these things to anyone, than to show the mutability of this time and the variety of alternating vicissitudes, and to form the mind to a contempt both of worldly prosperity and [1002A] of worldly adversity, and to provoke it to the desire of things eternal? ⟦s16⟧ He says therefore: Morning comes, and night.
