## CHAPTER VIII.

Behold, we have now heard what the things already said sound according to the history. Let us hear further what they themselves signify according to the mystical understanding: Keeper, what of the night? keeper what of the night? The keeper said: Morning comes, and night, as has already been said above, many not only of the Jews but also of the Gentiles had foreknown the coming of Christ and were awaiting it with great desire, according to that word: *Behold, there shall come the Desired of all nations [n: (Agg. II)]*. He does not say, he who is to be desired, but, the Desired, that you may understand that in every nation some at some time burned with desire for him. His coming, therefore, was foreknown by many [1002B] of the Gentiles, and ardently desired. Yet they had risen to this knowledge little by little, and step by step through intervals of time. For first, certain of them, divinely illumined and taught by natural reason, understood in how great a darkness almost the whole human race was wrapped, in that it was in bondage to a multiformity of idolatry so deformed, and to a deformity so multiform, that it said to wood and to stone: Thou art my God. Yet not even these same men were able to understand fully under how great a burden of captivity they labored — even those who believed in and worshipped the one and true God. Others afterward, more deeply illumined, understood more fully the misery of our captivity and the sentence of damnation: [1002C] how human nature after sin, the wound of mortality having been inflicted upon it, was cast out of paradise into the world, as from a fatherland into exile, and was afterward to be cast out by death from the world into hell, as from exile into a house of bondage. And so they understood the greatness of the peril, but they were not equally able to understand the manner of escaping. And so neither those, nor yet these, had escaped the darkness of the night of the earth, who were darkened under the wrapping of so great an ignorance. Others, searching still more deeply and gazing more subtly, discerned by the leading hand of grace, found and beheld with much gazing not only the greatness of the damnation, but also the manner of reparation. And so those who were of this sort understood the manner of restoration, but concerning this [1002D] very thing they did not know the divine good pleasure. But last of all, to certain men not only of the Jews but also of the Gentiles the divine purpose in this very matter was revealed, so that they foreknew the mystery of our reparation in so great a part, and some of them even preached it, and some at length even committed it to writing. To these things the proclamations of the prophets and the oracles of the nations bear witness. But, passing over the rest, let us for the present attend only to those who, as we have said, understood the manner of restoration, but did not know the divine disposition in that very matter. And so, with reason as their guide, yet with the light of divine revelation going before, they understood that, if man had not sinned, he could have ascended to the supernal beatitude by the mediation of justice. But if by mercy alone, with no justice intervening, [1003A] he should receive after his fall what he had lost, or even what had been promised, he would bear the everlasting reproach of his own casting down. For the devil could always cast it in his teeth that he had no right in that which he obtained; and thus it would be established that he had not recovered his ancient dignity either. But where there would not be a full reparation of the first dignity, neither would there be a full *resturatio*. In order, therefore, that man might rise again according to the reckoning of justice, satisfaction was needed. And this too is added to that: because, if man paid no satisfaction, even if he had outwardly no exactor of his fault, he would never fully blot out the exaction of a biting conscience and the mark of confusion. Did not Eve believe the serpent rather than her own and so beneficent Creator? Did not Adam choose rather to offend the Almighty than [1003B] his own wife? How then, after the boldness of so great a presumption, would they obtain a tranquil conscience, or blot out from the heart the mark of confusion, if they made satisfaction in nothing? But now the devotion of the faithful glories more in the satisfaction of its redemption than it was formerly confounded by the reproach of so great a casting down — so much so that everywhere on earth the Church of the faithful sings with all confidence. O truly necessary sin of Adam, and ours, which has been blotted out by the death of Christ! O happy fault, which merited to have so great and such a Redeemer! A clear reason, therefore, teaches that without satisfaction man could not be fully repaired. But for the fullness of satisfaction it was needful that the humiliation in the expiation [1003C] be as great as the presumption had been in the transgression. Now of rational substance God holds the highest place, but man the lowest. When, therefore, man presumed against God, there was made a lifting up from the lowest to the highest. It was needful, therefore, that for the remedy of expiation there be made a humiliation from the highest to the lowest; but this none at all could do save some one of the persons in the Trinity. Again, if man could recover, and did recover, justice through a creature rather than through the Creator, and through the mediation of justice the glory of the beatitude to come, would he not thenceforth, against the excellence of his first dignity, be more beholden to the creature than to the Creator, to the thing made than to the maker? For it is surely more to be justified and beatified than to be created. And so for the full restoration of man there would in no way suffice [1003D] a person who was not God. But it is likewise agreed that, as far as the reckoning of justice goes, nothing would pertain to man of whatever any person whatsoever might do who was not man. But who would not see that it is equitable and full of right and altogether reasonable that a son make satisfaction for a father, a brother for a brother, if the one cannot, if the other is not fit for these things? The redemption of man, therefore, demanded a man who, for the debt of his condition, should deservedly come to the aid of his own nature. Again, man by disobedience incurred the debt of death; and for the expiation of that same debt it was needful, by obeying, to pay an undue death for one that was due. But this none at all could do save a man mortal and just, and one who was in no way liable to the sentence of death. And so, that the person of the human Redeemer [1004A] might be fit for the satisfaction of the debt, it was needful that he be true God, who would suffice for this, and that he be true man, who by the right of kinship would deservedly pay it. And how, I ask, would he be a true and perfect mediator between God and man, who had nothing in common with either of the two? But on the contrary, no mediation could be stronger, none firmer, than that which should be made through him who was a partaker of either nature. See how it is gathered from reason how it was needful that the mediator of God and men be true God and true man.

## CHAPTER IX.

Moreover, by the same reason investigating, it is found to which person of the highest Trinity [1004B] this dispensation of mercy is chiefly to be applied. As was shown above, in order that man might fully recover his first dignity, satisfaction was needed. But that satisfaction might be made, there had to be both one who should exact it and likewise one who should pay it. But to punish a fault which the Father would pardon, or to exact any satisfaction for it, would in no way be fitting either to the Son or to the Holy Spirit, each of whom has from the Father all that he has: and so on this side it was needful that either the Father, or none at all, should employ exaction; and it was likewise needful that either the Son or the Holy Spirit make satisfaction for man. But if the Holy Spirit took up the office of mediator, man would perhaps hold this mediation suspect. [1004C] What wonder, I ask, if in this matter man thought as a man, feared as a man? He would perhaps fear that the Son might favor the Father, from whom he has all that he has, more than the Holy Spirit, from whom he has nothing of what he has. And if man had two against himself and one for himself, he would perhaps not wholly trust in such a mediation. But it was needful that the nature which had fallen by believing should rise again by believing. It had fallen by believing the serpent; it would rise again by believing the Redeemer, so that what it had lost through faith it might recover through faith, and be compelled to return by the same gate through which it pleased the good pleasure of God that man should go out. But if the Son of God took up the cause of man, he would bring man no ground of suspicion. For, according to the reason set forth above, the Holy Spirit could not fail to [1004D] pardon a fault which the Son wished to remit — how much more one which he wished to blot out by his own satisfaction? Nay, without doubt, the Son taking up the cause of man, it was more fitting that the Holy Spirit exercise the office of a fellow worker than of an exactor. See how open reason demonstrates that the cause of man required, for its own expiation, the person of the Son especially.
