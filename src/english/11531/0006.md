## CHAPTER XIV.

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1008A]

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ And so the coming of Christ is foretold — and not only of Christ, but also of Antichrist. ⟦s2⟧ Morning comes, and night. ⟦s3⟧ The coming of Christ makes the day; the coming of Antichrist brings on the night. ⟦s4⟧ O night dark and full of every horror, which the presence of Antichrist makes! ⟦s5⟧ For at that time so great a mist of seduction is to come upon the whole world, that even the elect, if it can be done, may be led into error. ⟦s6⟧ He says therefore: Morning comes, and night. ⟦s7⟧ But if we still have recourse, in the words aforesaid, to the tropological sense, we shall perhaps find something whereby we may be edified. ⟦s8⟧ Keeper, what of the night? ⟦s9⟧ Keeper, what of the night? ⟦s10⟧ Assuredly, as many of us as have been reborn in Christ, being sons of light, ought to walk as sons of light. ⟦s11⟧ Beyond doubt the night of the Idumaean is one thing, [1008B] and that of the Christian another. ⟦s12⟧ So too the day of the one is one thing, and of the other another. ⟦s13⟧ The day of the Idumaean, as we have said, is the prosperity of this world, and his night the adversity of the same. ⟦s14⟧ But to the faithful man what is said: *He shall bring forth thy justice as the light, and thy judgment as the noonday [n: (Psal. XXXVI)]*. ⟦s15⟧ *For the path of the just shineth as a light, and groweth even to the perfect day [n: (Psal. IV)]*. ⟦s16⟧ And so our day is justice, not injustice. ⟦s17⟧ Diabolical temptation brings on the night; divine inspiration makes the day. ⟦s18⟧ But we run into the darkness of night as often as we sin through ignorance or through malice, and we must needs cry out to our Samaritan. ⟦s19⟧ Keeper, what of the night? ⟦s20⟧ Keeper, what of the night? ⟦s21⟧ Who is this keeper to whom the cry is made in the night, unless that Samaritan who had mercy on him who fell [1008C] among robbers, and took care of him [n: (Luc. X)]? ⟦s22⟧ For Samaritan is interpreted *keeper*. ⟦s23⟧ This Samaritan surely [keeps us] in the day, this one keeps us in the night. ⟦s24⟧ This one, I say, keeps us in the day, so long as he grants us to persevere in good. ⟦s25⟧ In the night he keeps us, lest after the fall of sin we be given over to a reprobate sense, and, going from evil to evil, fall at last into despair, or into a crookedness of mind. ⟦s26⟧ He, therefore, is our keeper, he likewise our consoler. ⟦s27⟧ He it is who keeps us in temptation, he who consoles us in tribulation. ⟦s28⟧ Him we have as keeper, him as consoler; and at his asking, at his granting, we receive another also: *I will ask,* he says, *the Father, and he shall give you another Paraclete [n: (Joan. XIV)]*. ⟦s29⟧ Paraclete sounds the same [1008D] as consoler. ⟦s30⟧ Of the same Paraclete whom we have received through the Son, the same Son says in another place: *When the Paraclete Spirit shall come, whom I will send you from my Father [n: (Joan. XV)]*. ⟦s31⟧ Since, therefore, the Only-begotten of God both keeps us by consoling and consoles us by keeping — and now indeed through himself, now through his Spirit — it is rightly said to him by doubling. ⟦s32⟧ Keeper, what of the night? ⟦s33⟧ Keeper, what of the night? ⟦s34⟧ But since he keeps us both by day and by night, as has been said, he who cries thus suggests that he is in the night, in that he makes mention not of the day's keeping but only of the night's. ⟦s35⟧ But the entreaty of one walking in the day ought to be one thing, and that of one walking in the night another. ⟦s36⟧ One thing, in short, is his who does the works of light, and another his who does the works [1009A] of darkness. ⟦s37⟧ We know that *he who doth truth cometh to the light, that his works may be made manifest [n: (Joan. III)]*. ⟦s38⟧ Do you wish to hear how confidently a worker of this kind prays, in that he himself was walking in the light: *In whatsoever,* he says, *day I shall call upon thee, swiftly hear [sic: exaundi] me [n: (Psal. LV)]*. ⟦s39⟧ Hear on the contrary how timidly, how modestly he entreats who considers that he has run into the darkness of iniquity. ⟦s40⟧ Keeper, what of the night? ⟦s41⟧ Keeper, what of the night? ⟦s42⟧ He does not say, spare, spare; he does not say, have mercy. ⟦s43⟧ O how tremblingly he entreats who does not dare to say, have mercy on me, O God, have mercy on me; who does not presume to say, O God, be merciful to me a sinner! ⟦s44⟧ Often we hear those who ask alms cry nothing else to the rich man passing by, but only, Lord, Lord. ⟦s45⟧ So this man cries with the voice of one entreating to the ever-watchful and untiring [1009B] keeper of our infirmity. ⟦s46⟧ Keeper, what of the night? ⟦s47⟧ Keeper, what of the night? ⟦s48⟧ But what wonder if the Christian cries so humbly, when, as was said above, the Idumaean cries thus? ⟦s49⟧ For the higher the step, the worse the fall. ⟦s50⟧ For hence it is that any Christian whatever is with much more difficulty renewed to life at the fall of a single crime, than any unbeliever whatever, liable to however many crimes. ⟦s51⟧ Hear on this not my judgment but the Apostle's: *It is impossible,* he says, *for those who were once illuminated, have tasted also the heavenly gift, and were made partakers of the Holy Spirit, have tasted likewise the good word of God and the powers of the world to come, and are fallen away, to be renewed again unto penance [n: (Hebr. VI)]*. ⟦s52⟧ For to any unbelievers whatever [1009C] flocking to baptism, penitence alone suffices, without any satisfaction. ⟦s53⟧ But for the faithful who sin and repent, penitence alone does not suffice, without a worthy satisfaction. ⟦s54⟧ For the expiation of Christians after a fall is not wont to be made without the slaying of their own flesh — which the passion of Christ, that is, the slaying of another's flesh alone, is wont to confer upon penitent unbelievers. ⟦s55⟧ For if we sin willfully after having received the knowledge of the truth, there is now left no victim for sins. ⟦s56⟧ What wonder, then, if those groan humbly who recognize the greatness of their guilt? ⟦s57⟧ Let them say, then, let them say who are of this sort: Keeper what of the night? ⟦s58⟧ Keeper, what of the night? ⟦s59⟧ And so by hinting he shows what it is that they would ask, and do not presume to. [1009D] As if they said more plainly: we long to ask indulgence, to repair grace. ⟦s60⟧ But recognizing how shamelessly we have acted, we do not presume to ask. ⟦s61⟧ And so we have of our own accord forsaken the ways of truth, we have of set purpose fallen into the works of darkness. ⟦s62⟧ We wish now at last to return, but amid nocturnal fears, amid nocturnal horrors, we neither can nor know how. ⟦s63⟧ You surely are he who can call back such men, who are wont to call back such men. ⟦s64⟧ And so you will do it, if you will. ⟦s65⟧ What you bestow on others, you can bestow on us also. ⟦s66⟧ Keeper, what of the night? ⟦s67⟧ Keeper, what of the night? ⟦s68⟧ But if any Christian whatever deservedly prays so humbly, so suppliantly after a fall, how much more anyone who has professed an order and religion? ⟦s69⟧ For [to] the reproach and the confusion of those who grow lukewarm and melt away in religion, it is objected [1010A] and cast in their teeth by the Lord what secular persons too often to do: To me he cries out of Seir: Keeper, what of the night? ⟦s70⟧ Keeper, what of the night? ⟦s71⟧ If the inhabitant of Seir cries so humbly, what more is he to do whom the name of religion honors, especially if he considers that he has fallen shamefully? ⟦s72⟧ Or do those who are of this sort perhaps not know that a little leaven corrupts the whole lump? ⟦s73⟧ What does it profit to gather wages, and to put them into a pierced world, and to lose, out of a little and in a moment, whatever is gathered from this side and that over much time and with many sweats? ⟦s74⟧ O how many years' merits, how many wages of merits can be lost at the fall of one, and one, of a crime? ⟦s75⟧ Or do you not know that he who offends in one thing is guilty of all? [n: (Jac. II.)] For he who by even one [1010B] criminal act has rendered God offended toward himself has constituted himself guilty also in the rest which he has observed; guilty, I say, though not so far as to be punished in hell as it were for the transgression of them, yet so far as to be deprived of that reward of incorruption which he was to have had in heaven for the keeping of the rest. ⟦s76⟧ He, therefore, who for a momentary delight despises God and the Lord of all things, forsakes the fellowship of the angels, loses the kingdom of heaven — what, I ask, will he say when he has stood up to entreat God? ⟦s77⟧ Let him learn at least by example what he ought to do. ⟦s78⟧ Let him learn, I say, how humbly he ought to pray, how modestly he must entreat, if even the inhabitant of Seir prays so reverently, if he cries with so humble a devotion who never held any place of holiness, who never [1010C] deserved to taste how sweet the Lord is, how good the God of Israel is to those who are right of heart. ⟦s79⟧ To me, he says, he cries out of Seir: Keeper, what of the night? ⟦s80⟧ Keeper, what of the night?
