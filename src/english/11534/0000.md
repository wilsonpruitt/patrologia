## On the Difference between the Sacrifice of Abraham and the Sacrifice of the Blessed Virgin Mary

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1043A]We have heard of the great patriarch Abraham that he offered in sacrifice to the Lord a turtledove and a dove; but the sacrifice of the blessed Virgin Mary was a pair of turtledoves and two young doves. ⟦s2⟧ The sacrifice of the patriarch Abraham seems altogether to differ greatly from the sacrifice of the blessed Virgin Mary. ⟦s3⟧ Which of these is greater, which of these is better? ⟦s4⟧ But who would dare to set any of the saints before the blessed Virgin Mary, or to make him her equal — save only the Holy of holies? ⟦s5⟧ Without hesitation, therefore, we assign what is best to so great a lady; but again it is very hard that we should take anything away from the perfection of so great a patriarch. ⟦s6⟧ Yet altogether such and so great a difference of victims does not seem to be without cause. ⟦s7⟧ But if it helps to understand these things more fully, there is need of a deeper consideration. ⟦s8+9⟧ Now it seems to belong to the flight [1043B] and song of the turtledove, in the consideration of eternal evils, to pour out groaning and lamentation; but to groan and sigh in the contemplation of eternal goods, this is to have the dove's song. ⟦s10⟧ Let a man therefore call to mind what good he has lost by sinning, or what evil he has in the same way incurred, that he may better and more fully understand toward what state he ought to pant by laboring, groaning, and sighing. ⟦s11⟧ For by sinning he incurred a twofold captivity, and lost a twofold dignity. ⟦s12⟧ For being of his own will made subject to fault, he was against his will made over to punishment, and thereafter lost the stability of grace and the perpetuity of glory. ⟦s13⟧ Stripped therefore of such grace and such glory, man, willing or unwilling, is enslaved to sin and subjected to the scourge; and living but a short time he is filled with many miseries — he who comes forth like a [1043C] flower and is crushed, and flees like a shadow and never continues in the same state. ⟦s14⟧ And so we contract by being born a twofold captivity, namely the exaction of fault and of punishment; and as long as we live here we cannot recover the fixed stability of grace and the perpetuity of glory. ⟦s15⟧ We ought therefore with all diligence, and with as much insistence as we can, to labor for this above all, and to keep the right measure in all things, so that the evils which we have contracted by being born we may lay down by dying, and those goods which we cannot recover in the course of this life we may obtain immediately at the entrance of the life that follows. ⟦s16⟧ For perfect men, at the very moment of the soul's departure, at the very beginning of the life that is beginning, are set free from fault and delivered [1043D] from punishment, are confirmed in grace and exalted in glory. ⟦s17⟧ Now this twofold liberty from fault and punishment, and this twofold dignity in grace and glory, which the perfect alone receive at the soul's going forth — what else should I call them than certain firstfruits of the fullness to come? ⟦s18⟧ The perfect therefore receive these firstfruits in the soul alone, being afterward to receive the fullness in both natures. ⟦s19⟧ But to groan and sigh for the firstfruits of the twofold [1044A] liberty, and to hold a firm hope of obtaining them through death: this will be, as I judge, to offer a pair of turtledoves. ⟦s20⟧ But to pour out groans and sighs for the firstfruits of the twofold dignity, and to presume upon the Lord's mercy for the obtaining of them at the soul's departure: this will be, if I am not mistaken, to expend two doves in the Lord's sacrifice. ⟦s21⟧ The twofold liberty and the twofold dignity, as we have said, the perfect alone receive at death; but one liberty and one dignity only do all the elect receive. ⟦s22⟧ For immediately after the going forth of the soul, in the same moment of time, they are both set free from sin and confirmed in good. ⟦s23⟧ For the souls of the elect, though less perfect, like the rest receive at once, in the laying down of their body, the sentence of their lot; [1044B] and from the fact that they have escaped eternal damnation, from the fact that they foreknow their future glorification, from the fact that they recognize that after so many offenses they were never forsaken by the grace of Christ the Savior, they grow strong unto so great a love of their Creator that none of them would thereafter be willing to do anything at all whereby he might offend God, even if it were permitted him with impunity. ⟦s24⟧ But on the contrary all the reprobate, after they receive the sentence of their damnation, never rise up to even the least little spark of divine love; and from the moment they utterly despair of eternal goods, they desire to return to those things which they had known in this life and had perversely loved, and they would wish to live in them forever, if they could do so with impunity. ⟦s25⟧ It is clear, then, that they are enslaved to sin forever, not so much by action as by will. [1044C] And so the souls of the wicked at their departure are confirmed in evil, but the souls of the good are confirmed in good.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ And although certain elect souls, but less perfect, are still held by the debt of satisfaction, and are perhaps purged of vice for a long time, yet far be it that they should thereafter in any way be enslaved to sin. ⟦s2⟧ They are therefore set free from fault, yet are not delivered from punishment; they are confirmed in grace, yet are not exalted in glory. ⟦s2⟧ But what is done after the Savior's death toward the less perfect, this was done, before the price of our redemption was paid, even toward the very perfect. ⟦s3⟧ For they were set free from the servitude of sinning, yet were not delivered from every debt of suffering. ⟦s4⟧ For, to say nothing of the rest, they certainly suffered exile and the prison-house. [1044D] But what sort of prison-house? ⟦s5⟧ Assuredly hell. ⟦s6⟧ I pass over that they were not caught up to the joys of heaven, nor yet did they return to the delights of paradise. ⟦s7⟧ Although therefore they were confirmed in good, yet they were not consummated in joy. ⟦s8⟧ I say boldly that not even Abraham himself received in the laying down of the body that twofold liberty which is from fault and punishment; for that which is from the captivity of a far-off pilgrimage he presumed neither [1045A] to ask nor even to hope for. ⟦s8⟧ Rightly therefore he offered not turtledoves but a turtledove, and on this side he ought not to have doubled his victim. ⟦s9⟧ What I say of the obtaining of the twofold liberty, the same I say of the obtaining of the twofold dignity. ⟦s10⟧ For the stability of the former grace, which he hoped for and sought, he received in death; but before the Savior's coming he could not pass over to the glory of eternity. ⟦s11⟧ Rightly therefore his offering, in this kind of birds, was but a single dove. ⟦s12⟧ But the blessed Virgin Mary, since she was secure concerning both the liberty and the dignity to be obtained in the laying down of the flesh, could rightly double her victim on both sides. ⟦s13⟧ By now, I believe, you grasp how rightly such a difference had to stand between the sacrifice of Abraham and the sacrifice of the blessed Virgin Mary. [1045B] But there still seems to be in these matters something worthy of inquiry: that she chose rather to offer a pair of turtledoves than a pair of doves, but conversely two young doves rather than two young turtledoves. ⟦s14⟧ One thing, however, I know: that the more surely, the more confidently we hope for heavenly goods, for eternal goods, the more gladly, the more readily we labor for them. ⟦s15⟧ And the more we sweat for them, the greater and the more excellent things we hope for. ⟦s16⟧ And the better the things we hope for, the more ardently we love. ⟦s17⟧ And the more ardently we love, the more vehemently, the more anxiously we desire. ⟦s18⟧ Thus indeed, thus from the security of hope is born the anxiety of desire. ⟦s19⟧ You have therefore the bird in the security of hope; you have its [1045C] nestling in the anxiety of desire. ⟦s20⟧ For hope, which gapes after heavenly and eternal things, flies after the manner of birds to what is lofty and far off. ⟦s21⟧ The nestling is born of the bird, and the anxiety of desire is begotten of the certainty of hope in the order we have said. ⟦s22⟧ It belongs to nestlings to cry continually, and rarely to cease from crying. ⟦s23⟧ He knew this who said: *Like the young of the swallow, so shall I cry [n: (Isa. XXXVIII)]*. ⟦s24⟧ The nestling, then, is wont to cry anxiously enough, and anxious desire has in the ear of the Creator the voice of a great cry. ⟦s25⟧ Hence it is that to Moses, silent but burning with anxious desire, it is said by the Lord: *Why criest thou to me? [n: (Exod. XIV.)]* Hence that word of the Psalmist: *The Lord hath heard the desire of the poor [n: (Psal. X)]*. ⟦s26⟧ Now such desire is wont to grow so great in some men that they are weary of living, so [1045D] that this truly seems to be their voice: *My soul is weary of my life [n: (Job X)]*. ⟦s27⟧ For that good which eye hath not seen, nor ear heard, neither hath it entered into the heart of man — who, I ask, will be able to await it with the certainty of hope, and not burn with immense desire, and live scarcely otherwise than impatiently? ⟦s28⟧ Now to the bird belongs, as has been said, the confidence of hope; but to its nestling the impatience of desire. ⟦s29⟧ Hear the voice of the bird sweetly warbling in the place of solitude: *We know,* he says, *that if our earthly house of this habitation be dissolved, we have a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens [n: (II Cor. V)]*. ⟦s30⟧ Behold how great the confidence of hope. ⟦s31⟧ Mark also in another the vehemence of desire, and understand at the same time the cry of the clamoring nestling: *When shall I come,* [1046A] he says, *and appear before the face of God [n: (Psal. XLI)]*. ⟦s32⟧ But when anyone burns with loathing of this life and with desire of the life to come, it matters greatly with what intention he does so. ⟦s33⟧ For one man fear drives to this very thing, but another the vehemence of love draws. ⟦s34⟧ The one is harried by the fear of doubtful peril and of impending evil; the other by the love and heat of a desire to enjoy. ⟦s35⟧ Hear the voice of one in anguish in the midst of perils: *Who shall deliver me from the body of this death? [n: (Rom. VII.)]* Take also the voice of one burning with excess of love: *I desire to be dissolved and to be with Christ [n: (Philipp. I)]*. ⟦s36⟧ In both you hear a desire of death and a weariness of the present life. ⟦s37⟧ The former voice belongs to the young of turtledoves, the latter to the young of doves. ⟦s38⟧ For he who now, out of hatred of the twofold captivity which we distinguished above and out of desire of the twofold liberty, desires to die, [1046B] and with many groans and sighs pants after this very thing — what else does he cry aloud in God's ears than the death of nestlings from the nest of turtledoves? ⟦s39⟧ But he who with the desire of dying prays and weeps for the abundance of grace and glory which he awaits in time to come — how else does he cry, after the manner of nestlings, than with a dove's voice? ⟦s40⟧ For to hope firmly for the twofold liberty in the laying down of the flesh, and to live patiently in this expectation, this is, as we have already said, to offer a pair of turtledoves. ⟦s41⟧ But to burn more ardently with desire of these liberties, and to wish to die the sooner for the obtaining of them, and to bear impatiently the very fact that one lives — this seems to be to immolate a victim from the young of turtledoves. ⟦s42⟧ Likewise he who patiently awaits the exaltation of the twofold dignity in the hope [1046C] of security, immolates a pair of doves in the Lord's sacrifice. ⟦s43⟧ But he who, impatient of delay, pants and burns for this very thing, prepares for the Lord a sacrifice from the young of doves.
