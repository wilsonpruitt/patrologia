## On the Difference between the Sacrifice of Abraham and the Sacrifice of the Blessed Virgin Mary (cont.)

For who, however perfect, could obtain in this life that fullness of grace and glory which he does not doubt that he will obtain immediately after the soul's departure — so that he might be able to cleave unceasingly to divine contemplation, and be drawn away from it by no thought; so that he might be able to take delight in him alone, and to glory in him at every hour and every moment? It does not belong to this life that one, however perfect, should not have to break in upon the sweetness of so great a gladness — to say nothing of the rest — at least by sleeping. Who in this life could fulfill to the full that first and greatest commandment, namely to love God with the whole heart, [1046D] the whole soul, the whole strength? He therefore who, out of the desire of obtaining so great a fullness, loathes this life, and sighs and groans and pours out tears for the hastening of that desire's fulfillment, expends young doves in the Lord's sacrifice. Many are in great tribulations and temptations, and act bravely, secure concerning the twofold liberty, if it be granted them to persevere. But often not the young ones of those who are of this sort, on account of the dread of falling and the labor of suffering, desire to die quickly, that they may thereafter be secure from every suspicion of peril. But take heed lest perhaps it seem to be a sign of some impatience to shun the contests of labors, or likewise a sign of some diffidence to tremble beyond measure under the fear of falling. For it ought to be certain to all the perfect that [1047A] God is faithful, who will not suffer us to be tempted above that which we are able with iron, but will make also with the temptation a way of issue, that we may be able to sustain it [n: (I Cor. X)]. Hence it is that certain men act so steadfastly amid the greatest perils, and stand so secure in the Lord's protection, that they dare without hesitation even to say that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come, nor might, nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature will be able to separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus [n: (Rom. VIII)]. As far, therefore, as concerns the consideration of escaping peril, the firm security of hope in the Lord's protection seems greater than an excessive vehemence of desire. But the former belongs to the pair of turtledoves, the latter to the [1047B] young of *corum*. It seems, therefore, a greater thing to offer a pair of turtledoves than to offer their young. But without doubt one law seems to be the law of fear, and another, if I am not mistaken, the law of love. To wish to be at rest and to be secure is not so much the mark of one who loves his friend as of one who loves himself. But to wish to see him whom one's soul loves, to burn with desire of him, and to be able in no way to rest until one sees him, lays hold of him, and cleaves to him — this seems to be a sign of the highest devotion and of a wonderful charity. And so, on this side, the impatience of burning desire seems to be something much greater than the security of hope alone without ardent desire. But this latter belongs to the pair of doves, that former to their young. And so it seems the greater sacrifice [1047C] to offer two young doves than the doves themselves. Behold, what we long sought we have at last found: that it is something greater to offer a pair of turtledoves than their young, but on the contrary it seems less to offer a pair of doves than their young. Now you no longer wonder, as I judge, if the most holy of holy women, the ever-Virgin Mary, offered in each kind of victim what she knew, according to the dignity of the mystery, to be best on either side. Far be it that we should say or believe that the blessed Virgin Mary was urged to the desire of death by dread of suffering or by fear of falling. For of none of the saints is it more rightly believed than of her that she was always ready, so far as lay in her, to endure every kind of torment for the name of Christ. And how could she be urged by fear of falling, [1047D] she who in the flesh wherein she bore and nourished the flesh of Christ carried nothing of contradiction? At the momentary contact of the saving right hand the leper is cleansed [n: (Matth. VIII)]; at the touch of the hem she too was cleansed who suffered an issue of blood [n: (Luc. VIII)]. How much more did it behoove her to be cleansed from every contagion of mind and body, who for so long embraced him whole within her womb, who so often embraced him whole in her lap, who had in sufficient measure the fullness of that healing, so to speak, now poured within, now applied without — of that healing, I say, which could suffice, and more than suffice, to take away all the diseases of all the diseased? How, I beseech you, could she be urged even to weariness of life by the fear of falling or of suffering, who according to the proclamation of the angelic salutation was full [1048A] of grace? But whither fear could not drive her, love could draw her. Who ever, of all the lovers of Christ, would more gladly have wished to die, that he might see his divinity, that he might cleave by perpetual contemplation to his majesty and immensity? Who ever burned more ardently with the fervor of such desire? Assuredly, as far as she loved beyond the rest, so far beyond the rest did she more ardently desire the sweetness of the divine vision, and the everlastingness of that sweetness. I say boldly that not even she who loved beyond the rest was able to cleave inseparably to this sweetness, or to drink it alone and without interruption, so long as she was in this life and bore a corruptible body. For the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and the earthly habitation presses down the mind that museth upon many things [1048B] [n: (Sap. VIII)]

Since therefore she could not obtain in this life that fullness of grace and glory which she received in her assumption, what wonder if she ardently desired the hour of that receiving — she whom no fear of falling or of suffering could drive to a desire of death? She offered a pair of turtledoves in this, that she awaited the twofold liberty in the laying down of her flesh as patiently as she awaited it confidently. But she offered young doves rather than a pair of doves in this, that she panted, and that even with a desire of death, after that twofold fullness of grace and glory already spoken of above. But Abraham, as has been said, was secure at the laying down of the flesh not concerning the obtaining of glory, [1048C] but only concerning the receiving of stable grace — and not even that in the fullness which the blessed Virgin Mary, or any of the perfect in this present time, received. What wonder therefore if he desired the hour of receiving more coolly, who hoped for less? Rightly, then, he offered not young doves, nor a young dove, but a dove alone. And so Abraham offered the turtledove in the sure hope of obtaining liberty from fault, and the dove in the sure hope of receiving perpetual stability in grace. If we wish to emulate the pursuits of perfection, if we wish to be made spiritual, we ought in the Lord's sacrifice, after the example of Abraham, to join birds also to the animals. Birds indeed suspend themselves in the air, which animals certainly cannot do. In the birds, therefore, understand spiritual pursuits, [1048D] in the animals bodily exercises. To the animals belong outward pursuits, to the birds inward pursuits. To the former the exercises of the body, to the latter the exercises of the mind. Now God alone sees our inward things, but our neighbor also is able to see our outward things. Those things which we do within are done for God alone; those which we carry on outwardly, partly for God alone, partly also for our neighbor. For many things are done for the divine good pleasure alone, many for the benefit or the good pleasure of men. Rightly therefore Scripture says of Abraham that he divided the animals, but the birds he did not divide [n: (Gen. XV)]. For in those exercises which lie open to the divine gaze alone, and which we have already said belong to the birds, one ought to intend nothing else than that they should deservedly please [1049A] the divine will. But in bodily exercises, on which we press both for God's sake and for our neighbor's, we double our intention, and on this side we divide our victim. But we ought not only to divide our victim after this manner, but also to set the divisions themselves on either hand over against each other, and to weigh both parts by an alternating consideration. For in those things which we do for our neighbor's sake, we ought with all caution to beware of offending God; and in those which we carry on for God's sake, we ought, so far as we can, to avoid scandal to our neighbor. Now only that victim which is of animals ought we to divide; but both this one and that which is of birds must, after the example of Abraham, be defended from the rapacity of the fowls [n: (ibid.)]. [1049B] For the fowls, as it is written, came down upon his victim, and Abraham drove them away. But what do we understand by fowls of this sort, save vain and foolish thoughts? Often, from the good things which we work within or without, we are touched now by pride, now by empty glory, and we glory immeasurably within ourselves. But if we do not manfully resist such foolish thoughts, we empty out for ourselves, in God's eyes, the fruit of the good work. Now the aforesaid fowls come down, as it were, to the plundering of our sacrifices, when wicked thoughts, sent in by malignant spirits for the corrupting of our merits, come upon our heart. For the malignant spirits are as it were above us by this very fact, that they exist in a more eminent condition [1049C] and in an angelic nature; and in the Gospel they are called the fowls of heaven [n: (Matth. XIII; Marc. IV; Luc. VIII)]. We ought therefore to strike foolish thoughts, and whatever suggestions come through evil angels, with rebuke; and this will be to drive away fowls of that kind. Let him, then, who hears or reads these things learn from the apostle's teaching how he ought to drive away the rapacity of elation or of foolish boasting. *What,* he says, *hast thou that thou hast not received? And if thou hast received, why dost thou glory as if thou hadst not received? [n: (I Cor. IV.)]* Evil alone canst thou have of thyself, but no good, save from the divine gift. Consider therefore how shameless a boast it is to glory in an evil that is thine own, or in a good that is not thine own. If we go over these things diligently, if we go over them often, the more quickly do we snatch the victim of our devotion from that [1049D] rapacity of the fowls of which we have spoken. Behold, we have what sort of victims we ought to offer to the Lord our God; behold, we have how they must be defended from the assault of rapacity. Would that each one of us might press on in pursuits of this kind until the sun goes down, and the love of vanity gradually fails, and, the ray of carnal prudence being withdrawn, the fervor of concupiscence grows lukewarm. For by that fiery and bodily globe which does not cease to go about the mass of the earth, and which, proceeding in its circuit, never rests, what better, what more rightly do we understand in this place than the love of earthly things, the love of things passing away, which encircles the lowest things and never suffers the soul which it inflames to rest? This sun both shines by the prudence of the flesh, and burns by the concupiscence of the flesh. This sun [1050A] without doubt goes down when carnal prudence grows dull, and carnal concupiscence little by little grows cold.
