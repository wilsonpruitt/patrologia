## On the Difference between the Sacrifice of Abraham and the Sacrifice of the Blessed Virgin Mary (cont.)

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But that we may know what is wont at times to follow such a setting of the sun, let us hear what Scripture says: *And when the sun was going down, a deep sleep fell upon Abraham, and a great and darksome horror seized him [n: (Gen. XV)]*. ⟦s2⟧ And so deep sleep follows the setting of the sun, and horror follows the deep sleep. ⟦s3⟧ Through the body's deep sleep, as we all know, bodily sense is lulled, and the human mind is estranged from the things lying about it. ⟦s4⟧ Fittingly enough, therefore, by deep sleep is understood the estrangement of the mind. ⟦s5⟧ But in a sleep of this kind the mind often comes to know by divine revelation, and discerns as it were in a dream, by a fitting vision, what before, while as it were awake, it could scarcely know but faintly, or [1050B] could not know at all. ⟦s6⟧ Hence that which is said in Job by Elihu: *By a dream, in a vision by night, when deep sleep falls upon men and they sleep in their bed, then he opens the ears of men, and teaching them instructs them in discipline [n: (Job XXXIII)]*. ⟦s7⟧ Such sleep comes in no way at man's own choosing, but according to the good pleasure of him who is wont to send it in. ⟦s8⟧ Rightly therefore is it said of this sleep that it fell upon him, in that it comes suddenly and unlooked for. ⟦s9⟧ And so deep sleep falls when some spiritual man passes over by a sudden onset into estrangement of mind. ⟦s10⟧ Now when a man is lulled to sleep after this manner, and is caught up outside himself, the things which are seen in that ecstasy are often wonderful and astounding. ⟦s11⟧ You who hear these things perhaps marvel, because [1050C] you as yet experience nothing of the kind in yourself. ⟦s12⟧ You do not know that all things have their time. ⟦s13+14⟧ Waking, surely, and sleep have their time: waking by day, sleep by night. ⟦s15⟧ Perhaps for you the sun has not yet gone down, the love of vanity has not yet failed. ⟦s16⟧ While the sun is still burning, that sleep which we have defined knows not how to fall. ⟦s17⟧ The setting of the sun, as we have said, is followed by deep sleep; and deep sleep is nonetheless at times followed by horror. ⟦s18⟧ For the human mind often sees, in the time of this sleep, many things which it cannot see without dread and trembling, or even horror. ⟦s19⟧ Whence that word of Eliphat the Themanite: *In the horror of a vision by night, when deep sleep is wont to take hold of men, fear seized me, and trembling, and all my bones were terrified [n: (Job IV)]*. ⟦s20⟧ Hear another also, and mark what he understood as it were in a dream: *I said in the excess of my mind: [1050D] I am cast away from before thine eyes [n: (Psal. XXX)]*. ⟦s21⟧ What wonder if his soul shuddered, who saw himself cast away from the face of the Lord? ⟦s22⟧ Of such terror or horror he says through Job himself: *If I shall say. ⟦s23⟧ My bed shall comfort me, and I shall be relieved as I speak with myself upon my couch, thou wilt frighten me with dreams, and wilt shake me with horror by visions [n: (Job XVII)]*. ⟦s24⟧ For a man is frightened as it were by dreams, and shaken with horror by visions, when the human mind, led above itself and caught up to the contemplation of the divine judgments, sees by divine revelation to how great perils it lies subject at every daily moment. ⟦s25⟧ It sees how infinite are the eternal goods which it has lost, how dreadful the eternal evils which it has deserved; it sees how easy it is to fall at every hour, and how impossible it is for one who has once slipped to rise again by himself; [1051A] it sees how some fall from the highest pursuits of the virtues into the depths of the vices, and how conversely some rise up from the depth of the vices to the highest summits of the virtues; and in all these things man knows not his own end, but, as fishes are taken with the hook and as birds are caught in the snare, so are men taken in an evil time when it comes upon them of a sudden. ⟦s26⟧ What wonder, then, if they are shaken with a certain horror, who are taught by God under how great an uncertainty of perils human weakness labors? ⟦s27⟧ Consider, in fine, what Abraham learned as it were in his sleep, and you will observe how rightly he shuddered. ⟦s28⟧ For it was said to him: *Know beforehand that thy seed shall be a sojourner in a land not their own, and they shall subject them to servitude, and afflict them four hundred years [n: (Gen. XV)]*. [1051B] By the seed of Abraham, if you will, understand the virtues themselves. ⟦s29⟧ By the seed of Abraham, if you will, understand those who emulate the virtues, living after the example of Abraham, and imitators of his life. ⟦s30⟧ Do not the virtues themselves serve king Pharaoh, when they are done with a sinister intention, and, depraved by hypocrisy, seek nothing but the gains of death?

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ And it often happens that pursuits of the virtues which had been begun with a good intention are at length carried on with an evil intention. ⟦s2⟧ Does not the seed of Abraham serve king Pharaoh in mud and brick, when some strong emulator of the virtues, who, going forth out of his land and out of his kindred, long according to the exemplar of Abraham, [d: according] at length at some time falls after the manner of David into some outrage? ⟦s3⟧ For any such man too, if he returns again to righteousness, [1051C] is reckoned among the sons of Abraham. ⟦s4⟧ Consider therefore how full of fear and likewise of horror it is, that not even upon some height, or from the summit of virtue, is any place of security granted to man in this life. ⟦s5⟧ It is very rare, and truly the mark of few, to do great things and not think oneself great, to stand above many and to despise no one. ⟦s6⟧ For when any one of us does wonderful things, and outstrips the rest in the nimbleness of good pursuits, it is easy for him to say within himself that word of the Pharisee: *I am not as the rest of men [n: (Luc. XI)]*. ⟦s7⟧ Whence it often happens that from those who are such the divine grace is withdrawn, and they are permitted to fall, and are sometimes led on little by little so far that at length they are as obstinate in evil as they had before [1051D] been steadfast in good. ⟦s8⟧ Is not this what the Lord threatens, when it is said to Abraham: Know beforehand that thy seed shall be a sojourner in a land not their own? ⟦s9⟧ Whereas the other elements are wont always to be in flux, the earth alone stands forever; whence fittingly enough it designates the stability of the heart. ⟦s10⟧ But as good as it is to be stable in a good purpose, so evil is it to be stable in an evil purpose. ⟦s11⟧ The former belongs to steadfastness, the latter to obstinacy. ⟦s12⟧ And so steadfastness in good is the land of the good; obstinacy in evil is the land of the wicked. ⟦s13⟧ And so, when any of those predestined to the eternal way are led on little by little to obstinacy of this kind, they truly sojourn in a foreign land, in a land not their own. ⟦s14⟧ Behold what pride does, behold what arrogance does. ⟦s15⟧ And so the vessels of mercy, [1052A] when they begin to swell over their own merits, are by God's loving dispensation permitted to fall, that they may learn to be wise humbly, and to think truly of their own weakness. ⟦s16⟧ Now the knowledge of human weakness, that it may be full, consists in four degrees: two we have in the consideration of evil, two in the consideration of good. ⟦s17⟧ In the consideration of evil: first, that we may know that we can guard against no evil by our own strength; second, that we may know that after a fall we can rise again from no evil by our own strength. ⟦s18⟧ In the consideration of good: that we may recognize that we can grow strong unto no good by our own industry; second, that we may recognize that not even in that which we have received from God can we persevere by our own industry. ⟦s19⟧ Now the manifold advancement of progress in the knowledge of oneself — what else is it than a certain succession of alternating days? [1052B] But every alternation of lengthening or shortening days is enclosed within a year, and is completed by the course of the sun. ⟦s20⟧ Rightly, then, by a year is understood the fullness and completion of knowledge in any matter whatsoever. ⟦s21⟧ And to this end, according to what has been said above, a man is permitted to fall many times and in many ways into some one vice, that through many trials he may learn fully and entirely that he can rescue himself from such servitude by no strength of his own. ⟦s22⟧ But when this manifoldness of experience is carried through even to the fullness of knowledge, a year is as it were completed. ⟦s23⟧ But when a man learns the same defect of his own weakness in one vice after another through daily trials, and gathers also by reason, in others, the same verdict of truth which he has proved by experience in many, [1052C] and at last judges the same universally in all, then the number of years, in the multiplication of knowledge, rises to a hundred. ⟦s24⟧ For since all the digits, and the joints named from them, and the numbers composed of both, are enclosed within a hundred, rightly is universality figured in the number one hundred. ⟦s25⟧ In a year, then, understand fullness, and in a hundred understand universality. ⟦s26⟧ When therefore we have the abundance of necessary knowledge both sufficiently in each single thing and generally in all, we extend the yearly course of our schooling to a hundred. ⟦s27⟧ Now we distinguished the knowledge of our weakness above through four degrees. ⟦s28⟧ It is necessary, therefore, to fill out as it were the number of four hundred years, if we wish [1052D] to lay hold of this fullness of fourfold knowledge of which we have already spoken. ⟦s29⟧ Hold it for certain, nor is there any ground on which you ought to hesitate, that you must be subject to Pharaoh and to the Egyptians, and be enslaved to vices and to the kindlers of vices, until by the mastery of experience you are taught to think truly of your own weakness, until you learn fully to presume nothing at all upon your own strength in guarding against or correcting evils, in obtaining or preserving goods. ⟦s30⟧ Behold, we have now heard whence the patriarch shuddered, and indeed whence he rightly ought to have shuddered. ⟦s31⟧ We have heard the things that made for terror; let us hear also those which could make for consolation: *Nevertheless the nation,* he says, *whom they shall serve, I will judge. ⟦s32⟧ And after this they shall go out with great substance. ⟦s33⟧ But thou shalt go to thy fathers in peace, [1053A] buried in a good old age: in the fourth generation they shall return hither. ⟦s34⟧ For the iniquities of the Amorrhites are not yet complete unto the present time [n: (Gen. XV)]*.
