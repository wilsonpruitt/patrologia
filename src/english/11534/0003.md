## On the Difference between the Sacrifice of Abraham and the Sacrifice of the Blessed Virgin Mary (cont.)

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Behold, as we can observe from these things, by divine revelation and as it were through a dream we learn now sad things, now glad things, and now things mingled of both. ⟦s2⟧ The nation, he says, whom they shall serve, I will judge. ⟦s3⟧ For whether by the nations you wish to understand the vices or the kindlers of vices, God assuredly is to grind down both by his judgment and to drive them out of the hearts of the good — the former indeed by so grinding them down that they be nowhere at all; the latter by damning them, so that they may thereafter be forever wretched, and may never in anything prevail against the good for eternity. ⟦s4⟧ And after this, he says, [1053B] they shall go out with great substance. ⟦s5⟧ We know indeed that the sons of reconciliation are schooled by their own fall unto discretion, unto circumspection, unto humility and compassion, unto steadfastness, unto patience and perseverance together. ⟦s6⟧ And so they go out with great substance when they carry back with them such gains of the virtues and such talents of knowledge. ⟦s7⟧ You have, then, the substance of souls in the fruits of penitence and in the gains of the virtues; you have the substance of the virtues in the treasures of knowledge and wisdom. ⟦s8⟧ Of a treasure of such substance is understood that word in the psalm: *And he brought them out with gold and silver [n: (Psal. CIV)]*. ⟦s9⟧ See therefore how wonderful, how delightful, how astounding it is that even the very ruin of morals [1053C] works together with the good unto good, and that what we read in the Apostle is fulfilled: *We know that to them that love God all things work together unto good, to them who according to his purpose are called saints [n: (Rom. VI)]*. ⟦s10⟧ It works together unto good, therefore, that they serve for a time in a land not their own, who at length go out thence with great substance. ⟦s11⟧ It works together unto good that they succumb to the vices for some little while, who afterward rise again the more cautious as they are the more practiced, and the more steadfast or the more robust as they are the more humble. ⟦s12⟧ But those are to be judged happy beyond measure who advance to the grace of humility by the gift of foresight, who learn the measure of human weakness not so much from their own ruin as from another's. ⟦s13⟧ Their person is expressed in Abraham, [1053D] to whom it is said by the Lord: But thou shalt go to thy fathers in peace, buried in a good old age. ⟦s14⟧ Neither does the Lord foretell, nor does any Scripture hint, that Abraham was anywhere made over to anyone's servitude. ⟦s15⟧ Not therefore by often falling, often rising again, and by serving the vices did he advance to the grace of humility. ⟦s16⟧ But while, God revealing it, he reads and reads again in the book of providence, he proves by the examples of others what he ought to think of his own weakness. ⟦s17⟧ Let us see, then, whether we can find in the sayings of this man, taught by God, that revealed humility which we distinguished above through four degrees. ⟦s18⟧ Let us hear him, then, speaking with the Lord: *I will speak,* he says, *with my Lord, whereas I am dust and ashes [n: (Gen. XVIII)]*. ⟦s19⟧ Dust is shaken out of its place at every breath, and, cast down wherever it may be, is never [1054A] lifted save by an impulse from without. ⟦s20⟧ Behold, O men, the form of human defect, so far as concerns the consideration of evil.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ For when grace and the divine protection are withdrawn, man is cast down at the enemy's suggestion into whatsoever evil; and once cast down, he is strengthened to the uprightness of his state by his own power. ⟦s2⟧ Ashes bring forth of themselves no shoot at all; and what is taken up from elsewhere they are wholly unable to nourish by any richness of their own — I will not say unto increase, but even unto sustenance. ⟦s3⟧ Here likewise you have the form of human defect, so far as concerns the consideration of good. ⟦s4⟧ For man can bring forth of himself no shoot of good work without operating grace; and when by operating grace he has already begun to have it, by himself alone he will [1054B] be wholly unable to keep it without her cooperation. ⟦s5⟧ Behold, Abraham, as is understood from these his sayings, is found taught by God unto that fullness of humility to which many come only through a fourfold hundred of years, after many trials. ⟦s6⟧ What some, therefore, scarcely learn through many trials, others learn easily from divine revelation. ⟦s7⟧ But thou, he says, shalt go. ⟦s8⟧ Thou, whom beyond the rest I forestalled with more abundant grace; thou, whom beyond the rest I enlightened more deeply. ⟦s9⟧ Thou shalt go to thy fathers in peace, free from every oppression of unworthy and hard servitude. ⟦s10⟧ But what is it to go to one's fathers, save to hasten, by that same path of the virtues by which others went before on the way of life, to the tranquillity of inmost rest by the nimbleness of good [1054C] works? ⟦s11⟧ He therefore was hastening to peace who was to go in peace. ⟦s12⟧ He went through peace and to peace; through inward peace, to eternal peace. ⟦s13⟧ Through that peace which reconciles every sense mutually to itself, to that peace which surpasses every sense. ⟦s14⟧ That discord, therefore, which is between the flesh and the spirit must be lulled to sleep, that one may be able to pass over to that peace which is above every sense. ⟦s15⟧ But what is it for the Lord to make promises of this kind to anyone, save to quicken him by inward inspiration unto such hope and such certainty? ⟦s16⟧ But thou shalt go to thy fathers in peace, buried in a good old age. ⟦s17⟧ In old age both the sense and the appetite of the outward man are wont to grow faint, and by daily losses to draw near to failing. ⟦s18⟧ A good old age, when at the same point of time at which the appetite for outward and passing [1054D] things is diminished, the appetite for inward and eternal things is thereby made new. ⟦s19⟧ A good old age, when the outward sense decreases and the inward increases. ⟦s20⟧ A good old age, when the inward man is strengthened by that very thing whereby the outward man is weakened: *For when I am weak,* he says, *then am I stronger and mighty [n: (II Cor. XII)]*. ⟦s21⟧ Better is an old age of character than of years, of merits than of seasons. ⟦s22⟧ It is more glorious that the appetite for outward goods should grow faint from the zeal of industry than from a defect of nature. ⟦s23⟧ Buried, he says, in a good old age. ⟦s24⟧ First a man dies, that afterward he may be buried. ⟦s25⟧ The dead man indeed ceases to see, but does not at once cease to be seen. ⟦s26⟧ But the buried man ceases to see, and at the same time to be seen. ⟦s27⟧ He therefore who wishes to be dead to this world must close those eyes which we read were opened in our first [1055A] parents, and must not attend to the delights of the senses, nor turn them away.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But he who is such finds, as it were, burial after death at that time when, through the pursuit of wisdom and the desire of eternity, passing from worldly things and above worldly things, he advances into such a state as no carnal man could — I will not say know — but not even suspect. ⟦s2⟧ By the active life we are mortified; by the speculative and the contemplative we are buried with Abraham, as it were in a twofold cave. ⟦s3⟧ It is proper to the speculative to behold the invisible things of God, understood through the things that are made, and to see [sic: *evidere*] them as through a mirror and in [sic: *aenigmat*]. ⟦s4⟧ It is proper to the contemplative to see the truth without wrapping, and as it were face to face. ⟦s5⟧ By the speculative, therefore, and the contemplative we are absented from human [1055B] affairs, and, as has been said, are buried as in a twofold cave. ⟦s6⟧ It is good, therefore, that the appetite of desire and the effort of zeal should grow faint toward human affairs, should fail, should absent themselves; and that, immersed in heavenly contemplations, we should lie hidden from the gaze of those who are wise after the flesh. ⟦s7⟧ Thus the man who emulates the virtues grows old, dies, and is buried. ⟦s8⟧ Behold, we have heard concerning Abraham and concerning those whose figure he bears; now let us hear what consolation he adds concerning the sons of the sojourning: And in the fourth generation, he says, they shall return hither. ⟦s9⟧ That he says "in the fourth generation," you must reckon not from Abraham's time but from the time of the sojourning, as you have it from the Gospel or from other places of the Scriptures. ⟦s10⟧ When that inward household of thoughts or of wills [1055C] is led away into foreign parts, and under the pressure of temptations is made over to the servitude of the vices, at first indeed the conscience does not cease to murmur at every hour and to be restless; but gradually it is brought, to grow strong by depraved custom, so far that it ceases to kick against its exactors, and, like a heifer taught to love the threshing, serves without murmur and without contradiction. ⟦s11⟧ But when the time of good pleasure and the time of mercy has come before the Lord, and after many evils, the Lord having mercy and visiting as it were by the office of Moses, it has been pricked to penitence, it now begins to kick back and now wishes to shake off the yoke of captivity — yet it is not able to prevail as yet. ⟦s12⟧ Then, as the divine consolations grow frequent, and many things flash out as it were like miracles, it begins little by little to grow strong, [1055D] to acquire vigor, to take on boldness, and at length at last to prevail over its enemies — but not without great labor and the utmost difficulty.
