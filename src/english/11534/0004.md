## On the Difference between the Sacrifice of Abraham and the Sacrifice of the Blessed Virgin Mary (cont.)

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But at the last, when the tyranny of the vices has been subdued, and all that soldiery, as it were of Pharaoh, has been drowned in the cleansing of inmost compunction, and the zeal of good custom has grown strong, and the terror of enemies has been taken away, it begins now to rejoice in the tranquillity of a good conscience, and to draw near to the promised land of glad security. ⟦s2⟧ The first generation, then, is of those who serve and do not protest; the second, of those who protest and do not prevail; the third, of those who prevail and labor; the fourth, of those who prevail and rest. ⟦s3⟧ For those who abide in the wilderness and live upon heavenly food, what other kind of office ought there to have been than to be at leisure and [1056A] to see that the Lord is sweet? ⟦s4⟧ In the first the conscience is tranquil and evil; in the second, restless and evil; in the third, restless but good; in the fourth, tranquil and good. ⟦s5⟧ But through the tranquillity of conscience one draws near to the security of conscience. ⟦s6⟧ The gladness [securitas] of a secure conscience is that land, as was said above, flowing with milk and honey, which is promised by the Lord and given to the heirs of Abraham. ⟦s7⟧ Rightly therefore is it said: But in the fourth generation they shall return hither. ⟦s8⟧ And it is added: For the iniquities of the Amorrhites are not yet complete unto the present time. ⟦s9⟧ According to the difference of characters are the merits of individuals dispensed. ⟦s10⟧ There are those who are more confounded, or more pricked, over a shameful thought than certain others over the most shameful deed. ⟦s11⟧ Hence it is that some, [1056B] after a shameful thought, are swept away even to depraved delight. ⟦s12⟧ Others from depraved delight slip into consent to depravity alone; others even after consent are hurled headlong into the act. ⟦s13⟧ Some even bring the perverse action into a habit. ⟦s14⟧ And so the fall of each is measured out according to the judgment of him by whose dispensation all things work together with the good unto good. ⟦s15⟧ Certain men indeed are found of such shamelessness, such hardness and pride, that after many evils they can scarcely be schooled unto full knowledge of their own weakness, scarcely be humbled in truth, scarcely be inwardly pricked over their own depravity. ⟦s16⟧ And so the iniquities of the Amorrhites then seem complete when, by the dispensation of him who measures all things, [1056C] they are permitted to grow to that enormity which in its own time may abundantly suffice to confound the shamelessness of the heart, to crush its hardness, to trample down its pride. ⟦s17⟧ Behold, we now hear how great things Abraham learned from divine revelation after his sun had gone down, the deep sleep had fallen upon him, the horror had seized him. ⟦s18⟧ Scripture yet again repeats what it had already said above, that it may show more fully what those things are which are wont to follow the setting of the sun: *When therefore,* it says, *the sun had gone down, there arose a dark mist, and there appeared a smoking furnace and a lamp of fire passing between those divisions [n: (Gen. XV)]*. ⟦s19⟧ It has said what he heard; it says again what he saw, after that sun of his had gone down and carnal love had failed. ⟦s20⟧ We have heard what he heard; let us mark also what he saw. ⟦s21⟧ First a smoking fire, then [1056D] a flaming fire. ⟦s22⟧ First a fire that kindles, then a fire that illumines. ⟦s23⟧ He saw, then, a smoking furnace and a lamp. ⟦s24⟧ Surely no one lights a lamp for heating, but for illumination alone. ⟦s25⟧ On the contrary, no one kindles a furnace for the benefit of illumination, but for heating. ⟦s26⟧ In the smoking furnace, then, understand the burning of love; in the lamp, the light of revealing grace. ⟦s27⟧ Mark this also, that we put fire into a furnace to this end, that we may heat its inward parts and not its outward. ⟦s28⟧ In the burning of the furnace, therefore, understand the love of spiritual things, inward love, the love of the virtues. ⟦s29⟧ Fire, then, is kindled as it were in a furnace, and is held shut up as it were within, when love is nourished in the inward parts of the heart; and then truly, as though [1057A] shut up within, it is confined in the very place where it arises, when the soul is more sharply inflamed to love and to desire the goods of the soul. ⟦s30⟧ We kindle a furnace, as it were, when we more vehemently inflame the heart's desire toward those things which are to be more inwardly desired. ⟦s31⟧ The true and inward goods of the soul, which are truly and inwardly to be desired, we call virtue itself, goodness itself. ⟦s32⟧ When therefore, without regard to reward, virtue is loved for the sake of virtue itself, when also without any intention of recompense goodness itself is loved for its own sake, then truly and beyond doubt the burning of love seems to be kindled in the inmost parts, and to be confined in the inmost parts lest it break out to the things without. ⟦s33⟧ But this is rightly asked: why is a smoking furnace, and not rather a blazing one, shown to Abraham? ⟦s34⟧ But we know that in the kindling of a furnace, at the outset [1057B] smoke always rises; but, as the burning gradually grows, the smoke too is seen gradually to decrease, and at length to fail altogether. ⟦s35⟧ What else, then, is expressed by the fact that a smoking furnace is shown to Abraham in the vision, than a certain beginning of inmost love? ⟦s36⟧ For however much a man may seem in this life to have advanced in the increase of charity, if you consider the fullness of the life to come, in comparison with it he will seem to have had only a certain beginning of perfection. ⟦s37⟧ And smoke indeed stings the eyes and draws forth tears; and charity, according to the state of the present life, however far it has advanced, when well examined and carefully inspected, pricks the soul by the gnawing of conscience, since so long as one lives here it does not love, as it is divinely commanded, [1057C] with the whole heart, the whole soul, the whole mind. ⟦s38⟧ In the smoking furnace, therefore, is expressed the beginning of inmost and highest love. ⟦s39⟧ For in charity nothing loftier, nothing more splendid is found than when goodness itself is loved for its own sake. ⟦s40⟧ But as in the smoking furnace a certain beginning of inmost and highest love is rightly understood, so also in the lamp is there fittingly expressed a certain beginning of inmost and highest light. ⟦s41⟧ For no one lights a lantern to see the region round about, or the things that are round about in it. ⟦s42⟧ But we surely kindle a lantern for this, that we may see the things near at hand, and those that are within the inner parts of our own chambers. ⟦s43⟧ But what is nearer to the spirit than it is to itself, [1057D] unless perhaps when, by chasing after vanities, it forsakes itself, and by casting away its inmost things withdraws from its own self? ⟦s44⟧ What can be nearer to it, what more inward, than that Spirit who is everywhere and can be absent nowhere? ⟦s45⟧ Assuredly the heart of man is depraved and unsearchable, and can no more be known than it can be seen, save by the light of a lantern sent from above and divinely kindled. ⟦s46⟧ For to Abraham it is sent from above, and for the Psalmist it is divinely kindled: *For thou lightest my lamp, O Lord; my God, enlighten my darkness [n: (Psal. XVII)]*. ⟦s47⟧ If, then, without the benefit of the divine lantern you cannot see your own inmost things, how much less the divine secrets? ⟦s48⟧ And so a lantern is divinely kindled for a man when the surpassing eminence of inmost goods is made manifest to him in the divine light. ⟦s49⟧ But [1058A] if it behooved him to exhibit to Abraham the form of divine revelation, why is it expressed to him in a lantern, and not rather in the sun? ⟦s50⟧ But however far human understanding may advance in this life in the manifestation of the inmost light, it does not grow up to the perfect day, and takes on nothing but a kind of beginning of that fullness to come. ⟦s51⟧ As therefore in the smoking furnace a certain beginning of the fullness to come is rightly understood, in the love of the highest goodness, so in the burning lantern we rightly understand a certain beginning of the fullness to come, in the contemplation of the highest wisdom. ⟦s52⟧ Now these two are wont to furnish mutual increases to each other; for love grows from knowledge, and knowledge from love. ⟦s53⟧ Yet it happens that a man, often at the very time when he burns much with desire, [1058B] sees little; and conversely, at the time when he sees much, burns little. ⟦s54+55⟧ In these things, without doubt, that supreme craftsman assuredly shows the skill of his art. And it becomes known how little man can do save by the cooperation of the divine gift, when in the effects of causes things befall him beyond hope and beyond reckoning. ⟦s56⟧ This is why a smoking furnace and a lamp are shown to Abraham, and why the form both of divine inbreathing and of divine revelation is set forth separately and apart.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But it must be marked how it is seen here or there: *Passing,* it says, *between the divisions themselves [n: (Gen. XV)]*. ⟦s2⟧ What we discern through the figure, we prove by the experience of things. ⟦s3⟧ For the revelation of goodness and of truth always tends toward the fountain of all good things, and draws the soul which it affects after it into that same fountain. ⟦s4⟧ But [1058C] what is this, that the exemplar of spiritual things which the patriarch saw appeared to him in the midst of the divisions? ⟦s5⟧ As we have already said above, those divide their victims who in the good things they do strive to please both God and their neighbor, who purpose to avoid with all zeal both the wrath of God and scandal to their neighbor. ⟦s6⟧ Without doubt nothing is more laborious, nothing more difficult. ⟦s7⟧ See therefore how deservedly that twofold grace which was described above is conferred and heaped up from that very source whereby the human mind is more sharply pressed for God's sake: for this is why the furnace and the lamp are described as having appeared to Abraham in the midst of the divisions. ⟦s8⟧ But it must be noted that Abraham, before he saw these things, ran into a dark mist. ⟦s9⟧ Assuredly he who has not yet recognized the shadowed places of human [1058D] error and the lurking-places of the perils in which he moves does not reach that fire of true love which knows not how to cleave to the lowest things. ⟦s10⟧ He who does not yet understand that the life of man upon earth is a temptation, being ignorant of the exile which he suffers, knows not how to sigh for his homeland, knows not the inward light, knows not the blaze of true and inmost love. ⟦s11⟧ This is the fire which is sent in from heaven, which is given after the setting of the sun. ⟦s12⟧ This is the fire which begets assurance, prepares confidence, nourishes hope, takes away fear. ⟦s13⟧ For charity believeth all things, hopeth all things. ⟦s14⟧ In fine, perfect charity casteth out fear. ⟦s15⟧ For Abraham too, after he had merited to receive that fire sent from heaven, is nowhere afterward read to have doubted the Lord's promises. ⟦s16⟧ And we too assuredly, if we long to inherit that land flowing with milk and honey, [1059A] ought to pant, so far as we can, for the receiving of such a fire. ⟦s17⟧ Let us ourselves also prepare such a victim as the patriarch prepared, and in that order in which he himself [1060A] offered it; and without doubt, as a sign of the inheritance to be received, we shall receive such a fire as we desire.
