## BOOK ONE.



## CHAPTER ONE. *On the mystical dream of King Nabuchodonosor, and the sum of the intention of the whole work.*

[1229D]

What that dream of Nabuchodonosor signifies according to the letter, that most weighty exposition of Daniel teaches. But it ought not to displease, if a tropological discussion should effect [sic: *eficiat*] out of the same matter something that may build up the reader unto the knowledge of truth or unto the discipline of morals. That I may therefore touch in few words upon the sum of his intention: [1230D] the prophet in this place seems, out of a mystical vision, to signify how it befalls men of virtues little by little to flow away, and by certain steps of decline to fall down into the lowest things, and at some time, through visiting grace, to rise again to their former, or rather to a stronger, state of spirit. Now this corruption of change, or this remedy of repair, is wont assuredly to happen in either life, that is, as well in the active as in the contemplative. Of both, unless I am mistaken, the type is expressed in this one vision, [1231A] if it be rightly weighed. For what is it that Nabuchodonosor received a mystical vision, lost it, and afterward again came to know it more abundantly. For he deserved at the last to receive it together with its own interpretation. What, I say, is hinted to us in all these things, unless that the grace of contemplation is at one time divinely given, at times withdrawn, and at last repaired more manifoldly. But the declines of the active life are signified in the statue, which, begun from gold, ends in clay: at the top golden, in the middle silver or bronze, at the bottom iron or of clay. Behold, as thou seest, by how much the lower, by so much is it always found worse than itself. In the varied change of the metals are marked the varied declines of kingdoms, and in both the manifold failure of morals or of merits. [1231B] But behold, after the manifold failure of the kingdoms, that kingdom is raised up which shall not be scattered for ever. So often the human mind, after manifold ruin, pricked with compunction by divine inspiration, returns to righteousness; and, instructed and humbled by its own very fall, by how much the more instructed and by how much the more humble, by so much the stronger also does it rise again.

This also is to be noted, that the grace of the contemplative life slips away than [cj: *quam*; read *facilius quam*, "more easily than"] that of the active, but is repaired with much greater difficulty. Hence it is that Nabuchodonosor recovered with so great difficulty the vision of the dream which he had lost so easily. Hence it is that the decline of the kingdoms is described more at large, and is distinguished by steps, while the bringing in of the indestructible kingdom [1231C] is touched upon in few words, and is brought in suddenly indeed, and without any gradation. And perhaps what is said of either grace of the twofold life is given to be understood of both. For Holy Scripture often says one thing and hints another. And so both are wont by turns to befall: namely, that a man exercised in the active life should fall suddenly and unlooked for, and likewise that one cast down from the contemplative should rise again, through visiting grace, with wonderful ease. But fittingly enough in this place, where the fall or the repair of either life is treated, fittingly, I say, he has expressed the property of each, and has shown by what proportion they answer to one another. For from the contemplative we fall away more easily, and to the active we rise again more easily. Now this vision of King Nabuchodonosor (like the rest of his own) shows plainly enough how aptly [1231D] he received such a name. For beyond doubt the dream which he saw was a foretoken of things to come. And that also which through the foretoken he foresaw as future was none the less a sign of other things: for it expressed the accidents of morals. See then how rightly he obtains such a name. For Nabuchodonosor is interpreted prophesying a sign of this sort. Was not Nabuchodonosor prophesying through the mystical vision, whence also his vision was a prophecy, which portended the varied failures of kingdoms? And who does not know that all things befell them in figure? Therefore that change of kingdoms was a figure, because it signified something else through a mystery; and it was a sign of spiritual things. And so he was prophesying a sign, who in the foretoken of things to come was foreseeing figures of spiritual things. But what kind of sign? [1232A] A sign of this sort, that is, such a sign that what it is, or how true it is, can be proved by experience and shown as it were to the eye; or a sign of this sort, as it were of that thing which can be shown with the finger. For if in the decline of kingdoms the decline of morals is said to be understood, in what place, I ask, or at what time could this not be shown, could this not be seen. And this interpretation of the name indeed agrees with him well enough, according to what befell him. For there is another also, no less fitting, according to what he did: for Nabuchodonosor is interpreted *a sitting in the acknowledgement of anguish.* What is it to sit, unless to humble oneself? But consider how greatly this king humbled himself, who adored Daniel, a servant made over to him out of captivity, when he came to know the casting down of his kingdom [1232B] (which he could not know without anguish) by that man's own exposition. And so he sat in the acknowledgement of anguish, who, when he had heard of the imminent peril, humbly prostrated himself at the feet of him that prophesied. Now these things which have been said concerning the interpretation of the name seem to allude to the history; but none the less this too is to be considered, how they may be able to agree with the mystery. For what else does this so great person signify, who seems to be at once king and prophet, unless a spirit endowed with a twofold grace and fully exercised as well in the active life as in the contemplative? Does not the human spirit seem to thee to bear in itself in some manner a kingly person, when it has begun to preside strongly over the affections and the passions (both by the right of free will and by the bestowal of grace), and to command with authority, and [1232C] manfully to claim that kingdom of heaven which is within us? Does it not seem to thee to be strong with a certain gift as it were of prophecy, when now by the endowment of foresight it has received power to foresee and to foretell imminent perils; but most of all when it has now begun, through the grace of contemplation, to search into the deep things of the mysteries, and through excess of mind, and as it were through a dream, to marvel at the secrets of the divine judgments? And rightly is he also called Nabuchodonosor who is endowed with the gift of foresight; and then does he truly prophesy a sign of this kind, when from preceding causes he foresees the declines of morals. For the declines of merits always signify the declines of rewards. For beyond doubt, by how much is taken away from [1232D] merit, by so much is always taken away from the reward also. He therefore prophesies a sign, and a sign of this sort, who foretells a sign of this kind, or a sign of that thing which — of what sort they be — can in some manner be shown with the finger and proved by experience. But often the failure of good desires foreshows the failure of good works. For, when the will grows torpid, the spirit must afterward grow lukewarm from good zeal. But it is wont to befall conversely also. But to him to whom it is given to be able to foresee imminent perils and evils yet to come, if he takes care and is busy to bring them with grief and anguish of spirit into consideration, and to humble himself in the expectation of them, the same name can fit him according to the other interpretation. For Nabuchodonosor, as has been said, is interpreted [1233A] *a sitting in the acknowledgement of anguish.* But let no one wonder that by an unjust king a manly spirit and a man of virtues is said to be understood. For who does not know (unless he be unskilled in the sacred letters) how often in the Scriptures the serpent, a venomous animal, and the lion, a most ferocious animal, signify Christ? And if we believe blessed Gregory expounding it, in David the adulterer Christ is understood through a mystery, and in innocent Uriah is understood that faithless Jewish people. What wonder therefore if by the unjust king whom we disapprove we understand a just thing which we approve? And indeed in the unjust king, although the will was disordered, yet the power belonging to him was ordered. For there is no power but from God. *And the things which are from God,* the Apostle bearing witness, *are ordered [n: (Rom. XIII)]*. [var: Vulg. Rom. 13:1 *quae autem sunt, a Deo ordinatae sunt*, "the powers that are, are ordained by God"; Migne draws the agent into the relative clause and prints the neuter participle] [1233B] And so fittingly enough the royal power signifies the spirit of virtues, which claims for itself that inward kingdom by its morals; or free will, through which, by cooperating grace, the spirit commands its own affections and passions.
