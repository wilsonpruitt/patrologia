## CHAPTER II. *How the grace of contemplation is to some at one time given, at another withdrawn, and meanwhile, when lost, recovered.*

*In the second year of the reign of Nabuchodonosor, Nabuchodonosor saw a dream, and his spirit was terrified, and his dream fled from him [n: (Dan. II)]*. The first zeal of a manly spirit ought to be that it may have dominion over the affections; the second, that it may command the thoughts; the third, therefore — the spirit, and the soul that is victor over its own vices, [1233C] begins from that time already to have in its own kingdom all spiritual things whatsoever, when, its excesses being now subdued, it prevails to command not only the affections but also the thoughts, so that it may be able to restrain both, the former from unlawful concupiscence and the latter from unfitting wandering. But what is it to see a dream, and to foresee the foretokens of things to come or the mysteries of hidden things, unless to receive divinely the grace of contemplation, and through excess of mind to perceive out of divine revelation the knowledge of the divine judgments? And rightly did this king see the dream in the second year, because every spiritual man, after the taming of concupiscence and the discipline of his knowings, at length at some time prevails and grows accustomed to be at leisure for the zeal of contemplation. [1233D] But it often comes to pass that, while the human spirit is led to contemplate the subtlety, or even the strictness, of the divine judgments, it is shaken to the marrow with a sudden fear out of that very consideration of the divine strictness which it perceives. For while a mind that is untrained, and less instructed in heavenly disciplines, still presumes all things upon its own effort rather than upon divine grace, it easily falls into this fear from the contemplation of the divine severity and the consideration of that equity. For a presumptuous mind, when with wondrous subtlety it beholds how far it is above human virtue to answer to the divine benefits, and how strictly the divine severity, by the right of equity, may and ought to deal with us, is often wont to hurl itself into an immense dread [1234A] (nay, almost even into despair). But while the trembling spirit is confounded with a dread of this sort (to say nothing of how justly, beyond all doubt, and as mercifully as profitably), under this crisis of necessity the grace of contemplation is withdrawn, lest by the excess of its own subtlety it be hurled wholly into the pit of despair. And so the dream as it were flees from him, while he loses that light of contemplation which he was wont to see through excess of mind.

*But the king commanded that the soothsayers, and the magicians, and the sorcerers, and the Chaldeans should be called together, that they might declare to the king his dreams [sic: sommia].* He assuredly bears a kingly spirit who is wont to command his own desires, and to dispose his zeals and his exercises according to the judgment of deliberation. [1234B] And so a kingly spirit of this sort makes all the wise men of Babylon come together into one, and in a manner drives diverse sects into one by scrutiny, when it spends upon the one and only business of heavenly doctrine all that solicitude and searching which it was wont to scatter through varied zeals and exercises. But from wise men of this sort, coming together as it were into one, we seek back as it were our lost dreams, when we busy ourselves to repair, by the manifold and untiring urgency of our zeals, the spectacles of our contemplation that have been withdrawn. *Who, when they had come, set up before the king.* [var: Vulg. Dan. 2:2 *steterunt*, "they stood"; the word Migne prints is transitive and leaves the clause without an object] Behold what is said; behold what manner of assembly is declared to stand before the king. Thou mayest reckon that thou hast not yet a kingly spirit, if thou art not yet able to gather from every side, at thy wish, those most subtle points of thy thoughts, [1234C] and, by the liberty of thy judgment, to fix them unmovably upon the zeal of whatever intention thou wilt. *And the king said to them: I saw a dream, and being confounded in mind I know not what I saw.* We address the assembly of our wise men when in our inmost parts we rebuke the wandering of our searchings, or inflame it to the investigation of sublime things. But let us be zealous, after the likeness of this man, not to neglect the losses of our advances, but rather to stir up our zeals unto their repair.

This surely is to be noted, that above he says that he was terrified, but here that he was confounded in mind. But it is to be known that the human mind, while it is lifted up to contemplate more clearly the steep things of the divine equity, [1234D] is, by the beating back of its own infirmity, at one and the same time confounded from that same source whence it is terrified. For beyond doubt, in the consideration of the divine justice and in the comparison of human weakness, a man shudders at his own infirmity, and equally blushes at it. *And the Chaldeans answered the king in Syriac: O king, live for ever.* Tell the dream to thy servants, and we will declare the interpretation thereof. In the first place it must be considered to whom and in what manner they answered. Namely, to a Chaldean king, in Syriac — which indeed is the same thing, as Jerome has determined upon this same place, as in Chaldee. What then is it to answer the king in his own tongue, unless expressly and manifestly to convey that which it behooves to be known? For when anything is said to a man in [1235A] a foreign and unknown tongue, the noise of the voice indeed comes to the ears, but the sense of it does not enter as far as the spirit. Therefore answer is made as it were in one's own and known tongue, when that which the occasion demands is fittingly made known. But if we have recourse to the interpretation of the name, we are beyond doubt brought back to the same meaning. For Syria is interpreted *sublime.* What then will it be to answer in Syriac, unless to answer sublimely? and what will it be to answer sublimely, unless to answer fittingly and sufficiently? But the spirit knows nothing better, nothing more certain, and nothing more sublime than what it has learned through experience; and perhaps this is the proper and chief, and altogether the most sublime, manner of learning belonging to the human soul, when by our own experience [1235B] we prove something. Do not thy own thoughts or occupations answer thee as properly as openly, and instruct thee unto the truth sublimely enough, when out of their own failure they teach, and as it were through experience declare, how vainly and how rashly anything above the human measure is presumed upon one's own effort? Surely the whole answer of these men seems chiefly to contain this (namely, that) they were altogether unable to do what the king was violently demanding. In wise men of this sort therefore, such as we have showed thee above, this will be, as I judge, their answering: to teach their own impossibility by their own experience. But let us handle the several parts of their answer, and first let us see concerning the first.
