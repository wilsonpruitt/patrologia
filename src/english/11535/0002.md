## CHAPTER III. *That in all that we do we ought to be zealous for a good intention and to lean upon divine grace.*

[1235C]

*O king,* they say, *live for ever [n: (Dan. II)]*. That we may therefore compare together outward things with inward, bodily with spiritual: would that we had that inward household as well disciplined as we have known this outward one to be disciplined. We have heard what they answer at the first to their king, and perhaps he himself had taught them this very thing; let us also teach our own household (namely, in every endeavour of ours) to answer things most like: O king, live for ever. Yet what will this be, I ask, a wish, or an admonition? A wish, that he may avail for this; or an admonition, that he may spend all zeal upon this very thing? But what is [1235D] the life of that inward prince, and lord of that inward household? What, I say, is the life of that which is chief in the soul (namely, of the human mind), unless a good intention? Whatever a man may do, even if it be good, yet it will be unprofitable unless it be done with a good intention. But then the mind of a man dies even amid good works, when the intention is corrupted. It is the Truth's own attestation: *Because, if thine eye be evil, thy whole body shall be darksome [n: (Matth. VI)]*. What therefore will it be to live for ever, unless to have a good intention in every good work? Would that at every endeavour of ours, at every good beginning, this answer might first meet us; that all our thought might sigh and busy itself for this, that we may live for ever, which indeed will then come to pass, [1236A] if we do nothing but what is good, and nothing without a good intention. For, to say nothing of the rest: oh, how often do we seek the truth itself, not for truth but for vanity, and the truth once found we love, not in truth, but unto vanity; and, what is most wretched, in the words of life we traffic for the gains of death; or at any rate we rightly understand the life of the inner man to be divine grace! For as the body can do nothing without its own life, that is, without the soul, so our inner man can do no good thing without divine grace: *By the grace, of God,* says the apostle, *I am what I am [n: (Cor. XV)]*. And the Truth himself concerning himself: *Without me you can do nothing [n: (Joan. XV)]*. What therefore is it to say, to live for ever, unless unceasingly to cleave to grace? Whatever thou mayest purpose, presume not of thine own effort, but of divine grace. [1236B] Whatever thou accomplishest, ascribe to him, not to thyself this assuredly was to be said to that king, and at that time above all, because he was seeking in a human way that which he had received divinely, and for the other thing that was hoped for was leaning upon human effort rather than upon divine grace. But this is very marvellous (nay, astonishing beyond the human measure), that often, when we say these things silently within ourselves and feel them inwardly, none the less, and at the very same time, we lean upon our own effort, as if we were wholly ignorant of cooperating grace. Often, when we say something subtly to our neighbours concerning the commendation of divine grace, we glory within ourselves over the subtlety of our own speaking. O infinite folly of man, [1236C] and dost thou indeed rightly say that a man can do nothing without grace? But if thou speakest the truth, whence dost thou glory? Let us therefore teach those most subtle searchers-out of hidden things, namely, those thoughts of ours, that in all our zeal, in every business, they busy themselves first and chiefly with this, that they first beget in us this solicitude: that we be zealous to presume of grace all that we propose, and to ascribe to divine grace all that we accomplish; and this belongs to living for ever.

## CHAPTER IV. *How, according to the varied successes of their zeals, some either feebly cast themselves down or emptily exalt themselves.*

*Tell,* says he, *the dream to thy servants, and we will declare the interpretation [1236D] thereof (Dan, II)*. Mark what they say. One thing they demand, and another they promise. They demand of the king the vision, and they promise its interpretation. And yet as vainly as they demand, so falsely do they promise. But there was one thing which they might have said truly, if they had been willing to confess the truth: namely, that they in no wise knew the vision, nor could of themselves have known it at all. They therefore who profess false things, why do they not at least confess true ones? A common modesty; a general shamelessness. It is common modesty to conceal one's own impossibility; it is general shamelessness to presume beyond possibility. *And the king answering said to the Chaldeans: The word has departed from me. Unless you declare to me the dream and the conjecture of it, you shall perish, and your houses shall be made public. But if you shall relate the dream and the conjecture [1237A] of it, you shall receive from me rewards, and gifts, and much honour.* Who, I ask, would not freely rebuke King Nabuchodonosor, that for such a cause he consigns his wise men to destruction, when perhaps he himself does the like, or worse? For what is it to destroy one's own wise men of one's own accord, unless utterly to lay aside the zeals of wisdom, and to be now unwilling to exercise those most subtle points of one's wit any further in the investigation of some doctrine? But what is it to make public the houses of the wise men, and to reduce them to the common use of all? Now by houses we are wont to mean that place of our dwelling to which, after manifold labours and runnings to and fro, we are always accustomed to return for the sake of rest. What therefore is more rightly given to be understood by the house of the wise men [1237B] than those private and secret leisures of their rest, wherein all the studious are wont to be free for the zeals of doctrine? The houses of our wise men therefore are in a manner made public, when those studious leisures of our rest are exposed to every wandering of dissoluteness, and the secret inner chambers of doctrine are prostituted to every care or curiosity. How many do we see, after the glorious marks of their zeals and the great things of their knowledge, when perchance they have been promoted to some step of honour, or perchance have taken up the care of some administration, straightway despise all the discipline of their doctrines, be free for secular affairs only and always, destroy in one day all the cultivators of wisdom, and make public their hidden secrets? [1237C] What would these men do if they received the kingdom of Nabuchodonosor? How many likewise do we see, after much urgency of spiritual exercises, after having at last received the grace of contemplation, when perchance they have been cast down from that state of their sublimity by some temptation or by weariness of whatever kind, and have perceived that they cannot return to that art of their height without some, or perchance without great, labour, straightway lay down or condemn all the effort of spiritual zeals, and expose themselves at every hour to all vagrant and empty runnings to and fro. Thou mayest see them not only not seek again those leisures of their contemplation, but even utterly lay aside all urgency of meditation or of reading. What [1237D] else do these men do than slay their own wise men, who overspread all spiritual understandings within themselves by the varied fluctuation of their own desires? Behold, we have heard concerning the threats; let us see concerning the promises. *But if the dream,* says he, *and the conjecture of it you shall relate, you shall receive rewards and gifts, and much honour from me.* A reward is when we receive that which we have deserved; a gift, when we receive that which we have not yet deserved; but an honour, when we obtain even that for the deserving of which we are less fit. And so a reward is what is rendered to merits; a gift, what is bestowed freely; an honour, what is conferred from dignity alone: rewards, says he, and gifts, and much honour you shall receive from me. Nothing, brethren, is truer: how gladly do we bestow upon our wise men the wages of their merits, [1238A] how thankfully do we confer upon them honour due or gratuitous? And that I may more fully declare what I think thereon: I look for nothing more certain, I fear nothing more, than that every man should busy himself that too much honour, and greater reverence than is fitting, be bestowed upon wise men of this sort, when perchance they have satisfied his desires and have answered his wishes according to his wish. Who would not ascribe more to his own zeals and disciplines than the Truth itself dictates? Who, I ask, would not wish to extend the reputation of his own knowledge and wisdom, not only according to the measure of equity, but even beyond, nay above, what equity owes? And so as often as the spirit gladly presses on in its own zeals, and thence promises itself an enlargement of fame and glory, it speaks in a manner to its own wise men: both rewards, and gifts; [1238B] and much honour you shall receive.
