## CHAPTER V. *In how many ways the spirit deceives itself, and not unwillingly hides its own vice from itself.*

The reward being set forth, the king straightway subjoins and says: *Tell me therefore the dream and the interpretation of it [n: (Dan. II)]*. Two things he seeks: the dream, and the interpretation of it. It is one thing to know the secrets of the divine judgments out of divine revelation, and another to have been able to weigh or to penetrate the reason of the same. This belongs to the knowledge of the dream, that to the interpretation of it. How many of the divine judgments do we know, whose reason we penetrate by no discussion of ours. *They answered a second time [1238C] and said: Let the king tell the dream to his servants, and we will declare the interpretation of it.* What is it to answer again, unless to teach their own impossibility more fully and more perfectly by the repeated failure of their own [sic: *conanimis*]? O how hardly is human shamelessness restrained from its own presumption. Behold, after many experiences, still impossible things are demanded, still impossible things are promised. But let us see what follows: *The king answered and said: I know for certain that you buy up the time, knowing that the word has departed from me. If therefore you shall not declare to me the dream, there is one sentence concerning you, that you have framed an interpretation also that is deceitful and full of deception, that you may speak to me until the time pass away. Tell me therefore the dream, that I may know that you speak a true interpretation [1238D] of it also.* Behold, from his urgency the king profited not nothing, who at length at some time detected their duplicity; and do not thou think that thou hast profited but little, when thou shalt be able clearly to detect the duplicity of thine own heart. How many things do we often feign, how many do we dissemble. Our weaknesses we gladly dissemble, and those good things which we are altogether unable to do we feign that we can do or have: and it is very marvellous that the spirit gladly deceives itself, and not unwillingly hides its own vice from itself. Behold, the king detected both vices, of simulation and of dissimulation, in his subjects: that of dissimulation indeed in that he says, I know for certain that you buy up the time. That of simulation, in that after a few words he adds, There is one sentence concerning you, that you have framed a deceitful interpretation [1239A] also. They dissembled their own ignorance, and were willing [cj: *volebant*; read *nolebant*, "were unwilling"] to confess it openly, and in this they were buying up the time, and yet concerning the interpretation of the dream they were promising false things. But the evil of dissimulation in them the king proved by experience, while the evil of simulation he knew from reason, gathering the one vice out of the other. For if they could not do even that which was the less, how could they have done that whereby it was the greater? From these things, as I judge, thou weighest and observest that some things in us we prove by experience, and others assuredly we gather from reason. *The Chaldeans therefore answered before the king and said: There is not a man upon earth that can fulfil thy word, O king.* Behold, now a third time they answer, and at length at some time they manifestly teach their own failure. And that [1239B] we may distinguish by steps the three alternations of the answer, see whether perchance that first answer be of doubt, the second of fear, the third of despair. And at the first endeavour indeed, when we begin something and yet do not finish it, we often begin to doubt of the consummation of what we have begun. But at the second, to dread the more. But at the third endeavour, when we obtain no effect, we are wont utterly to despair.

And note that doubt and dread have an ambiguous answer, but despair has a manifest answer. So assuredly at the first and the second those men rather lessen their own ignorance than speak it. But at the third, they now openly profess the light. Yet let us hear how falsely they speak true things: There [1239C] is not, say they, a man upon earth that can fulfil thy word. They do not say simply, We cannot, which assuredly was true, but, There is not a man upon earth that can, which beyond doubt was false. For Daniel also was a man, and was upon earth, who was able to do this, as the outcome of the matter afterward proved. Yet he was able, not of his own effort, but of divine grace. Who would say that he could not do what he was able to obtain by praying? See in how many ways, and how often, the human spirit deceives itself; but who would suffice to explain this fully? How many things do we often presume that we can do, which yet we altogether cannot; and how many things which we altogether cannot do, do we think that no one can? What is it, I ask, [1239D] that the human spirit, according to the good which it approves in itself, does not at all proclaim others, and yet, according to the evil which it finds in itself, adds others? Behold, what these wise men presumed concerning the interpretation of the dream, they did not yet attribute equally to all in common. But nevertheless, what they could not do concerning the recovery of the dream, they affirm that no one can do. But that we may consider all this according to one and the same spirit: surely we ought neither rashly to presume anything in ourselves, nor rashly to despair. For often what we cannot do in one way, we easily accomplish in another. How many things which we often cannot get by doing, we obtain by praying.
