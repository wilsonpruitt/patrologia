## CHAPTER VI. *How rashly, or how vainly, we presume upon human effort for that which we ought to await from angelic revelation alone, or from divine.*

[1240A]

Yet let us see what these men further add, subtly discussing what they say faultily, or what perchance they say truly. For things uttered incautiously avail for our caution, and things rightly uttered have wherewith to form us unto the zeal of imitation. *But neither doth any of the kings,* say they, *great and mighty, inquire such a word of every soothsayer, and magician, and Chaldean [n: (Dan. II)]*. He is rightly called a king who is exalted unto a kingdom. But what says that King, truly King of kings and Lord of lords? *The kingdom of heaven,* says he, *is within you [n: (Luc. XVII)]*. [var: Vulg. Luc. 17:21 reads *regnum Dei*, "the kingdom of God"] Unto this kingdom surely we can be exalted, [1240B] if we perfectly will it. This kingdom, existing within us, surely has many inhabitants and dwellers without number. But by how much the more each man enlarges such a kingdom, more faithfully dispenses it, more strictly rules it, more prudently orders it, by so much the mightier is he made, by so much the greater is he judged. But unto this kingdom poverty of spirit advances and conducts more than abundance of sense: for *Blessed* are *the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven [n: (Matth. V)]*. He who receives such a kingdom sings confidently, when he says: *But I am appointed king by him [n: (Psal. II)]*. And well is it said of kings of this sort. But neither doth any of the kings, great and mighty, inquire a word of this kind of every soothsayer, and magician, and Chaldean. But in this the said kings are great, and defend their own power for themselves, [1240C] that they presume nothing of their own virtue: how much less those things which are above man? Certainly the king had received that word divinely, and it was altogether such that it could not be known unless from God's revelation. That therefore which could not be known save from God, with what rashness is it presumed of a man, a soothsayer, a magician, or a Chaldean? What therefore is it to be unwilling, and not to be bound, to inquire a word of this kind of any wise man of this sort, unless that those things which are above man ought to be presumed of no human effort or art? Hence it is that it is added, when it is said: *For the word which thou, O king, seekest is grievous, neither shall there be found any one that may declare it in the sight of the king, except the gods, whose conversation is not with men.* Certainly [1240D] what was above man, the king ought not to have demanded of man. But what is more grievous, or what more burdensome, than that a man should demand of a man things impossible for a man, and should wish to wrest from human possibility things that are above the human measure? But the things that are impossible with men are possible with God. Except, say they, the gods, whose conversation is not with men. Rightly enough in this place do we understand by the gods the holy angels. For if because of the participation of the image, or even of the likeness, men have been called gods, how much more rightly the angels? *I have said: you are gods and sons of the Most High [n: (Psal. LXXXI)]*; and angels indeed are engaged among men at every hour, nay at almost every moment, and yet they do not converse with men, because even then, when they are among men, [1241A] they live not in a human way, but in a heavenly, and truly in a divine way. Who would deny or not know that angels have been able to reveal to men many things which are above men, and are wont to reveal them? But that we may still draw a likeness from outward things to our inmost parts, so often the things which we penetrate by no investigation of ours, we come to know from divine revelations and inspirations. But if any one should wish that by the gods there be understood not angels, but men divinely inspired, whose conversation is now not with men (that is, with such as savour of human things), in that they can now truly say, Our conversation is in heaven, nothing truer can be said. For they come to know many things from divine inspiration which exceed human investigation. But if that former sentence be accommodated to this sense, [1241B] nothing in it seems to sound but what is right, where it is said. There is not a man upon earth that can fulfil thy word, O king: as if it were said, None of those who are upon earth, save only those who by a heavenly conversation ascend the heavens.
