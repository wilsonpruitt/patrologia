## CHAPTER VII. *How, after the often illustrious distinctions of its own studies, the mind lays aside the care of doctrines both secular and spiritual; and exposes itself wholly to outward business alone.*

*And when this was heard, the king in fury and in great wrath commands that all the wise men of Babylon should perish. And when the sentence had gone forth, the wise men were being slain [n: (Dan. II)]*. Behold what we have already spoken of above. After the king had despaired of the recovery of his dream, in fury [1241C] and wrath he decreed that all his wise men should perish. As we have already said, this is to destroy his wise men: no longer to seek out, according to the former custom and the accustomed exercise, those keen and subtle apprehensions of knowledge and of wisdom, and to spend no labor at all upon the studies of any teaching whatever, nor to bestow care upon them. The dream that was seen is as it were lost when that grace of contemplation by which the mind was frequently lifted up to sublime things is withdrawn; and then indeed we as it were despair of its recovery, when, laboring long and much and profiting nothing, we distrust that we can any longer recover that delight of our spiritual sights. But it often happens that, when the spirit loses that gazing upon divine revelations which it had been [1241D] wont to love, being now broken by long weariness, it lays aside the care not only of that in whose restoration it failed, but of studies of every kind, and in a manner disposes to destroy all its wise men. In fury, he says, and in great wrath. Fury is a disturbance of the mind wholly devoid of reason. Wrath is a great disturbance of the mind, yet not utterly alien from reason. We are driven and driven about as it were out of fury, when, with no regard to reason, we cast ourselves headlong into that which ought not to be done. Often, moved by wrath, we understand what ought to be done, though it cannot be reined in, if it be great. And so in the first onset of our disturbance we often do not so much as look back to reason; but afterwards we do indeed see reason, [1242A] yet cannot follow it; and at last we both behold it and follow it. Rightly therefore is fury set first, and wrath afterwards.

It is certainly to be noted that those who in this place are commanded to perish are called the wise men of Babylon. I think that the wise men of the Babylonians are one thing, and the wise men of the Jews another. The former, indeed, are occupied about earthly things, the latter about divine. The former follow after the wisdom of the world, which is foolishness with God; the latter search even the deep things of God, being spiritual, and comparing spiritual things with spiritual. What then do we understand by the wise men of the Babylonians, but secular sciences? and what by the wise men of the Jews, but spiritual teachings? So then we as it were consign the wise men of Babylon to condemnation, when we cut off the secular disciplines from our [1242B] studies. Our conscience acquiesces more easily in the cutting off of these, and the more lightly consents to their being cast away, the more it observes them (however useful for something to human salvation) to be in no way necessary. First, then, the wise men of Babylon are slaughtered; whence it is that of them it is said: And when the sentence had gone forth, the wise men were being slain. The sentence goes forth when at length a man manifests in deed what he had deliberated and determined in his mind. That is as it were still within which is still turned over and lies hidden in the mind; and it begins as it were to be already put into practice when it now appears in deed. There follows: And Daniel and his companions were sought, that they might perish. Behold how the evil spreads. We have heard above how the wise men of Babylon were being slain; behold, now the wise men [1242C] of the Jews are sought, that they may be slain. Behold by what steps a studious mind is often loosened from its own liveliness, and slips back little by little from the highest things to worse ones: first it lays aside the care of secular, afterwards of spiritual teachings [sic: *doctrinam*], and exposes itself wholly to outward business alone, and slays within itself as it were all the cultivators of knowledge and of wisdom, while after its contempt for the human arts it utterly forsakes the urgency of contemplation, of meditation, of reading and of prayer.

It is to be noted, however, that of the wise men of the Jews it is said that they were sought for destruction. What, I ask, is it to seek a place where wise men of this sort may be found for destruction, but to snatch at an occasion, [1242D] or even at the opportunity of some excuse, whereby a dissolute mind may the more safely and the more freely, and with a more secure conscience, cast away even the pursuits of sacred instruction? For we often plead infirmity of the body when we cast away spiritual exercises. Often we pretend this to be charity, that, forsaking the contemplative life, we utterly expose ourselves to outward business. In this way an occasion, and as it were the opportunity of a place, is sought, where Daniel with the rest of the wise men of the Jews may be found for slaying. If we consult the deeds of this Daniel, what shall we more rightly find figured in him than the devotion of the heart? See how, under every adversity and in whatever necessity, he always has recourse to the Lord and consults him, implores and receives the divine aid. [1243A] And who does not know that it belongs to true devotion, under every crisis of necessity, to flee to the divine defenses, and in doubtful matters as well as in adverse ones humbly to ask counsel from the one side and aid from the other, and effectually to obtain it? But we know that the mind never prays inwardly and devoutly which does not first rouse itself to devotion by diligent premeditations. For these are the inseparable companions of true devotion: circumspect, discerning and provident thoughts. For without companions and fellow workers of this kind, whatever devotion is ours neither knows nor is able to pray as it ought.

## CHAPTER VIII. *How in every purpose or ordinance we ought to consult the rule of equity, and to call back what has been improvidently decreed to a sounder judgment.*

[1243B]

*Then Daniel inquired concerning the law and the sentence from Arioch, the prince of the king's soldiery, who had gone forth to slay the wise men of Babylon; and he questioned him who had received power from the king, for what cause so cruel a sentence had gone forth from the king's face. When therefore Arioch had made the matter known to Daniel, Daniel went in and asked the king that he would give him time to declare the solution to the king [n: (Dan. II)]*. As in Daniel the fervor of devotion is understood, so by the prince of the soldiery we rightly, as I judge, understand the zeal of rectitude and the rigor of strict severity. Certainly, without the rigor of just severity, not even that kingdom may be possessed in peace or in security in which men are wont to command men, and in which men are wont to disturb the peace [1243C] of those who possess it. What ruler was ever able to enjoy a lasting peace, or to rejoice in it, who was unwilling strictly to judge and strongly to strike the adversaries of peace, when he could and when he ought? But how much more in that kingdom, in which our wrestling is not against flesh and blood, but against the spiritual things of wickedness in the heavenly places; how much more, I say, in a kingdom of this sort ought we to act strongly, and with strict punishment to strike all the tumults of clamorous thoughts or affections, and whatever disturbers there be of inward peace? Good indeed is this prince of the soldiery (namely rigor; and the severity of justice), whose laws or disciplines whoever does not obey estranges himself [1243D] from that fellowship of the heavenly soldiery. And rightly, as I judge, is this prince of the soldiery called Arioch. For Arioch is interpreted *reducing to solitude*. We know that the kingdom of heaven is within us [var: Vulg. Luke 17:21 *regnum Dei intra vos est*, "the kingdom of God is within you"]. And so this prince of the soldiery ought, whatever kingdom or region he sees rising up or rebelling against this kingdom and not obeying the laws of the eternal King, to overthrow it by a strong assault, and to reduce it to everlasting solitude, and, if it can be done, to blot it out forever. If therefore by Daniel we ought to understand devotion, and by Arioch the zeal of rectitude, what is it for Daniel to inquire of Arioch concerning the law and the sentence of the king, but for a mind stricken with compunction to consult the judgment of equity, and out of great devotion vehemently to examine whether that be perhaps just or unjust which it remembers itself [1244A] to have formerly determined through the impulse of its own spirit? For in the time of compunction and of prayer the mind of man finds many things to be evil and unjust which it had formerly defined out of the impulse of its spirit, and as it were headlong, and which nevertheless it did not perceive at that time to be unjust; and often, out of that same impulse of his spirit, a man dictates many things against his own conscience, and yet afterwards, in the time of his prayer and of his devotion, he tempers that same headlong haste of his spirit, and reforms it to a sounder judgment. And so we ought afterwards diligently to examine those things which we have decreed headlong rather than prudently, and which we greatly fear to be evil, and to search out by shrewd reconsideration whether they be just or unjust, useful or useless; and in this way the office [1244B] of Arioch is as it were inquired of through Daniel concerning the law or the sentence given, when at length, out of great devotion, the rule of true equity and of the highest discretion is consulted concerning any less considered purpose of the mind. But what it is to go forth to slay the wise men can easily be weighed from what has gone before. It is to be known, however, that we often more easily detect, and more fully dread, the determination or the quality of an ill-considered sentence at that time when we now prove by the deed itself, and as it were by experiment, what sort it is. Hence it is that Arioch in this place is consulted about the giving of the sentence then first, when he is already going forth on occasion of it.
