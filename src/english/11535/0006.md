## CHAPTER IX *That whatever is wont to befall against us answers, by God's dispensation, to the free will of the mind according to the quality of merits.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1244C]

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But if by Arioch the strictness of equity is rightly understood, it will perhaps seem strange to someone, and not without reason, how the execution of a sentence of this kind is said to belong to the office of Arioch, since that same sentence is by no means judged just, either according to the history or according to the mystical understanding. ⟦s2⟧ Or did the king perhaps, according to the history, being troubled with fury, believe that to be just which was not truly just, in that he might then truly have said: My eye is troubled with fury? ⟦s3⟧ Just so, indeed, certain men after the type of this likeness, begetting a zeal of rectitude but not having it according to knowledge, believe it to be right, or feign it to be right, [1244D] that they slay their own wise men, setting before themselves or promising themselves greater gains of merit to be acquired from bodily exercises than from spiritual ones, in which meanwhile they cannot make as much progress, or as easily, as they wish. ⟦s4⟧ But if we look still more deeply, and have recourse to the divine judgments, we shall at once see that many things which a man disposes or does unjustly, God permits to be done in no way but justly. ⟦s5⟧ Why then should not those things also be said to pertain to the office of Arioch which, according to this consideration, are proved by the equity of the divine strictness to answer to our merits? ⟦s6⟧ And he questioned, he says, him who had received power from the king. ⟦s7⟧ Mark, then, that this Arioch received power from the king, because whatever [1245A] befalls us, whether profitably or unprofitably, comes down from the merit or the fault of free will. ⟦s8⟧ And Arioch receives from the king the power of executing a sentence now profitable, now unprofitable: sometimes from one who knows and wills, sometimes from one who knows and does not will, sometimes from one who does not know and wills, sometimes from one who neither knows nor wills. ⟦s9⟧ For, as has been said, what is wont to befall each man answers, by God's dispensation, to the free choice of liberty according to the quality of merits. ⟦s10⟧ Whatever therefore is done concerning us is regulated, for the requital of merits and by the right of equity, by the just judgment of God. ⟦s11⟧ But that provident gaze of the spirit often, under the onset of its own great disturbance, recognizes the good pleasure or judgment of the Almighty and acquiesces in it. ⟦s12⟧ Sometimes it recognizes it and does not acquiesce; but sometimes [1245B] it is so wrought that it desires the will of God alone to be done concerning itself, although meanwhile it does not fully understand it; and sometimes it is so affected that it cannot at all acquiesce in the will of God, even if it had been able fully to know that will (which meanwhile it cannot know). ⟦s13⟧ But what wonder if the human spirit cannot always acquiesce even in a divine will that is known, since indeed it often cannot acquiesce even in its own advantage? ⟦s14⟧ For often through impatience it dictates a sentence which it is in no way ignorant to be contrary to its own advantage. ⟦s15⟧ Therefore that prince of the soldiery which we have named receives from his king the power of executing a sentence not then only, when a king of this sort disposes something justly and usefully, but even now [cj: *nunc*; read *tunc*, "then"] indeed, when he ordains something through shamelessness or even [1245C] through impatience which, by God's disposition and his own preceding merits, answers to the requital of equity; nay, even then, when he does something whereby he incurs a hateful, though just, sentence.

## CHAPTER X. *By what considerations the things that befall us are to be weighed.*

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But let us further see what that is which Daniel again inquires of Arioch. ⟦s2⟧ For what cause so cruel a sentence had gone forth from the king's face. ⟦s3⟧ A fitting order, and diligently to be observed: first he is questioned concerning the law and the sentence, afterwards concerning the cause of that same sentence. ⟦s4⟧ Without doubt there must be diligent inquiry [1245D] and vehement examination (in matters doubtful, at all events, and most perilous) first concerning the quality of the action, and consequently concerning the quality of the intention. ⟦s5⟧ For often the fault that is contracted from the action is not a little lightened by the quality of the intention. ⟦s6⟧ Yet that he inquires concerning the cause of the sentence we can, as I judge, nay we ought, to refer to two things: namely, with what intention it is done, or by what disposition of God it happens to be done. ⟦s7⟧ And concerning the action it is asked whether it be advantageous or disadvantageous, just or unjust. ⟦s8⟧ But concerning the intention, whether it be perhaps pious or impious, discerning or undiscerning. ⟦s9⟧ And concerning God's disposition let each man especially search this out in the investigation of a dread or a hard sentence: whether he strikes us for our profit or for vengeance. ⟦s10⟧ For certainly, when he smites us [1246A] or permits us to be struck, he does it now for our instruction, now for our correction, now for our rebuke, now for our condemnation, or in yet some other way whatever. ⟦s11⟧ All these things are to be diligently inquired into and subtly examined. ⟦s12⟧ All these things strike in a profitable fear, and prick the mind with compunction in prayer. ⟦s13⟧ All these things humble the mind, and rouse our devotion, and inflame it to supplication. ⟦s14⟧ Thou seest surely how rightly, how discreetly Daniel inquires into all these things before he presses on in prayers or applies himself to supplication. ⟦s15⟧ Hence it is that it is said, when there is added: When therefore Arioch had made the matter known to Daniel, Daniel went in and asked the king But what is that aforesaid learning of Daniel from Arioch, but that the devoted mind, the rule of equity having been diligently and devoutly [1246B] and strictly consulted, should at length, after much examination, more fully recognize the quality of the sentence sought and of its cause.
