## CHAPTER XI. *How in the time of our disturbance we ought to flee to the remedies of prayer, and to bring a support of this kind into use.*

It remains now to ask what it is for Daniel to go in to the king. Rightly perhaps by the king, who presides and commands over all, we understand in this place the vehement motion of the spirit and the strong impulse of free will, to which any other affection whatever readily yields or gives obedience. And to how many (when they are vehemently affected or sharply moved) does it not occur at all to consult reason or discretion, how much less [1246C] to flee to prayer, or to admit any devotion? But what else do we say this to be, than that none of the wise men dares to appear before the king? Yet often those who are of this sort, after the impulse of the spirit has been settled, when they come back to themselves, have recourse to the judgment of discretion and to the office of devotion, busying themselves to correct the things in which they went beyond measure in the time of disturbance. But if we are diligent about the discipline of the heart, as we ought to be, let us teach and accustom our spirit, under any impulse whatever and in the time of disturbance, to flee to the counsels of discretion; and this is to lose the wise men before the king's sight. But because it often happens that by no attestation of reason can it soothe the headlong impulse of its own [1246D] disturbance, the mind ought, under a crisis of necessity of this kind, to have recourse to the helps of devotion, and to obtain the supports of the divine mercy for the pacifying of the violence of its disturbance; and a remedy of devotion of this kind must be brought into use; and this is for Daniel to go in to the king. For Daniel as it were goes in to the king when, for the pacifying of the impulse of the spirit, devotion lends its support even under the very drive of headlong haste.

But it is to be noted that above he is said to have been brought in, whereas here it is simply set down that he went in. And I think that to be brought in by another and to go in by oneself are one thing and another; as is plain from what has gone before, to have the support of devout prayer ready at hand under every vehement impulse of the spirit, this is [1247A] that Daniel is not wanting in the presence of the raging king. But, as we all in common effect things by carefulness too, other things we obtain by prayers, by praying and beseeching God. When therefore by human shrewdness and by exercise the defense of devotion is made habitual for settling the impulse of the spirit, that Daniel of ours is in a manner brought into the presence of the aforesaid king by another's office. But when this is effected not so much by meditating as by praying, Daniel without doubt goes in to the king by himself. And so we ought by diligent and shrewd premeditation to rouse our mind to devotion; and this will be to bring Daniel in by another's office. Likewise, because often even when necessity demands and presses we cannot pray devoutly, let us pray in the time of the divine visitation and of our own compunction, [1247B] that in the time of necessity we may be able to pray. If, then, the faculty of praying is obtained by prayer, Daniel assuredly goes in by himself. But neither is this to be passed over negligently, that he is read to have gone in during the time of the king's disturbance and fury. For at the time of his being brought in we assuredly receive not even any indication of the king's disturbance. For we must not believe that this king is always angry and troubled with some fury. It is one thing for the impulse of the spirit to be vehement, another for it to be headlong. One thing, I say, to be strong and unconquered, and another to be full of wrath and madness. One thing, finally, to be invincible and almost immovable; but another to be intolerable and plainly irrational. [1247C] But who, I ask, in the time of the royal fury would presume, or would dare, to lead Daniel before the king? I judge it to be above human effort and virtue to apply oneself to prayer in the time of so great and so inward a disturbance, since scarcely, or rather in no way at all, does it happen at that time even to remember it. It behooves therefore, in the manner we have said above, that Daniel go in by himself before the troubled king, if he wishes under the crisis of so great a necessity to fight back against the peril. And so let the mind that is diligent and solicitous about its own salvation, which as yet, when it has been disturbed, does not know how to fight back against its disturbance by praying, and has not yet obtained the practice or the grace of this kind, pray and obtain in a time of tranquillity that the grace of praying in a time of disturbance be given it from God. [1247D] In this way it will perhaps be granted to Daniel at some time to go in to the king by himself, and while he is still raging; and then he will perhaps be able, not once only but often and assiduously, to ask and to obtain a respite for the deferring of an evil sentence, to this end, that he may afterwards utterly blot it out. When, in the time of its inward disturbance, the devoted mind humbly asks and beseeches that the divine goodness not permit him, under the impulse of a roused fury, to do anything through impatience whereof he must afterwards repent, if it obtains what it desires, what else does the Daniel of our devotion seem to thee to do, than to soothe the madness of the raging king? But when it feels itself drawn by the vehemence of its headlong affection to something which meanwhile, under the cloud or the whirlwind of so great a disturbance of its own, it cannot fully recognize whether [1248A] it be right, if meanwhile it asks patience for itself until in the time of its tranquillity it can more rightly discern what is to be done, then, by receiving what it asks, what else does it seem to thee to accomplish, than to ask and receive a respite? Daniel, he says, went in and asked the king that he would give him time to declare the solution to the king. Thou seest surely that devotion piously and profitably presumes often upon the Lord's goodness for things which human effort could by no shrewdness of its own bring about; and how justly we presume great things upon the Lord's goodness, if for love of him, with his help, we conquer our own magnanimity.
