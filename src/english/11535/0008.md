## CHAPTER XII *With what meditations the spirit must be inflamed to devotion, and how the urgency of our studies must be reformed.*

[1248B]

But let us see what Daniel did after these things, or what is written of him: *And having gone into his house, he made the matter known to Ananias, Misael, Azarias, his companions, that they might seek mercy from the face of the God of heaven concerning this sacrament, and that Daniel and his companions might not perish with the rest of the wise men of Babylon [n: (Dan. II)]*. By these three, Daniel's undivided companions and inseparable comrades, we understand circumspection, discretion, deliberation; without which we prove by daily experience that the office of true devotion can in no way be fulfilled. For if we wish to pray inwardly and devoutly, the first thing is that we look about upon our own [1248C] life. Let us see with what studies or exercises we have been accustomed to be occupied, or with what we are occupied still, or what purpose we have in these for the time to come. But what does it profit to bring these things into consideration, unless with the same solicitude we busy ourselves to discern each single thing by strong examination, and to separate the good from the bad by watchful shrewdness, that we may know for what things we ought to beseech the Lord, or even to give thanks? But when our good things or our evil ones are known, it still remains to search out by provident deliberation by what occupation or carefulness we may both make satisfaction for past evils, and extricate oneself from present ones, and more carefully exhibit caution concerning things to come. The first belongs to circumspection, the second to discretion, the third to deliberation. It belongs [1248D] to circumspection to bring the measure of our morals into consideration; it belongs to discretion to make judgment in all things; but to deliberation, to seek and to find counsel. Circumspection says: *I will recount to thee all my years in the bitterness of my soul [n: (Isai. XXXVIII)]*. Discretion confesses: *I have done judgment and justice [n: (Psal. XCVIII)]*. Deliberation speaks: *What shall I render to the Lord for all the things that he hath rendered to me? [n: (Psal. CXV.)]* She is called back to the pursuit of circumspection to whom it is said: *Lift up thine eyes round about [n: (Isai. XLIX)]*, and see then where thou hast lain prostrate, harlot [var: Vulg. Jer. 3:2 *vide ubi non prostrata sis*, "see where thou hast not lain prostrate"; the received text carries the negative, which is absent here]. He wished to encourage us to the examination of discretion who said: *If we judged ourselves, we should not indeed be judged [n: (I Cor. XI)]*. The office of deliberation is enjoined, or even commended, when it is said through the wise man: *Do all things with counsel, [1249A] and unto eternity thou shalt not repent [n: (Eccli. XXXII)]* [var: Vulg. Eccli. 32:24 *Fili, sine consilio nihil facias, et post factum non poeniteberis*, "Son, do nothing without counsel, and after the deed thou shalt not repent"]. But who does not see how necessary the offices of these are to true devotion? For who was ever able to pray inwardly and devoutly, if he has put off looking about upon his life, examining it, ordering it? As often therefore as necessity urges us to apply ourselves more vehemently to devout prayer, we must have recourse to the offices of circumspection and of discretion, or even of deliberation, and by such pursuits and busy meditations inflame our spirit to devotion; and this is for Daniel to take up the supports of his companions for the use of his prayer. And so Daniel as it were runs back to the inner chambers of his house, and seeks fellow workers, as it were, for the more perfect fulfilling of the business of his prayer, when, the time of prayer now pressing, or even the crisis of urgent [1249B] necessity, the devoted mind seeks again the accustomed opportunity of its own quiet, necessary for that office, and strives to animate or to inflame its devotion by certain provident meditations, apt for such a use. To his companions, he says, he made the matter known. The matter is as it were made known by Daniel to Daniel's companions, when out of devotion it is brought about that the impending matter is brought into consideration, examination, consultation. But mercy is set forth as a thing to be obtained, if the mind stricken with compunction, in that which it devoutly performs, leans not upon its own merits, but upon the divine mercies.

It is certainly to be noted, according to the history, that Daniel the prophet calls that matter a sacrament; whence who would doubt that a mystical understanding of things lies hidden in it? [1249C] And that Daniel, he says, and his companions might not perish with the rest of the wise men of Babylon. After the example of this thing, when we have seen our mind, after its contempt of secular studies, grow torpid toward the spiritual exercises also, and little by little fail, and the peril of our wise men press upon us, we too ought by all means to ask that of the Lord whereby we may be able to repair or to confirm the accustomed urgency of our studies. But there is, as we alike know, a very great labor both in the pursuit of praying and in that of meditating, and to minds that wander and are restless it is almost intolerable. But in contemplation the delight is wonderful and almost singular. When therefore the grace of contemplation is conferred upon diligent minds, [1249D] that labor of the other studies is by its sweetness lightened or tempered just so far as it is sweetened. If therefore we have not yet received grace of this kind, or grieve that it has been withdrawn, we ought to seek it from the Lord for the confirming or the reforming of our studies. When therefore we do these things, what else, I ask, do we seem to have done than to seek the dreams of divine revelations, that we may have wherewith we can preserve in their integrity those wise men of ours designated above? Then the mystery was revealed to Daniel by a vision in the night. The revelation of a mystery is as it were perceived by night, when the illumination of truth is seen thinly indeed, and in part, and as it were by the light of the moon. We perceive the revelation of a mystery as it were by night, when concerning some [1250A] secret of the Godhead we ourselves indeed understand what is to be thought, but do not yet have ready to hand the reason by which we can prove it to others also. The light of revelation appears as it were in the night and as though in the midst of darkness, when a man is not able to unfold in speech, and as it were to bring forth into the open light, the declaration of the mystery which he sees through understanding. But when afterwards by long reconsideration we bring it to pass that we can declare to others by clear demonstration the received understanding of the truth, which we ourselves formerly could scarcely or but thinly see, what else do we bring forth under the light of day than what we perceived by night? Rightly therefore is no mention made afterwards of night, where Daniel at length speaks openly in the presence of the king what he had formerly received by night.

## CHAPTER XIII. *How at the renewing of understanding we ought always to magnify, not our own effort, but the Lord's magnificence.*

[1250B]

But, the revelation of the mystery having been received, let us further see what Daniel does or says: *And Daniel blessed the God of heaven, and speaking said: Blessed be the name of the Lord from everlasting and even unto everlasting, for wisdom and fortitude are his, and he changeth times and ages, transferreth kingdoms and establisheth them [n: (Dan. II)]*. By this example let us learn what we ourselves ought to do when we receive the light of revealing grace. And so let us busy ourselves, after Daniel's example, to render glory and honor and thanksgiving to him from whom we have it to be wise [1250C] and to understand, and whatever of good it is agreed that we have. For there are many who, when they receive the light of truth, at once ascribe this to their own merits or studies, magnify and exalt themselves, and despise all others in comparison with themselves. A soul of this kind speaks within herself: I have understood above all my teachers. She strives to draw to herself that saying of Solomon, as imprudently as emptily: *I have gone before in wisdom all who were before me [n: (Eccle. I)]*. On the contrary, let us give glory to him from whom we have received understanding. Let us be zealous always, at the very rising of revealing grace, at the very renewing of an illumined understanding, to render proclamations of praise worthy of God. Blow up the trumpet on the new moon, on the notable day of your solemnity. Neomenia, the new moon; the new moon, renewed for the understanding and divinely [1250D] inspired. We know that the moon has not light from itself, but receives it from the sun that it may be able to shine. So indeed the human understanding has received from God, that it may be able to shine back with the rays of the true light. As often therefore as we receive new rays of divine illumination, and know from divine revelation something that exceeds human understanding, we ought at once to rise up into thanksgiving, and with the trumpet of magnificence to resound in the proclamations of the divine goodness: *Magnify,* he says, *the Lord with me, and let us exalt his name together [n: (Psal. XXXIII)]*. Behold how the trumpet has sounded; behold how at the blast of the trumpet he has roused others also along with himself. He wished a blast of such a trumpet to be made at the new moon, or promised that he would make it, when he said: *I will bless the Lord who hath given me understanding* [1251A] [n: (Psal. XV)]. So Daniel, when he received the light of revealing grace, sounded as it were the trumpet of magnificence at the rising of the new moon, when he said: Blessed be the name of the Lord from everlasting and even unto everlasting, for wisdom and fortitude are his.
