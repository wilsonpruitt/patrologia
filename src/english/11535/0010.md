## CHAPTER XV. *How we ought to consult the rule of equity the more strictly, at that time above all when some office of sublimity must be fulfilled.*

*After this Daniel, going in to Arioch, whom the king had appointed to destroy the wise men of Babylon, spoke to him thus: Destroy not the wise men of Babylon; bring me in before the king, and I will tell the king the solution* [1254B] [n: (Dan. II)]. If by Daniel the fervor of devotion is rightly understood, and by Arioch the strictness of equity, what else will it be to wish to go in to Arioch than to seek out more deeply the [sic: *secretioria*] secrets of equity, and to wish to discern by innermost searching what strict justice demands? Now this business is rightly ascribed to Daniel at that time when the human mind is urged to this very thing out of a great devotion of spirit. Daniel therefore goes in to Arioch when devotion seeks out the counsels of equity. There is also yet another cause for going in to him (and perhaps not a less necessary one), namely when we wish to pour into him, to the very marrow, what we have recognized from divine revelation as needing correction in his senses. There we go in to him that, from much investigation and examination of justice, we may recognize what is right; here we enter in to him [1254C] that what has been divinely recognized as right we may press home the more strictly with a zeal for rectitude. There, then, we enter for the sake of learning, here for the sake of teaching; or there for the sake of inquiring, here for the sake of persuading. But we perceive the cause of his entering the sooner if we attend to what he says: Destroy not the wise men of Babylon. Behold, what the king in his trouble commanded to be done, Daniel, divinely enlightened, forbade to be done; and it often happens that what, being troubled and carried by an impulse of the spirit, we discern as though it were right, and presume can or ought to be done as by the office of Arioch, afterwards, having turned to prayers and supplication and been divinely enlightened amid the tears of our compunction, we find to be unjust, which before we thought right. So devotion often finds that to be iniquitous, and forbids its being done, which a violent impulse of the spirit had decreed should be done as it were [1254D] under a royal fury: Destroy not, he says, the wise men of Babylon. And he adds: Bring me in before the king, and I will tell the king the solution. Why, I ask, does he in this place beg to be brought in by another, who already above entered before the king's sight by himself? But above he entered that he might make supplication to the king; here, however, that he might teach the king. That was of humility, this of great sublimity. Of great sublimity, I say, was it to teach the king that which he could have learned from no one else. To the services of humility, therefore, he thrust himself willingly; to the offices of eminence he declined to thrust himself shamelessly. By his example let us learn what we ought to do in matters of this kind. When the place of mastery must be looked up to, whether from a command or for an office, [1255A] let the mind first enter in to Arioch. Let it seek out the counsels of justice, and by anxious examination let it look ahead, that it may carry out a good work well and justly. Do the good well, he says. The good is then done, when a good work rises from a good intention and proceeds in a fitting manner. It is easy for ambition or vainglory to drive us to a conspicuous and illustrious work. It is easy for the leaven of the Pharisees to corrupt the whole lump of our good work. In no way are we brought in by Arioch when we are driven to a good work by a sinister intention. As often, therefore, as we take up the place of teaching, let us look ahead to how justly, how orderly we are to fulfil the office we have taken up. Let us see to it that we say nothing false or frivolous, nothing inept or disordered. This rule of learning and of teaching, unless I am mistaken, [1255B] he keeps to whom that word of the Psalmist could apply: *Pleasant is the man who has mercy and lends; he shall order his words in judgment [n: (Psal. CXI)]*. And perhaps this will be to consult Arioch outwardly: to fortify ourselves beforehand with counsels of rectitude against the vices of speech or of work. But he is consulted as it were inwardly when inquiry is made concerning inward things, and the soul is fortified beforehand by the ordinances of justice against the corruption of thoughts or of afflictions. Above all at that time ought we to go in to Arioch, and to seek his leading by the hand, and to do nothing without the forethought or the fortification of justice; above all, I say, at that time when we must appear in a place of eminence, or fulfil an office of sublimity. Bring me in, he says, before the king, and I will tell the solution [1255C] to the king. Now our Daniel truly uses the leading by the hand of the said Arioch when, from the recollection of the divine strictness and the consideration of true justice, the spirit is pricked with compunction. And then he is brought in as it were before the royal presence by such a leader, when under any impulse of the spirit whatever, and as it were in the presence of royal power, the heart of man is easily inflamed to devotion by the regard of a strict examination.
