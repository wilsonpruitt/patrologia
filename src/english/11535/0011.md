## CHAPTER XVI. *By what steps the knowledge of mysteries advances, and to how great a perfection of learning the spirit often ascends by the merit of devotion.*

And I will tell the king, he says, the solution. What is this solution of the mystic vision, but the easy unravelling and ready [1255D] explication of the divine sacraments and mysteries? But if by the king we must understand the power of free will, what else will it be for such a solution to come to the royal cognizance, than to have the account of the divine mysteries openly and ready at hand? If therefore we have it free and ready and in our own power to bring forth in common the unravelling of hidden things whenever we will, we do not doubt that the learning and unravelling of the divine mysteries is already in our own choosing, and has already come, as it were, to the royal power. Now so great an enlargement of learning or of teaching is rightly ascribed to Daniel at that time when such grace is acquired and possessed by the merit of devotion. Let us see, then, by what steps of advancement the knowledge of divine things is enlarged. First of all Daniel received cognizance of the mystic vision from divine [1256A] revelation. Afterwards the same thing became known to his companions also, and at last the learning of that same thing came even to the king's cognizance. Of the first it is said: *Then to Daniel the mystery was revealed by a vision in the night.* [var: Vulg. *per visum nocte*, "by a sight in the night"] But afterwards Daniel professes, as much for himself as for his companions: *Because the word,* he says, *of the king thou hast opened to us.* And now the same man promises concerning the publishing to the king of the cognizance of that same thing. Bring me in before the king, and I will tell the king the solution. And that I may briefly discharge what I think upon these things: some in their prayer, some in their meditation. and others often in their very speaking, receive divine revelation, and come to know whatever things are sublime and profound out of divine inspiration. Nevertheless not only that which we penetrate subtly by praying, but that also which we penetrate by meditating [1256B] or by speaking, we then rightly attribute to Daniel, when we acquire any of these by the merit of devotion. There are certain men, then, who can never ascend to the contemplating or the penetrating of sublime things save after inward compunction and as it were after a cleansing of the heart; whose person Job expressed in himself when he says: *Before I eat I sigh, and like overflowing waters, so is my roaring [n: (Job. III)]*. He must needs sigh before he eats, who in no way can reach the inward refreshment of the soul and the pastures of truth save through the grace of compunction and after a pouring out of tears. Others in their meditations abound copiously [1256C] in divine revelations; and perhaps there are still such as cannot at all, without premeditation, either penetrate subtle things or discourse subtly. Those former, then, kindle for themselves the fire of divine revelation by their prayers, but these by their meditations: *And in my meditation a fire shall flame out,* says the Psalmist [n: (Psal. XXXVIII)]. The fire flames out in meditation when the spirit advances, even by its own investigations, to the light of divine revelation and to the manifestation of hidden things. The fire flames out in meditation when, out of its own inquiry, the spirit grows white-hot toward the knowledge and the love of truth. But it is agreed that some have advanced to so great an instruction that they can at every hour discourse subtly upon any subtle matters whatever, and can answer sufficiently, almost always, to whatever is asked them (without any premeditation [1256D]), being always ready, as it is written, to give satisfaction to everyone that asks a reason, and with that faith and hope which is in us [n: (I Petr. III)] [var: Vulg. *de ea, quae in vobis est, spe*, "concerning that hope which is in you"; the received text has no word for faith here, and reads "in you" for "in us"]. But certain of those who are of this kind have the light of divine revelation so familiar that many things are revealed to them even while they are speaking, out of those things which they themselves before knew not. As long, therefore, as the spirit obtains divine revelation through compunction of heart alone, Daniel as it were alone has thus far the cognizance of the mysteries. But when so divine a revelation occurs to our meditations, then the recognition of the mystic vision has come even to Daniel's companions. But when the mind has begun to have the illuminating grace so familiar and as it were at hand that [1257A] it can, at its own choosing and will, whenever it please, be sufficient at almost every hour for whatever things are to be discussed, established, defended, then the unravelling of hidden things begins to enlarge itself even to the royal cognizance. Is not the learning, or the instruction, of deeper things possessed as a faculty by a kind of royal right, when at the choice of the will one discourses easily and without any difficulty upon whatever mystic and profound matters? Behold to how great a perfection of learning the spirit is often led by the merit of devotion; behold what fruit often follows true devotion. This fruit of its devotion the provident mind often and prudently foresees before it obtains it, and confidently awaits. Whence it comes above all that it presses the more vehemently upon the zeal of devotion. I will tell the king, he says, the solution. And it is added: [1257B] *Then Arioch in haste brought Daniel in to the king, and said to him: I have found a man of the sons of the transmigration of Judas, who may signify the solution to the king.* [var: Vulg. *annuntiet*, "may announce" — the reading Richard's own exposition re-quotes twice at 1257D and again at 1259A] Behold, as thou hearest, when the promise of that solution which the king greatly desired had been received, Arioch brought Daniel in to the king. As was said above, Daniel is led by the hand to Arioch when, from the sight of strict equity, the fervor of devotion is inflamed. Now the human spirit is pricked with compunction in many ways and in many regards. But almost the best mode of compunction is that which rises from the consideration of the strict examination. Daniel therefore is then brought in not by anyone whatever, but as it were by Arioch, when from the consideration of true justice too little kept, the spirit is inwardly pricked with compunction. He is brought in to the king [1257C] and stands as it were beside the royal power, when true devotion is possessed out of virtue, and the spirit uses it at its own choosing. He is brought in in haste when easily, and without delay when need requires, the spirit is softened to contrition of heart. Let him therefore be zealous to have such a faculty, or facility, of compunction and of devotion, who desires to attain to that enlargement of learning which we assigned above. For it comes first that Daniel enter in through Arioch, and with haste, before the desired word becomes known to the king.
