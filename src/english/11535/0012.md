## CHAPTER XVII. *That he attains the highest summit of devotion who ascribes to his own merits whatever trouble befalls him, even beyond his desert.*

I have found, says Arioch, a man of the sons of the transmigration [1257D] of Judas, who may announce the solution to the king. That is found by Arioch which by the judgment of equity is discerned to be just and right. Arioch therefore found Daniel fit, and worthy to receive so great a grace, to perceive the revelation of the mysteries from God, to announce the solution to the king. And perhaps the fact that he was of the sons of the transmigration of Judas, and bore that long captivity patiently, helped Daniel's merit toward this very thing, and showed him the fitter for the receiving of so great a grace. This Daniel had assuredly been carried captive from Jerusalem into Babylon, and perhaps had committed nothing such as to incur a captivity of this kind, save that the common lot enfolded him and another's malice unjustly oppressed him. Nonetheless he ascribed to his own merits the captivity [1258A] which he bore without guilt. *We have sinned,* he says, *we have committed iniquity, we have done wickedly, and we have departed, and we have turned aside from thy commandments and thy judgments [n: (Dan. IX)]*. Rightly, then, is he said to be of the sons of the transmigration of Judas, who even amid the scourges of an undeserved oppression humbly confesses to the Lord. For Judas too is interpreted *confessing*. See therefore how rightly he deserved to be divinely glorified, who in so great and such an affliction sought the divine glory (not his own) by living and by confessing. How many incur the mark of suspicion not so much by their own fault as by another's malice, and pass as it were from Hierusalem to Babylon, and as though from a place of peace and quiet to a place of confusion and captivity. For Hierusalem is interpreted *a vision of peace,* Babylon *confusion*. What, then, is it to pass unjustly, as a captive, from Jerusalem into [1258B] Babylon, but to labor under a sinister, yet false, suspicion? It belongs to the depraved always to tear at the life of the good, and, when they cannot do it with true charges, to assail them nevertheless with false accusation; and when they fail at the defaming of their works, they overspread that simple eye of their intention, if they can, with the mark of a sinister suspicion. Just so, therefore, after the manner of Daniel, the mind of the just man is held under an unjust captivity at Babylon, when, laboring under a false suspicion, it is afflicted as shamelessly as irreverently by the reproach of an unjust confusion. Was he not groaning under a Babylonian affliction, so to speak, who said: *All the day my shame is against me, and the confusion of my face has covered me [n: (Psal. XLIII)]*. Consider [1258C] nevertheless that captivity of his confusion which he then bore, which at that time he carried without guilt, who with a secure conscience could say: *All these things came upon us, and we have not forgotten thee, and we have not dealt unjustly in thy testament [n: (ibid.)]* But when, amid pressing scourges, we detect in ourselves no present fault, not even then ought we to be secure. To this he instructed us by his own example who says: *I am conscious to myself of nothing, but I am not hereby justified [n: (I Cor. IV)]*. For often we are struck for a preceding fault, often for a present but hidden one, and indeed for such a fault as we believed to be no fault at all, or perhaps blotted out by satisfaction. And indeed, as must be altogether confessed, we are not always struck for correction or for purgation. But we are scourged now for instruction, [1258D] now for testing, or in some other way whatever. But it belongs to true and perfect devotion always to ascribe to its own merits the punishment it suffers, and to fear from what is hidden the fault which it does not detect in itself. That fervor of devotion, therefore, has already reached as it were the highest summit of perfection, which ascribes to its own merits any unjust reproach of confusion, and whatever befalls it even beyond its desert; which glorifies the Lord in all its affliction, and accuses itself in all things without pretense. How rightly is that fervor of devotion reckoned among the sons of the transmigration of Judas, which amid whatever ignominies of unjust reproach always humbly confesses to the Lord. But often those who are of this kind, who seek the divine glory before all others, who see themselves [sic: *scipsos*] [1259A] without glory and do not shrink from being so seen, receive grace from God above the rest. I have found, he says, a man of the sons of the transmigration, who may announce the solution to the king.
