## CHAPTER XVIII. *That we often presume less upon the virtue of devotion than we ought, and that the gift of mystic understanding is one thing, and the gift of mystic teaching another.*

*The king answered, and said to Daniel, whose name was Balthasar: Thinkest thou that thou canst truly tell me the dream which I saw, and the interpretation thereof?* For the king to answer Daniel and to show his own diffidence is nothing else than that such a vice of the spirit cannot lie hidden from true devotion. For often certain vices which at another time we cannot detect, we detect easily [1259B] in the hour of our compunction and amid the tears of our devotion. For perfect devotion sifts all things, discerns all things. For the spiritual man discerns all things, and he himself is judged by no one.

It is surely to be noted that Daniel, while he seeks out things profound and mystic, when he promises things sublime and subtle, is called Balthasar, which is interpreted *a hair of the head*. Now a hair of the head, as you know, is exceedingly long and subtle. What, then, is a hair of the head, but the subtle profundity and the profound subtlety of the mind? And perfect devotion assuredly both illuminates the mind unto profundity and sharpens it unto every subtlety. And a hair of the head is also an ornament; and the mind of man, assuredly, out of true devotion, in [1259C] so far as it is purged from vices, in so far as it is illuminated unto truth, in so far as it is inflamed unto virtue, in so far without doubt is it adorned, or ornamented. Note therefore that even he distrusted Balthasar's promise who had laid such a name upon him. For it was from the king assuredly that he received such a name. And it does happen that the mind feels and understands itself to be sharpened unto a wondrous subtlety out of much devotion, and yet in a time of necessity presumes less upon the virtue of devotion than it is able, or than it ought. Thinkest thou, he says, that thou canst truly tell me the dream which I saw, and the interpretation thereof? And Daniel above: Bring me in before the king, and I will tell the king the solution. Behold: where Daniel presumes and confidently promises, the royal [1259D] anxiety distrusts. All this is often enacted within one and the same spirit; and by almost as many affections as it is touched, by so many opinions is it varied. One and the same thing it now hopes for, now despairs of, and according to its various effects or regards it alternates the opinions of its estimation. But this fluctuation of alternating vicissitudes is at last concluded with a useful issue and a best end, when what is despaired of from human effort is at length, by the mastery of devotion, wholly presumed upon from divine grace. For what is Daniel's answer, but the mastery of true devotion? *And Daniel, answering before the king, said: The mystery which the king asks of, the wise men, the magi, and the soothsayers, and the diviners are not able to tell the king. But there is a God in the heavens revealing mysteries, who has shown to thee, Nabuchodonosor, what things [1260A] are to come in the last times.* [var: Vulg. *tibi, rex Nabuchodonosor* — the royal title is absent here, though Richard's own re-quotation at 1261A restores it; the received text also has the singular *in caelo*, "in heaven"] But because Daniel had already recognized the mystic vision which the royal anxiety was still seeking, someone perhaps desires to have it shown him more openly how one and the same thing can, by the same spirit, in one respect be known and in another respect not be known. But it is one thing to grasp the depths of the mysteries by understanding alone, and another to be able to explicate and expound them easily as often as thou shalt wish. See how rare they are who are able to penetrate the mystic things of the Scriptures without another's labor and exposition; yet far fewer are they who can publish the exposition of them, by word or by writing, to the cognizance of others. Thou weighest therefore, as I judge, that the gift of mystic understanding is one thing, and the gift of mystic teaching another. Some are strong in the one, some in the other, [1260B] but others are eminent in both. If upon this point we consult what is said of Moses, we recognize that he stands pre-eminent in wisdom, and his brother in eloquence. If we look to David, we shall be able to find both in one: *The uncertain and hidden things of thy wisdom thou hast made manifest to me [n: (Psal. L)]*. Behold, for the one. And likewise for the other: *I will open my mouth in parables, I will utter propositions from the beginning [n: (Psal. LXXVII)]*. But certain men have already received the first, and yet utterly distrust as to the second. Jeremiah had already known the mystic vision, who, being questioned upon this very thing, said: *I see a rod that watches;* and likewise: *I see a pot set burning, and the face thereof from the face of the north [n: (Jer. I)]*. Behold, thou already hearest him professing concerning the one gift; hear the same man still distrusting concerning the other: *Ah, ah, ah, [1260C] Lord, I know not how to speak.* Such a diffidence, surely, is expressed when it is said to one who already knows the vision. Thinkest thou that thou canst truly tell me the dream which I saw? Such diffidence is refuted, and it is shown whence such a grace ought to be despaired of, or hoped for, when it is said: The mystery which the king asks of, the wise men, the magi, and the soothsayers, and the diviners are not able to tell the king. But there is a God in the heavens revealing mysteries. Our Daniel therefore teaches us in these words utterly to despair of human effort as to those things which are above man and exceed human sense, and to look for them from the Lord's revelation alone. But in saying that there have been shown to him the things that are to come in the last times, he seems without doubt to look to that [1260D] which is added at the end of the vision after the rest: But the stone which had struck the statue became a great mountain, and filled the whole earth. How great, and how profound, and how worthy of much veneration are those things which this vision speaks according to the allegorical, nay almost according to the historical sense, no one skilled in the Holy Scriptures is permitted to be ignorant. But we sift these things according to the moral sense, for our own edification or that of others, in such a way that we presume to diminish or to weaken nothing of the authority of so great a mystery according to its more sacred understanding. This therefore presents itself as astonishing and worthy of much wonder: how widely there lies open to the eyes of contemplation the man who, by the cooperation of divine revelation, enlarges himself even to the surveying of those things which come to pass at the last. [1261A] Who has shown to thee, king Nabuchodonosor, what things are to come in the last times: he does not say, what things are to come to thee, but what things are to come in the last times.

It must therefore be asked whether perhaps something lie hidden in these words which may in some respect fit not one man only, but many, or even almost all prefects. For the kingdom of heaven is not raised up anywhere else than among the elect. A kingdom which, according to Daniel's word, is not scattered for ever; a kingdom which is said to be within us: *The kingdom,* says the Lord, *of heaven is within you [n: (Luc. XVII)]* [var: Vulg. *regnum Dei*, "the kingdom of God" — the received text of Luke 17:21 does not read "of heaven"]. This kingdom assuredly the Lord raises up in his elect at their last, in whatever manner he may permit them in their beginnings to waver [1261B] through various trials.
