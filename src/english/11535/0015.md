## CHAPTER XX. *A concise recapitulation of what has been said, with the attestation of a likeness.*

It is profitable at this point to bring into consideration how this reading and the Gospel reading seem to accord together in a mystical manner of speech. Luke the evangelist relates how the Lord willed to show his presence to the disciples on their journey in the appearance of a stranger, and [1263D] afterwards to withdraw it; but to remain with them as they asked, to enter in to them as they constrained him, and at last, amid the feast, to show that appearance which they might recognize [n: (Luc, XXIV)]. What then is it to have Christ, that is, the Wisdom of God, present in the appearance of a stranger, but to see the truth not in its own simplicity, but through a glass, or in an enigma? But this vision is shown outside the house and as it were on a journey, when the contemplation of sublime things is seen through an excess of mind and as it were in passing. There are, as it were, two going out, who also as they walk behold such things, when reason and intelligence, exceeding the human measure, run through the spectacles of sublime things, stealthily indeed and hurriedly. But then the Lord feigns to go further, and yet does not wholly withdraw, [1264A] when the grace of contemplation, which he is wont mercifully to bestow, he withdraws for a time, no less profitably, that he may better teach by experience with how great eagerness it ought to be sought and to be held fast. And he is constrained to remain and to enter in to them, when by much instancy of prayers the lost grace is repaired, so that he may draw inward the vision offered outwardly, and that the spirit, returned to itself, may retain in memory the things seen through excess, and thereafter possess the grace of contemplation more familiarly. We set before him such foods as the Lord loves and is wont to be delighted with, when, for the recovery or the accumulation of grace, we praise, honor, and magnify him in the voice of exultation and of confession: *In the voice,* he says, *of exultation, and [1264B] of confession the sound of one feasting [n: (Psal. XLI)]*. Amid these hymns of our exultation and confession the bosom of the mind is often enlarged, the sense is sharpened, and the intelligence is enlightened for penetrating mystical things, and for often searching out even the secrets of the Godhead: *The sacrifice,* he says, *of praise shall honor me, and there is the way by which I will show the salvation of God [n: (Psal. XLIX)]*. [var: Vulg. *ostendam illi salutare Dei*, "will show him the salvation of God"] Beyond doubt the bread is broken before us by the Lord, when the profundity of the mysteries is opened by mystical interpretation, and is known by his own revealing. From this breaking of bread and revelation of the mysteries it comes about that the Lord is recognized in his own appearance, and that Truth itself (the veil of figures being removed) is discerned in its own simplicity, and the Lord's presence is weighed from [1264C] the infusion of his grace. But the Lord (who is also the Wisdom of God) vanishes from the eyes of those beholding, when, from the contemplation and admiration of the divine wisdom, it becomes known to the spirit of him who contemplates and admires how far its incomprehensibility exceeds every human intelligence: *O the depth,* he says, *of the riches, of the wisdom, and of the knowledge of God: how incomprehensible are his judgments, and his ways unsearchable [n: (Rom. XI)]*. For the human spirit, the more subtly it is enlightened for contemplating the wisdom of God, so much the more strongly is it beaten back upon itself by the immensity of that light; and this is to vanish from the eyes of the beholder, and, as it were, in showing itself to withdraw itself. From these things, therefore, one may weigh with how great a proportion of likeness this twin reading seems [1264D] to answer to itself. For that the Lord showed himself in the appearance of a stranger, this is that Nabuchodonosor received the revelation of the truth under a mystical veil. That there the Lord feigned for himself a longer journey, this is that the vision shown to the king withdrew itself for a little from his memory. That there he who had appeared is constrained to remain and to enter in, this is that here the withdrawn vision is sought with much instancy, and at last, by Daniel's solicitude, is perceived and retained far more profoundly, far more perfectly. That he who had appeared there is at length known in a manifest vision, this is that in this place the clouded truth of the vision taken away is at last made manifest by prophetic interpretation. In these things, and in certain others, he will see this twin reading to accord with itself, [1265A] if there be any who is willing to search these matters out more deeply. Yet that reading has certain things proper to itself, and this one too, of which there is no need to speak now one by one. These things, therefore, having been shaken out by way of digression, and brought together for the attestation of the likeness, let us return to the explication, or explanation, of the vision.
