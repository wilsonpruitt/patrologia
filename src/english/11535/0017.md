## CHAPTER XXII. *On the measure of the human condition, or of human corruptibility, and that on any summit of virtues one must fear the ruin of morals.*

What is a statue, but a human effigy? What then is it to discern a statue, that is, a human likeness, through a vision, but to look upon the measure, or the order, of human life as through a glass, and in an enigma, [1267A] with the eye of contemplation? *Thou, O king,* he says, *wast seeing, and behold, as it were one great statue [n: (Dan. II)]*. If thou wouldst search out the measure or the quality of human life, if to the dignity of its condition, if to the ease of its reparation, to whichever of these thou turn thyself, thou wilt see that thou failest in each, and canst not fully comprehend any of them. See therefore how great that is in the eyes of the beholder which cannot at all be comprehended by his own sight: *That statue,* he says, *the great one, and lofty in stature, stood over against thee, and its look was terrible.* But rightly, after he said "great," he perhaps adds "lofty" also. For it is one thing to be great, and another to be high. For a field also is called great which yet is raised by no height. So indeed some things are almost incomprehensible [1267B] which yet are not worthy of much investigation or admiration. But that we have heard this statue to be great, lofty, terrible, we can rightly accommodate to a threefold consideration. For the condition of human life, which is figured in this statue, although each of these is found in some respect in each, is yet more especially and more expressly found great from one thing, lofty from another, and terrible from another. One consideration therefore of the human condition is monstrous, on that side by which it lies subject to corruption; and it is lofty on that side by which it is reserved for reparation; and terrible on that side by which it lies open to perdition. Do you wish to hear how great is the monstrousness of corruption? *To what shall I compare thee, or to what shall I [1267C] liken thee, O daughter of Jerusalem; to what shall I make thee equal, and comfort thee, O virgin daughter of Sion? Great as the sea is thy crushing. Who shall heal thee? [n: (Thren. II)]*. [var: Vulg. *magna est enim velut mare contritio tua*, "for thy crushing is great as the sea"] Do you wish to know how great is the loftiness of reparation? *For whom he foreknew, he also predestinated to be made conformable to the image of his Son, that he himself might be the firstborn among many brethren. Whom he predestinated, them he also called; and whom he called, them he also justified; but whom he justified, them he also magnified [n: (Rom. VIII)]*. [var: Vulg. *glorificavit*, "glorified"] Do you wish to know this also, how great is the terror of the divine stroke? *Who,* he says, *knoweth the power of thy wrath, and for fear of thee can number thy anger? [n: (Psal. LXXXIX)]*. For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and injustice of those men who detain the truth of God in injustice. Consider therefore the greatness of our statue [1267D] in the consideration of fault; its height, in the consideration of grace; its terror, in the consideration of punishment. Seek the greatness of fault both according to original fault and according to actual fault. And for original fault, hear what is said of man to man: *Man, when he was in honor, did not understand; he is compared to senseless beasts, and is become like to them [n: (Psal. XLVIII)]*. And concerning actual fault, receive the voice of reproach: *On every high hill, and under every leafy tree thou didst prostrate thyself, a harlot [n: (Jer. II)]*. Whence it is that it is said to her: Lift up thine eyes round about, and see where thou now liest prostrate, a harlot. O truly the height of grace, both according to justifying grace and according to confirming grace, and according to glorifying grace! [1268A] Measure, if thou art able, the loftiness of that sublimity, according to justifying grace: *For as far as the rising is distant from the west, so far hath he put our iniquities from us [n: (Psal. CII)]*. Receive also the height of eminence, according to confirming grace. For according to the height of heaven from the earth, he hath strengthened his mercy over them that fear him. Estimate, if thou canst, the sublimity of dignity, according to glorifying grace: *For whosoever are led by the Spirit of God, they are the sons of God. And if sons, heirs also. Heirs indeed of God, but joint-heirs with Christ [n: (Rom. XIV)]*. O the aspect of our statue, truly terrible according to the adjudging of punishment; terrible according to the judgment of corruption; terrible according to the judgment of reprobation; terrible according to the judgment of damnation! O the aspect [1268B] of terror, according to the judgment of corruption! For evils without number have surrounded me; my iniquities have overtaken me, and I was not able to see. They are multiplied above the hairs of my head. O the aspect full of horror, according to the judgment of reprobation! *And as they did not approve,* says the Apostle, *to have God in their knowledge, God delivered them up to a reprobate sense, to do those things which are not fitting, being filled with all iniquity, malice, fornication, avarice, wickedness [n: (Rom. I)]*. O the aspect full of terror, full of horror, according to the judgment of damnation! For there is, according to the same Apostle, a certain terrible expectation of judgment, and rage of fire which shall consume [1268C] the adversaries. For their fire shall not be extinguished, and their worm shall not die. Of those, namely, who lead their days in good things, and in a moment go down to hell. From all these things, as I judge, thou weighest how horrible it is to fall into the hands of the living God. What can be said more terrible than these, what more horrible can be devised? And its look, he says, was terrible. But that which is said of the statue, it stood over against thee, needs no long exposition. Then beyond doubt is such a statue raised up against any single man, when the foulness, the infirmity, the calamity of the beholding spirit is thrust upon his eyes, and it is shown him by revelation what he is, or what he may yet become, so that his shamelessness, his pride, his hardness may be confounded, humbled, crushed: *I will reprove thee,* [1268D] he says, *and I will set thee against thy face [n: (Psal. XLIX)]*. He is divinely reproved, when the spirit, already touched by divine inspiration, is confounded by the accusation of his own conscience. And the transgressing soul is set as it were against its own face, when in the consideration of its own foulness it seems to serve against itself by rebuke. A statue therefore is in a manner set against the spirit of him who beholds, when, for his own confusion and humiliation, by a certain divine nod the image of his own corruptibility is set before him. Learn therefore, O man, learn to lay aside confidence in thyself (if suddenly and at once thou canst not, then at least by degrees and through intervals of time); be earnest to do it; be busy to think of thyself ever more and more humbly, that thou mayest attain, even [1269A] late, to true and perfect humility. These are the unshaken foundations of consummate charity; these are the firm defenses of the eternal security of the reigning soul. Lay aside arrogance, press down pride, crush that perverse trust in thine own virtue, cast thy care upon the Lord. Reckon all thy virtue, all thy fortitude, all thine effort as ash and cinder. What else has marked the cinder than to yield to every whirlwind, nay, to fly apart at any breath, however slight, and to forsake the place of its rest? A form fitting enough for our corruption, and an image suitably expressed in ash and cinder. See, whoever thou art, lest perhaps it behoove thee, willing or unwilling, to be such perhaps at some time inwardly or outwardly: inwardly with thyself in thine own estimation, or outwardly also before [1269B] others in thine action. Inwardly, I say, from the purpose and zeal of the will, or outwardly from the failing and the fluctuation of human corruptibility. And how many there are who in no way come down from the height of their own estimation, save then first, when outwardly they fall shamefully in action. For to this end beyond doubt are some permitted to fall deformedly in action, and to change their gold into silver, silver into bronze, bronze into iron, iron into clay, that from their own failing, and as it were by experiment, they may learn what they ought to think of their own virtue and fortitude. Let us see therefore how it befalls some, according to the form of the image shown, to fall as it were from the highest goods almost to the utmost evils through the various failings of corruptions, [1269C] and to end a head of gold, as it were, in feet of clay; that we may learn, on any summit of virtues, to fear the ruin of morals, and may be busy always and everywhere to keep the form and the measure of true and perfect humility.
