## CHAPTER XXIII. *That from a great summit of virtues the human mind is often wont to fall by degrees into the depths.*

*He that standeth,* says the Apostle, *let him take heed lest he fall [n: (I Cor. X)]*. [var: Vulg. *qui se existimat stare*, "he that thinketh himself to stand"] From however lofty a summit one may easily fall into the depths, or be plunged into the deep; nay, beyond doubt, the higher the step, the worse the fall. That dream of Nabuchodonosor ought always both to render us cautious and to make us solicitous. See, I beseech thee, the statue of his vision; [1269D] see, I say, from how great a loftiness it begins, and to what an end it is at last brought down. It has indeed a head of gold, and an end of clay. What is dearer than gold, what viler than clay? Note this also, how by degrees it inclines to worse things, and at length makes its end in a most foul ending. The first part is described as of gold, the second of silver, the third of bronze, the fourth of iron, and the fifth of clay. In the gold is understood charity; in the silver, truth; in the bronze, pretense; in the iron, cruelty; in the earthenware clay, fragility. For clay is easily broken, and by it the fragility of impatience is rightly figured. Iron, which crushes and tames all other metals, fittingly designates the violence of cruelty, which presses upon whatever resists, that it may crush it. But bronze imitates silver [1270A] in sound, yet is much at variance in price. So simulation has the sweet ring of flattery in speech, and resounds sweetly, and echoes pleasantly in work; but as much as by word and practice it soothes without, so much by counsel and desire it grows pale within. Silver too, the more frequent the use by which it is worn, so much the brighter is it made: so truth, the more it is shaken by vehement discussion, so much the better is it commended, if the counsels of truth, made more certain by the use of experience, are thereby rendered the brighter. The splendor of gold expresses the brightness of charity, which blazes with the fire of heavenly desire; and as gold is preferred to the other metals, so charity surpasses the other virtues. O how great is the distance between clay and gold, between the end of the statue and the beginning of the statue! The statue which begins from gold ends in clay. [1270B] I think that no one becomes most base of a sudden. But he who neglects the least things slips away little by little. As one climbs to the heights by certain steps of progress, so again one descends by degrees to the depths. Note how in this statue there is first gold, afterwards silver, and after these, with bronze between, iron also follows. O how great the difference of gold and iron! O how greatly do charity and cruelty differ from one another! Yet what says the Truth concerning these? *Iniquity shall abound,* he says, *and the charity of many shall grow cold [n: (Matth. XXIV)]*. [var: Vulg. *quoniam abundavit iniquitas*, "because iniquity hath abounded"] See, I pray, from whence one comes to what: from the abundance of charity to the abundance of iniquity. Charity belongs to gold, iniquity belongs to iron. O the wonderful (nay, the detestable) change from charity to iniquity! *If the just man,* he says, [1270C] *turn away from his justice, and do iniquity, all his justices which he had done shall not be remembered [n: (Ezech. XVIII)]*. A detestable work, and worthy of confusion, which is begun from gold and consummated in clay and iron. For thus is described a part of the feet as of iron, and a certain part as of clay. O shameful workmen, who at the beginning of their conversion begin their work from gold, and at last consummate it in mire. We know that often, when the unclean spirit has gone out of a man, seeking rest and not finding it, having taken with him seven other spirits more wicked than himself, he returns into his house, and entering in dwells there, and the last things of that man become worse than the first. How many do we see at the beginning of their conversion rejoicing in hope, fervent in spirit, patient in tribulation, solicitous in work, [1270D] studious in reading, devout in prayer. What do these seem to thee to do, but to work in gold, and to be initiated in charity? *For charity,* as it is written, *is patient, is kind, beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things [n: (I Cor. XIII)]*. Does he not seem to thee to work in gold, to whom it was said by the Lord: *I know thy works, and thy charity, and thy faith, and thy ministry, and thy patience? [n: (Apoc. VIII.)]* [var: Vulg. *et fidem, et caritatem tuam*, faith before charity] But there are many who believe for a time, and in the time of temptation fall away. Yet those who are such do not at once plunge themselves into the lowest things, but fall first from the good into the worse, then from the less good into the bad, and at last from the bad into the yet worse. For a falling mountain slips away little by little, and the earth is consumed by a flood. For such as these begin little by little to grow lukewarm from their former desire, [1271A] and to fail more and more from their earlier fervor.
