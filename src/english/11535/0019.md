## CHAPTER XXIV. *How, or by what steps of corruption, the human spirit often falls from the highest things into the lowest.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ Yet they do not at once forsake what they have begun, but still do the good things they can. For when charity has grown cold, they do indeed work good things out of deliberation. ⟦s3⟧ Yet what do we think there is between that former state and this second one? ⟦s4⟧ Much in every way. ⟦s5⟧ It is one thing to follow the good out of desire, and another out of counsel alone. ⟦s6⟧ It is one thing, I say, to work the good with great delight, and another out of deliberation alone. ⟦s7⟧ This is good, but that is best. ⟦s8⟧ This belongs to silver, that belongs to gold. ⟦s9⟧ A precious [1271B] metal indeed is in silver, though it be far viler than gold. ⟦s10⟧ Let him therefore study to work in silver who perchance lacks abundance of gold: if he cannot do good things out of delight, yet do it at least out of truth, and the truth shall make thee free. ⟦s11⟧ But do you wish to know the work that is made out of such silver, how it shines, or how much it pleases, and how rich or how secure it renders its possessor? ⟦s12+13⟧ *Everyone,* he says, *that doeth truth cometh to the light, that his works may be made manifest, because they are good [n: (Joan. III)]*. [var: Vulg. *Qui autem facit veritatem … quia in Deo sunt facta*, "But he that doeth truth … because they are done in God"] It is good therefore to abound in silver of this kind, but no less foolish to change one's gold into silver. ⟦s14⟧ For every man that putteth his hand to the plough and looketh back is not fit for the kingdom of God. ⟦s15⟧ But do you wish to know how the divine word strikes with rebuke [1271C] him who had changed a golden work into silver? ⟦s16⟧ *I know,* he says, *thy works and thy labor, and thy patience [n: (Luc. IX)]*. ⟦s17⟧ Behold a good work; behold the arms, behold the breast of silver. ⟦s18⟧ But let us see what follows, when afterwards it is subjoined: *But I have,* he says, *against thee, that thou hast forsaken thy first charity [n: (Apoc. VIII)]*. ⟦s19⟧ He therefore who still does good is reproved because he has left his first charity, and has bent the golden head down into a breast of silver. ⟦s20⟧ Whosoever thou art that judgest thyself to be of this kind, be mindful whence thou art fallen, and do penance, and do the first works. ⟦s21⟧ Otherwise fear that which follows, which the divine word threatens against him who by no means comes to his senses: *But if not,* he says, *I come to thee, and will move thy candlestick out of [1271D] its place, unless thou do penance [n: (Apoc. II)]*. ⟦s22⟧ Fear therefore, unless thou return the more speedily to thy former good things, lest thou fall yet into worse. ⟦s23⟧ Choose which thou wouldst rather, to ascend or to descend. ⟦s24⟧ For a man cannot stand fixed in the same state for long. ⟦s25⟧ For he flees as a shadow, and never continues in the same state. ⟦s26⟧ Hold it in memory, and fear it not moderately, that he who does not return to his first charity, his candlestick is quickly moved out of its place. ⟦s27⟧ What is meant by the candlestick, the bearer of light, but a good work, a work of truth? ⟦s28⟧ Such a candlestick stands in its place, when a good work rises out of a good purpose. ⟦s29⟧ But such a candlestick is moved out of its place, when the good intention of a good work is changed into an evil one. ⟦s30⟧ So a work of silver passes into one of bronze. ⟦s31⟧ And this evil is indeed frequent [1272A] among the sons of men. ⟦s32⟧ For often what one formerly did out of truth, being dissolved toward the praise of men, he afterwards keeps doing for human favor: *O my people,* he says, *they that call thee blessed, the same deceive thee, and destroy the way of thy steps [n: (Isa. III)]*. ⟦s33⟧ He therefore who shows himself bland in speech, kind in work, but crooked and perverse in thought and in will, he assuredly, having cast away silver, works now in bronze. ⟦s34⟧ For after the manner of bronze he rings sweetly without, but by the depravity of his will he grows pale within. ⟦s35⟧ Does not the work of those seem to thee to be such, who speak peace with their neighbor, but evils are in their hearts. ⟦s36⟧ In such a work, as I judge, he sweated whom the Lord reproved in rebuking him, when he says: *I know thy works, that [1272B] thou hast a name that thou livest, and thou art dead [n: (Apoc. III)]*. ⟦s37⟧ But whosoever thou art that art such, do not think that thou canst long lie hid: *But if thou be exalted as an eagle, and set thy nest among the stars, thence will I draw thee down, saith the Lord [n: (Abd. I)]*. ⟦s38⟧ One day thou must hear the prophetic, nay the Lord's, voice: *Uncover thy shame, lay bare thy shoulder, make bare thy legs, pass over the rivers. ⟦s39⟧ And then shall thy ignominy be revealed, and thy reproach shall be seen [n: (Isa. XLVII)] [var: Vulg. Isa. 47:3 runs on without *Et tunc*, *transi flumina, revelabitur ignominia tua*; the same verse is printed at 1361A of this work without the added words]*. ⟦s40⟧ But when thy shame has been uncovered, when the belly and the thighs of bronze have begun to appear, thou wilt soon begin to grow foul to thy praisers, nay now to thy mockers, and from day to day to grow viler and viler. ⟦s41⟧ Then wilt thou begin to be angry with them, to be indignant, to detest them and cruelly to persecute them, whom [1272C] before thou hadst studied much to please. ⟦s42⟧ And as bland as thou wast before, so harsh and hard wilt thou be afterwards. ⟦s43⟧ So thy bronze shall be changed into iron, and it shall be said to thee: *How art thou turned into bitterness, O strange vine? [n: (Jer. II.)]* [var: Vulg. *conversa es mihi in pravum*, "turned unto me into that which is depraved"] There is no doubt that he has changed a work of bronze into iron. ⟦s44⟧ Gentleness into severity: whose mouth is full of cursing and bitterness, who before dealt deceitfully with their tongues, the venom of asps under their lips. ⟦s45⟧ You who before showed yourselves so bland, so kind, whence now are wars and quarrels among you? ⟦s46⟧ Now you have bitter zeal, and contentions among you. ⟦s47⟧ You kill and you envy, you go to law and you make war; and whence is this so savage cruelty and hardness of iron in you? ⟦s48⟧ What thinkest thou is the cause that their feet are so swift [1272D] to shed blood, unless that they have shins and soles of iron, who rejoice when they have done evil, and exult in the worst things? ⟦s49⟧ But this in them is above all wonderful, nay above all things pitiable, that as they are prompt and bold in inflicting evils, so are they faint-hearted and beyond measure impatient in bearing the evils inflicted; so that thou mayest rightly wonder in what manner iron can agree with clay, so great a cruelty with so great an impatience. ⟦s50⟧ Behold to what an end our statue comes, which after a head of gold received soles of earthenware. ⟦s51⟧ The noble sons of Sion, and clothed with the finest gold, how are they esteemed as vessels of clay, the work of the potter's hands? ⟦s52⟧ What is more precious than gold, what viler than clay? ⟦s53⟧ How then is the gold become obscured, the finest color changed? [1273A] What is more tractable than gold, what more fragile than clay? ⟦s54⟧ How does charity permit herself to be handled; how to be drawn thin, she who beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things? ⟦s55⟧ But what is more contemptible than the will, what more abject than a delicate mind, which both above and in prosperous things is dissolved through concupiscence, and in adverse things is broken through impatience? ⟦s56⟧ *Woe to them that have lost endurance, that have forsaken the right ways, and have turned aside into crooked ones [n: (Eccle. II)]*. ⟦s57⟧ For crushing and unhappiness are in their ways. ⟦s58⟧ Crushing, because of the corruptibility of clay; and unhappiness, because of the rigor of iron, in that a certain part of the feet is of clay, but a certain part is of iron. ⟦s59⟧ But what is it that men of this kind are turned again to their own desires, that after the works of bronze and of iron they are drawn to [1273B] the work of the potter, unless that they have not (save in their own concupiscences) wherein to be delighted, because by simulation they first lost the joy of a good conscience, and afterwards, by cruelty, the glory of reputation. ⟦s60⟧ But that we may recall to a summary the things we have now said more diffusely: in the head of gold is understood devotion; in the members of silver, discretion; in those of bronze, simulation; in those of iron, indignation; in those of clay, dissolution.
