## CHAPTER XXV. *On the difference of the aforesaid steps, and the quality of each.*

It is indeed to be noted what is said of which members, and of what sort each of them is described. Now there is described [1273C] a golden head, a silver breast, silver arms too, a belly and thighs of bronze, iron shins, feet of iron, or in part of clay. By the head the whole body is ruled, whence by it intention is rightly signified, because by intention the whole body of our action is led to an end, whether good or evil. Counsel belongs to the heart, and the heart lies hidden beneath the breast. With the arms we carry out our works, when we will something to be done by us. The belly, the more tender part of the body and the covering of foulness, portends the service of compassion. The thigh is set beneath the shameful parts, and humility, which bends down to base and unworthy things, wishes to be seen as perfect. The shins carry bones longer and sturdier than the other members, whence they also rightly signify the rigor of severity. With the feet we touch the earth, even when we [1273D] stand upright; and however much the mind stretches itself out toward heavenly things, without the supports of this life it does not sustain its own necessity. By the head, therefore, as has been said, intention is understood; by the breast, deliberation; by the arm, exercise; by the belly, compassion; by the thigh, humility; by the shin, severity; by the foot, necessity; by the head, the desire of intention; by the breast, the zeal of deliberation; by the arm, the exercise of action; by the belly, the service of piety; by the thigh, the servitude of subjection; by the shin, the scourge of severity; by the foot, the support of necessity. If then the head is intention, and gold devotion, what else is a golden head but a devout intention? If the breast is deliberation, the arm action, and silver discretion, what else will a silver breast and silver arms be than [1274A] discreet counsels and works? In the belly, compassion; in the thigh, subjection; and in the bronze, dissimulation. What else, then, is a bronze belly and bronze thighs but feigned piety and feigned humility? If by the shin severity, if by iron cruelty, what else is understood in iron shins but a cruel severity? The foot signifies necessity, the clay pleasure. The clay sole, therefore, signifies voluptuous superfluity. But the foot is described as in part of iron and in part of clay, because the pampered mind is now hardened into hatred of those by whom it is perhaps restrained from its own desires, and now, when the things it has coveted are at hand, is dissolved in the luxury of them. What, then, is the gleam of the golden head but the fervor of heavenly desire? The brightness of the silver breast and of the silver arm is the certainty of right counsel and the rightness of a certain work. The resonance [1274B] of the bronze belly and thigh is the allurement of false piety and of feigned humility. The strength of the iron shin is the rigor of immoderate discipline; the fragility of the clay foot, the unbridling of superfluity. The golden head, therefore, holy desires make; the silver breast and arm, right counsels and works. The bronze belly and thigh, feigned services and servitudes; the iron shins, savage scourges; the clay sole, dainty supports.

## CHAPTER XXVI. *It has been said how a man often flows down from good into evil; now it is said how he often falls headlong from evil into evil and from vice into vice.*

But if anyone should wish to take the whole of what is said of the statue in an evil sense, I readily yield, without doubt, [1274C] to his assertion. Yet let him who is of this mind be zealous so to approve his own meaning that he by no means dare to condemn what has been said before, in that the Holy Spirit is often wont to signify many things in the one thing he speaks. For as there is something in this writing which is more easily fitted to that reading, so also something is found in the same which is more lightly applied to this one. For the gold of this statue indeed, since it is read as the best, seems able to be taken more fittingly in the better sense. But as this is rightly taken, according to the aforesaid reading, in keeping with the truth of the matter, so in this reading it can indeed be referred suitably enough to the estimate which the soul that is proud of itself makes of itself. So the fact that what is beheld through the revelation is described as a statue and not as a living creature smiles the more eagerly [1274D] upon this second reading, where it is pleasing to take the whole of what is said in the evil sense. For what else is such a statue but a human figure, or a human life? And what else is a human figure without a rational soul but a fitting action without a right intention? So often, therefore — I will not say a living creature is nourished, but a statue is fashioned after the human likeness — as often as a man in an order regulates his manner of life according to the prudence of the flesh (which is an enemy to God). Let us see, then, what sort of members the divine discourse describes for such a statue, when it says: the head of this statue was of the best gold, and so on. According to this acceptation, then, by which we refer the whole to the evil sense, by gold arrogance can be understood. by silver boastfulness, by bronze obstinacy, by [1275A] iron savagery, by clay impatience. For gold goes before all metals in the dignity of its price, and the proud mind despises all others in comparison with itself. Silver has a sweet and exceedingly pleasant sound, and boastfulness shows itself soothing and delightful in the proclamation of its own praise. Bronze, in that it is exceedingly durable, figures obstinacy rightly enough, which can scarcely or never be turned aside from its own counsel or purpose. Iron, because it is wont to tame the other metals, suitably enough signifies savagery, which is wont to crush others. But by clay, which is easily broken, impatience is rightly enough signified. He therefore fashions a golden head for himself who arrogates holiness to himself. He is seen to carry about a silver breast and silver arms who glories in the rightness of his own counsel and [1275B] work. He has, as it were, a belly and thighs after a manner of bronze who remains obstinate even in those things which he ought to despise for their baseness, or to blush at for their dishonor. He shows himself to have iron shins who both raises himself against his superiors through boldness and crushes his inferiors through savagery. He leans upon clay soles who, bearing down too immoderately upon the love of earthly things, is unbridled into impatience.

It is indeed to be noted by what steps of corruption our failing takes on an evil increase, how, sinking gradually toward worse things, it descends even to the lowest. See how boastfulness follows arrogance; obstinacy, boastfulness; savagery, obstinacy; impatience, savagery. Arrogance indeed goes before boastfulness, because unless the mind, lifted up, first thought lofty things of itself, [1275C] it would never so shamelessly boast of its knowledge or of its holiness. But when it believes that what it thinks within itself of its own knowledge or holiness has been persuaded to others as well, what wonder is it now if it has stood obstinate in its own counsel or purpose? But unless it first persisted obstinately in its own counsel or purpose, it would never rage so wickedly against others in their defense. Savagery begets impatience, because by how much the less each man loves his own neighbor, by so much the less, assuredly, does he bear weak things the more troublesomely. But we know that the channel of love, the more it is blocked outwardly toward the neighbor, the higher it swells inwardly toward itself. And by how much the more wretchedly each man languishes in himself through private love, by so much the more troublesomely [1275D] does he endure his own troubles, and by so much the more hardly does he restrain his own desires. Hence it is that, severe toward others and slack in himself, so long as he rages against others, and now grows torpid in himself, he walks with feet in part of iron and in part of clay. And indeed certain ones often become so enormously empty of the great efforts which at the beginning of their conversion they boldly presumed upon, that in the great dissolution in which at last they live so nervelessly they cannot patiently hear even a slight word, I will not say of reproach, but not even of correction, nay, of gentle admonition: Woe to the wretched. What, I ask, can now be done with these, or what hope can now be had hereafter, who by reason of arrogance can in no way be cast down from their swelling, [1276A] nor by reason of obstinacy be convicted of their error, nor by reason of the hardness of iron be turned aside from their rigor, nor by reason of their clay fragility be reproved concerning their own body?
