## CHAPTER XXVII. *By what steps of repair the mind that falls from great goods into the lowest often rises again by divine working.*

But neither ought we for all that to despise the iron or stony hardness of these men, for the Lord is able of these stones to raise up sons to Abraham. For the things that are impossible with men are possible with God. For see what follows: *Thou wast beholding,* he says, *thus, until a stone was cut out without hands, and struck the statue upon its feet of iron and of earthenware, [1276B] and broke them small [n: (Dan. II)]* [var: Vulg. adds *de monte* after *lapis*: the stone is "cut out of a mountain"]. Behold, a stone is cut out without hands, whereby the hardness of iron may be crushed; and rightly indeed. For what hand could cut down, or even cut out, such a stone as would be strong enough to crush or to break iron small? But what human work, or any human effort whatever, cannot do is done by the divine will alone. It is assuredly God alone who, without any exertion, without any motion of himself, can work as often and whatsoever he will, and, remaining stable, grants all things to be moved. But what, I ask, is it, that he who was able and willed to do this, by no means willed to accomplish these things with hands? Or does he perhaps work better in such iron and hardened minds by inspiration than by striking, and is he often corrected by an inward illumination whom scourges cannot correct? *I have struck,* [1276C] he says, *your sons, and they grieved not; they refused to receive discipline [n: (Jer. V)]* [var: Vulg. Jer. 5:3 reads *Percussisti eos … attrivisti eos, et renuerunt accipere disciplinam*, "Thou hast struck them … thou hast bruised them"; the opening words are those of Jer. 2:30]. But although with us it is much lighter to say than to do, yet often even in those minds in which the work of the striking hand seems to have failed, he works indeed in a wondrous and incomprehensible manner by his own inward word or whisper: *The word,* he says, *that shall go forth from my mouth shall not return to me void, but shall do whatsoever I have willed, and shall prosper in those things for which I have sent it [n: (Isa. LV)]*. So too the Holy Spirit breathes where he wills. For by the word of the Lord the heavens were established, and all their power by the spirit of his mouth. The stone, therefore, cut out without hands is the fear of the Lord divinely inspired; at the stroke of this stone the statue is cast down, and the monstrous work is destroyed. [1276D] As by fear the mind is cast down from a good purpose, so by fear indeed it is shaken out of an evil purpose. For as pride is the beginning of every sin, so the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom. By such a stone, then, the statue is shaken, that the mind that can be confounded may be confounded and humbled. What is such a striking but inward compunction? And what is the breaking small of the stricken statue but the contrition and humiliation of a heart pierced with compunction? But we know that God will not despise a contrite and humbled heart [var: Vulg. Ps. 50:19 addresses God, *Cor contritum et humiliatum, Deus, non despicies*, "thou, O God, wilt not despise"]. It is therefore indeed better to be crushed upon this stone than to stand erect and to be high-minded. It struck, he says, the statue upon its feet of iron and of earthenware, and broke them small. The statue, assuredly, is shaken in its lower and worse parts, when the mind, lifted up and [1277A] perverse, is beaten back by the spirit of the fear of the Lord to the consideration of its own iniquity and weakness. This loving striking of the Lord is threatened as mercifully as it is profitably completed in us in this life, when it is said: *I will reprove thee, and will set against thy face [n: (Psal. XLIX)]*. In this manner assuredly he had been reproved and struck in the weak part of himself, and by such a striking had raised himself against himself, who said: *For I acknowledge my iniquity, and my sin is always against me [n: (Psal. L)]*. But after the iron and the clay are shaken, the rest also are crushed. *Then were broken in pieces together,* he says, *the iron, the clay, the bronze, the silver, and the gold.* Thou hast seen above by what steps goodness failed and perversity grew. See now how the foundation of all goodness, humility, [1277B] advances step by step: first we blush at true evils, afterward at feigned goods. Last of all, at true goods also, since they are imperfect and unconsummated. So indeed first the iron and the clay are crushed, then the bronze members, last of all the silver and the golden. Among evils we blush more easily at iniquity than at weakness. But among goods we blush more quickly at imperfection than at interruption. Hence it is that in the lowest members the iron is crushed before the clay. But higher up the silver members are broken small more quickly than the golden. First, therefore, in the crushing of the iron we condemn the evils which we have committed through iniquity. But in the crushing of the clay, those things which we have contracted through weakness. And what is the crushing [1277C] of the bronze but the reprobation of a simulated good? In the silver members this is what pleases us less, and what ought least to please, not that they are good, but that they are not the best. For in the golden work this alone is indeed discerned as blameworthy, that the beginning of the best work is not carried through even to the heel of a worthy or due end. In the crushing of the silver, therefore, the imperfection of a good work. But in the crushing of the gold the interruption of the best work is arraigned. This we have now already said concerning the crushing of this statue, according to that reading which we set down first, where we took it partly in a good and partly in an evil sense.

## CHAPTER XXVIII. *By what steps of advancement the mind that slips from great evil into worse things is often divinely repaired.*

[1277D]

But now let us say something according to that reading which is set in the second place. As there is nothing at which we blush more easily than malice, so there is nothing which we acknowledge or blush at more hardly than arrogance. For arrogance clouds over or blinds the eye of the mind, so that it cannot even recognize itself. For such as the mind lifted up feigns itself to be by its own estimate, such, beyond doubt, it believes itself truly to be. See then how difficult it is for us to reprehend in ourselves, and to arraign, and by rebuke and reprobation to crush, those things in which we have long believed that we truly ought to please others also. But since boastfulness, while it is zealous to spread its own glory abroad, mixes false things with true, and persuades others, concerning the estimate of itself, not only [1278A] of those things which it knows to be true of itself, but even of those which it knows to be false. There beyond doubt it is more easily cast down into confusion, where concerning its false ostentation it is convicted even by the proper testimony of its own conscience. Whence assuredly it comes that the silver of boastfulness, rather than the gold of arrogance, is worn away the more quickly by how much the more easily. But as we blush at arrogance much more hardly than at boastfulness, so we blush much more laboriously at boastfulness than at obstinacy. For we do not doubt that our reputation has often grown from boastfulness, but has always decreased from obstinacy. For we call nothing else obstinacy, save when we shamelessly persist in our own opinion against the counsels of others. But who would not blush more easily at that evil in which he is by no means ignorant that he has greatly displeased others as well? [1278B] Rightly, therefore, is the bronze described as crushed before the silver, because, as has been said, the mind is more easily confounded over obstinacy than over boastfulness. But to the crushing of the clay belongs the confusion that comes of the consideration of one's own weakness and impatience. But when the perverse mind has come to such weakness and impatience that it cannot at all resist its own passions, even when it has willed to, who would not see how easily it blushes in this consideration of its own fragility? But more easily, as has been said above, do we excuse those things which we contract through weakness than those which we commit out of malice alone, whence also we blush at them the more quickly. Rightly then, as it is read, the iron is broken small before the [1278C] clay, just as after these the bronze, the silver, and the gold are rightly described as broken small. Such an order of crushing, then, is described when it is said: Then were broken in pieces together the iron, the clay, the bronze, the silver, and the gold. Where also it is brought in: *and they were reduced as it were into the ashes of a summer threshing-floor, which were carried away by the wind; and no place was found in them.* [var: Vulg. *nullusque locus inventus est eis*, "no place was found for them"; chunk 0016 of this work prints the verse without *in*]
