## CHAPTER XXIX. *How, and to how great a perfection, a man often rises again after a manifold lapse by divine working.*

What do we understand by the summer season but the fervor of the spirit, and what by the threshing-floor but the prudence of the spirit? What, then, is a summer threshing-floor but spiritual understanding? On this floor the grains of truth are beaten out from the [1278D] chaff of figures by the threshing of a vehement examination. On this floor the corn of virtue is separated from the beards of the vices by the winnowing of a strict examination. In the threshing of this floor we labor, and, as it were, separate the grains from the chaff, as often as we sunder true and eternal goods from false and transitory ones. Nor let it suffice us to beat out the chaff, but let us be zealous also to turn the heap of it into ash by setting fire beneath it, and to change the brightness of passing things into blackness, and to transform our affection into contempt or hatred of them. On this floor sweats whoever desires to be spiritual. For the spiritual man judges all things, and he himself is judged by no man. Hear a certain spiritual man laboring on this floor: *I purposed,* he says, *in my heart to seek and to search out wisely concerning all things [1279A] that are done under the sun [n: (Eccle. I.)]* [var: Vulg. *in animo meo*, "in my mind"] Behold the floor, behold the threshing; and where is the chaff, where is the beard? *Vanity,* he says, *of vanities, and all is vanity.* Behold the chaff, *and I saw that in all things,* he says, *there was vanity, and affliction of spirit [n: (ibid.)]*. Behold the chaff with the beard. But where is the ash? *What fruit therefore,* says the Apostle, *had you in those things whereof you are now ashamed? [n: (Rom. VI.)]* What is the blackness of the ash but the confusion of shame? See then how they had turned the brightness of the stubble into the blackness of abomination, who were now blushing at the things in which they had once been wont to take delight. Hear him too who had turned the chaff of vanities not only into blackness but even into stench, when he said: *I have counted all things as dung, that I might gain Christ [n: (Philip. III)]* [var: Vulg. *arbitror … ut Christum lucrifaciam*, present, "I count … that I may gain"]. But the sorrow of penitence is [1279B] that fire with which spiritual men are wont to burn up the beards of vanities, and to turn them into the ashes of abominations. These ashes assuredly we endure still on the threshing-floor of our conscience, so long as we are confounded or suspect concerning past faults; but, when the wind blows, the ashes are taken away, when at the inspiration of the Holy Spirit all dread of our guilt and [sic: *suscipio*] is swallowed up, and through the hope of pardon the floor of our conscience is cleansed of all the blackness of confusion, so that we may truly sing to the Lord: thou hast cast behind thy back all my sins. Had it not been reduced to ash, I will not say the other members, the iron or the bronze, but even his golden head, when the Prophet said: *And the confusion of my face hath covered me [n: (Psal. XLIII)]*. Will he not make supplication to the Lord [1279C] for the winnowing away of the ashes, when he cried out to the Lord in prayer: *In thee have I hoped, let me never be confounded [n: (Psal. XXX)]*. Had not those black ashes of his confusions been carried away and taken up by the wind, when, cleansed from the stains of his faults through the presuming upon pardon, he broke forth into the confession of praise, saying: *Bless the Lord, O my soul, and forget not all his benefits, who forgiveth all thy iniquities [n: (Psal. CII)]*. See then that by the riches of corruption it is reduced to ash, and afterward is carried off and taken away by the wind; because, unless the transgressing soul first acknowledge its confusions, and blush at them, and burn them up and consume them with a worthy sorrow of penitence, it does not [1279D] cleanse the floor of its conscience from the stains of its faults, and never raises itself to the full security of pardon. But when they have been carried off by the wind, no place is found for them; because after a pardon long sought and scarcely obtained the mind is made not only more cautious but also stronger against like temptations, nay is rendered insuperable against the declines of like corruptions. No place indeed can be found for them, when neither can the sense be deceived nor the affection corrupted unto a like corruption. But what does that mean, that that stone by which the statue is struck, and being struck is broken small; what, I say, is it that the stone does not grow into a crag but is changed into a mountain, nor the stone into a great stone but rather grows out into a great and eminent mountain? But we know that perfect charity sends fear. [var: Vulg. I Joan. 4:18 *perfecta caritas foras mittit timorem*, "casteth fear out"] For the perfection of charity knows not [1280A] how to increase dread, but changes and magnifies it into a mountain of confidence. They that trust in the Lord, as mount Sion: he shall not be moved for ever that dwelleth in Jerusalem. Dost thou see, I pray, how the prophetic sentences run together with one another, how David agrees with Daniel, truth with figure, the sentence of the Prophet with the prophetic vision? *But the stone,* he says, *that struck the statue became a great mountain, and filled the whole earth.* How great a mountain, I pray, is that which filled the whole earth, which shall not be moved for ever? Will you hear how great, how firm, how vast, how immovable? Hear another who holds this mountain of confidence, and sets forth its greatness and immovability: *I am sure,* he says, *that neither death,* [1280B] etc., as far as, *which is in Christ Jesus our Lord [n: (Rom. VIII)]*.
