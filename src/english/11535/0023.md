## CHAPTER XXX. *What kind of liberty we have by nature, and [sic: quatem] power we receive from grace, and what belongs to liberty or to power.*

Behold, we have heard the dream. But do we still need an interpretation? As he has already said above, it is one thing to know the secrets of the divine judgments, and to know them by divine revelation, and another to have been able to weigh or to penetrate the reason of the same. It is one thing, I say, to see by clear contemplation what is wont or ought to happen in the universe of things, and another what ought to [1280C] befall to which person, for what cause, and with what profit to him to whom it happens. The one belongs, as it were, to the vision of the dream, the other to the interpretation of the dream; and what was given to king Nebuchadnezzar out of divine revelation was to see by a likeness from how great a height of loftiness it may befall a man to fall down into the lowest places, and again to rise up to the state of integrity and to the summit of perfection; but he did not receive equal things, to recognize the quality of the person to whom such a thing ought to happen. But what he could not see through himself he received from another, when he hears through Daniel: Thou art the head of gold, and after thee shall rise up another kingdom less than thee. One grace, therefore, is that by which we know the divine judgments, and another that by which we judge our own merits or those of others. The former belongs to contemplation, [1280D] the latter to meditation, or rather to devotion. For that meditation is indeed rightly ascribed to devotion by which the measure of our life and the quality of our merits are judged. And indeed we never better know the corruption of our weakness, nowhere more fully detect it, than when amid the tears of compunction we pour out our whole heart to the Lord. If, then, devotion is rightly understood by Daniel, deservedly is that grace ascribed to him by which the state or the corruption of our life is detected. But it often happens that one man is perfect in one of those graces which I have named, and another in the other, and that what each cannot do through himself he learns from the other. But sometimes one and the same man will receive the fullness of both graces, and yet in one and the same [1281A] spirit the one, as it were, consults the other, when what he could not attain through one state of mind he learns through another. For many things which we cannot do out of the grace of contemplation we obtain by the merit of devotion. This is why Daniel is consulted by the king, this is why the king is instructed through Daniel: *This is,* he says, *the dream; and its interpretation also we will tell before thee, O king.* Happy is he to whom, while there is yet time, this vision is shown and its interpretation revealed. While he is still a king, he can have dominion over his own passions: *Thou,* he says, *art the King of kings.* We know that bodily sense presides over the motion of the body, appetite over sense, affection over appetite, and rational consent over all these, according to what is written, that another is higher than the high, and over these there are yet [1281B] others more eminent, and moreover the king rules over all the earth that serves him. The king of kings, therefore, is rational consent, which presides over all the motions of the heart and of the body, and restrains them within the rights of its own dominion (the power having nonetheless been received from God). For hear what is said, and understand by whom this power is given: *Thou,* he says, *art king of kings, and the Lord of heaven hath given thee a kingdom, and strength, and empire, and glory.* [var: Vulg. Dan. 2:37 *Deus caeli*, "the God of heaven"; chunk 0025 of this work prints the verse with *Deus*] The free will of man has received and has a kingdom in this, that it ought to preside over all other things that are in man; it has received strength, and has it so long as it is able bravely to resist its own passions; it has received empire, and has it so long as it exercises, or is able to exercise, its actions and wills and thoughts in good. It has received glory, and has it so long as it still rejoices in a good conscience: *Our glory,* he says, [1281C] *is this, the testimony of our conscience [n: (II Cor. I)]*. We know who it is that says: *Without me you can do nothing [n: (Joan. XV)]*. So much, then, can we do as we have received the power to do. For what hast thou that thou hast not received, for to have such power and strength is nothing else than to have cooperating grace. Thy power, cooperating grace. As much grace as thou hast, so much power also hast thou; and as much as the grace received grows, so much also does thy power advance; and as much as grace is diminished, so much also is taken from thy power; and if ever grace is withdrawn, power perishes together with it. It is plain, then, from these things that the power of the will is one thing, and the liberty of the will another. For it is one thing to be able to do great things, and another to be [1281D] free. Thou canst be both free and weak, just as, on the contrary, thou canst be both sound and a slave. If thou art sound and strong, thou canst do both many and great things; but nevertheless, if thou art free, thou canst not, nor oughtest to, be compelled to anything. And if thou losest soundness, thou losest thy former power, yet not thy liberty as well. It belongs, therefore, to power that we can do good so long as we are able to do it. But to liberty, that we cannot be compelled unwilling to anything, whether good or evil. How many err in the definition of free will, because they know not, or dissemble, how to distinguish between power and liberty. See nevertheless what difference there is, since a man often loses power, but can never lose liberty. Likewise no one receives power unto all goods, but every man receives liberty unto all goods or evils. [1282A] For, as has already been said, he can be compelled unwilling to no good or evil. But although he is free for all things, he does not for all that have power over all things, otherwise he would be omnipotent. But it is much in every way when he receives power unto those things which suffice him for salvation. And what when those things also are at hand which suffice for perfection. As it is said here: and all things in which the children of men dwell, and the beasts of the field, and the fowls of the air he hath given into thy hand, and hath set all things under thy dominion? He holds, as it were, in his own hand all things in which the children of men dwell, when he has cooperating grace unto those things which, by his help, are wont to lie subject to human counsels and zeals. He possesses the beasts of the field among the rights of his own power, when he is able bravely to restrain the onsets of worldly desires [1282B] within the bounds of equity. He powerfully commands the fowls of the air, to whom he occupies his own thoughts, as often as he wills, with good meditations and spiritual *theoriae*. In this manner, then, he has deserved that all things be set under his dominion who has received grace from God unto each of these things which we have named.
