## CHAPTER XXXII. *On the diverse steps of corruption, and their properties, in those who descend from evil to evil.*

[1284A]

But we must again return a little to what has gone before, and show how this interpretation also fits the other reading. Now according to that reading, the first kingdom is that of arrogance, the second that of boasting, the third of obstinacy, the fourth of savagery. For arrogance belongs to the golden work, boasting to the silver work, obstinacy to the bronze work, savagery to the iron. Let us see what our interpreter says of the head of gold, or what he understands, or how it fits him: *Thou,* he says, *art king of kings: and the God of heaven hath given thee a kingdom, and strength, and empire, and glory, and all things in which [1284B] the children of men dwell, and the beasts of the field and the birds of heaven he hath given into thy hand, and hath set all things under thy dominion.* Often, when we receive such and so great a grace as we have heard described here or expounded above, it comes about that we grow proud against God from that very thing for which we ought to have been more devout and more beholden to God. But that, I ask, how merciful and how worthy of wonder it is, that when our swollen mind grows proud against God he does not at once strike us with strict punishment, nor withdraw his grace on the spot. But first he approaches us with a devout admonition, now inwardly, now outwardly; within, by divine revelation, without, by human exhortation. Within, by inward inspiration; without, by human reasoning, as here first in secret by a dream, afterwards openly [1284C] by a word. Thou, he says, art king of kings, because, stronger than the strong and than the stronger, thou art strongest and canst rule, because thou still hast grace as thy helper; do penance therefore while thou canst, while thou still hast grace working with thee. Thou art still king of kings, and thou canst still in princely wise be prince over the vices too, and still lord it over thy passions. For although thou art deserting grace, thou art not yet deserted by it. Be not proud, he says, be not high-minded, lay aside the sooner the arrogance of thy heart, and what thou canst do of God's gift, ascribe not to thyself: for the God of heaven hath given thee a kingdom, and strength, and empire, and glory. Still in the motions of thy heart or of thy body thou hast a kingdom, because thou canst dispose the good; thou hast strength, because thou canst resist the evil; [1284D] empire, because thou canst urge the more remissly; glory, because thou canst overcome the rebellious. Yet there is nothing whence thou shouldst be proud, there is nothing thou shouldst ascribe to thyself: for the God of heaven hath given all these things to thee, and all things in which the children of men dwell, and the beasts of the field and the birds of heaven he hath given into thy hand, and hath set all things under thy dominion. That thou presidest over the beasts of the field, that thou presidest over the birds of heaven, that thou canst lord it over the appetites of the flesh and over the thoughts of the mind, that thou art able to work good things and to meditate on useful ones: this thou hast not of thy own virtue, but of the divine gift. For all things in which the children of men dwell, and which obey reason and right counsels, come to be of his gift; by his co-working they come forth into act, they turn out well, they prosper. Whatever therefore thou canst do usefully, [1285A] thou canst do of him and through him, who is incapable of nothing, because he can do all things. And so all thy possibility comes of his gift, who hath set all these things under thy dominion. Thou therefore, he says, art the head of gold, who, grace still accompanying thee, canst both correct evils and practise good things. Press down thy pride, therefore, lest thou lose grace as well: for God resisteth the proud, but giveth grace to the humble. And we know that pride is the beginning of all sin. And he that committeth sin is the servant of sin. What then? Will there not now be another kingdom, when sin has already begun to hold dominion and thou to render service, than there was then, when thou couldst press sin down, so that it did not reign in thy mortal body? I think therefore that when thou hast begun through pride to lose grace, [1285B] without which thou wilt be able to do nothing, and to lose thyself; I think, I say, that another kingdom, less than thou, will now rise up, according to what is written: Thou, he says, art the head of gold, and after thee shall rise another kingdom, less than thou. But since every arrogant man glories in himself, while boasting glories in another's favour, it can rightly be said in some respect of the head of gold, that is, of the arrogant man, that he is still himself, in that he has not yet deserted himself, he who has set his glory in himself. But he withdraws from himself when he places his glory in another. Consider then how much less a thing it is to glory in the hissing of another's mouth and in false praises, than in goods of one's own, and often true ones, though received from another. Rightly therefore is it said: Thou art the head of gold, [1285C] and after thee shall rise another kingdom, less than thou; and there is added, and another third kingdom of bronze, which shall rule over all the earth. Bronze, in that it is exceedingly durable, rightly denotes obstinacy, as we said above. And so a man then has a belly and thighs as it were of bronze, when he clings obstinately even to those things at which he ought rather to blush. See then how aptly it seems to agree with this kingdom that it is said to rule over all the earth. For how does that not rule over all the earth which extends its tyranny even to the commanding of shameful things? And the fourth kingdom, he says, shall be as iron. As iron breaketh in pieces and subdueth all things, so shall it break in pieces all these, and shall crush them. If we ought to understand savagery in the iron, how, [1285D] I ask, can a kingdom of this sort break in pieces and crush the other kingdoms? Without doubt, however shameless the mind may be, yet the glory which it was wont to have in itself through arrogance, or in another through boasting, it altogether (nor is this a wonder) disturbs and confounds. For who, amid so many and so open madnesses of his own savagery, would either believe himself holy, or be confident that this could be believed of him by another? But those violent laws of savagery are wont so to crush and confound the kingdom of obstinacy, that in the time of its fury the mind can persevere in no purpose (however right, however honourable). But whatever in the time of tranquillity the mind deliberates and disposes concerning peace or patience, even with a vow or an oath, in the time of its disturbance an oncoming fury [1286A] scatters and dissolves. Those things which we have thus far said concerning the exposition of this vision we have handled according to a twofold consideration, after the measure of our understanding. But the rest which follow run together, for both readings, into the same sense, and do not require a twofold exposition, as do those that have gone before. For what is savagery but cruelty? So too impatience: what else is it than a pitiable frailty? Thus under diverse names we ought to understand the same thing.
