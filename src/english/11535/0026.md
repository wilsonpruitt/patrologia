## CHAPTER XXXIII. *How the mind is cleft against itself, torn and confounded, when the extremities of corruption are reached.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Now therefore let us consider what follows: *Moreover,* he says, [1286B] *whereas thou sawest both of the feet and of the toes a part of the potter's clay and a part of iron, the kingdom shall be divided, which nevertheless shall rise out of the plantation of iron [n: (Dan. I)]*. ⟦s2⟧ But if by the foot necessity is understood, and by iron cruelty, what does it mean that a certain part of the feet is described as being of iron? ⟦s3⟧ Is anyone found hard and harsh, or even inflexible, towards his own necessities? ⟦s4⟧ We know that the covetous man shall not be filled with money, and he that loveth riches shall reap no fruit from them; and yet he does not cease to labour, nor are his eyes satisfied with riches, nor does he consider, saying: For whom do I labour, and defraud my soul of good things? ⟦s5⟧ Does he not seem to thee to walk upon an iron sole, who all the days of his life eateth in darkness, and in many cares, and in misery and [1286C] in sorrow? ⟦s6⟧ Truly, as it is written, *there is nothing more wicked than the covetous man [n: (Eccle. X)]*, who neither has compassion on his neighbour nor pities himself. ⟦s7⟧ Why do we now wonder that a man of this sort has legs of iron, when he already has the soles? ⟦s8⟧ Why do we now complain that he is harsh towards his neighbours, who is already hardened towards his own uses? ⟦s9⟧ He that is evil to himself, to whom will he be good?

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Note however that he who is of this sort is first hardened in iron legs towards his neighbour. ⟦s2⟧ Afterwards he is hardened also in iron feet towards himself. ⟦s3⟧ See therefore, whoever thou art that readest these things, that thou do not perchance, after the love and zeal of wisdom, turn back to the love and gain of money; this is to bend the head of gold down into feet of iron. ⟦s4⟧ The bowels of the covetous are cruel: set now before thine eyes and consider any [1286D] covetous men thou wilt. ⟦s5⟧ See, I pray thee, how anxious they are, how laborious, how sparing, how austere towards themselves. ⟦s6⟧ Yet what have these same men often been wont to do, or what will they yet do, if at another's cost they are fed, clothed, and sheltered free of charge? ⟦s7⟧ Thou wilt surely see them wishing to be clothed in soft raiment, to be soaked with surfeiting and drunkenness, to grow numb with sleep and idleness, to be angry and indignant when they see something of their accustomed comforts wanting to them. ⟦s8⟧ Does such a man not seem to thee to walk upon feet in part of iron and in part of clay, when thou seest one and the same man so sparing in his own goods, so prodigal in another's, serving now austerity, now luxury? ⟦s9⟧ Moreover, he says, in that thou sawest of the feet and of the toes a part of the potter's clay and a part of iron, the kingdom shall be divided, which nevertheless shall rise out of the plantation of iron. [1287A] The foot is divided into five toes, and the condition of necessity is beyond doubt parted into five, when it is extended to all the senses of the body. ⟦s10⟧ But when we studiously seek the delights of the senses or warily shun them, what else do we do than lean upon two feet, on this side and on that, on the right hand and on the left, and walk between prosperity and adversity along the road of our desires? ⟦s11⟧ But thou mayest often see these men indulge one sense more vehemently and those another, and for the delights of some one sense willingly endure the troubles of the other senses. ⟦s12⟧ How many acquire over much time and great labour what they spend in the surfeiting or drunkenness of a single day or hour? ⟦s13⟧ How many fears, how many honours, how many chills, of the enemy, of the night, and of the cold, do adulterers willingly endure, that they may attain to the momentary blandishments [1287B] of foul pleasure? ⟦s14⟧ Have not all such men a part of the feet or of the toes of clay and a part of iron, who in the one thing are so patient and almost insensible, and in the other so impatient? ⟦s15⟧ Moreover, whereas thou sawest, he says, of the feet and of the toes a part of the potter's clay and a part of iron, the kingdom shall be divided. ⟦s16⟧ Truly divided will that be which is of this sort. ⟦s17⟧ For, to set down by way of example some few things out of many in one man: since one and the same man is often both covetous and gluttonous, how great, I ask, is the quarrel and contention that is frequently in his heart, when gluttony bids him spend, and avarice bids him keep back, one and the same coin. ⟦s18⟧ In this way, then, many times upon a slight occasion the kingdom is divided against itself, disturbed and confounded, until it is utterly laid desolate. ⟦s19⟧ For every [1287C] kingdom divided against itself shall be brought to desolation, and house upon house shall fall.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ It is surely to be noted what is said of this kingdom, which nevertheless, he says, shall rise out of the plantation of iron. ⟦s2⟧ For when the transgressing mind first turns back to worldly desires, the spirit is hardened for the enduring of the world's labours, and at that time, according to the saying of Job, it counts it a delight to be under the briers [n: (Job XXII)], so long as it may in any manner attain the end of its desires. ⟦s3⟧ O how happy they would be, if the things they endure for the love of money, or for the gaining of some worldly pomp or glory, they endured patiently for the love of justice! ⟦s4⟧ But they who so patiently bear so many troubles, so many injuries, so many contumelies [1287D] for the gain of covetousness, do not hear with an even mind so much as one light word of correction for the love of virtue. ⟦s5⟧ This I take to be what follows, when it is at once subjoined: *According as thou sawest the iron mingled with clay of mire, and the toes in part of iron and in part of earthenware, in part it shall be solid, and in part it shall be broken.* ⟦s6⟧ For men of this sort are strong and patient, as has been said, in that very part in which their labour might have served virtue as a soldier. ⟦s7⟧ But as to what he says, of clay, of mire: what else is clay of this sort than foul habit? ⟦s8⟧ For mire is hardened as it were into clay when a foul action is drawn into a habit; but out of the hardening of foul habit a man is cast down into impatience, and after the manner of clay is lightly broken, now by wrath, now [1288A] dissolved into concupiscence. ⟦s9⟧ But, as we have shown concerning the covetous, many are seen to satisfy their own pleasures gladly when they can do so without expense of money; but when they cannot do so without loss of gain, to go vehemently against them. ⟦s10⟧ Do not these men seem to thee to mingle iron and clay of mire alike? ⟦s11+12⟧ *And whereas thou sawest,* he says, *the iron mingled with clay of mire, they shall indeed be mingled to human seed, but they shall not cleave one to another, even as iron cannot be mingled with clay.* [var: Vulg. *humano semine*, ablative, "mingled with human seed"; Migne's dative governs the gloss that follows] What are human seeds, but the counsels of carnal prudence? ⟦s13⟧ The prudence of the flesh teaches us to seek money for the things necessary for use. ⟦s14⟧ The same teaches us to withdraw much from the necessaries of life, and to spare money, lest it seem to fail for necessary uses. ⟦s15⟧ See how it cleaves to human seed, since [1288B] the one seems as it were by counsel to require the other; yet they are not mingled together, because the one often seems to contradict the other. ⟦s16⟧ The love of riches commands one thing, and the love of delights another; the heat of pleasure one thing, the gain of covetousness another. ⟦s17⟧ But when in this manner diverse vices hurry a man in diverse directions, or drive him to contrary things, they wretchedly afflict him, they suffer him to rest neither by day nor by night; and in a manner as wonderful as it is wretched, they who are of this sort anticipate the times of their own damnation, while by the inward anxiety of the spirit, or by outward affliction, they rage savagely against themselves; and unless the clemency from above look upon them, they go through temporal torments to eternal ones.
