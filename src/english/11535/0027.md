## CHAPTER XXXIV. *How a man is sometimes advanced by God from the depth of iniquity to the highest summit of charity.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1288C]

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ But the divine mercy often comes to the help of salvation in that which, so far as lies in the men themselves, is a heaping up of damnation, according to what is written: where iniquity abounded, grace also did more abound [n: (Matth. XXIV)]. [var: Vulg. Rom. 5:20 reads *delictum*, "offence", where Migne's noun is that of Matt. 24:12, to which his own note points] For often, when the grace from above visits men of this sort amid the straits of their calamities, it sets this very enormity of their own unhappiness before their eyes, and in the consideration of it pricks them to the lamentations of penitence; and having drawn them out of the pit of misery and out of the mire of dregs, by certain steps of advance it carries them from the depth of iniquity to the highest summit of charity. ⟦s3⟧ Is not this what Scripture speaks, [1288D] where it is consequently added: *In the days of those kingdoms the God of heaven shall raise up a kingdom which shall never be destroyed, and his kingdom shall not be given to another people. ⟦s4+5⟧ It shall break in pieces and shall consume all these kingdoms, and itself shall stand for ever, according as thou sawest that a stone was cut out of a mountain without hands, and it broke in pieces the clay, and the iron, and the bronze, and the silver, and the gold [n: (Dan. II)]*. [var: Vulg. *non tradetur*, "shall not be delivered over"] And so in the days of those kingdoms, in the time of so great malice and so great misery, an abundance of grace is given, and there is raised up a kingdom of charity which can never perish. ⟦s6⟧ For he that abideth in love shall abide for ever. ⟦s7⟧ And so this kingdom shall never be destroyed. ⟦s8⟧ *Charity* indeed *beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things [n: (I Cor. XIII)]*. ⟦s9⟧ And his kingdom shall not be given to another people, [1289A] for this is that fountain (as Augustine says) whereof a stranger doth not partake: *It shall break in pieces,* he says, *and shall consume all these kingdoms.* ⟦s10⟧ But would you know how charity breaks in pieces and consumes all these kingdoms: *Charity,* says the Apostle, *is patient, is kind; charity envieth not, dealeth not perversely, is not puffed up, is not ambitious, seeketh not her own, is not provoked to anger, thinketh no evil, rejoiceth not upon iniquity, but rejoiceth with the truth.* ⟦s11⟧ Further it is added of this kingdom, that itself shall stand for ever; and *Charity,* says the Apostle, *never falleth away,* according as thou sawest, he says, that a stone was cut out of a mountain without hands, and it broke in pieces the clay, and the iron, and the bronze, and the silver, and the gold. ⟦s12⟧ We know that the kingdom of God is begun from fear, but is consummated [1289B] in charity. ⟦s13⟧ And so by the stone of fear the work of confusion is cast down, and the kingdom of charity is founded. ⟦s14⟧ But the stone by which the kingdom of charity is founded is cut out without hands: for the striking in of fear and the inspiration of charity comes to be in the Holy Spirit, not by human work: *The charity,* he says, *of God is poured forth in our hearts, by the Holy Spirit who is given to us [n: (Rom. V)]*. ⟦s15⟧ It shall break in pieces, he says, and shall consume all these kingdoms, according as thou sawest that a stone was cut out without hands, and it crushed the clay, and the iron, and the bronze, and the silver, and the gold. ⟦s16⟧ The destruction of the opposing kingdoms is begun from fear and perfected in charity. ⟦s17⟧ Through fear they are rooted out of consent, through charity they are plucked out of effect. ⟦s18⟧ Charity therefore, which is patient, breaks [1289C] in pieces the fragility of clay; charity, which is kind, breaks in pieces the rigidity of iron. ⟦s19⟧ What, I ask, is that in which the bronze of simulation is broken in pieces, but charity which envieth not? ⟦s20⟧ And what is it that breaks in pieces the obstinacy of bronze, but charity which dealeth not perversely? ⟦s21⟧ If in the silver boasting is understood and in the gold arrogance, what is so contrary to them as charity, which is not puffed up, nor is ambitious, nor seeketh the things that are her own? ⟦s22⟧ If either in the silver that work is understood which is done out of deliberation alone and not out of love, it is not done so much out of love as out of truth: what is so agreeable to charity, which rejoiceth with the truth? ⟦s23⟧ If in the head of gold the failure of the best work is reproved, in that it is not [1289D] brought through to the heel of its due end, what better convicts or corrects this work than perfect charity, which never falleth away? ⟦s24⟧ O happy kingdom of charity, which abideth for ever! ⟦s25⟧ O happy kingdom which shall never be destroyed, since to another people it shall for ever be given [cj: *dabitur*; read *non dabitur*, "shall not be given"]! ⟦s26⟧ Happy kingdom in which patience endures and kindness perseveres! ⟦s27⟧ Happy kingdom, in which no one envies; happy indeed, in which there is no perverse dealing, no one is puffed up; truly happy, in which no ambitious man seeks the things that are his own; no one is burned! ⟦s28⟧ Perhaps he hints something to us in this, that whereas above, in the enumeration of the crushing, he set first the iron and afterwards the clay, in this place he plainly sets first the clay and afterwards the iron. ⟦s29⟧ But we know that in some men fear rules, and in others shame. ⟦s30⟧ For some indeed are more fearful than modest, [1290A] but others more modest than fearful. ⟦s31⟧ Those therefore who are the more timid are the more easily pricked by the consideration of their own cruelty than of their own pleasure. ⟦s32⟧ But those who are the more modest are the more readily softened to penitence by the recollection of foul pleasure than of iniquity. ⟦s33⟧ If therefore by the iron cruelty is understood, and by the clay pleasure rightly so, what wonder if elsewhere the iron and the clay are read as broken in pieces in one order and another, since according to the quality of spirits; or of manners, the order or the occasion of inward compunction and true contrition varies in one man and another?

## CHAPTER XXXV. *What prudence is far too late, and what is useful and sure.*

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ Daniel still goes on, and adds: *The great God,* [1290B] he says, *hath shown the king what shall be hereafter [n: (Dan. II)]*. [var: Vulg. *ventura*, "what shall come to pass"] God, he says, is great: perhaps he had grown great in the thought and the love of the man to whom he had revealed so great a depth of mysteries, so that not undeservedly he ought to magnify his greatness above all others. ⟦s3⟧ The great God hath shown the king: not to any man whatsoever, but to a king the Lord hath shown these things, so that he who pants after the foreknowledge of things to come and after grace of this sort may recognize, with royal liberty and authority, that this realm of his is to be ruled and ordered. ⟦s4⟧ The great God hath shown the king what shall be hereafter. ⟦s5⟧ O with how great mercy of God is it done, that while it is still to come, while it can still be guarded against, a man's own ruin is revealed to him, that with so great solicitude he may foresee and recognize what he ought to fear, what to beware of, what [1290C] peril he ought to fight against. ⟦s6⟧ Slow indeed, and far too late, is that foresight when the peril already imminent cannot be avoided. ⟦s7⟧ The great God hath shown the king what shall be hereafter. ⟦s8⟧ Happy is he who can foresee the imminent ruin while he is still king, and can still lord it over his passions and turn aside the pressing peril! ⟦s9⟧ But let us see what is subjoined after all these things, when it is said: *And the dream is true, and the interpretation faithful.* ⟦s10⟧ We often penetrate many deep things of the divine mysteries by the liveliness of the reason implanted in us, and some things concerning what is to come we foresee with the eye of prudence, in wary solicitude, before they arrive. ⟦s11⟧ But in these things constancy often totters, and it does not fully grasp what it might hold unshaken through the security of certitude. ⟦s12⟧ For the thoughts of mortal men are fearful, [1290D] and our foresights uncertain. ⟦s13⟧ But on the contrary, what we discern in the light of God, what we know from his revelation, we hold with such certitude and confidence that we can be shaken by no doubtful ambiguity concerning it. ⟦s14⟧ This is what the Psalmist figuratively expresses when he says: *At the brightness that was before him the clouds passed away [n: (Psal. XVII)]*, for the Lord to look upon is to illuminate. ⟦s15⟧ For since he is the true light, after the manner of the sun, by that very act by which he looks he lights up, kindles, and illuminates. ⟦s16⟧ And so at the divine regard we are often filled with so great a splendour of the divine light, that in that which is divinely revealed every clouding of ambiguity or hesitation is straightway taken out of the midst. ⟦s17⟧ And so the clouds pass away, when those doubts which were wont to becloud the spirit withdraw. ⟦s18⟧ For the clouds to pass away [1291A] is to hold the clear-sightedness of truth and the serenity of understanding without any questioning. ⟦s19⟧ He who confidently asserted it showed that he had certitude of this sort, and the security of certitude, in the assertion of his own judgment. ⟦s20⟧ And the dream is true, and the interpretation thereof faithful. ⟦s21⟧ But what the king did at these things, Scripture subjoins.
