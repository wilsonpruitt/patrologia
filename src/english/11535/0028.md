## CHAPTER XXXVI. *That an unstable and indiscreet humbling is indeed worthy of reproof.*

*Then king Nabuchodonosor fell upon his face and adored Daniel, and he commanded that they should sacrifice victims and incense to him [n: (Dan. II)]*. Greatly, and no wonder, did he humble himself, and deservedly ought he to have been humbled after he knew that so great a peril hung over him. [1291B] But who would not justly reprove him in this, that he rendered to a mortal man the honour which is owed to God alone? But perhaps, because he perceived him to be a true temple, he judged him worthy of such honour. But for our own edification we must diligently sift these things, and subtly inquire what is wont to be enacted in us after their likeness: He fell, it says, upon his face: by the face a man is known, and that part in a man is held more comely and more worthy than the rest. What else therefore is it to fall upon one's face, than even to despise, with a lofty longing, that which is chief or best in oneself, and to reckon that very thing whereby one was wont to become known to others and to appear as it were venerable, as something weak and almost [1291C] or utterly nothing? Consider how he fell upon his face, how he humbled himself, or rather how he would have us humbled, who compared all our justices to the cloth of a menstruous woman. But it is neither a very great thing nor much of a wonder if we cast ourselves down at that time in which we already discern that our peril hangs over us. Then, it says, the king fell upon his face. Then he fell, when, at Daniel's teaching, he recognized his own peril. But it must be known that that humbling which is without perseverance cannot fight against the imminent peril.

This also is to be noted, that often the more inconsiderately and immoderately a man casts himself down, the more insanely and enormously does he afterwards break out into pride. Consider in the king a humbling unstable and indiscreet, [1291D] and afterwards a pride more than human. For he who first adored Daniel, that same man afterwards commanded his own statue to be adored.

## CHAPTER XXXVII. *That we ought to trust in no virtue of our own, nor to wish to glory in it.*

Victims, it says, and incense he commanded that they should sacrifice to him. To kill animals for Daniel, or to burn incense, and to expend anything whatever of that kind for his use or profit, would not be blameworthy at all. But it is blameworthy to sacrifice these things to him, and to expend divine honour upon a mortal man. In the immolation of a victim the flesh of brute and irrational creatures is slain; in the burning of incense a sweet odour is spread more widely As therefore living but brute flesh is slain, [1292A] when any irrational appetite or affection in us is mortified by the crushing of the flesh; and then we spread abroad more widely as it were the odour of incense, when we enlarge the repute of any virtue with worthy heralding of praises. And so to slay for our own Daniel some of those things which still live bestially in our appetites or affections, and to practise a mortification of that sort for the use and profit of our devotion, will without any contradiction not be unprofitable. But when we commend Daniel's office with manifold praise, and concerning the virtue of devotion spread abroad on every side the fragrance of good repute by the commendation of our speech, we do indeed a good work, if in all these things we seek not so much the glory of our Daniel as his profit. And, that I may say more openly [1292B] what I think, it is surely good to restrain our life for the sake of the grace of devotion and of instancy in prayer; it is good to commend the virtue of prayer and to extol it with praises, provided that it be done to this end, that our devotion may grow to the better and rise up more fervent, not that it may appear more glorious. How many there are who, when they believe that they have obtained some grace through instancy in prayer, already presuming much upon their own devotion or holiness, wish great things to be thought of it by others also. This is what Nabuchodonosor did, who, after he had come through Daniel to the desire of his vow, heaped upon him an honour as foolish as it was vain. But what we reprove concerning this virtue of which we speak, we ought to beware of in any virtue or [1292C] grace whatever. Hear it openly, mark it diligently, hold it in memory: if in any grace, in any effort, if in any virtue of thine own thou trustest, presumest, gloriest, thou art indeed bestowing divine honour upon it, and shamelessly enough; not to say that thou art making a God for thyself. He that glorieth, let him glory in the Lord. Cursed be the man that trusteth in man; therefore also he who trusteth in himself or in any virtue of his own: *My glory,* he says, *I will not give to another, nor my praise to a graven thing [n: (Isa XLII)]*. [var: Vulg. *et laudem meam sculptilibus*, "and my praise to graven images", plural]

## CHAPTER XXXVIII. *How many seek the knowledge of the virtues and the teaching of morals for display, not for profit.*

How many glory, and wish to be glorious in the eyes of men: I will not say over the fact that they have virtues or holiness, but over the fact that they know how to discourse prudently [1292D] about the virtues and to dispute eloquently. O how much better, O how much more profitable it would be for thee not to have the gold of knowledge and the silver of eloquence, than to make thyself an idol out of them! The idols of the nations are silver and gold, the works of the hands of men. To paint out the measure and the mere form of the virtues by understanding and teaching, and to hold them in memory, but to lack their effect: what else is it than to carry certain idols in the heart? The knowledge of holiness without a good intention: what else is it than an image without life? Knowledge alone, without the effect of holiness and the affection of goodness: what else is it than a vain idol without motion and without sense? *A mouth,* he says, *they have, and they shall not speak; eyes they have, and they shall not see; ears they have, and they shall not hear; nostrils they have, and they shall not smell. [1293A] Hands they have, and they shall not feel; feet they have, and they shall not walk; neither shall they cry out in their throat [n: (Psal. CXIII)]*. The mouth, as we all know, is the instrument of speaking; the eyes, the instrument of seeing; the ears, the instrument of hearing; and in this manner it ought to be understood of the rest also. What therefore is it to have a mouth, eyes, ears, or any such things, and to exercise them for no uses at all, but to have the instruments of the offices and to lack the offices of the instruments? Thou readest that with the heart we believe unto justice, but with the mouth confession is made unto salvation [n: (Rom. X)]. And so to the mouth belongs confession; to the eyes, circumspection; to the ears, obedient hearing; to the nostrils, discretion; to the hands, working; to the feet, advance; to the throat, supplication. Behold, that empty knowledge of thine perhaps knows exceedingly well what the virtue of confession is. [1293B] It knows that all things are washed away in confession. It knows perhaps how it ought to confess, and yet it does not confess. It has a mouth therefore, and speaks not. It knows perhaps how it ought to look around upon its own life, yet it dissembles to look around upon it as it should. Thou hast in a manner the instrument of seeing, but thou lackest the office of seeing. Thou knowest perhaps what the virtue of obedience is, and of what kind it ought to be, and yet thou wilt not obey. This is to have ears, and not to hear. Through knowledge thou hast perhaps the instrument of discerning spiritual fragrance. But while thou spendest no zeal upon discernment in matters of conduct, thou gloriest emptily in a useless instrument. Thou knowest how thou oughtest to exercise thyself in good work, and yet thou wilt not take the fruit of good work by experience: this is to have hands, [1293C] and not to feel. For what is it to feel with the hands, but to prove the fruits of works by experiencing them? Thou hast received through knowledge the feet of advances, if thou hast learned how one ought to stretch forward to the things that are before. But having feet thou dost not walk at all, if thou tendest not to advance. Thou hast received the teaching of supplication, and thou wilt not supplicate: this is to have a throat, and to be unwilling to cry out.
