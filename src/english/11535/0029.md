## CHAPTER XXXIX. *That in the carrying out of any virtue these seven things ought to be observed.*

But if we attend diligently, we ought to observe these seven things in the carrying out of any virtue. The first is that which belongs to the mouth: to condemn and accuse the evils done before. The second, which belongs to the [1293D] eyes: to investigate diligently what is to be done, and through investigation to know it. The third, which belongs to obedient hearing: when counsel has been found, to rest in it by consenting, and to be willing to comply. The fourth, which is as it were a various thing: warily to detect the evils that lie in wait for the good that is to be done, and prudently to discern them. The fifth, which likewise looks as it were to the hands: to fulfil in work the good that has been deliberated. The sixth, as it were by a certain advance of the feet: from a good beginning always to tend to better things. But because we are of ourselves sufficient for none of these, we ought to implore the divine help for them all. But if we know these things and yet do not practise them in work, what else, in acquiring and cultivating idle and useless kinds of knowledge, do we make [1294A] or worship than vain and empty idols, while we are content with the mere acquaintance with the virtues? See how perverse and damnable a thing it is to seek spiritual teaching for display alone, and not for edification. This is assuredly the prudence of the flesh, and one that is utterly an enemy to God? What, I ask, does it profit; nay, how much does it hurt, to seek with great labour and the utmost zeal, and to investigate, and anxiously to wish to know, the things which thou art in no way willing to practise in work? For it stands clearer than the light that the servant who knows his lord's will and does it not, but rather does things unworthy, shall be beaten with many stripes. O what a counsel, and how useless, to seek the counsels of life for this alone, that thou mayest have wherewith thou canst appear wiser than the rest, and obtain the name of master! How sharp a prudence, how cunning a foresight, [1294B] to press vehemently after the searching out of those counsels in which, once obtained, thou art in no way willing to rest! But we often see it happen to minds of this sort, by the just judgment of God, that such as their spiritual foresights were before towards spiritual things, such afterwards do their carnal foresights become towards carnal things. Of what kind, I say, their spiritual counsels stood before towards heavenly and divine things, of that kind after a little are their carnal counsels found to be towards worldly and human things. For just as in spiritual things and in the gains of the soul they first see what they might healthfully do, and yet are unwilling to do it; may it be [cj: *sit*; read *sic*, "so"] that afterwards in advantageous fleshes and in temporal [sic: *temporalibis*] gains they see what ought usefully to be done, and yet, conquered by concupiscence or bound fast by sloth, are wholly unable to do it. But this very thing is wont to happen to some men [1294C] even profitably, so that from that very fact they now begin to detest and abhor their own vices, and over this very thing to implore the divine help, when they see that not even amid the greatest perils can they restrain themselves from their own excesses; whence comes that prophetic imprecation, or petition: *Let them that make them become like unto them, and all such as trust in them [n: (Psal. CXIII)]*. But that we may return to that from which we digressed, let us at least gather from this how foolish and empty a thing it is to wish to glory in the mere empty knowledges of the virtues, and in certain idols as it were of the virtues themselves, since with God it is detestable that a man should presume upon any virtue of his own. Behold, we reprove Nabuchodonosor, and deservedly ought we to reprove him, in that he had commanded Daniel, though [1294D] a faithful man, to be presented with divine honour; how much the more if he had wished the same thing to be rendered to Daniel's image.

## CHAPTER XL. *That desire is often corrupted from the very source by which the understanding is illuminated.*

But let us see amid these things how truly he thought and spoke concerning God, who willed such empty things to be done. The king therefore, speaking, says to Daniel: *Verily your God is the God of gods, and the Lord of kings, revealing mysteries, since thou couldst open this secret. [n: (Dan. II)]*. Behold how soundly he thinks even concerning divine things, and yet [sic: *tamem*] what he practised in deed was like to idolatry. Thus, thus, at one and the same time both Moses on the mountain is lifted up to the contemplation of the highest and divine things, and [1295A] the people committed to him is cast down in the valley to the crime of idolatry and to the worship of the calf; and often indeed it comes to pass in one and the same spirit, in a manner as wonderful as it is pitiable, that from the very source by which human understanding is illuminated on high toward divine things, from that same source the crowd of desires is the less corrupted. For who does not know how the greatness of revelations lifts an incautious mind into pride, since the Apostle himself says of himself: *Lest the greatness of the revelations should exalt me, there was given me a sting of my flesh, an angel of Satan, to buffet me [n: (I Cor. XII)]*. For often certain men, while still set amid the very sights of divine revelations, grow proud within themselves with a silent mind; already thinking great things of themselves, they count it worthy to be esteemed great by others; already they search out with shrewd solicitude how they may become known [1295B] to others also; already they catch at the breeze of empty favour, and change into a vain glory that true glory which they were wont to have with God. Of such the Psalmist says: *And they changed the glory into the likeness of a calf that eateth hay [n: (Psal. CV)]*. Since indeed it is written: *All flesh is hay, and all the glory thereof as the flower of hay [n: (Isa. XL)]*. [var: Vulg. *flos agri*, "the flower of the field"] What else will the calf that eateth hay be, but the appetite for human favour, courting a carnal and temporal glory? What else therefore will it be to worship a calf, an animal wanton indeed and brute, than in the courting of temporal glory to serve an irrational appetite, and to set one's own glory in a temporal and empty glory? And Nabuchodonosor indeed, [1295C] whom nevertheless we justly reprove, did something more tolerable, in that he presented with divine honour a man, an animal to be sure, but a rational one. For beyond doubt it will be more irrational to worship an irrational animal than a rational one. Some trust and glory in their own virtue, but others in the mere estimation of virtue. Those, over what they have or believe themselves to have; these, over what they are esteemed by others to be. Those, over the testimony of conscience, yet not as before God; these, over the testimony of report, as before their neighbour only. And so the former is as it were an irrational animal, the latter as it were a rational animal. Happy is he who glories over the testimony of conscience, yet so as in the Lord. Otherwise it is a very thankless thing to glory over the gift, but not in the giver of the gift. How much better, how much more rightly [1295D] would the said king have adored God rather than Daniel, and sacrificed to him who had indeed taught him (though by Daniel's office) what he was seeking.
