## CHAPTER XLI. *That some things we know from divine revelation, and others we gather from reason.*

But since he did things to be reproved, as we have already said, let us see how rightly he thought concerning God in that which he said: Verily your God is the God of gods, and the Lord of kings. Since the royal power is chief and highest, what is it for the king to say this, but from the inmost sense of the soul to hold it firmly and unmovably? What is it for Daniel to hear this from the king, but that the inmost sense of the soul neither ought nor is able to lie hidden from the true devotion of the spirit? Or perhaps in this, that some things Daniel [1296A] learns from God and others he heard also from the king, Holy Scripture mystically hints that, for the use of our devotion, some things are those which we learn from divine revelation, others those which we weigh out from the liveliness of reason implanted in us. But in that, speaking of God, he says "your" and "our", he attends to the regard of grace, not to the condition of nature. For in a certain special way he belonged properly to those who, out of the gift of his own grace and by the prerogative of merits, had obtained with him a singular place of familiarity beyond the rest, and the boldness of confidence. And without doubt it is devotion which both renders God propitious and makes him familiar. God, he says, of gods, and Lord of kings. For although there be some that are called gods, whether in heaven or on earth, according to that word: *I have said: You are gods [n: (Psal. LXXXI)]*, by name [1296B] indeed they can be so called: for what God is naturally and substantially, that they can in no wise be. He is therefore the God of gods, and the Lord of kings. Because, as the Apostle testifies: *There is no power but from God [n: (Rom. XIII)]*. For that some are set over others, that men of virtues are able to have dominion over their own vices and passions, they receive assuredly from the gift of the highest King. He says therefore: Verily your God is the God of gods, and the Lord of kings, revealing mysteries; but whence he gathers and conjectures these things he at once subjoins, when he says: Since thou couldst open this sacrament. For from this, that he revealed to him the secrets of his own heart, and from this likewise, that he revealed to him the hidden things of the divine counsel, the one concerning the past, the other concerning the future, at one and the same time, [1296C] he weighed, with a wonderful liveliness of wit, the revelation of the highest Godhead. And from this, that he heard concerning the secret of his own heart and proved it true, he gathered that that also was true which meanwhile he could not prove by experience concerning the counsel or the purpose of the Godhead. Prudently therefore he observed that it was the highest wisdom which was able to open to Daniel, in so dark an abyss of the human heart, the secrets of another's conscience. For, according to Solomon's sentence, God alone it is who knows the heart of all the sons of men. Shrewdly none the less he perceived that it was the highest power which was able to reveal to Daniel, according to its good pleasure, the order and disposition of things to come and of things to be done. For who else than God could manifest to him the divine counsel? According to that word [1296D] which Isaiah said: *Who hath known the sense of the Lord, or who hath been his counsellor? [n: (Isai. XL.)]* [var: Vulg. Isa. XL:13 opens *Quis adjuvit spiritum Domini*, "Who hath helped the spirit of the Lord"; the wording printed here is that of Rom. XI:34, *Quis enim cognovit sensum Domini*] For, as the Apostle witnesses, just as no one knows the things that are of man save the spirit of man which is in him, so no one knows the things that are of God, save the Spirit of God. Rightly therefore is it said: Verily your God is the God of gods, and the Lord of kings, revealing mysteries, since thou couldst open this sacrament.

## CHAPTER XLII. *That it is best that our devotion advance so far that no occupation can interrupt its office.*

Then the king raised Daniel on high, and gave him great and many gifts. If by Daniel we rightly understand devotion, what else will it be to lift Daniel on [1297A] high than to reckon the office of devotion, among all other offices whatsoever, as chief and principal, and to hold it as it were in the highest place? And it is wont to come to pass, after the mind proves by experience how great the virtue of true devotion is, that it stretches out more vehemently toward its pursuits and diligently presses upon its advancements, that by investigating it may seek, by seeking may find, whence it may advance in the selfsame thing toward better things. What else therefore than great and many gifts does the Daniel of our devotion receive, when out of zeal eagerly bestowed he easily finds, as it were, certain talents of understanding and gains of prudence, whereby he may grow toward greater things and be enlarged to very many? *And he set him,* he says, *prince over all the provinces of Babylon, and chief of the magistrates over all the wise men of Babylon.* [1297B] O would that our devotion might advance so far that it should appear higher than all the princes of Babylon, wiser than all the prudent of Babylonia, and stand out more precious, by the judgment of our mind, than everything that pleases in the world or seems precious, and by the decree of the will obtain the higher gain! This will be, as I judge, for Daniel to receive from the king the citadel of princedom and of mastery. Would that it might at length at some time befall our devotion to attain that measure of perfection wherein no affection, however vehement, no occupation, no solicitude, no suggestion or persuasion could in us, I will not say scatter, but not even interrupt its zeal, and that unmoved apostolic word be fulfilled: *Without ceasing [1297C] pray! [n: (I Thess. V.)]* Would that all other offices whatsoever were so disposed or ordered that they might be proved to serve as soldiers, or even to be subservient, to the uses and advantages of our devotion!

## CHAPTER XLIII. *That even in the uses of our necessity we ought to do nothing improvidently, nothing inconsiderately.*

*But Daniel requested of the king, and he appointed over the works of the province of Babylon, Sidrac, Misach and Abdenago [n: (Dan. II)]*. The king without doubt consents to Daniel asking, when the will of the mind readily accepts and receives whatever conscience suggests to it in the time of inmost devotion. But what, I ask, is that province of Babylon that is spoken of, or what are the works of that province? Certainly God made the province lying round about Babylon: [1297D] for the walls indeed and the buildings human effort completed. If therefore Babylon is rightly interpreted *confusion*, if whatsoever things are hers seem to lie subject to confusion, how, I ask, could that which God made and instituted belong to Babylon, or be under confusion? But we know that God has subjected man to a manifold necessity: for the necessity of eating, of drinking, and of any things of that kind, he received from the very right of his own condition. But if he had persisted in that first state of his condition, man would in no way blush at these things, because he could have practised them without any sin. [1298A] But now we justly blush at those same offices of necessity, because, overcome by the vice of our corruption, we have not strength to practise them without fault. Rightly therefore is that broad, and truly much widened, corruption of our infirmity assigned to the Babylonian possession, which is not doubted to lie subject to confusion. In a province of this kind, therefore, and in the office or the works of our necessity, it will be altogether most useful to have set those aforesaid men in charge, and to have brought all things under their disposition. As we have already said above, by those three men we ought to understand circumspection, discretion, deliberation. We ought therefore, not only in spiritual pursuits and exercises, but also in secular business and in the uses of necessity, to do nothing incircumspectly, nothing [1298B] indiscreetly, nothing improvidently. And this will be to have appointed Sidrach, Misach and Abdenago over all the works of the province of Babylon. And so in these things also, in which we take counsel for human necessity, or serve the common infirmity, let us do nothing without modesty, nothing without measure, nothing without good intention, nothing beside order, nothing beside reason, and we obtain such mastery as Daniel willed us to have in the Babylonian province. Finally, according to the sentence of the Wise Man, do all things with counsel, and thou hast such mastery as the prophet designates for thee out of the mystery. For there is altogether need of circumspection and of discretion, if we wish to do nothing improvidently, nothing inconsiderately.

## CHAPTER XLIV. *That whatever strikes the spirit through thought ought to arouse the devotion of the mind.*

[1298C]

But at the last, in the conclusion of the discourse, it is brought in: *But Daniel himself was in the doors of the king [n: (Dan. II)]*. Now we know that through the doors [sic: *perfores*] the entrance to the inner parts lies open. Daniel, finally, keeps watch as it were in the doors of the king, when our devotion warily keeps vigil over all suggestions whatsoever, and in the very entrance of temptation fights back with prayer against all the machinations of the evil one, lest any infestation or violence oppress the incautious mind, or circumvent it by fraud. Happy the state of the spirit, and to be sought after with all zeal, when everything that strikes the spirit through thought also at once, without any delay, arouses the devotion of the mind. The devotion [1298D] of the mind ought therefore studiously to forestall every consent of the will, and to fortify it beforehand with prayer. And this will be for Daniel to preside over the doors of the king. At every suggestion, at every emerging thought, let us ask of the Lord that he lead us not into temptation, and let us have Daniel presiding over the doors of our city. Hear what the Wise Man admonishes according to these things, when he says: *At all times bless God, and ask of him that he direct thy ways [n: (Tob. IV)]*. And may he direct our ways to the true life, who without end lives and reigns. Amen.
