## CHAPTER ONE. *Of the security of conscience, or the renewal of advancements.*

[1299A]

*I Nabuchodonosor was at rest in my house, and flourishing in my palace [n: (Dan. IV)]*. What do we understand by the house, but conscience, and what by the palace, but the security of conscience and the confidence of security? For a palace also is a house, yet not any house whatsoever can be called a palace. For a palace is a certain house that is strong, lofty and royal. If by the house we ought to understand conscience, rightly by the palace is understood the security of conscience. And so he sits at rest in his own house whom his own conscience does not bite back. A quiet conscience is made by a worthy satisfaction for past [1299B] evils, and by a wary and provident turning aside from present evils. And so he remains at rest in the house whom his own conscience bites back neither for past nor for present fault. He was at rest in the house who truly said: *For neither hath my heart reproved me in all my life [n: (Job XXVII)]*. He stood at rest in his own house who could truly say: *For I am conscious to myself of nothing [n: (I Cor. IV)]*. He was assuredly at rest in the house at that time, and was flourishing in his own palace, when he said: *Our glory, this is the testimony of our conscience [n: (II Cor. I)]*. Because in the flower fruit is hoped for, rightly by the flower is figured the certain expectation of the goods to come. Because the flower is the beginning of the fruits to come, rightly none the less [1299C] by the flower is understood the renewal of advancements. In the flower therefore is figured either the certain expectation of rewards, or the new promotion of merits. And so he truly flourishes in his own palace who, under the testimony of his own good conscience, securely awaits the crown of glory. Hear one resting in his own house and flourishing in his own palace: *But I shall appear before thy sight in justice; I shall be satisfied when thy glory shall appear [n: (Psal. XVI)]*. That he says, I shall appear in justice, pertains to the rest of the house. That he says, I shall be satisfied when thy glory shall appear, pertains to the flowering. He was none the less flourishing in his own palace who with the conscience of a great security said: *I have fought the good fight, I have finished the course, I have kept the faith; for the rest there is laid up for me a crown of justice, which the Lord [1299D] will render to me in that day, the just judge [n: (II Tim. VI)]*. Does not he also seem to thee to flourish in his own palace, whose sense is renewed from day to day? He sits and flourishes in his own palace who, amid his own great works which he gladly does, purposes in his heart yet greater and better ones.

## CHAPTER II. *Of the illumination of the intellectual sense, or how the sights of our contemplation are wont to strike a useful fear into the mind.*

*I saw a dream,* he says, *which terrified me, and my thoughts [1300A] upon my bed, and the visions of my head troubled me [n: (Dan. IV)]*. We sleep before we see a dream. Because through the dream the bodily sense of the body is lulled, rightly by the dream is understood that alienation of the mind through which the memory of all outward things is utterly cut off. But to see a dream is to pass over in mind into the secret place of divine contemplation. And so he sleeps and sees a dream who through excess of mind ascends into the contemplation of sublime things: I saw a dream, he says, which terrified me. Of dreams of this kind, some deter, others soothe, others sadden, others gladden. The contemplation of eternal joys assuredly gladdens (nor is it a wonder), but the beholding of the divine judgments terrifies. For who could worthily express how [1300B] glad, how joyful his own vision or revelation makes him to whom God has revealed through his Spirit that which eye hath not seen, nor ear heard, nor hath ascended into the heart of man? But how, on the contrary, does his own vision terrify him who by the lifting up of the mind sees through contemplation by how wonderful, by how hidden a judgment of God it is that one man sometimes goes forth even from prison and from chains unto a kingdom, and another born in a kingdom is consumed in want? Whom, I ask, would the abyss of the divine judgments not terrify, when, touched by the divine light, the understanding of the mind perceives clearly that the course is not the swift men's, nor the war the strong men's, nor bread the wise men's, nor riches the learned men's, nor favour the craftsmen's, but time and chance in all things? The Prophet had [1300C] certainly seen that the judgments of the Lord are a great abyss, and the visions of his head had terrified him, when he said to the Lord: *For I have feared thy judgments [n: (Psal. CXVIII)]*. Yet visions of this kind are wont to terrify, not so much at that time in which they are seen through excess of mind, as at that time when, after sleep has been shaken off, they are recalled to memory, when with subtle examination they are gone over again with oneself in a silent mind. Hence is that which follows, when it is said: And my thoughts upon my bed, and the visions of my head troubled me. For indeed he asserts that he is troubled by two things: by his own thoughts, and by the visions of his own head. For when the mind considers out of divine revelation something to be wondered at, nay rather to be dreaded, and afterwards, returning to itself and going over it with itself, judges itself [1300D] with a strong examination, lest perchance it be worthy that some such thing should befall it, it is dealt with by its very own thoughts, so that its own vision terrifies it. My thoughts, he says, upon my bed. What is here understood by the bed, but the leisure of inmost quiet; and the vision of the head, but the illumination of the intellectual sense. And the visions, he says, of my head troubled me. And so his own thoughts upon his own bed, and the visions of his own head, then terrify each man and by terrifying trouble him, when out of that which, after the secret of divine revelation, he handles with himself [1301A] in the busy leisure of his own meditation, he wakes up unto a wholesome solicitude and a useful dread. For when a man knows out of divine revelation how one is wont to advance from good to better, another to sink from evil into worse things, or how it befalls that some fall down from the highest goods into the lowest evils, or climb up from the lowest evils to the heights of the virtues; what wonder, I ask, if his own vision compels him to dread? *Man knoweth not* indeed *his own end; but, as fishes are taken with the hook, and birds are caught with the snare, so are men taken in the evil time, when it shall suddenly come upon them [n: (Eccle. IX)]*.

## CHAPTER III. *That the grace by which we know the divine judgments is one, and that by which we discern our own merits another.*

[1301B]

But see how astonishing a thing it is, and how dreadful, that often the mind, irradiated by the divine light, sees through contemplation what ought worthily to befall such a man as it is itself, and yet knows not that it is at that time such a one, upon whom by the divine sentence such a judgment deservedly presses. Did not this same thing befall king Nabuchodonosor, who knew from God's revelation the divine sentence concerning the cutting down of the tree, and yet knew not how to draw to himself the likeness of the tree that was shown? He saw what by the divine sentence would press upon such a man as should bear in himself the image or the likeness of a tree of that kind, [1301C] and yet that he himself stood forth such a one, because he could not through himself, he knew through another. See therefore that the grace is one by which we know the divine judgments, and that other by which we discern our own merits. For those we find by contemplating, these by meditating. And so that power we have received from the grace of contemplation, but this from the zeal of meditation and the grace of discretion. And often one and the same man receives both graces together, although he cannot exercise both together at one and the same time. And so it is as though one consulted another, when what he could not fully do through one and the same state of mind, he learns from another state and at another time, which meanwhile may suffice for the full knowledge of himself. Hence it is that Nabuchodonosor is instructed through Daniel [1301D] concerning those things about which he himself seems to doubt in his own vision. And in a wonderful manner, while the king narrates his own vision to the prophet, not knowing it he teaches another what he may learn from him, and Daniel learns from the questioner who does not know, what he ought to teach the learner. Thus indeed, thus often the mind, prudently unknowing, brings back from the light of contemplation that which afterwards it may find through meditation, and may more fully know. And meditation receives from the foreknown light of revelation that whereby it may loose the knots of doubt. As we have already said above, by the king we understand free will, by Daniel devotion. And so to a king of this kind belongs that contemplation which lies subject to the will of the mind, when the spirit already has it at hand, and exercises it as it were out of its own power. So also that meditation [1302A] seems to belong to Daniel which, out of the zeal of devotion, a religious mind is wont to exercise about the circumspection of its own morals. The king thus designated therefore seeks, as it were, the interpretation of his own dreams from Daniel, and finds it, when the spirit afterwards, by meditating and by looking about, reads in its own morals the things which it descried amid the free flights of its own contemplation, and understands them more deeply. But let us now see how urgently Nabuchodonosor sought what he knew not.
