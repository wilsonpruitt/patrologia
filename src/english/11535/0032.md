## CHAPTER IV. *How we ought to lay aside secular pursuits, and to turn them to the investigation of spiritual things.*

But amid these things, let those whom the visions of the head and the thoughts that follow trouble learn from the example of king Nabuchodonosor [1302B] what they ought to do: *And by me,* he says, *a decree was set forth, that all the wise men of Babylon should be brought in before me, and that they should show me the solution of the dream [n: (Dan. IV)]*. Behold how diligently, behold how urgently he seeks what he knows not, unless [cj: *nisi*; read *nec*, "nor does he first desist"] he first desists, until he finds the solution of his dream which he seeks. Hence it is that Job says of himself: *The cause which I knew not, I diligently investigated [n: (Job XXIX)]*. [var: Vulg. *diligentissime*, "most diligently"] To set forth a decree is to establish something firmly, and such as it may in no wise be lawful to violate. See therefore how vehemently he willed that to be done concerning which he sets forth a decree. To the wise men of Babylon belongs the wisdom of this world, which is foolishness with God. To the wise men [1302C] of Babylon belongs the prudence of the flesh, which is an enemy to God. And so the wise men of Babylon are the exceedingly sharp searchings of worldly philosophy. The wise men of Babylon are the exceedingly sharp counsels of the prudence of the flesh. What therefore will it be to bring in wise men of this kind, and to call them back to the inner parts, but to turn to spiritual pursuits those exceedingly sharp searchings of our investigation which we were wont to bestow upon secular business? For those things are as it were outside which can be perceived by the outward sense. But those things are as it were within which cannot at all be comprehended save by the inward sense. Within, therefore, are spiritual things; without, bodily things. And so we bring in the wise men of Babylon when we turn secular pursuits, and all outward pursuits whatsoever, to spiritual understanding and to the investigation [1302D] of spiritual things. To bring in the wise men of Babylon is to postpone the care of outward things, and to bestow zeal upon spiritual exercises alone. He was keeping his own wise men in the inmost parts who said: *I thought upon the days of old, and I had the eternal years in mind. I meditated in the night with my own heart, and I was exercised, and I swept my spirit [n: (Psal. LXXVI)]*.

But it is to be noted that our wise men are now within, now without, and yet are not in our sight. That is held as it were in the sight of our will which is done through intention and out of deliberation. But it often befalls, out of the great accustomedness of our pursuits, that the spirit runs back incidentally to its ingrained searchings, and, before it notices what it intends, weaves in [sic: *invectere*] much delay there; that is, not to have its own wise men [1303A] before its own sight. But he without doubt makes his own wise men stand before his own sight who restrains his own pursuits from all wandering, and, according to the purpose of his own deliberation, bestows them upon useful and necessary things alone. The mind holds and watches its own wise men as it were in its own sight, when it keeps vigil with so great care over its own zealous meditations that it may nowhere be allowed to turn aside the foot of its investigation, save whither it had directed them out of deliberation, insomuch that no wandering of its own searchings can escape past the effort of its own shrewdness even for a little.

## CHAPTER V. *How he ought to press on who pants after the unravelling of the divine mysteries.*

[1303B]But let us now see why he gathers his own wise men into one, or drives them to the inner parts: that they should show me, he says, the solution of the dream. As we have already said in the exposition of the other vision, it is one thing to discern the divine judgments with the eye of contemplation and as it were through a dream, and another to weigh the reason of the same, and by the liveliness of one's own discretion to judge with what equity or with what usefulness they come to pass. That belongs to the seeing of the dream, this to the interpretation of the dream. We therefore see a dream in a certain manner, when we bring the hidden judgments of God into contemplation and admiration. We seek the interpretation of our dream when, after the sights of our contemplation, we investigate the reason, [1303C] the manner and the order of those things which we have seen through the lifting up of the mind. Does it not belong to the seeing of the dream to bring into consideration those astonishing great works of the divine judgments which we read in the Scriptures, and to cleave longer and more diligently to the admiration of them? Does it not belong to the interpretation of the dream to seek their mystical understanding, and to be able to explain it through exposition? Was he not panting after dreams of this kind who said in prayer: *Enlighten mine eyes, and I will consider the wondrous things of thy law [n: (Psal. CXVIII)]*. [var: Vulg. Ps. CXVIII:18 *Revela oculos meos*, "Uncover mine eyes"; this work prints *Revela* at 1253A and at 1262C] But how great a thing he would count it to receive grace divinely for the interpretation of dreams of this kind, you can weigh from that saying of his. For when he has premised concerning the Lord: *Who declareth his word unto Jacob, his justices and his judgments unto Israel,* he at once subjoins: [1303D] *He hath not done in like manner to every nation, and his judgments he hath not made manifest unto them [n: (Psal. CXLVII)]*. He had already seen as a dream the things which he had caught out of the light of his own contemplation: that all things happen alike to the just and to the wicked, to the good and to the evil, to the clean and to the unclean, to him that immolates victims and to him that despises sacrifices. He had sought the interpretation of the dream, but had not yet found it, when in another place he said: *Behold this I have found,* said Ecclesiastes, *one and another, that I might find out the reason which my soul still seeketh, and I have not found it [n: (Eccle. VII)]*. He must therefore gather his own wise men into one, and call them back to the inner parts, who desires to obtain grace for the interpretation of dreams of that kind which we have already determined. *Then came in the soothsayers, the magicians, the Chaldeans and the diviners. Then,* he says, [1304A] they came in. What is then, but after the decree, after the purpose of deliberation? For many often set forth many things, yet by no means do them, because as easily as they establish something, so easily do they again reject what was established.

First therefore it is to be observed by us that we establish useful things. But secondly, that we firmly hold what has been usefully established. Mark how firmly he established that which that best deliberator weighed to be best: *I have sworn,* he says, *and I have determined to keep the judgments of thy justice [n: (Psal. CXXVIII)]*. Thou hast heard of the establishing of the vow; hear also of the performing of the vow: *I will render my vows in the sight of all his people [n: (Psal. CXV)]*. [var: Vulg. Ps. CXV:14 *Vota mea Domino reddam coram omni populo ejus*, "I will pay my vows to the Lord before all his people"] Then came in the soothsayers, the magicians, the Chaldeans and the diviners. Above he set forth generally concerning the wise men of Babylon; [1304B] here he expressed certain of them specially. The soothsayers, he says, the magicians, the Chaldeans and the diviners. But because it truly stands that wise men of this kind are not truly wise, Holy Scripture almost nowhere calls them wise save with a determination, but now of Babylon, now the king calls them the wise men of his own kingdom, or something of this sort. If therefore by the soothsayers, the magicians, the Chaldeans and the diviners we understand depraved pursuits, frivolous, foolish and empty pursuits, see how manfully, see how wholesomely we turn pursuits of this kind to spiritual exercises. And so we drive the wise men of Babylon to the inner parts, when the care and the solicitude which we were wont to bestow upon superfluous things we bestow upon spiritual things alone. We occupy the wise men of Babylon as it were [1304C] in the interpretation of dreams, when, superfluous pursuits being wholly set aside, we press singularly upon the unravelling of the divine mysteries or judgments.

## CHAPTER VI. *How empty a thing it is to search into the deep things of the mysteries without the mastery of the Holy Spirit.*

But when the wise men had been brought in, the king still adds and says: *And I told the dream in their sight, and they showed me not the solution thereof [n: (Dan. IV)]*. What, I ask, is it to tell our dreams in the sight of our wise men, but, after contemplation, to recall to memory the sights of our contemplation, to go over them with shrewd meditation, to discuss them with zealous thoughts, and out of the comparison of those things which we know [1304D] to loose the knots of our questions, and to wish to shape the investigation of spiritual things according to the accustomed manner of our searchings? Do we not tell our dreams in the sight of our wise men and seek the solution, when we bring together in a mutual comparison the things which we know of human matters and the things which we desire to know of divine ones, when, according to the learned manner and order of the disciplines handed down, we presume to discuss and to penetrate the perplexed things of the divine mysteries or judgments? But whatsoever in the divine secrets is presumed out of human effort is attempted in vain, and so long as it is sought in this manner, the labour is spent for nothing. According to that which is read of this dream: and they showed me not the solution thereof. Assuredly our wise men by no means find the solution [1305A] of dreams divinely shown, because the disciplines of our learning, or any human traditions whatsoever, cannot suffice for the discussion and the investigation of the divine secrets. For who would presume to search out that height of the divine counsel out of his own sense, when he hears the Lord's prophet crying and crying again: *Who hath known the sense of the Lord, or who hath been his counsellor? [n: (Isai. XL)]*. [var: Vulg. Isa. XL:13 opens *Quis adjuvit spiritum Domini*, "Who hath helped the spirit of the Lord"; the wording printed here is that of Rom. XI:34, and this work prints the same verse with *Quis novit* at 1296D] Who would trust to penetrate that depth of the mysteries and of the divine Scriptures out of his own shrewdness, when he reads the apostle Peter, how he equally restrains all men from presumption of this kind? *This,* he says, *first understanding, that prophecy of Scripture is not made by private interpretation [n: (II Petr. I)]*. [var: Vulg. *quod omnis prophetia Scripturae*, "that no prophecy of Scripture"] Without doubt the interpretation of the holy Scriptures [1305B] is never properly made without the mastery of him who inspired them.
