## CHAPTER VII. *That toward the unravelling of the mysteries we advance better by devotion than by investigation, and that it belongs to true devotion to discuss all things, that it may be able to judge all things.*

If therefore we wish to be instructed unto the mystical understanding of the divine Scriptures, unto the learning of the divine judgments, we advance better toward this same thing by praying than by investigating, and we are illuminated more deeply by devout compunction than by profound searching. Whence it is that to those things which we have premised there is at once subjoined: *Until my colleague Daniel came in before me, whose name is Balthassar according to [1305C] the name of my God, who hath the spirit of the holy ones in himself [n: (Dan. IV)]*. [var: Vulg. Dan. IV:5 has *deorum* before *sanctorum*, "the spirit of the holy gods"; Richard restores the word in both of his own re-quotations below] Behold, he is said to have the spirit of the holy gods who is found fit for the interpretation of the dream. In this indeed, that these gods are called holy gods, those are assuredly excluded of whom it is sung in the psalm: *All the gods of the Gentiles are devils [n: (Psal. CXIII)]*. But assuredly they who are designated in this place. They are believed to have the Holy Spirit; because, if they had not the Holy Spirit, they would not be holy gods. He is perceived to be of the number of these gods to whom by the Lord's voice it is said: *Behold I have appointed thee the god of Pharao [n: (Exod. VII)]*. And so the Psalmist expressed gods of this kind, when in another place he said: *I have said: You are gods, and all of you sons of the Most High [n: (Psal. LXXXI)]*. Every [1305D] holy man receives the spirit of gods of this kind, that he may be able to be holy. For all our justification, all our sanctification, is without any doubt from that spirit. Without this spirit we know not what we should pray, as we ought; for we have learned, by the Apostle's teaching, that it is he who maketh intercession for us with unspeakable groanings [n: (Rom. VIII)]. [var: Vulg. Rom. VIII:26 reads *postulat*, "asketh"; *interpellat*, "maketh intercession", is the verb of Rom. VIII:34] That our devotion therefore may be able to be true, it must have this spirit. If therefore by Daniel true devotion is understood, rightly is it said of him through Nabuchodonosor: Who hath the spirit of the holy gods in himself. But since he expressed above certain wise men, and such as it truly stands are not truly wise, it is a wonder how Daniel is in this place said to be their colleague. For unless he has something in common with them, [1306A] I know not how he should stand as their colleague. But this I know, that that spirit which Daniel, according to the testimony of Scripture, had received, makes men spiritual. But the spiritual man judgeth all things, and he himself is judged of no man. For the Spirit is wont to search all things, even the deep things of God. If the spiritual man judgeth all things, if the spirit searcheth all things, then those same things also which worldly philosophy searches; then those same things also which carnal prudence searches. If all things, then those also which any perverse doctrine whatsoever searches. For he who is spiritual is wont to discuss all things, that he may be able to judge all things. Hear finally how that spiritual man tried all things in his own wisdom, that he might be able to judge all things: *I gave,* he says, *my heart, that I might know prudence and doctrine, and errors and folly [n: (Eccle. I)]*. Thou seest [1306B] how this man searches out, how he searches, not only good morals, but also errors, not only prudence, but also folly. And so it belongs to true devotion to bring both good things and evil into contemplation, to investigate all things, to discuss all things, that thereafter it may also be able to judge all things. And so true devotion has, out of investigation and searching, something in common with the wise men of Babylon, so that it is deservedly said on that account to be their colleague. Yet they differ in intention. For true devotion investigates empty and perverse doctrines not for this, that it may cleave to them or place any confidence in them, but that by judging it may convict, confute and condemn them. And so our Daniel and the wise men of the world run by the same way through investigation, although [1306C] through intention they do not reach the same issue. Rightly therefore is Daniel called their colleague, with whom in the courses of his own pursuits he seems in some part to share.

## CHAPTER VIII. *With what pursuits of devotion he ought to press on who awaits from God the revelation of hidden things.*

Until my colleague Daniel came in before me. Thou weighest from these things, as I judge, how this Daniel now enters from outward things to inward, now goes forth from inward things to outward. He is outside when our devotion, by meditating, by judging, by praying, is solicitous concerning outward things. He is within when it is occupied with the same exercises or [1306D] pursuits, and solicitous for inward things. For it must needs provide good things before God and before men. Before men in speech and in work, before God in thought and in will. And so to call our deeds and our words back to judgment, and to make supplication to the Lord for them, this is for Daniel to be outside. To search out thoughts, affections, wills, intentions with deep searching, and to be pricked with compunction in prayer for such things, this is for Daniel to abide within. Or this is for Daniel to be outside: to weigh both our inward and our outward things according to human judgment; and this is for Daniel to be within: to esteem both these and those according to the divine judgment. When, even amid the good works which we do, when amid the useful things [1307A] which we say, we weigh with subtle examination what men would think of us, what they ought to think, if they saw our intentions, if they saw our sordid thoughts, if they saw our depraved affections; when, I say, we do this, we hold Daniel as it were outside. But when it is sought with zealous discussion how our works or our words are judged in the sight of God, even then when according to human judgment they seem good, when such a pursuit is pressed with great devotion, our Daniel is found within. What therefore is it for Daniel to enter to the inner parts, but out of inmost devotion, in those things which are to be discussed or to be sought concerning outward things as well as inward, to have recourse to the divine judgment? And it often comes to pass, while our wise men fail in the interpretation [1307B] of dreams, while zealous meditations, while inmost searchings presume to penetrate the hidden things of the divine judgments, the deep things of the divine mysteries, and cannot; it comes to pass, I say, often, that the mind, after a failure of this kind, admonished by that very failure of its own, returns to itself, attends more diligently to its own infirmity, and more fully acknowledges that without God's help it can do nothing. Then it begins, out of the consideration of its own infirmity, to groan, to sigh, to reprove its own presumptions, and now to hope from the Lord's revelation alone for the things it had before emptily presumed upon out of its own pursuits, and to press wholly upon the zeal of devotion. And this is for Daniel to enter after the wise men.
