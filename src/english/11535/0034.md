## CHAPTER IX. *How great the virtue of true devotion is, which makes a Son of God, which joins the human spirit to God.*

[1307C]

Until my colleague, he says, Daniel came in before me, whose name is Balthassar. Balthassar is interpreted *hair of the head*. Rightly therefore is Daniel called Balthassar, because our mind is adorned out of true devotion, and out of true devotion is made subtle unto every depth. But in this place, when Daniel is called Balthassar, something is added that still lies hidden, whereby, as I judge, we are called forth to a deeper understanding. Daniel, he says, whose name is Balthassar, according to the name of my God. But this we now wish to forewarn in this place, that many places of the Scriptures sound something foolish or perverse according to the history, [1307D] which nevertheless, when mystically discussed, speak something right according to the spiritual understanding. For that Daniel is called Balthassar by the king according to the name of his god, what else does it seem to sound, according to the letter, than idolatry? But as blessed Gregory says: « Often any thing whatsoever is, through the history, a virtue, and through the signification a fault, just as sometimes a deed done is, in the fact, a cause of damnation, but in the writing, a prophecy of virtue. » For when he was treating of king David's fault, while he was treating of Urias's innocence, when he had wished to interpret Christ by the adulterous David, and by the innocent Urias the Jewish people: what, he says, through this effect can be said more wicked than David, what more clean than Urias? But again, through the mystery, what is found more sacred than David, what more faithless than Urias, when the one by [1308A] the fault of his life signifies the innocence of prophecy, and the other by the innocence of his life expresses in prophecy a fault? For the virtue of the sacred discourse so narrates things past that it may express things to come. So it approves a thing in the fact, that it may contradict it in the mystery. So it condemns things done, that it may persuade that these same things are to be done mystically. So also in this place assuredly, that which according to the history is reprehensible can, according to the mystery, express something commendable. Whose name, he says, is Balthassar according to the name of my God. If by the royal person we rightly understand the royal spirit, whom, I ask, does he call his God but Christ? For although the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit is truly one and the same God, yet every religious man rightly says, according to a certain manner of speaking, that Christ is his own: by whose name [1308B] he remembers that he was marked, by whose grace he was prevented, and to whose person, finally, he acknowledges that he cleaves more familiarly through the mystery of the Incarnation, out of the communion of nature. But if Balthassar is interpreted *hair of the head*, let us now see how this name may seem to agree with Christ, or how according to this name of his God the mystical Daniel is deservedly named. But we know that the head of the man is Christ, and the head of Christ, God. We know without doubt that God the Trinity is the beginning of all creatures; we know none the less that the person of the Father is the beginning of the other persons in the Trinity. And although it is called a beginning here and there according to one manner and another, yet in both places it can be called a beginning as fittingly [1308C] as truly. Since therefore the person of the Father is the beginning of all things, he can by right be designated by the head. If therefore the Holy Spirit is called by a likeness the finger of God, why should not Christ likewise be called by a likeness the hair of this head? The hair arises from the head, and Christ is begotten of the Father. The Lord said to me: *Thou art my Son, this day have I begotten thee [n: (Hebr. I)]*. The head is adorned with hair, and the Father is glorified through Christ: *Father,* he says, *glorify thy Son, that thy Son may glorify thee [n: (Joan. XVII)]*. Thou seest certainly how justly Christ is called Balthassar, that is, the hair of the head, whom the Catholic faith truly confesses to be the true Son of God the Father. Do not we also receive this name, when from Christ [1308D] and through Christ we obtain that we ought deservedly to be called sons of God? *Brethren,* says John the apostle, *we are the sons of God, and it doth not yet appear what we shall be [n: (I Joan. III)]*. [var: Vulg. I John III:2 opens *Carissimi, nunc*, "Dearly beloved, now"; and it reads *apparuit*, "hath appeared", for the present *apparet*] Hence it is that to him who of his own will begot us by the word of his power we say in our daily prayer: *Our Father who art in heaven [n: (Matth. VI)]*. If therefore Christ can fittingly enough be called Balthassar, because he is the Son of the highest Father, why should not the true Christian also be called Balthassar according to the said reason of the name, who according to the grace of adoption is reckoned among the sons of the same God the Father? Yet not every Christian is worthy of this name or of this honour. Devotion alone it is that distinguishes them; devotion alone makes sons of God. If therefore devotion is understood in Daniel, see how rightly he is called Balthassar in effect. Through whom [1309A] any man whatsoever is adopted into the liberty of the sons. But what else is devotion, but the fervent love of the mind toward God? *But the charity of God is poured forth in our hearts by the Holy Spirit, who is given to us [n: (Rom. V)]*. Rightly therefore is devotion called the hair of the head, which seems as it were to flow forth from the highest head of all, while it is begotten out of the inspiration of the highest Father. Rightly is he called Balthassar who after the manner of hair seems to cleave to the highest head of all. Thou weighest, as I judge, from these things how great is the virtue of true devotion, which makes a son of God, which joins the human spirit to God, and makes it with him as it were one spirit. *He who* indeed *cleaveth to God, is one spirit [n: (I Cor. VI)]*. [var: Vulg. I Cor. VI:17 reads *Domino*, "to the Lord", where the plate prints *Deo*]

## CHAPTER X *That most of all in the time of praying the intellectual sense is wont to be opened to certain men.*

[1309B]

Daniel having entered, the king speaks the dream before him, as is shown by the words that follow: *And the dream,* he says, *I spoke before him [n: (Dan. IV)]*. To speak a dream of this kind before Daniel is, in the time of praying, to be touched by the memory of the mysteries concerning which we desire to be divinely instructed, and while praying to be affected with the desire of divine revelation. For as those recognize by experience who have deserved to be divinely relieved and gladdened by frequent relievings, in the time of praying most of all are they wont to receive the light of illuminating grace, and out of its inspiration to penetrate the deep things of the mysteries. For out of inmost compunction [1309C] the spirit is washed and cleansed, and beyond doubt the purer it is rendered, the more easily, the more deeply is it illuminated. Hence it is that Balaam testifies of himself: *He hath said, the hearer of the words of God, who hath beheld the vision of God almighty, who falleth, and so his eyes shall be opened [n: (Num. XXIV)]*. [var: Vulg. Num. XXIV:4 has no *Dei* after *omnipotentis*, and reads *aperiuntur*, "are opened", for the future *aperientur*] What is it that is said, that by falling the eyes are opened, but that while praying the intellectual sense is often opened. We are indeed wont often to prostrate ourselves upon the earth with the whole body, when we wish to pray more attentively. So far therefore as it seems to look to the exposition of the letter, his eyes are opened as it were by falling who receives the light of revealing grace while he prostrates himself in prayer. But if the same letter is mystically discussed, it seems to run together into the same meaning. For what is it to fall, [1309D] but to humble oneself out of the remembrance of one's own sins, and out of true contrition to lay aside the rigidity of the heart? But the spirit often, while it casts itself down in this manner, rises again in a greater grace, because by falling it recovers the light of its own eyes, which, being ill upraised, it was holding closed. Yet we can say that this without doubt is to fall: to consent to a flattering fault. But we know that there are some who lift themselves up out of the multitude of their own revelations. But those who are of this kind are permitted, by the worthy judgment of God, to fall, that they may cease to be proud. While therefore they slip into fault, they are confounded, they are pricked with compunction; being pricked they are purged, being purged they are illuminated. But if, being illuminated, they are again proud, by being proud they lose the light which they had received. And so, being illuminated [1310A] and lifted up, they are cast down again and again; having fallen and been humbled, they are illuminated again and again. This vicissitude of falling and of rising again they often alternate, and many long and much frequent these manners of alternating. But many at length conclude this custom of alternating with a most evil end, of whom it stands that they received the grace of spiritual charisms not so much for their own advantage as for that of others. Of these without doubt Balaam bore the type by his own works, and expressed their property in his own words, when he says: He hath said, the hearer of the words of God, who seeth the vision of the Almighty, who falleth, and so his eyes are opened. [var: Vulg. Num. XXIV:4 has the perfect, "hath beheld", where this second printing prints the present *videt*, "seeth"; the printing at 1309C keeps the perfect]
