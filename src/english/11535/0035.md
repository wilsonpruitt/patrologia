## CHAPTER XI. *That the secular kinds of learning ought to be subservient to spiritual studies, and to serve as soldiers for the gains of true devotion.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1310B]

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But that we may return to that from which we digressed, we hold this for certain: that many often, in the time of their prayer, receive the light of revealing grace, and that many advance to grace of this kind out of the virtue of devotion. ⟦s2⟧ Hence it is that the king with all confidence demands of Daniel the interpretation of his dream, when he says: *Balthassar, prince of the soothsayers, whom I know that thou hast the spirit of the holy gods in thee, and every sacrament is not impossible to thee. ⟦s3+4⟧ Tell the visions of my dreams which I saw, and the solution of them [n: (Dan. IV)]*. [var: Vulg. *quoniam ego scio*, "forasmuch as I know"; the relative printed here leaves the clause without a governing verb] In these words this first is to be noted, that he is here called prince of the soothsayers who above was called their colleague, and not theirs only, [1310C] but of the other wise men of Babylon also. ⟦s5⟧ Among the secular kinds of learning we assuredly know some to be useful and necessary for use, but others (such as the art of soothsaying is) altogether perverse. ⟦s6⟧ If therefore true devotion can turn those which are without doubt perverse to some use of virtue, and can constrain within them a certain princedom of the spiritual soldiery, even as here we can gather from the art of soothsaying, what then is to be thought of those which can be useful and good? ⟦s7⟧ For, to say nothing of the rest, does not Daniel then hold the princedom, is not any perverse learning whatsoever compelled to be subservient to devotion, when from the consideration of it human error is more fully recognized, when from the going over of it again the spirit is humbled, confounded, pricked with compunction, and stirred up to the [1310D] zeals of devotion? ⟦s8⟧ Does not any perverse learning whatsoever serve devotion, when the spirit exacts of itself the times which it was wont to waste in useless studies of this kind, when from the comparison it gathers how much he ought to watch over spiritual studies who goes over again in his mind that he once sweated at follies so great? ⟦s9⟧ And so let Daniel be understood as the colleague of the said wise men in such wise, that he be believed to hold among them, by the right of equity, a certain princedom of the spiritual soldiery. ⟦s10⟧ All secular kinds of learning, therefore, ought to be subservient to spiritual studies, and to presume nothing in their exercises which does not serve as a soldier for the gains of true devotion. ⟦s11⟧ When therefore we press importunately upon the secular kinds of learning, when by the importunity of our studies we interrupt the exercises of our devotion, we assuredly compel him who ought to hold the princedom, [1311A] Daniel namely, to serve. ⟦s12⟧ But it must be attended to how great an evil it is to abuse even good kinds of learning, and by the abuse of them to have better studies hindered, when out of the office of devotion any perverse learning whatsoever ought, and is wont, to be bent to good uses.

## CHAPTER XII. *That it is useful to bestow the highest care upon the highest devotion.*

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Now rightly is devotion set before good studies, rightly does it hold the princedom over evil ones, rightly is the primacy among all the wise men of Babylon granted to Daniel, if that stands truly which is said through Nabuchodonosor: I know that thou hast the spirit of the holy gods in thee, and every sacrament is not impossible to thee. ⟦s2+3⟧ Does not [1311B] this seem to agree with that sentence, if it be rightly weighed, which is heard from the mouth of Truth: *Amen I say to you, whatsoever you shall have asked in praying, believe that you shall receive, and it shall be done unto you [n: (Marc. VI)]*. [var: Vulg. Mark 11:24 *Propterea dico vobis, omnia quaecumque orantes petitis … et evenient vobis*, "Therefore I say to you, all things whatsoever you ask … and they shall come unto you"] But that devotion is not true and full which cannot believe the attestation of Truth. ⟦s4⟧ But if it can believe, it can by asking and believing obtain. ⟦s5⟧ And so to the highest devotion no sacrament becomes impossible, if it can be obtained out of faith. ⟦s6⟧ Rightly therefore does the judgment of the spirit strive to obtain, out of the virtue of devotion, that which is proved by the testimony of Truth not to be impossible to it. ⟦s7+8⟧ Tell, he says, the visions of my dreams which I saw, and the solutions of them. [var: Vulg. and the lemma at 1310B print the singular, *solutionem*] The best kind of zeal, therefore, is to bestow the highest care upon the highest devotion, in that no sacrament, according to the reason demonstrated, [1311C] is impossible to it. ⟦s9⟧ According to the example, then, of king Nabuchodonosor, let us set our Daniel before any wise men of ours whatsoever; let us compel all our studies, all our exercises, to be subservient to the gains of true devotion; and then beyond doubt we shall not attempt in vain whatsoever we usefully presume, with God's help, concerning the divine mysteries out of his office. ⟦s10⟧ But now let us pursue the order of the vision, and let us search out the sense of the spiritual understanding, at least according to moral edification, leaving greater things to greater wits.

## CHAPTER XIII. *Of the manner, or the quality, of contemplating; and that things are seen otherwise in their own being, and otherwise in reason alone, or in the highest truth.*

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1311D] *The vision,* he says, *of my head in my bed. ⟦s2⟧ I was seeing, and behold a tree in the midst of the earth, and the height thereof excessive. ⟦s3⟧ A great tree, and strong, and the tallness thereof reaching unto heaven; the sight of it was even unto the ends of all the earth. ⟦s4⟧ The leaves thereof most beautiful, and the fruit thereof excessive, and in it the food of all. ⟦s5⟧ Under it dwelt the animals and the beasts, and in the branches thereof abode the birds of heaven, and of it all flesh did eat [n: (Dan. IV)]*. ⟦s6⟧ As is plain from the things above, the vision of the head is the understanding of the mind. ⟦s7⟧ The bed, moreover, is the tranquillity of the heart and the leisure of a zealous quiet. ⟦s8⟧ The vision of the head in the bed is divinely perceived when, amid the secret leisures of its zeal, the human mind is lifted up by divine inbreathing to the contemplation of truth. ⟦s9⟧ I was seeing, he says, and [1312A] behold a tree in the midst of the earth. ⟦s10⟧ It is one thing to see the things themselves in their own being, and another to see them in reason alone, or in the highest truth. ⟦s11⟧ It is one thing to see each of them in its own proper essence, and far another to see them in wisdom alone. ⟦s12⟧ It is one thing to see the substances of things in their genera and species, and another to see them singly in their individuals. ⟦s13⟧ The latter belongs to sense or to imagination, but that first one belongs to reason and to contemplation. ⟦s14⟧ When therefore the mind is caught up to a certain universal consideration of things, and is divinely lifted up to behold the beauty of the universe, and is delightfully fixed and wonderfully refreshed in a contemplation of this kind and in the sweetness of contemplation, it often sees in the divine light how many and of what sort the kinds of things must needs be, [1312B] and holds it unshaken that nothing of all those which the order of the highest disposition and the beauty of the universe require can be utterly lacking. ⟦s15⟧ For example, it sees that there must needs be good men and likewise evil, and among the good some better than others, and some best of all; and so among the evil, some worse than others, and some worst of all. ⟦s16⟧ It sees none the less that some must needs be evil and persevere in evil, others be evil and forsake malice for ever, and others forsake it for a time and return again to malice. ⟦s17⟧ So, conversely, it sees that some are good and persevere in good, that others fall irrecoverably, that others after a lapse return to goodness. ⟦s18⟧ Of this sixth kind of things he seems to be whom Nabuchodonosor contemplates in the light of God, who is shown to him by a likeness, at whom also, after [1312C] he had returned to himself, he wondered within himself, or even had pity. ⟦s19⟧ He was seeing that the beauty of the universe required such a one, and that such a one could by no means be lacking; and yet he knew not that he himself was such a one, or that he bore in himself the image of such a man as should fall suddenly from so great a sublimity, and lie long in the lowest places, and yet at last return to his former state. ⟦s20⟧ This is what it is that the great tree is first raised up on high, is afterward cut away, and yet its roots are kept in the earth, whence it may be repaired again. ⟦s21⟧ Let us therefore see, we also, and let us learn from him what kind of tree it is that he sees, or how it fell, or how that could fall which was so great and of such a kind.
