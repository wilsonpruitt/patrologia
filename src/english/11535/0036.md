## CHAPTER XIV. *Of the greatness of grace in a mind that is afterward to fall, and what is wont to be the cause of ruin amid so great a fullness of grace.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1312D]

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ I was seeing, he says, and behold a great tree in the midst of the earth, and the height thereof excessive. [var: Vulg. Dan. 4:7, and the lemma printed at 1311D, have *ecce arbor in medio terrae*, without "great"] What shall we understand by the greatness of the tree, but the immense strength of virtue? ⟦s3⟧ Was not that also a great tree which received the fullness of grace and of fortitude? ⟦s4⟧ *Stephen,* he says, *full of grace and fortitude [n: (Act. VI)]*. ⟦s5⟧ Behold a great tree in the midst of the earth, and the height thereof excessive. ⟦s6⟧ This tree, therefore, was great, was high, and was in the midst of the earth. ⟦s7⟧ It spread itself out to many and great things by virtue, it raised itself to high things by subtlety, and it stood in the inmost things by intention. ⟦s8⟧ It stretched itself out to strong things, it raised itself to the highest things, and it remained fixed in the inmost things. [1313A] It was in great things by exercise, in the highest by zeal, in the inmost by desire. ⟦s9⟧ Because therefore it did strong things, it was a great tree; because it desired inmost goods, it was in the midst of the earth; because it thought subtly of sublime things, its height was great. ⟦s10⟧ A great tree, he says, and strong, and the tallness thereof reaching unto heaven. ⟦s11⟧ Behold a great tree, a robust tree, a tall tree. ⟦s12⟧ Great for doing good things, strong for enduring evil things, tall for contemplating sublime things. ⟦s13⟧ Great in actions, strong in temptations, sublime in investigations. ⟦s14⟧ Consider how high it was, whose tallness even touched heaven. ⟦s15⟧ Its tallness touched heaven, because the sublimity of its investigation penetrated the heavenly things. ⟦s16⟧ And so its tallness was high, and its height was tall. ⟦s17⟧ Truly it was a great height [1313B] which plunged itself into the highest and heavenly things. ⟦s18⟧ Choose therefore, in the consideration of this tree, what thou mayest wonder at the more: its greatness, its strength, or its tallness. ⟦s19⟧ A great tree, he says, and strong, and the tallness thereof reaching unto heaven, A great tree it was, which could truly say: *Everywhere and in all things I am instructed. ⟦s20⟧ I know how to be humbled, I know also how to abound, and to suffer want. ⟦s21+22⟧ I can do all things in him who strengtheneth me [n: (Philipp. IV)]*. [var: Vulg. Phil. 4:12 *Scio et humiliari, scio et abundare (ubique et in omnibus institutus sum) et satiari, et esurire, et abundare, et penuriam pati*: the received text has *institutus*, "taught," for *instructus*, and sets the "everywhere and in all things" clause after the two verbs, not before them] A strong tree it was, which could confidently say: *I will not fear what man can do unto me. ⟦s23⟧ If armies in camp should stand together against me, my heart shall not fear [n: (Psal. LV)]*. ⟦s24⟧ *If a battle should rise up against me, in this will I hope [n: (Psal. XXVI)]*. ⟦s25+26⟧ Beyond the other trees it was tall, which doubted not to say of itself: *And I went beyond in wisdom all that were before me in Hierusalem, and [1313C] my mind hath contemplated many things wisely, and hath learned [n: (Eccle. I)]*. [var: Vulg. Eccl. 1:16 *et didici*, "and I have learned," in the first person; Migne's third person makes "my mind" the subject] Its tallness touched heaven, who could truly say: *We speak wisdom among the perfect, yet not the wisdom of this world, nor of the princes of this world that are brought to nought. ⟦s27⟧ But we speak the wisdom of God in a mystery, which is hidden, which he predestined before the worlds, which none of the princes of this world hath known [n: (II Cor. XXIX)]*. ⟦s28⟧ But see, whosoever thou art that hearest or readest these things, if perchance thy height is great; see, I say, and diligently attend, lest perchance it be also excessive. ⟦s29⟧ This perhaps was blameworthy in the tree here described, this perhaps stood as the cause of its cutting down, that its height was excessive. ⟦s30⟧ And the height thereof, he says, excessive. ⟦s31⟧ Perhaps Nabuchodonosor was walking in great things and in things wonderful [1313D] above himself, and this perhaps stood to him as the cause of his ruin. ⟦s32⟧ Perhaps Nabuchodonosor was minded of high things, and therefore his height was excessive: *Be not,* says the Apostle, *high-minded, but fear [n: (Rom. XI)]*. ⟦s33⟧ For as for him who eats much honey it is not good for him, so he who is a searcher of majesty shall be overwhelmed by glory. ⟦s34⟧ Seek not therefore the things that are higher than thee, lest perchance thy tallness be too high. ⟦s35⟧ The searcher of majesty is overwhelmed by glory, and that tree deserves the sentence of cutting down whose height is excessive. ⟦s36⟧ Were not trees of this kind high, and did they not justly deserve to be cut down, which, when they had known God, glorified him not as God, or gave thanks: *But they became vain in their thoughts, and their foolish heart was darkened [n: (Rom. I)]*.

## CHAPTER XV. *Of the greatness of authority in a mind that is afterward to fall into ruin.*

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1314A]

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But let us hear what is further said of this tree in that which follows: The sight of it was even unto the ends of all the earth. ⟦s2⟧ The sight of a tree of this kind reaches unto the ends of all the earth, when the dignity of his name who is of this kind is alike revered by those near him through likeness of morals and by those far off through unlikeness of morals, and his authorities are alike loved by the good and feared by the evil. ⟦s3⟧ Did not his sight stand forth even unto the ends of all the earth, of whom the truth of that sentence stood firm: *We are the good odour of Christ unto God in them that are saved, and in them that perish. ⟦s4⟧ To some indeed the odour of death unto [1314B] death, but to others the odour of life unto life [n: (II Cor. II)]*. ⟦s5⟧ Was not the sight of those trees also spread abroad even unto the ends of all the earth, of which it is truly said: *Their sound hath gone forth into all the earth, and their words unto the ends of the world [n: (Psal. LXXXI)]*. ⟦s6⟧ But according to a certain other sense also the sight of our tree in our own selves lies open even unto the ends of all the earth, when now the experience of his own virtue is hidden from no one of us, neither in great things nor in the least. ⟦s7⟧ For if by the tree we take virtue, and by the sight of the tree the cognizance of virtue, when we have now learned with much zeal and much exercise what we can do in each several matter, what else have we done than publish the sight of our tree even unto the ends of all the earth? ⟦s8⟧ Had he not published the sight of his tree [1314C] even unto the ends of all the earth, and had he not already begun the experience of his own virtue through all things, who truly affirms of himself that which we have already set down above. ⟦s9⟧ For I have learned in what things I am sufficient. ⟦s10⟧ *I know how to be humbled, I know also how to abound everywhere, and in all things I am instructed [n: (Philipp. VI)]*.
