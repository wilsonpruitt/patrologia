## CHAPTER XVI. *Of the multitude of learning in a mind that is to fall into ruin, and what is wont to be the cause of ruin amid so great a fullness of learning.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ There is further added concerning this tree: The leaves thereof most beautiful, and the fruit thereof excessive, and in it the food of all. ⟦s2⟧ In the leaves, words; in the fruit, learning; in the food, teaching. ⟦s3+4⟧ By the leaves the fruit is clothed, and by well-composed words [1314D] the sentence is adorned: *For the mouth of the wise adorneth learning [n: (Prov. XV)]*. [var: Vulg. Prov. 15:2 *Lingua sapientium*, "the tongue of the wise"] Good fruit has savour and feeds; and spiritual learning sweetly refreshes spiritual hunger by holy admonition. ⟦s5⟧ And so the most beautiful leaves are eloquent words; the copious fruit is an instructed sense, a manifold intellect. ⟦s6⟧ The food of all is the full instruction of all, the discreet admonition of each. ⟦s7⟧ Beautiful leaves of words, as I judge, that tree had, and it abounded in the fruits of good senses and of useful counsels, which could truly say: *They that heard me waited for my sentence, and being attentive held their peace at my counsel. ⟦s8⟧ To my words they durst add nothing, and my speech dropped upon them [n: (Job XXIX)]*. ⟦s9⟧ Copious fruit that tree had, which could say in truth: *But to me [1315A] God hath given to speak according to my mind, for he himself hath given me the true learning of those things which are: that I may know the disposition of the whole world, and the virtues of the elements; the beginning and the ending, and the midst of the times; the alterations of their courses, and the endings of the times; the changes of manners, and the divisions of times; the courses of the year, and the dispositions of the stars; the natures of animals, and the rages of beasts; the force of winds, and the thoughts of men; the differences of shrubs, and the virtues of roots; and whatsoever things are hidden and unforeseen, I have learned [n: (Sap. VII)]*. ⟦s10⟧ Was not the food of all in him who acknowledged himself a debtor unto this very thing, when he said: *To the Greeks and to the barbarians, to the wise and to the unwise, I am a debtor [n: (Rom. I)]*. ⟦s11⟧ Is not the food of all in him who by his teaching can edify [1315B] all? ⟦s12⟧ Has he not the food of all in himself, who by the spirit of counsel which presides over his mind, and by the grace of discretion in which he is strong, is sufficiently able to instruct with wholesome counsel and to raise up with kindly admonition those beset by any temptation whatsoever, those oppressed by any tribulation whatsoever? ⟦s13⟧ Does not he likewise, so far as pertains to himself, find the food of all in himself, who knows how by his own meditations and spiritual theories to nourish any holy desires whatsoever, and to inflame his own spirit to any good works whatsoever, as often and as much as he has willed? ⟦s14⟧ But, as we hinted above concerning the height of the tree, let us see whether perchance this also be blameworthy in it, that it is said to have excessive fruit. ⟦s15⟧ For it is not [1315C] said that its fruit was much, or its fruit copious, but its fruit, he says, excessive. ⟦s15⟧ He reproves excessive fruit who says, In superfluous matters search not manifoldly. ⟦s16⟧ It is one thing importunately to seek things impossible, though perchance they be useful, and another curiously to investigate things useless, though these also be possible. ⟦s17⟧ The former pertains to the excess of height, the latter pertains to the superfluity of fruits. ⟦s18⟧ The presumption of things incomprehensible is forbidden when it is said: *Seek not the things that are too high for thee, and search not out things stronger than thee, but the things which God hath commanded thee,* etc. [n: (Eccle. III.)] The curiosity of superfluous things is reproved in that it is said, In superfluous matters search not manifoldly, and in many of his works be not curious. ⟦s19⟧ Will you hear of trees [1315D] which are of this kind, which will not temper the boldness of presumption and of curiosity, how often they incur the sentence of cutting down: *For the suspicion of them,* he says, *hath supplanted many, and hath held their sense in vanity [n: (Eccli. III)]*.

## CHAPTER XVII. *Of the rigor of discipline which a mind that is to fall into ruin is often wont to exercise toward others.*

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ We have heard from what precedes of what learning he was who is designated by this tree; let us hear yet from what follows of what discipline he was. ⟦s2⟧ We have heard how by teaching he could either instruct the untaught or inform the ill-advised; let us hear how by discipline he is wont both to press down the carnal and to raise up the spiritual. [1316A] Under it dwelt the animals and the beasts, and in the branches thereof abode the birds of heaven, and of it all flesh did eat. ⟦s3⟧ By the animals and the beasts understand animal men, carnal and secular minds; by the birds take the spiritual, flying to heavenly things on the wings of good zeals and of holy desires. ⟦s4⟧ Now we are wont to see two kinds of carnal men. ⟦s5⟧ One indeed which serves concupiscence, the other which yields to wrathfulness. ⟦s6⟧ And so by the animals we understand minds given over to pleasure; by the beasts, wrathful minds. ⟦s7⟧ If by beasts we can fitly understand none but wild animals, it remains that under the name of animals we ought here to understand tame ones only. ⟦s8⟧ Rightly therefore are voluptuous minds designated by the animals, which serve the belly alone. [1316B] Rightly none the less are wrathful minds designated by the beasts, which rage with tooth and claw. ⟦s9⟧ Of such, therefore, it is rightly said: Under it dwelt the animals and the beasts, in that minds of this kind, dissolute through concupiscence or wrathfulness, ought indeed justly to be pressed down by the rigor of discipline. ⟦s10⟧ Rightly did the birds of heaven abide in the branches, and as it were in the higher places, in that spiritual men, kindled with heavenly desires, ought deservedly both to be raised into authority and to be exalted with worthy honor: Under it, he says, dwelt the animals and the beasts, and in the branches thereof abode the birds of heaven. ⟦s11⟧ Therefore it confined the bestial in the lowest places, but lifted the spiritual into the high places. ⟦s12⟧ Let our prelates learn, let them learn here what they ought to do; [1316C] let them learn, I say, to press down undisciplined morals, to lift up and to honor the good. ⟦s13⟧ What, I ask, is this kind of monstrous thing, that the trees of our time bear in their branches bears and lions, or any other bestial minds whatsoever of this sort, but press down in the lowest places the birds of heaven and any feathered flying creatures whatsoever, against the laws of their condition and the ordinances of the Creator? ⟦s14⟧ How often have I myself seen, and seeing have groaned over, the wicked man exalted above measure and lifted up above the cedars of Lebanon. ⟦s15⟧ What, I ask, is it that you do, who lift up on high brute and bestial hearts of this kind, and suspend them as it were in the air? ⟦s16⟧ If you truly love them, why do you not spare them? ⟦s17⟧ What, I ask, do you prepare for them but ruin, while you suspend them on high and bear them as it were in your branches? ⟦s18⟧ What else shall each man say to him who exalted him, or rather [1316D] to him who subverted him, at the end, in the very border of ruin, but that lifting me up thou hast dashed me down? ⟦s19⟧ Let the birds of heaven abide in the branches, and let those whom the wing of sanctity lifts up hold their place in the highest. ⟦s20⟧ Let the beasts hold their place beneath the branches in the lowest, and let those who have downward-bent faces through the appetite of the flesh feel the strictness of a devout severity and of a just humiliation. ⟦s21⟧ Under it dwelt the animals and the beasts, and in the branches thereof abode the birds of heaven, and of it all flesh did eat. ⟦s22⟧ Of that tree assuredly does all flesh eat, by whose examples of virtues all are edified, by whose consolations all are cherished, by whose benefits all are strengthened and are refreshed with teaching. ⟦s23⟧ O what a tree, O of how great a sublimity, to which so great a proclamation of praise belongs!
