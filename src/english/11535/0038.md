## CHAPTER XVIII. *Of the rigor of discipline which a mind that is to fall into ruin is wont to exercise upon itself.*

[1317A]

But this very thing also which is said of this tree we fulfil in our own selves, if we restrain carnal affections under the rigor of discipline, and occupy our thoughts in the circumspection of our morals, and through spiritual meditations nourish all our senses in good desires. For what do we understand by the flying creatures but subtle thoughts, and what by the fourfooted and earthly creatures but carnal affections? By the animals, those desires which arise beside reason; by the beasts, those which strive against reason: beside reason, the vain and frivolous; against reason, the perverse and hurtful. By the tree, as has been [1317B] said, we take the strength of fortitude, and rightly by the branches of the tree we understand the works and the exercises of virtue. And from branches of this kind we gather in their own season the fruits of good merits, according as we have heard through the Prophet: *Thou shalt eat the labours of thy hands, blessed art thou, and it shall be well with thee [n: (Psal. CXXVII)]*. From branches of this kind come forth the fruits of wholesome counsels and of subtle intellects, when through many trials the exercises of the virtues instruct a man unto the perfection of discretion and of learning. For many things which we cannot attain by investigation we learn more fully and more certainly by experience; hence that saying of the Psalmist: *By thy commandments I have understood [n: (Psal. CXVIII)]*. He understands from the commandments who, from that [1317C] which he does, advances to a greater understanding. Hence it is that elsewhere it is written: *In the intellects of his hands he led them [n: (Psal. LXVII)]*. He leads another in the intellects of his hands who directs him by sure steps of advancement to the way of truth which he has learned through the trials of works. Small and of little certainty is that learning which the experience of things does not help and confirm: hence it is that it is said through the Wise Man: *He that is not experienced knoweth few things [n: (Eccle. XXXIV)]*. But not only from the good things which we do, but also from the evils which we endure, are we instructed unto discretion, and from the very temptations we are made more prudent. Of this that voice of the Wise Man admonishes us, which says: *He that hath not been tempted, what doth he know? [n: (Ibid.)]* [var: Vulg. Sir. 34:11 *Qui tentatus non est, qualia scit?*, "what manner of things doth he know?"] A man experienced in many things hath thought many things, and he who [1317D] hath learned many things shall declare intellect. [var: Vulg. Sir. 34:9 *cogitabit*, future, "shall think many things"] And so from the branches of the tree fruit is gathered, because from much exercise of the virtues learning shall be multiplied. And the more perfect and the more approved each man is, the more abundant fruits he begets, and the more delightfully he refreshes. *But for the perfect,* says the Apostle, *is solid food, for them who by custom have their senses exercised unto the discerning of good and evil [n: (Hebr. V)]*. Good indeed, and branches of a good tree, are those which bear such fruit and beget the food of souls; which receive the flying creatures of heaven and suspend sublime thoughts on high; which cover the beasts and press down the assaults of the flesh. I think that the Apostle too was pressing down the animals beneath branches of this kind, when he said: *I chastise my body, and bring it into subjection [n: (I Cor. IX)]*. But he who [1318A] would accustom the birds of heaven to the branches of a tree of this kind, let his will be in the law of the Lord, and in his law let him meditate day and night.

Let us beware, therefore, of making our nest in the earth after the manner of certain birds, or of living in watery places, and of forsaking the branches of the tree; and let us be ashamed to cleave to worldly cares and to the desires of the flesh by the importunity of unremitting thoughts: *Mind the things that are above,* says the Apostle, *not the things that are upon the earth [n: (Coloss. III)]*. And so our birds ought for a season to be lifted up to heavenly things, and to be suspended in the contemplation of eternal things, and again, after their sublime flights, to return to the branches of their own tree, and there to tarry, nay even to dwell. For what else is it for the birds of heaven always to return to the branches of their own tree, and to inhabit there, [1318B] but for the thoughts of one's own heart frequently to be called back to the consideration of one's own morals and works, and to press the more often upon the circumspection of them, and to lean upon it the more vehemently? Hence it is that it is said through Solomon: *The sun riseth and setteth, and returneth to his own place [n: (Eccle. I)]*; for the sun of the understanding, after its rising and after the ascent of sublime contemplation, returns again to its own place, and the eye of the heart is called back to the circumspection of itself, that it may again be lifted up to higher things. For the heart which sleeps as to the keeping of itself quickly loses the grace of contemplation which it had received. Rightly therefore is it said of this tree, and in the branches thereof abode the birds of heaven, in that the circumspections of the mind ought chiefly to be watchful about the custody of themselves. Behold, we have now [1318C] heard what we ought to think concerning a tree of this kind. Yet let no one suppose that I believe king Nabuchodonosor to have been such as he hears me describe out of the mystical signification: for neither was he such in his own person, but out of his own person he prefigured such a man, and in the tree he saw he designated such a one. But let us meanwhile pursue the mystical description, and we shall find perhaps in it what we may justly wonder at; and not only what we ought to admire, but rather what we may worthily bewail, and what we ought deservedly to fear concerning ourselves.

## CHAPTER XIX. *That upon any summit of the virtues whatsoever one ought always to fear the ruin of morals.*

Let us hear therefore what he further says of this tree, and let each man learn and groan at what he ought to fear: [1318D] *I was seeing in the vision of my head upon my bed, and behold a watcher, and a holy one came down from heaven, and cried out mightily, and said thus: Cut down the tree and cut off its branches; shake off its leaves and scatter its fruit. Let the beasts flee which are under it, and the birds from its branches [n: (Dan. IV)]*. [var: Vulg. Dan. 4:11 *dispergite fructus ejus*, plural, "scatter its fruits"; the exposition at 1321C–D follows the singular printed here] Woe to the sons of Adam, woe to the human condition. What, I ask, how much soever he shall have advanced, how much soever he shall have ascended, will he be able to be secure for the rest, if so great a man and one so perfect could fall from so great a sublimity, and that as it were within the moment of a single hour? Let us call to mind, therefore, of what sort he is described above out of the mystical tree who here is bidden to be cut off, and then we shall the better recognize how justly the ruin of morals [1319A] is feared by those who are set upon any height of virtue whatsoever. In the greatness of the tree we wonder at the measure of fortitude; in its height, at the profundity of subtlety; in the spreading of its sight, at the greatness of authority. In the beauty of its leaves, at the eloquence of speech; in the excess of its fruits, at the multitude of instruction; in the food of all, at the teaching of edification; in the subjection of the beasts, at the rigor of severity; in the cohabitation of the birds, at the providence of circumspection. Who therefore, unless he be mad, promises himself security amid so great a hazard of mutability, if he was truly once such a one who afterward was cut down and fell? Truly, as the Scripture says: *Blessed is the man who is always fearful. For he knoweth not what the day to come may bring forth [n: (Prov. XXVIII)]*. [var: Vulg. Prov. 27:1 *ignoras enim quid superventura pariat dies*, "for thou knowest not what the day to come may bring forth"; the second clause is not part of Prov. 28:14, to which the note points]
