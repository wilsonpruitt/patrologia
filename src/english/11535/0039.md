## CHAPTER XX. *That whatsoever is done concerning us is dispensed by God's disposition and by angelic administration.*

[1319B]

Yet let no one think that God has no care for human things, let no one dare rashly to reprehend his judgments, when he sees or hears that something has happened beside expectation, or against reason, as it seems to him. Behold, he by whom the sentence of cutting down is given or dispensed is said to have come down from heaven and to have been holy. Behold, he says, a holy watcher came down from heaven. [var: Vulg. Dan. 4:10 and the lemma at 1318D read *vigil, et sanctus*, two subjects, "a watcher, and a holy one"] When therefore thou hearest that the minister of this work comes from heaven, understand that this is done by God's disposition; and when thou readest that he is holy, believe that he justly executes that which is just. Behold, a watcher and a holy one came down from heaven. About the care of men both the good angels and the evil expend a perpetual solicitude, and neither by night nor by day do they withdraw themselves even for a little from this [1319C] solicitude; but the evil are watchful for assailing, the good for protecting, so that both may rightly enough be called watchers. But that it is a good angel who either learned this sentence from heaven or disposed it on earth is sufficiently shown from this, that he is reported to be holy and to have come down from heaven. O how rightly does this name befit the good angels, upon whom the sleep of error or of forgetfulness never creeps as touching the business of our salvation! O height of angelic devotion! They who are secure concerning themselves yet bear solicitude for us. Of this watch of the angels about our salvation, and of their watchful custody, is that apostolic sentence: *Are they not all ministering spirits, sent in ministry [1319D] for them who receive the inheritance of salvation? [n: (Hebr. I.)]* [var: Vulg. Heb. 1:14 *qui haereditatem capient salutis*, future, "who shall receive the inheritance of salvation"; the received text also runs *Nonne omnes sunt administratorii spiritus*] What else, therefore, is it for watchers of this kind to come down from heaven, but for the ministers of our salvation to have a care of earthly things, and, according to what they divinely learn in the highest things, to dispose human affairs also in the lowest? Rightly therefore is it said: Behold, a watcher and a holy one came down from heaven, and cried out mightily; and said thus: Cut off the tree. [var: Vulg. Dan. 4:11 and the lemma at 1318D read *Succidite arborem*, "Cut down the tree"] And so some things there are which the good angels do by themselves, others which they permit to be done, nothing at all is anywhere done without their disposition. Good things therefore are done by their cooperation, but evil things by their permission. Good things therefore are administered through the good angels, evil things through the evil. Since he knew that to overthrow, as regards the state of rectitude, was an evil, the good angel was unwilling to cut off the tree; nevertheless, because he knew that the merit of this one man, [1320A] or the good of the universe, required it, he permitted it to be done. For in that he says, Cut off the tree, understand a permission and not a precept. For herein obedience is not enjoined upon the malignant spirits, but licence is granted. This at length he permitted them at some time to do, which he was not ignorant that they had long and much desired. Hence it is that it is said by the Lord to Judas the betrayer: *That which thou dost, do quickly [n: (Joan. XIII)]*. For herein a precept is not enjoined upon him, but the faculty of acting is conceded.

Now it is known to all that we are wont to use the outcry of a great voice as often as we would make something known to those set far off. But what stands farther apart one from another than an angelic spirit and a malignant spirit, not so much by the distance of place as by the [1320B] diversity of will? Hence it is that that prodigal son, who is read to have wasted all his substance in living riotously with harlots, is reported in the Gospel to have gone away into a far country [n: (Luc. XV)], in that, made distant by the depravation of his will, he is estranged from the good pleasure of the supreme Father. According to this manner, therefore, they are assuredly far distant from one another, distinguished and disjoined by a lot so various and so contrary: an angel confirmed in good, an angel confirmed in evil; an angel deputed to glory, and an angel consigned to Gehenna. Rightly therefore is that watcher and holy one reported to have cried out mightily, that from this it might be openly given to understand of what kind those angels were, and how far removed from a good angel they might be seen to be, to whom a sentence of the watchers, dictated from heaven, is made known not without a mighty outcry. [1320C] Yet there is among certain of the good angels also a great distance, a difference not of contrary will but of supereminent dignity, becomingly ordained of God. Whence neither does it seem incongruous if some outcry is described among them, whereby the difference of their dignity may be denoted. Rightly therefore is the angel who is here brought in as having conceded to the malignant spirits the licence of tempting reported not only to have cried out, but to have cried out mightily. Behold, he says, a watcher, and a holy one came down from heaven and cried out mightily, and thus said: Cut down the tree.

## CHAPTER XXI. *That the malignant spirits can do nothing against us save by God's permission.*

[1320D]There are two things which from the present passage we can plainly gather. The first, that the malignant spirits cannot tempt us save so far as they are permitted. The second, that if they were let loose for our assailing according to all their power, no one could endure their onset. For since they who can and will cut down the tree yet do not cut it down unless permitted, it plainly appears that without God's permission they presume nothing in their persecution of us. But what would they presume upon men without God's permission, who, as the Gospel attests, dared not invade even the herd of swine without divine licence? [n: (Matth. VIII; Marc. V; Luc. VIII.)] So much licence, therefore, is let loose to them in the assailing of the elect as their profit [1321A] requires, or as justice deserves. For what human virtue could sustain their onset, if, having received licence from God, they were able straightway to overthrow a tree so great and of such a kind? But in this the apostle Paul consoles us: *Faithful,* he says, *is the Lord, who suffereth you not to be tempted above that which you are able, but will make also with the temptation issue, that you may be able to sustain it [n: (I Cor. X)]*. [var: Vulg. 1 Cor. 10:13 *Fidelis autem Deus est, qui non patietur vos tentari*, "God is faithful, who will not suffer you to be tempted"] Unless therefore that tree had been too high, and had swollen concerning its own virtue, and presumed too much, it would not have received from God the sentence of cutting down. Consider now a great tree, and one presuming too much upon the abundance of its own fortitude: *I said,* he says, *in my abundance, I shall never be moved [n: (Psal. XXIX)]*. But straightway, from the withdrawal of grace and the temptation that came upon it, it learned [1321B] what, or how much, it could of itself: *Thou hast turned away,* he says, *thy face from me, and I became troubled [n: (ibid.)]*. And so the tree is cut down when the virtue of constancy is little by little worn away by temptations, and at last is utterly taken away. Was not the strength of that tree cut down which said: *My virtue hath forsaken me, and the light of my eyes, even that is not with me [n: (Psal. XXXVII)]*. Was not its virtue withdrawn, and the tree, being cut down, fallen to the lowest, when at the regard of a single concupiscence it fell into adultery, and from adultery into homicide?

## CHAPTER XXII. *How, or in what order, the human mind is wont to fall to ruin upon the withdrawal of grace.*

[1321C]When the tree is cut down, the branches also are cut off; because, as the virtue of the spirit fails, good works are little by little withdrawn. The tree is cut down and the branch is cut off, when virtue fails in secret and the work of virtue in the open. First therefore the virtue of the spirit is withdrawn, afterward the faculty also and the occasion of doing well is taken away; and, the strength of the tree being cut down and the branches cut off, the leaves are shaken off and the fruit is scattered; because, as the purpose of the virtues and the exercise of the virtues fail, first the grace of speaking well is taken away, and at last the grace of understanding rightly also is withdrawn. Rightly indeed, after the zeal of good working fails, is the eloquence of speech withdrawn, because every chastisement or exhortation of a depraved preacher can scandalize his hearers rather than edify them, since to him as he speaks [1321D] anyone may silently answer: Why then dost thou not thyself do these things? But to the sinner God hath said: Why dost thou declare my justices, and take my testament in thy mouth? The grace of the leaves is shaken off when, from an infamous suspicion first entertained, the confidence or the authority of speaking is lost. The fruit is scattered when his teaching is despised, which, although through its sense or its counsel it may perchance seem useful, yet on account of the depraved example of his life is easily trodden underfoot. The fruit is scattered when, by the importunate incursion of various concupiscences, the zeal of meditation is dissipated, and the subtlety of investigating and the facility of understanding are confounded by the wandering of thoughts. Behold how easily, behold in what order that mind is cast down from on high which, its merits requiring it, is deserted by divine grace, [1322A] which by divine permission is exposed to the malignant exactors. Rightly therefore is it said of this tree: Shake off its leaves, and scatter its fruit.
