## CHAPTER XXIII. *How the mind, after a foul casting down of itself, is wont afterwards both to be despised by others, and to be dissolved into itself.*

But when a man has begun to lose the branches of the virtues and the fruit of counsel, and to have not at all either uprightness of life or the authority of teaching, who now among carnal men would deign to obey him, or who among spiritual men to cleave to him through familiarity? Rightly, therefore, is that added which is still said of this tree: Let the beasts flee which are under it, and the birds from its branches. For the beasts which before were under it [1322B] flee away, when his own subjects refuse obedience to him, and all carnal men despise his mastery. For the birds of heaven to flee from its branches is for all spiritual men to shudder at the infamy of that life, and to shun its fellowships. Did not the younger men abominate the shady coverts of that great tree to which, according to the Lord's testimony, there was none like upon the earth, in that he was truly a great man among all the men of the east, and the first among the chief; did not, I say, the younger men abominate the shady coverts of that great tree, and did not the beasts now disdain to be under it, when he truly protested of himself: *But now they that are younger in time deride me, whose fathers I did not deign to set with the dogs of my flock. The strength of whose hands was [1322C] to me as nothing, and they were thought unworthy of life itself (Job XXX)*. What, I ask, were the elders doing, if the younger, and men so vile, derided him, in that so great a tree had been cut down by the Lord's permission, and smitten with a most grievous ulcer from the sole of the foot even to the crown? How great also was that tree to which the Lord bore testimony, that he had found a man according to his own heart. Did not the birds of heaven dwell in its branches, when he truly protests of himself: *that the innocent and the upright have cleaved to me? [n: (Psal. XXIV.)]* [var: Vulg. Ps. 24:21 begins *Innocentes et recti adhaeserunt mihi*; the *quia* Migne prints inside the quotation is the conjunction binding it to *protestetur*] Had they not also at that time fled away from its branches, when again in prayer he cried out: *Thou hast put my acquaintance far from me, they have made me an abomination to themselves [n: (Psal. LXXXVII)]*; and again: *Thou hast removed far from me friend and neighbour, and my acquaintance because of misery [n: (ibid.)]*. [1322D] But then in any man of virtue is fulfilled what is written of this tree, that, the strength of the wood being cut down, the beasts dwelling beneath flee away, when, the vigour of the spirit being broken and drained by temptations, the mind is no longer strong enough to check carnal onsets, or to coerce any irrational desires under the mastery of reason. We lose the dwelling together of the birds of heaven, when, disturbed by the onrush of so many phantasms, we are not strong enough to practise the pleasant spectacles of sublime investigations. The tree loses the birds that dwell in its branches, when it can no longer practise its meditations about the accustomed circumspection of its own morals. We often grieve that we have lost the companionship of such birds, when we are not strong enough to restrain our thoughts from unfitting wandering, so that each one [1323A] of us may rightly say within himself that word of blessed Job, when he now sees his own birds to have flown away, not without grief of his own: *My thoughts are dissipated, tormenting my heart [n: (Job VII)]*.

## CHAPTER XXIV. *That the divine mercy often both reserves unto repentance those who have basely fallen and been cast down from on high, and waits for their correction.*

But how merciful a thing is this, and how worthy of great wonder, that the divine mercy often neither utterly forsakes such men, nor after such a fall and so great a ruin wholly abandons them, but still often waits for such men unto correction, and reserves them unto repentance. Woe to thee, whosoever thou art that dost catch thyself to be such a one, if thou despisest the riches of his goodness, and of his patience, and of his longsuffering. [1323B] Or knowest thou not that the kindness of God leadeth thee to repentance, who has not yet cut away the roots of thy hope, but has hidden them in the secret place of his judgments, and as it were in the bosom of the earth? Let us hear, therefore, how that height of the divine mercy tempers the sentence of its own severity, when after the waste of so great a scattering it still adds something concerning this tree for our consolation: *Nevertheless,* he says, *leave the shoot of its roots in the earth.* This is that which we neither can nor ought to pass by without wonder at the divine kindness, in him who bids the shoot of its roots be left in the earth, whence it may again be repaired, and reformed to its former fullness. Nevertheless, he says, leave the shoot of its roots in the earth. Then is the tree as it were plucked up by the roots, when [1323C] after evils have been perpetrated it is hurled headlong even into despair. For the tree loses as it were its own roots, when it loses all hope of correction, and falls into despair. That tree had been plucked up by the roots, and had remained in its falling without any shoot of repair, which, speaking to the Lord and despairing of pardon, said: *My iniquity is greater than that I may deserve pardon [n: (Gen. IV)]*. For what else are the shoot, or the root, of a tree of this kind, but the purpose of correction and the hope of the divine propitiation? For every good work germinates out of a good purpose, that it may break forth into effect and grow strong unto the sturdiness of good habit; and in vain does the tree of virtue strive after other things, which is not rooted in the hope of the divine propitiation: *For [1323D] it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that sheweth mercy [n: (Rom. IX)]*. What else, then, is it for the shoot of the roots to remain to a tree of this kind after its ruin, than for anyone after the fall of sin to have a purpose of repentance and to hope for the fruit of repentance from the divine mercy, so far that, feeling concerning the Lord in goodness, he may dare, and say: *He will not be angry unto eternity, neither will he threaten unto eternity? [n: (Psal. CII.)] For when he shall have been angry, he will remember mercy [n: (Habac. III)]*. [var: Vulg. Ps. 101:9 *Non in perpetuum irascetur*, "not for ever", and the clause is a statement; chunk 0014 prints the same verse at 1262B with *in perpetuum* and no question mark] [var: Vulg. Hab. 3:2 *cum iratus fueris, misericordiae recordaberis*, second person, "when thou shalt be angry, thou wilt remember mercy"] How many do we daily see who, amid the outrages which they assiduously commit, do not lose the hope and the purpose of coming to their senses, and who purpose not only to put away their sins, nay rather to leave all things that are of the world, and to come to an order and to religion. What else do these retain than the roots of a good shoot? Whence, [1324A] when the time of showing mercy shall have come according to the divine good pleasure, they sprout forth unto good zeal. But others, when they are admonished concerning conversion, affirm even with an oath that they will never come to an order or to religion, and when they are rebuked concerning their sins, asseverate under solemn oath that they cannot restrain themselves from their own pleasures. They therefore who are of this kind, who have lost the hope and the purpose of correction, have they not, together with the tree of virtue, lost also the shoot of the roots? Against which it is said of this tree: Nevertheless leave the shoot of its roots in the earth.
