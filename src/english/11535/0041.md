## CHAPTER XXV. *To how great depravity the mind is sometimes hardened, which nevertheless is divinely reserved unto pardon.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ It is truly a wonder how at a single outrage [1324B] many often fall at once into despair. ⟦s2⟧ Others do not, even after many and very great and long-continued evils, lose the hope or the purpose of coming to their senses. ⟦s3⟧ And of this is rightly understood that also which is here still brought in concerning this tree, namely, that *it be bound with a bond of iron and of bronze among the grasses which are without, and let it be dipped in the dew of heaven, and let its portion be with the wild beasts in the grass of the earth. ⟦s4⟧ Let his heart be changed from man's, and let the heart of a beast be given him [n: (Dan. IV)]*. ⟦s5⟧ From these things assuredly, as I judge, thou weighest how great the evils are; but hear yet how long-continued they are: *And let seven,* he says, *times be changed over him.* ⟦s6⟧ For what is more rightly understood in the bond of iron and of bronze, than the habit of untameable perversity? ⟦s7⟧ In the iron, audacity; in the bronze, obstinacy; in the grasses of the earth, the lowest goods, earthly goods. ⟦s8⟧ For what are these earthly [1324C] goods, which are common to us with the beasts, but certain grasses of the earth. ⟦s9⟧ Of which it is rightly said: which are without. ⟦s10⟧ For by that very thing they exist without, in that they are bodily and carnal, and not spiritual. ⟦s11⟧ For the inward goods are spiritual, but the outward are bodily. ⟦s12⟧ Those belong to the inner man, these to the outer man. ⟦s13+14⟧ Those, then, are invisible, but these visible; those eternal, these temporal: *For the things which are seen,* says the Apostle, *are temporal, but the things which are not seen, eternal [n: (II Cor. IV)]*. [var: Vulg. closes *quae autem non videntur, aeterna sunt*; Migne's printing drops the second *sunt*] But those belong to the mind, and these to the flesh. ⟦s15⟧ But all flesh is hay, and all the glory thereof as the flower of hay. ⟦s16⟧ When therefore the depraved mind has begun to incline itself to lowest goods of this kind, and as it were in the grass of the earth to sate its carnal desires, [1324D] and to bring delights of this kind into habitual use, then, as the crooked habit little by little grows strong, it often comes to pass at last that it can no longer temper its own appetite away from the love or the use of them. ⟦s17⟧ The depraved mind, therefore, is bound as it were with a bond of iron in the grass of the earth, when out of a crooked habit it is hardened in the love of carnal pleasures. ⟦s18⟧ Because iron tames all other metals, it rightly, as has been said, designates audacity, which reveres no one, dreads no one. ⟦s19⟧ And since bronze is more durable than all the rest, we have already said that obstinacy is rightly designated by it. ⟦s20⟧ The crooked habit, therefore, grows hard as it were in the rigidity of iron, or also of bronze, when the depraved mind presses upon its accustomed desires as obstinately as audaciously. ⟦s21⟧ Is not that mind bound with a bond of iron and of bronze which is turned away [1325A] from its accustomed concupiscences by the terror of no man ever, by the admonition of no man ever? ⟦s22⟧ Does it not already feel bonds of iron or of bronze, which neither yields to threats, nor acquiesces in counsels? ⟦s23⟧ It stands firm, therefore, that minds of this kind can in no wise be corrected, save assuredly in the power of him who, as often as he willed, has ground down the gates of bronze and shattered the bars of iron. ⟦s24⟧ With gates, surely, we block the passage of those going forth, but with bars we fortify and make fast the closure itself. ⟦s25⟧ What, then, do we understand by the gate but contradiction, and what by the bar but rebellion? ⟦s26⟧ And we know indeed that for certain men it is not enough to contradict the admonitions or the mandates of their prelates, but when they cannot refute or turn aside their ordinances by words, they strive also to withstand them by deeds, and to rebel against them by works, [1325B] and as it were to fortify the gates of their contradiction with the bars of rebellions. ⟦s27⟧ If therefore, as has already been said above, by bronze is rightly understood obstinacy, and by iron audacity, what else will the gate of bronze be, but obstinate contradiction, and what the bar of iron, but audacious rebellion? ⟦s28⟧ What else, then, is it for the Lord to shatter the gates of bronze and the bars of iron, than to coerce obstinate contradiction and audacious rebellion by an inward inbreathing and by compunction of heart? ⟦s29⟧ It belongs, therefore, to God alone, as has been said, to grind down this so great hardness of heart, and to soften it unto repentance and humiliation. ⟦s30⟧ When therefore anyone, according to the aforesaid manner, is bound with a bond of iron and of bronze among the grasses which are without, let the loosing of so great a hardening [1325C] and binding be looked for from the mercy of God alone.

## CHAPTER XXVI. *How the mind, which is depraved in its morals, is afterwards imbued with errors or deceits.*

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But there is still added, to the increase of the evil, that which is joined on: And let it be dipped in the dew of heaven. ⟦s2⟧ What do we understand by the dew of heaven, but the subtle and scarcely intelligible suggestions of the devil? ⟦s3⟧ For if by the earth the body is fittingly enough designated, why should not the spirit be equally rightly designated by heaven? ⟦s4⟧ In comparison with bodies, therefore, those spiritual substances in the demons can be called heavens. ⟦s5⟧ And in the Gospel they have been called birds of heaven, not on account of justice, not on account of glory, but on account of their angelic and spiritual nature. [1325D] The depraved mind, therefore, is dipped in the dew of heaven, when through the suggestions of the devil it is imbued with divers errors. ⟦s6⟧ For out of the exultation of its own concupiscences it often runs into so great a darkness of errors, that it calls evil good, and good evil, putting darkness light and light darkness, putting bitter for sweet and sweet for bitter. ⟦s7⟧ He therefore who is bound without among the grasses of the earth must needs be dipped in the dew: for after the depravation of morals, the sense also is depraved and is easily entangled in many errors. ⟦s8⟧ Hence it is that in the overthrow of Jerusalem the king first lost his sons, and afterwards his eyes [n: (IV Reg. XXV)]. ⟦s9⟧ He assuredly loses spiritual sons, who loses the offspring of the virtues which he had long nourished. ⟦s10⟧ But when the sons have been lost, the light of the eyes is plucked out, because often from the depravation of the wills the keenness of understanding [1326A] is overdrawn and beclouded. ⟦s11⟧ He therefore who loses the desires of the heavenly, who wraps himself in the love of earthly things, whom the bond of cupidity binds fast as it were by a pasturing upon grasses, must needs at some time run into the darkness of errors, and, as the clouds of heaven drop down, infect himself with a certain dew of seduction. ⟦s12⟧ Does not he also seem to thee to be dipped in the dew of heaven, who by the suggestion of malignant spirits is trained unto every deceit, so that after their likeness he may willingly will, and easily be able, to deceive his neighbours and to circumvent them by frauds? ⟦s13⟧ For without doubt, when an excessive love of earthly things begins to reign, it little by little brings in fraudulence, but at the last it drives on to violence and rapine. ⟦s14⟧ Whence it is that he subjoins, and let its portion be with the wild beasts in the grass of the earth.

## CHAPTER XXVII. *How the mind is sometimes let loose unto every cruelty, though it be nevertheless divinely reserved unto repentance.*

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1326B]

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ And it is a wonder indeed, yet none the less true, that the divine disposition permits some to be let loose unto the highest cruelty, whom nevertheless it disposes to reserve unto repentance, inasmuch as it has preordained them unto eternal life. ⟦s2⟧ It is said, therefore, of a man of this kind: And let his portion be with the wild beasts in the grass of the earth. ⟦s3⟧ By the wild beasts, which rage with tooth and claw (whence also from their fierceness they are called ferocious), all cruel men can rightly enough be understood. ⟦s4⟧ Of such the Psalmist: *The sons of men,* he says, *their teeth are weapons and arrows, [1326C] and their tongue a sharp sword [n: (Psal. LVI)]*. ⟦s5⟧ By the grass of the earth we understand the delights of a flesh that is to die and the glory of a life that passes. ⟦s6⟧ For all flesh is hay, and all the glory thereof as the flower of hay. ⟦s7⟧ They therefore seem to have a portion with the cruel, and as it were with the wild beasts in the grass of the earth, who for the sake of avarice and ambition are let loose unto rapine and deceit and unto every cruelty. ⟦s8⟧ For what is it to have a portion with the ministers of cruelty, and with wild planks, but to receive a lot with such companions in the due of retributions, even as in the likeness of morals? ⟦s9⟧ But on the contrary the Prophet utterly shunned to have the fellowships of such men, or to receive a portion with them, when he said: *I have hated the church of the malignant, and with the wicked I will not sit [n: (Psal. C)]*. ⟦s10⟧ Whence also [1326D] elsewhere: *The crooked heart hath not cleaved to me [n: (ibid.)]*. ⟦s11⟧ Let his heart be changed from man's. ⟦s12⟧ The heart of a soul that prevaricates and rushes into the depths is changed from man's, when all affection of humanity and of mercy is utterly lost, when out of the love of temporal things and out of the vice of avarice it is hardened to the marrow against the works of mercy, according to that word. ⟦s13+14⟧ The bowels of the covetous man are cruel [n: (Prov. XII)]. [var: Vulg. Prov. 12:10 *viscera autem impiorum crudelia*, "the bowels of the wicked"; Migne prints *avari*] But after so many steps of corruption, that also is still added to the increase of the evil which is brought in below, and let the heart of a beast be given him. ⟦s15⟧ The heart of a beast is given and received, when a neighbour pursues his neighbour without regard to any advantage of his own, by the mere instinct of malignity, as emptily as cruelly. ⟦s16⟧ Hear what the Prophet feels concerning such men: *The bloody and deceitful man,* he says, *the Lord will abhor [n: (Psal. V)]*.
