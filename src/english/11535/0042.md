## CHAPTER XXVIII. *How evil goes rioting on in those who ever slip into worse things.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1327A]

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Attend, I beseech thee, how evil goes rioting on. ⟦s2⟧ First goods are lost, afterwards evils also are committed. ⟦s3⟧ In the shaking off of the leaves, and the scattering of the fruits, understand the declines of goods. ⟦s4⟧ In the binding among the grasses and the hardening of the heart, understand the increases of evils.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But this is to be noted, by what steps the accumulation of vices grows: first one is cast down into avarice, but at last through manifold steps of corruption is let loose unto every cruelty. ⟦s2⟧ First, then, the prevaricating soul is bound with a bond of iron and of bronze, when with strong labour and obstinate solicitude it is untiringly occupied about the acquisition of earthly things. [1327B] It is dipped in the dew of heaven, when by a malignant suggestion it is imbued unto deceit, so that at the instigation of avarice it may acquire by fraudulence what it cannot acquire by effort. ⟦s3⟧ But at last its portion comes to be with the wild beasts in the grasses of the earth, when that which it can acquire neither by effort nor by deceit, it desires and busies itself to obtain by violence and rapine. ⟦s4⟧ Behold how the disease of avarice grows, and brings in the evil of cruelty. ⟦s5⟧ Now the first step of savagery is when for some advantage of his own a man presumes, or endures (yet not without remorse of conscience), to rage against his neighbour. ⟦s6⟧ The heart is changed from man's, when it is moved by no compassion, softened by no humanity, when in the practising of rapines it is stung by no [1327C] regard of the divine fear, by no goading of punishment or of conscience whatsoever. ⟦s7⟧ He receives the heart of a beast who rages even gratuitously, who without any cause breaks out into open malice. ⟦s8⟧ The first step of malice, then, is when without any cause at all a man is hardened toward his neighbour. ⟦s9⟧ The second, when in his neighbour's miseries he is touched by no compassion. ⟦s10⟧ The third and worst, when he now feeds and delights himself in that neighbour's evils.

## CHAPTER XXIX. *That it often happens that the mind is corrupted in every form, which nevertheless is agreed to be reserved unto repentance out of the divine mercy.*

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But one thing more is added, and as it were in the last place, in which the sentence of severity seems to be consummated. [1327D] And let seven, he says, times be changed over him. ⟦s2⟧ Seven times are changed, when by the various steps of corruption the quality of our infirmity is varied, while it is ever turned unto worse things. ⟦s3⟧ Do we not then run into another time, and one as it were contrary to the former, when against a vice which we were before able to resist we now begin to be wholly unable to prevail? ⟦s4⟧ The time, then, is manifoldly changed about anyone, when the manner of his corruption is multiformly varied. ⟦s5⟧ For the domination of the vices little by little softens the spirit, and renders it ever more and more feeble, and step by step drives it on to worse things. ⟦s6⟧ But in seven days, as we commonly know, all time is unrolled, and therefore universality is often figured by that same sevenfold number. ⟦s7⟧ Likewise the capital vices are read to be seven. ⟦s8⟧ Hence it is [1328A] that in the Gospel we read that the unclean spirit brings in seven spirits with him, and inhabits his house which he had left for a time, because any one vice, when it has already begun to have dominion, is wont step by step to bring in after it that whole detestable college of vices. ⟦s9⟧ Hence on the contrary the sinful woman is described as purged of seven demons, who is freed from every tyranny of the vices by the sevenfold grace. ⟦s10⟧ The sevenfold change of times, then, is a multiform, nay rather a universal depravation of morals. ⟦s11⟧ For seven times are as it were changed over him who is corrupted in every form, and who at last at some time nervelessly bends to every servitude of the vices. ⟦s12⟧ But see now how wonderful it is that the divine mercy is sometimes wont to reserve a man of this kind [1328B] unto repentance.

## CHAPTER XXX. *How it is wont to serve the profit of many that some men happen to fall from a height.*

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1+s2+s3+s4+s5+s6+s7+s8+s9+s10+s11+s12+s13⟧ *In the sentence of the watchers it is decreed, and the word of the holy ones, and the petition, until the living know that the Most High ruleth in the kingdom of men, and to whomsoever he will he shall give it, and he shall set the lowliest man over it [n: (Dan. IV)]*. [var: Vulg. Dan. 4:14 *donec cognoscant viventes quod dominetur Excelsus in regno hominum … constituet super eum*; Migne prints the indicative *dominatur* and *super eo*] As we have already said above, by the watchers in this place we understand the holy angels. But by the Apostle's teaching we have learned that all ministering spirits are sent for the sake of those who receive the inheritance of salvation. The angels, therefore, fellow-workers of our salvation and ministers of the divine [1328C] ordination, dispose human affairs according as they know, by God's revealing, that it agrees with the common profit of men. But it is often wont to serve the profit of many that some men happen to fall from a great summit of loftiness, that the rest may learn not to be high-minded, but always to think humble things concerning themselves, and upon any summit of the virtues to fear the ruin of their morals, and may truly acknowledge that it is the Lord alone who works in us to will and to accomplish according to good will. [var: Vulg. Phil. 2:13 *qui operatur in vobis et velle et perficere*, "in you"; Migne prints *in nobis* and drops the first *et*] This profit of such a ruin the prophetic word intimates, saying: *Until the living know that the Most High ruleth in the kingdom of men.* [var: Vulg. *dominetur*, subjunctive] By the divine dispensation, therefore, when the common profit demands this, men of great authority are permitted to fall, at that time only [1328D] in which they are found to grow proud over their own greatness. And so about one and the same person there comes one and the same judgment, as just as it is merciful: just indeed as regards that person, but merciful inasmuch as it profits toward the wariness of others. For it is exceedingly just that he who insolently swells up over the divine gifts should learn from his own ruin how much he is nothing, and how much he can do nothing by himself. But the same decree is said to be also the word of the holy ones, for this reason, that what is foreseen by certain of them in the light of God as to come is brought into common. But it is the petition of the holy ones, because they not only will it to be done, but even with desire seek after that which they foreknow to be useful to many and to serve the divine glory. A judgment of this kind, then, will be the decree of the holy ones, because it is mercifully just; [1329A] the word of the holy ones, because it is truly to come. A petition, because it is justly merciful.
