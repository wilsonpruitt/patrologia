## CHAPTER XXXI. *How some men learn from their own fall, and some from another's, that they ought to attribute nothing to their own powers.*

Until the living know that the most High ruleth in the kingdom of men. Behold the profit: that the living may know. The affections in us surely live according to God, while they are strong in good, and grow strong unto the works of the virtues, while they have cooperating grace, and are able to exercise themselves in spiritual zeals. Then without doubt they die, when the cooperating grace is withdrawn in them. But it is withdrawn when the wicked mind grows proud over its own goods and over the divine gifts, and ascribes its own merits to itself. When grace has been withdrawn, the good affections [1329B] fail and languish toward every good, and thus from living they become dead. For what else is the failure of the virtues, than as it were a certain perishing of them? They therefore who first attend to their own infirmity and to the divine grace only when they are already failing in good, these men learn and acknowledge the mastery, or the benefit, of the divine dominion and moderation after death, and as it were through death. What, then, is it for the living to acknowledge the dominion and princedom of the supernal majesty in the kingdom of men, but for each one, even at that time while he still lives in good works, to attend to his own infirmity, and to ascribe nothing to himself, nothing to his own merits, and amid any exercises whatever of the virtues not to desert the guard of humility, [1329C] but truly and without dissimulation to ascribe whatever of evil to himself, whatever of good to the divine grace? And the supernal mercy is indeed wont to humble even men of virtues in certain things, and to withdraw grace, until they learn amid any distinctions of morals to be in nothing high-minded, to presume nothing concerning their own virtue. Happy are they who learn the weak things of human frailty from another's fall rather than from their own, and advance in the grace of humility. But it goes well indeed even with those who from their own ruin wake up to the guard of humility, since at last at some time after the fall of their infirmity they rise again the stronger. The type of both things is expressed in the ruin, or the repair, of king Nabuchodonosor, because he himself also is repaired after his ruin unto greater glory, and many through his ruin are instructed unto the [1329D] wariness of circumspection. Until the living know, he says, that the Most High ruleth in the kingdom of men. It belongs, then, to the living to have knowledge of this kind: for one leading a just life of whatsoever sort, and a heavenly one, to persevere in good zeal, and to attribute nothing to himself out of his own goods. The living assuredly have knowledge of this kind, when they both endure in a good intention, and presume nothing concerning their own virtue, but ascribe to the Lord all that they usefully can, in that he it is who alone ruleth in the kingdom of men, since the kingdom is the Lord's, and he shall rule over the nations. Surely both that outward kingdom by which men are wont to be set over men, and likewise that inward one by which they ought to have princedom over their own vices and passions, are disposed by him and dispensed, and both are possessed out of his [1330A] gift. But the former we must assuredly avoid and flee altogether, even as the mistress Truth teaches us by her own example. But the latter we ought to desire, and to seek, and with the highest desire to ask for, and with the Prophet to entreat: *Give rule to thy servant [n: (Psal. LXXXV)]*. [var: Vulg. Ps. 85:16 *Da imperium tuum puero tuo*, "thy command"; Migne drops *tuum*] Must we, I ask, believe the prophetic soul to be of so great ambition that he would seek the kingdom of Israel from the Lord, and that not even shame would restrain him from a petition of that kind? But if he could not restrain his tongue from seeking this, why did he not at least forbid his hand to write any such thing? But far be it to suspect any such thing of a soul so holy, and a soul prophetic. But he was assuredly asking of the Lord's mercy such a kingdom, or rule, whence he hoped for the outcome, or the advancement, of his own salvation. [1330B] *Give rule,* he says, *to thy servant, and save the son of thy handmaid.* [var: Vulg. *Da imperium tuum puero tuo*; Migne again drops *tuum*] Ask thou also, therefore, such rule, or kingdom, from the Lord thy God, that on this behalf thou mayest be able to sing: *But I am appointed king by him [n: (Psal. II)]*. For it is a saying true enough: to whomsoever he will he shall give it. For it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that sheweth mercy. [var: Vulg. Rom. 9:16 *Igitur non volentis, neque currentis, sed miserentis **est** Dei*; Migne prints *enim* and moves the copula forward, where chunk 0040 prints the same verse at 1323D as *Non enim volentis neque currentis, sed miserentis est Dei*] For if thou hast nothing which thou hast not received, how much more a kingdom?

## CHAPTER XXXII. *That the measure of greatness hangs upon the manner of humility.*

And he shall set, he says, the lowliest man over it. For he resisteth the proud, but to the humble he giveth grace, and assuredly the humbler a man is, the more sublime. [1330C] Without doubt, if we think upon that inward kingdom, thou wilt be in this kingdom the more sublime the humbler thou art, and without ambiguity the greater, the less thou shalt have been in thine own eyes. To whom, I ask, can it be doubtful that in that outward kingdom it often happens far otherwise, or rather contrariwise? But in that inward kingdom the measure of greatness ever hangs upon the measure of humility. Because he will set the humble man in it, and the lowliest over it. He is humble who truly despises himself in his own sight, but he is humbler who does not shrink from being despised also by others. But he is the lowliest who not only despises his own contempt, but even greatly covets it. What, then, I ask, can pride do against him, to whom his own contempt has come into contempt? Since therefore, as [1330D] Scripture testifies, pride is the beginning of all sin [n: (Eccle. X)], what, I ask, can sin do against him, from whom, by the victory over pride, the beginning of all sin is far off? See therefore how strong and how powerful he who is of this kind is found in that kingdom, which is established against sin, which is destroyed or scattered by sin alone. Behold, now from the end of the vision we manifestly gather what we spoke of far above, that the common good and the profit of the universe demanded such a man, perhaps such as is designated in the tree; such a man, I say, who according to the likeness of the tree should fall from a certain sublime state and rise again, so that out of his own ruin he might strike into those who stand a fear of falling, and out of his own repair might reform those who lie prostrate unto the hope of rising again.
