## CHAPTER XXXIII. *That it is one thing to discern the divine judgments through contemplation, and another to weigh the reason of them.*

[1331A]

Behold the dream which Nabuchodonosor saw; but he still requires its interpretation, whence also he says: *This dream I, king Nabuchodonosor, saw. Do thou therefore, Balthassar, tell the interpretation in haste, because all the wise men of my kingdom are unable to declare the solution to me. But thou art able, because the spirit of the holy gods is in thee [n: (Dan. IV)]*. [var: Vulg. Dan. 4:15 *Hoc somnium vidi ego Nabuchodonosor rex*; Migne inverts the title to *rex Nabuchodonosor*, and prints *nequeunt* where the Vulgate has *non queunt*] As I have already said in what went before, it is one thing to discern the divine judgments with the eye of perspicacity or of foresight, and another to penetrate and know the reason of them by the judgment of discretion and the liveliness of understanding. The former belongs to the vision of the dream, the latter to the interpretation of the dream. [1331B] To the vision of the dream it belongs to know from divine revelation what must come to pass; but to the interpretation of the dream it belongs to understand the quality of the person to whom this must happen, namely, to weigh the cause which is wont to drive a person of whatever kind to a fall of this sort. This is what Daniel found out by investigating, and opened to the king by interpreting, when he says: Thou art that tree which thou sawest. For what is it to say, thou art, but: I weigh thee to be such a one, I find such a cause in thee, whence I doubt not that some such thing must happen to thee? But wilt thou know yet more openly how, after a vision has been divinely shown, we are wont, nay rather ought, to seek its interpretation? Behold, perhaps someone sees through the light of contemplation how the common profit requires the fall [1331C] and the repair of certain sublime men. He sees how this very thing commends the grace of God, how this very thing forms those who still stand unto wariness, how it repairs the fallen unto hope, how from this the beauty of the universe appears more wonderful; and he holds it certain that it must come to pass, in that the omnipotent goodness neither wills nor ought to hinder the common good. But again he begins to wonder how it can agree with the highest justice, or how it can be just, that some should be permitted to fall even for the profit of the universe. For as he who made all things is debtor to the universe, so he who established each several thing is debtor also to each. And as it is merciful not to hinder the common good, so it must also be [1331D] just that there be always in him who is about to fall some cause on account of which he deservedly ought to lie open to the impending ruin. He therefore who covets to know a cause of this kind, and utterly seeks the reason of the divine judgments, what else does he inquire after than the interpretation of the vision that has been shown? But then he demands an interpretation of this kind as it were from Daniel, when what he cannot obtain by investigation he ceases not to wrest from the Lord by a chief zeal, and one of the highest devotion. This dream, he says, I Nabuchodonosor saw. Do thou therefore, Balthasar, tell the interpretation of it in haste. Thou weighest, as I judge, that the king sees some things by himself, and wrests others from Daniel, because some things are such as occur to the spirit freely and easily, others such as cannot be known without great labour and huge solicitude. In the former [1332A] the spirit is provoked to rise up to greater things; in the latter it is exercised and humbled, lest it presume. But it is intimated that in those things which the king had seen he was not without fear, who requires the interpretation with haste. And we too are admonished to seek with all haste the resolution of that matter wherein a suspicion of danger can strike in fear.

## CHAPTER XXXIV. *That we often acquire by the merit of devotion the cognizance of those things which by no sagacity of ours, by no zeal of ours, are we able to penetrate.*

It is surely to be noted that Daniel is called *Balthassar*, that is, hair of the head, where he is invited to the subtle resolution of sublime things. For among human members, what is more sublime than the head, what more subtle than the hair? [1332B] Rightly, therefore, is he called hair of the head, from whom the subtlety of a sublime understanding is demanded. And the true devotion of the mind is rightly called, from its effect, unless I am mistaken, the hair of the head, because through it the mind is without doubt sharpened unto every subtlety. Through its effect, then, it is itself the hair of the head, it is itself the subtlety of the mind. Let us hear yet what he subjoins to these things: Because all the wise men of my kingdom are unable to declare the solution to me. Who are, I ask, the wise men of that inward kingdom, but the keenest points and the most piercing senses of the human heart? Attend, therefore, what the virtue of devotion is, how rightly it is called the hair of the head, if through it the mind can do what it cannot do through all the wise men of its own kingdom. When therefore the mind seeks in manifold ways what is above its own understanding, and by seeking presses on, by pressing on [1332C] fails, by failing distrusts, what else, I ask, does it now say within itself, silent through distrust, but this which king Nabuchodonosor openly professes? Because all the wise men of my kingdom are unable to declare the solution to me. But since Truth says: *Amen I say to you, whatsoever you ask when praying, believe that you shall receive, and it shall be done unto you [n: (Marc. XI)]*. [var: Vulg. Mark 11:24 *Propterea dico vobis, omnia quaecumque orantes petitis, credite quia accipietis, et evenient vobis*; chunk 0035 prints this same verse at 1311B with *petieritis* where Migne prints *petitis* here] If therefore the mind believes what it hears, if it turns itself to prayer, if through true and inmost devotion it does not distrust that it can obtain what it could not obtain by investigation, what else, I ask, does it assert within itself, through the confidence of hope and the constancy of faith, but that very same thing which the king said to Daniel: But thou art able, he says? And he adds the cause also: Because the spirit of the holy gods is in thee. We know [1332D] that in the Holy Scriptures holy men have been called gods, and by the same reason this can be said of the angels also. Who, then, is the spirit of those gods, but the Holy Spirit? He assuredly it is who intercedes for us with unspeakable groanings. He it is who suggests to us what we should pray. He who teaches how we ought to pray. Without this Spirit our devotion cannot be true; and what it shall have attempted with him and through him cannot go for nothing: But thou, he says, art able, because the spirit of the holy gods is in thee.

But it is to be noted that king Nabuchodonosor did not seek useless and superfluous things through Daniel's office, but such things as it behoved to be known for the wariness of avoiding danger. And that which we ask shall be given to us also, if after his example we seek with faith and out of true devotion [1333A] that which we cannot without danger be ignorant of.
