## CHAPTER XXXV. *That the spirit, while it is humbled by much consideration of its own weakness, is often divinely enlightened unto a sublime understanding.*

*Then Daniel, whose name is Balthasar, began to think silently within himself for about one hour, and his thoughts troubled him [n: (Dan. IV)]*. I think that it belongs to Daniel, and that it is the office of true devotion, to return to oneself, to forget outward things and to think upon inward ones, and this chiefly at that time when one must take counsel for his own salvation or another's. One is the time for investigating the things that are above oneself, and another the time for examining the things that are within [1333B] oneself. For all things have their time. There is therefore a time for contemplating the mighty works of God, and there is a time for considering our own weaknesses. That is the time of laughing. For in the evening weeping shall abide, and in the morning gladness. Why should there not be a time of laughing in the morning of divine revelation and in the contemplation of the eternal light? Why should there not be a time of weeping in the evening of a dreadful vision, in considering the darkness of the human conscience? It is evening time when before the eyes of the contemplative the light of revealing grace comes forth and turns him into admiration of itself, and often snatches him beyond himself through the height of the mind: *The goings out,* he says, *of the morning, and at evening thou shalt delight [n: (Psal. LXIV)]*. What are those goings out of the morning, but the excesses of the human mind toward the irradiation of the true sun? He says [1333C] therefore that the goings out of the morning delight; why not the evening also, or the goings in of the evening? For in the morning we are wont to go out from inward things to outward, and in the evening to return from outward things to inward. But who may we worthily wonder how sweet it is, or how delightful, when charity is now fervent and as it were under the summer season, to shake oneself out of sleep at the morning light of the radiant Godhead, and to break out beyond the straits of the human mind into the air of liberty, and to pass over by an alienation of the mind into that broad field of the divine spectacles? And so the goings out of the morning delight, but the goings in of the evening sadden, at that time without doubt when the spirit turns back to the consideration of itself: *Unto myself,* he says, *my soul is troubled* [1333D] [n: (Psal. XLI)]. But in the horror of the nightly vision, in the consideration of a dreadful conscience, there is one sure relief of the spirit, namely the memory of one's God; and this is what is added there: Unto myself my soul is troubled, *therefore will I be mindful of thee.* This is what he says elsewhere: *I was mindful of God, and was delighted [n: (Psal. LXXVI)]*. And in this place: *The goings out of the morning, and at evening thou shalt delight.* Thou, he says, shalt delight; not the hour itself, not the goings in themselves of the evening time. For what delight, I ask, could there be in considering that dark abyss of the human heart? For the heart of man is perverse and unsearchable, and who shall know it? This is why, with Daniel, while he thinks long, his own thoughts trouble him within himself. But he who willingly considers his own weaknesses, diligently goes over them again, [1334A] and humbles himself in the consideration of them, while he zealously examines that which he was able to detect in himself by himself, is often enlightened by God even unto that which he could not know by himself. This is why Daniel, while he meditates long and silently within himself, is divinely instructed to foreknow the ruin of the royal power and the cause of that same ruin.

## CHAPTER XXXVI. *On the true devotion of the mind, how it ought to examine itself.*

But he ought to meditate upon his inward things not slightly and as it were in passing, who wishes to be instructed unto a deep and full knowledge of himself, who desires that the things necessary for his own salvation or another's be revealed to him. So Daniel thought within himself for about one hour, and [1334B] obtained the cause which he sought. The hour of investigation is then at last closed with its due end, when that which is sought is now found. And we too, therefore, when the moment of necessity presses, let us be earnest to pray, to seek, to knock, as much as necessity compels, or opportunity allows and the continuance of our zeal has filled up the present hour. But if by Daniel devotion is to be understood, perhaps this will be for Daniel to think within himself: for our devotion to examine itself, to try itself, to inquire within itself how fervent, how frequent, how suppliant it is. It thinks within itself when it weighs the quality, the measure and the intention of its own zeal and exercise. It thinks within itself while it diligently inquires whether this very thing which it rightly intends it presumes not upon [1334C] its own powers, but upon the piety of the Lord alone. And so our devotion, when it often deals with this within itself, detects the vice of arrogance which before lay hidden within it. But without doubt the vice of arrogance, when it has begun to prevail over the mind, is always wont to be the cause of ruin. When therefore devotion has found that this vice has grown to such enormity, it gathers that a coming ruin of the spirit is at hand, and greatly fears it. For it knows that before ruin the heart shall be exalted, and that God resisteth the proud.
