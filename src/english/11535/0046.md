## CHAPTER XXXVII. *How devotion sometimes detects the whole state of the inner man, and as much discerns it present as it foresees it future.*

[1334D] *The king answering said: Balthasar, let not the dream and the interpretations thereof trouble thee [n: (Dan. IV)]* [var: Vulg. *interpretatio ejus*, singular, "the interpretation thereof"; the next lemma prints the singular]. That the king forbade Daniel to be troubled, what else is it but that the mind deludes itself, and by an empty hope often takes away a profitable fear? But behold, Daniel does what he willed to be done by us when danger is at hand, and what belongs to true devotion: he turns himself to prayer *Balthasar answered and said: My lord, the dream be to them which hate thee, and the interpretation thereof be to thine enemies.* [var: Vulg. *his qui te oderunt*, masculine, "to them that hate thee", and without *sit*; the re-quotation three lines below prints the masculine] They are malignant spirits who have blazed out against us with an inextinguishable hatred. The vices are the true enemies of the soul, which drive it to eternal destruction. We are taught to imprecate their ruin in that which is said: The dream be to them who hate thee, and the interpretation thereof to thine enemies. When therefore the mind is assailed by the spirit of pride and [1335A] by such kindlers of vices, when it is oppressed by arrogance and vainglory, it ought to flee to prayer, and after Daniel's example to demand their ruin. But when the devotion of the spirit cannot prevail by prayer against arrogance and vainglory, often from that very thing it understands more deeply, and gathers more surely, that the swelling of the spirit could not be healed save by a foul and shameful casting down of itself. And this is what Daniel speaks through a mystery, when he says: *They shall cast thee out from among men, and thy dwelling shall be with beasts and wild beasts.* Let us now hear what he says, and from it let us gather how devotion often detects the whole state of the inner man, and as much describes it present as it foresees it future. Let it say, then, when ruin is now at hand, what [1335B] the spirit meanwhile is by grace, what manner of thing it will be by fault, and let it add last of all the cause of that ruin, or of its own repair. This order, unless I am mistaken, thou wilt find in Daniel's words to the king, if thou examine rightly: *The tree,* he says, *which thou sawest [sic: sub limem] and strong, whose height reacheth unto heaven, and the sight thereof unto all the earth, and its branches most fair, and its fruit exceeding great, and food for all in it, the beasts of the field dwelling under it, and the birds of the air abiding in its branches: thou art the king, who art magnified, and hast grown strong; and thy greatness hath increased, and hath reached unto heaven, and thy power unto the ends of all the earth.* How in these words a certain state of great perfection is described, we have already said above, [1335C] and we ought not to repeat it here.

But note that which is said: The tree which thou sawest, thou art. If by such a tree we must understand a certain great strength of admirable virtue, if by the vision divinely shown we ought to take the revelation of contemplation, what, I ask, will it be to say, thou art the tree which thou sawest, but that thou hast received such grace as thou first sawest through contemplation; and that, having understood through the grace of contemplation what manner of man a man of great perfection ought to be, thou hast deserved of the divine gift to be such and so great a one. In the tree, therefore, is mystically described grace received from God; but in the words that follow is expressed the ruin that comes upon an arrogant soul: *But whereas,* he says, *the king saw a watcher and a holy one [1335D] come down from heaven, and say: Cut down the tree, and scatter it, yet leave the shoot of its roots in the earth, and let it be bound with iron and bronze among the grass without, and let it be sprinkled with the dew of heaven, and let its fodder be with the wild beasts, until seven times be changed over it. This is the interpretation of the sentence of the Most High, which is come upon my lord the king. They shall cast thee out from among men, and thy dwelling shall be with beasts and wild beasts, and thou shalt eat hay like an ox, and shalt be drenched with the dew of heaven. Seven times also shall be changed over thee.* How in these words is expressed the type of a soul falling from a certain lofty state into the depths, is held in what has gone before.

Note that here also, just as above, a thing is first shown by God through contemplation, and afterward to be fulfilled in deed. That the [1336A] king saw belongs to contemplation; that which is added in what follows, they shall cast thee out from among men, belongs to the carrying out of the vision, as if he should say: According to this, that thou hast seen through contemplation that such a ruin from so lofty a state can and is wont to happen, I see a like danger threatening thee, because in thee I find the cause of ruin. But the cause of ruin is hinted in that which is brought in last: Until thou know that the Most High ruleth over the kingdom of men, and giveth it to whomsoever he will. In that he says until thou know, he marks that he had not yet acknowledged this very thing as he ought. But in the words that follow is expressed what manner of man he will at length one day be by grace, and why, or how, he is to be repaired after his ruin. *But whereas he commanded,* he says, *that the shoot of its roots [1336B] should be left, that is, of the tree: thy kingdom shall remain to thee after thou shalt have known that the power is heavenly.* But to hear all these things through Daniel is, through the zeal of our devotion, to detect the enormity of our own arrogance, which is always wont to be the cause of ruin; and, the cause of ruin found and looked through, to incur a most violent fear of falling, yet to hold unshaken a most firm hope of coming to oneself again and of rising again. But how all these things ought to be more fully understood can easily be known from the exposition above.

## CHAPTER XXXVIII. *How one ought to fight back against an imminent danger, and how much alms avails, if it be done with a simple intention.*

[1336C] The imminent danger being now known and brought into the midst, Daniel adds in the last place what ought to be done under the moment of so great a necessity, or how the pressing danger ought to be resisted: *Wherefore,* he says, *O king, let my counsel be pleasing to thee, and redeem thou thy sins with alms, and thine iniquities with mercies to the poor; perhaps God will pardon thy beloved ones [n: (Dan. IV)]* [var: Vulg. *delictis tuis*, "thy sins"; Migne prints *dilectis* here and *delictis* at 1337B below]. What, I ask, is more prudent, or what more profitable, than, when danger presses, to flee to the divine protection, and to seek the propitiation of him to whom nothing is impossible? But it is very just that he who in his own necessity seeks support from his superior should show to his inferior the aid he can and ought, so that he who desires mercy for himself may [1336D] willingly spend mercy, that what is written may come to pass: *Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy [n: (Matth. V)]*. Rightly therefore is it said, redeem thy sins with alms, and thine iniquities with mercies to the poor. Perhaps in this twofold clause the sentence is repeated, so that to redeem iniquities and sins is one and the same. Yet we can understand in sins the transgressions of work, and in iniquities the perversities of the will. For that word of the psalm admonishes us that some distinction in these ought, or can, be made: *Thou hast forgiven,* he says, *the impiety of my sin [n: (Psal. XXXI)]*. In alms nonetheless the act of piety is rightly understood, in mercies the affection of piety. And see how rightly a work is expiated by a work, an act by an affection. Redeem thy sins, he says, with alms, [1337A] and thine iniquities with mercies to the poor. Indulgence in outward goods makes men poor, but far more does the want of inward goods render them poor. He is poor who lacks discretion, who has no counsel, who lacks the riches of virtues. But where poverty is, there alms too can be. And by how much the poverty is truer, by so much is the alms greater. It is a great alms to refresh with one's own food flesh that will one day die, but a greater to fatten with the nourishment of its own kind a soul that will live forever. It is an alms to clothe the naked, but a greater to adorn the undisciplined with the discipline of morals. The best kind of alms is to rebuke the restless, to comfort the fainthearted, to be patient toward all. It is a great mercy to suffer together with another's affliction, but a greater to be pricked with compunction for [1337B] another's straying; a noble kind of mercy, to weep for the delinquent, to pardon the penitent, to blot out another's offenses with one's own tears. In these works of mercy we ought to exercise ourselves, who have no outward riches. Perhaps, he says, he will pardon thy offenses. What is it that he says perhaps, and sets it as it were under a doubt, but that not even in these works of mercy can the spirit have full confidence, when, corrupted by the wasting of pride and vainglory, it cannot practice them with a pure and simple intention?
