## CHAPTER XXXIX. *How patiently, or mercifully, the long-suffering of God awaits our correction, or how a perverse mind is wont to abuse his patience.*

[1337C] *All these things came upon king Nabuchodonosor [n: (Dan. IV)]*. All things, he says, came upon the king, and upon such a king. All things came, all things can come upon a kingly spirit, and upon such a one as the royal person designates through a mystery: *After the end of twelve months he was walking about in the hall of Babylon. The king answered and said: Is not this the great Babylon, which I have built to be the house of the kingdom, in the strength of my fortitude, and in the glory of my comeliness?* Who, I ask, may suffice worthily to weigh the bowels of the divine [sic: *pietatatis*], in that he does not at once avenge his own [sic: *contemplum*], but awaits the satisfaction of our correction, and prolongs the times of his propitiation. After the end, he says, of twelve months: in the number twelve the number six is doubled, and in [1337D] six, because of its own perfection, perfection is figured. What then is it, that the divine moderation doubles the six in the delay of its waiting, but that it dispenses the postponement of its vengeance according to the fullness of its justice and of its mercy? For it is a small thing to him to await our correction as much as justice demands, unless he add over and above that which mercy requires, so that his waiting may be as just as it is pious, and as perfect in equity as it is full of piety. But many abuse the kindness of God, and are made worse by that whereby they ought to have been corrected. Behold, while Nabuchodonosor receives the times of making satisfaction given him by God, he swelled the higher by that whereby he ought to have corrected himself. He was walking about in the hall of Babylon. Perhaps true humility, the guardian [1338A] of virtues, is a palace and a most firm dwelling. But such a dwelling belongs to Jerusalem: *Jerusalem,* he says, *which is built as a city, whose participation thereof is in itself [n: (Psal. CXXI)]*. But as true humility belongs to Jerusalem, so feigned humility to Babylon. For what else does feigned humility prepare but disgrace, what else does it look for but confusion? The hall of Babylon, therefore, is feigned humility. The hall of Babylon is a discipline of humiliation without humility. In the hall of Babylon he does not sit, does not lie down, but walks about, whom his own conscience, while it gnaws him, disquiets. He walks about in the hall of Babylon whom his own conscience wearies by accusing him concerning his [sic: *fieta*] humility. But to walk about is not to go in one direction, but to wander here and there at random. He therefore walks about whom the goad of conscience drives on from every side.

## CHAPTER XL. *On the type of an arrogant soul, or how it is wont to magnify itself.*

[1338B]

And the king answered and said: What, I ask, is this answering, where no questioning goes before? Was he perhaps answering another's thought, and perhaps a thought which was not at all, but which he believed to be? Perhaps he was answering the thought of those whom he reckoned to be admiring his greatness and authority. But an arrogant spirit has this as its own, that it believes all men to admire its loftiness, which it magnifies within itself at every hour: whence it comes about that before others too it confidently breaks out into words of boasting, and expresses the same thing as Nabuchodonosor, though in other words. [1338C] Let us now consider how high he swelled who broke out into those words which we set down above. Is not this the great Babylon which I have built? Behold ostentation. To be the house of the kingdom; behold ambition. In the strength of my fortitude; behold arrogance. And in the glory of my comeliness; behold vainglory. This is the great Babylon which I have built. We say that Babylon sounds the same as confusion. For a soul that is proud and enters in doubly, to build Babylon is to bend itself to every humility, and, as it were under the pretext of religion, to expose itself to every service and abjection, and to whatsoever things men are wont to blush at, such as mean apparel, lowly bearing, thin food, voluntary servitude. Let him say then: This is the great Babylon, [1338D] a deep humiliation; this is a voluntary casting down, this is a confusion taken by vow, which I have built, which I have resolved to observe. But to what end, to what profit? To be the house, he says, of the kingdom. Does he not seem to thee to build as it were for a house, who acts to obtain authority and the art of mastery, that he may appear to all such and so great a man as ought worthily to be promoted, and preferred to others, and exalted in dignity or even in power? In the strength, he says, of my fortitude. Behold with how great arrogance he has attributed to himself that which wholly hangs upon divine grace. He does not say, in the gift of the divine compassion, but, in the strength of my fortitude. O how otherwise the Truth itself both thought and taught, when it says: *Without me you can do nothing [n: (Joan. XV)]*. But what the wicked mind reckons that it accomplishes by its own fortitude, it consequently twists back [1339A] to its own glory: In the strength, he says, of my fortitude, and in the glory of my comeliness. O how otherwise such a boaster acts than the Apostle enjoined, how otherwise than the Truth our mistress appointed: *He that glorieth,* he says, *let him glory in the Lord [n: (I Cor. II)]*; and that other word: *So let your light shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father who is in heaven [n: (Matth. V)]*. But this man says, in the glory of my comeliness.

## CHAPTER XLI. *How suddenly, often, and how deeply they fall who abuse the patience of God.*

*While the word was yet in the king's mouth, a voice rushed down from heaven: To thee it is said, king Nabuchodonosor: The kingdom has passed [1339B] from thee [n: (Dan. IV)]*. The voice rushed down while the word was yet in his mouth, that is, still under the very time of his glorying. For often a shameless spirit, while it glories beyond measure, in the very act of glorying, its conscience gnawing it, is suddenly dismayed, and by a certain divine nod is shaken with a sudden horror, and by the fear of falling is hurled headlong into the despair of not escaping. A voice rushes down from heaven when that which he perceives with the ear of the heart comes from the inmost senses of the soul. He hears as it were the earth, whatever he draws in through bodily sense. That comes from heaven which he acknowledges through the Spirit. He learns from heaven that which he comprehends intellectually. To thee it is said, Nabuchodonosor: to thee so emptily proud, to thee so emptily glorying. The kingdom has passed from thee, *and they shall cast thee out from among men, and thy dwelling [1339C] shall be with beasts and with wild beasts.* The kingdom passes from any man whatsoever when he loses the grace divinely conferred upon him, by which he was wont to rule over his own desires and passions. They shall cast thee out from among men. By as much as thou hast raised thyself above men through arrogance, by so much shalt thou be cast down beneath men through fault, and thy dwelling shall be with beasts and wild beasts. Cast out from among men, he goes down to beasts and wild beasts and lives there, who, forsaking rational manners, lives thenceforth irrationally, so that, living like a beast and acting like a wild beast, he is with those who live like beasts dissolved into every carnality, and with those who act like wild beasts unbridled into every cruelty. He adds further: *Thou shalt eat hay like an ox.* The ox, when it receives hay, counts it for the highest delights, and seeks nothing [1339D] more sumptuous. Since therefore all flesh is hay, and all its glory as the flower of hay, he eats hay like an ox who places all his joy in temporal glory and in carnal delights, and seeks spiritual delights not at all, neither by zeal nor by desire. Behold into how deep a place he falls who abuses the patience of God. Behold to what end he comes who has received in vain the times for coming to himself again.

## CHAPTER XLII. *That one and the same sentence is foreknown through contemplation, threatened through fear, confirmed through despair.*

*And seven times shall be changed over thee, until thou know that the Most High ruleth in the kingdom of men, and giveth it to whomsoever he will [n: (Dan. IV)]*. These things we have already [1340A] expounded above, and we ought not to repeat the same again.

This surely is to be noted, that one and the same thing is repeated rather often, and one and the same sentence of the divine severity is rehearsed a third time. And that I may distinguish it for thee by its own differences: first the spirit, through contemplation and as it were through a vision, acknowledges what is wont to happen; second, through devotion and as it were through Daniel's office, it finds what it must now fear concerning itself; last, broken already by despair at the withdrawal of grace, it plainly detects what it is now unable to avoid. And so one and the same sentence is foreknown through contemplation, threatened through fear, confirmed through despair.
