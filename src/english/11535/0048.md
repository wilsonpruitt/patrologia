## CHAPTER XLIII. *That without divine protection no one is able to resist his own passions, or malignant suggestions.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1340B]

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *In the same hour the word was fulfilled upon Nabuchodonosor, he was cast out from among men [n: (Dan. IV)]*. ⟦s2⟧ What is in the same hour, but at once, upon the withdrawal of grace, to yield to temptations, and to be unable to resist the malignant spirits that tempt? ⟦s3⟧ In the same hour: not that the wickedness conceived goes forth at once into act, since it does not always have the occasion of sinning at hand; but the mind is cast down, and falls, from the moment it consents to sin. ⟦s4⟧ It is cast down, and falls, from the moment it seeks an occasion. ⟦s5⟧ From this place, as I judge, we can plainly gather that without divine protection no one could resist his own passions, or malignant suggestions, [1340C] even for a little. ⟦s6⟧ And so in the same hour he was cast out from among men, *and he ate hay like an ox, and his body was stained with the dew of heaven.* ⟦s7⟧ All these things we have already expounded above. ⟦s8⟧ But something is added further which we did not read in what went before: *Until his hairs grew after the likeness of eagles', and his claws like the claws of birds.* [var: Vulg. *et ungues eius quasi avium*, "and his nails as birds'"; Migne sets *ungues* twice and drops *ejus*, which the exposition at 1341B restores]

## CHAPTER XLIV. *From how great a summit of virtues it may happen that a man falls into the depths of vices.*

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Now hairs are fine and long, and in them deep and exceedingly sharp counsels can rightly be understood. ⟦s2⟧ But the body of eagles is covered with a great thickness of feathers. ⟦s3⟧ And eagles are wont to see more keenly and to fly more loftily than all other living creatures. [1340D] He therefore has hairs as it were after the likeness of eagles' who is skilled in much prudence, in exceeding great cunning, who in finding out frauds, in weaving deceits, is found now slower than none, inferior to none. ⟦s4⟧ But birds of prey, as we all know, rage with their claws; and all birds in common tear and hurt what they seize with their claws. ⟦s5⟧ What then is it to have claws like birds', but to hurt whomsoever thou canst prevail against. ⟦s6⟧ But if under the type of claws the office of cruelty is rightly understood, is not this very thing expressed above under another figure, where it is said: And thy dwelling shall be with wild beasts. ⟦s7⟧ But wild beasts do not go fowling, nor have they anything at all that they may do to birds. ⟦s8⟧ Those, therefore, who willingly oppress all worldly and carnal men, and such as brood as it were upon the earth, [1341A] what else do they do but rage against animals after the manner of wild beasts? ⟦s9⟧ Are not they rightly to be called animals who serve the belly alone, whose God is the belly, and whose glory is in their confusion? ⟦s10⟧ But those who persecute spiritual men, what else do they do but lie in wait for birds after the manner of birds, and rave as it were with their claws? ⟦s11⟧ Do they not seem to thee to be birds of heaven who can truly say: But our conversation is in heaven? ⟦s12⟧ Are not they to be called birds of heaven who hang themselves aloft on the wing of contemplation? ⟦s13⟧ In the former sentence, therefore, we have how they are hostile to carnal men; but in this we have how at length one day they utterly do not spare even spiritual men themselves. ⟦s14⟧ Let him say then of such a man, let him say: Until his hairs grew after the likeness of eagles', and his claws [1341B] like the claws of birds, so that he is as ready to circumvent even the circumspect as he is without doubt prone to crush men even conspicuous in virtue. ⟦s15⟧ This man is effective in deceiving, this man forward and shameless in persecuting: *Thy mouth,* says the Psalmist, *hath abounded with malice, and thy tongue framed deceits [n: (Psal. XLIX)]*. ⟦s16⟧ Behold how he convicts the deceiver. ⟦s17⟧ Hear also how he confounds the forward: *They shall hunt after the soul of the just, and shall condemn innocent blood [n: (Psal. XCIII)]*. ⟦s18⟧ Do they not rage against the fowls of heaven, and as it were with claws, who hunt after the soul of the just, and condemn the innocent? ⟦s19⟧ Or by birds demons can be understood, as we have from the reading of the Gospel [n: (Luc. VIII)]. ⟦s20⟧ What then will it be to have claws after [1341C] the likeness of such birds, but to harass any good men whatever by the mere instinct of malignity? ⟦s21⟧ Behold, we now have from how high a summit of perfection it may happen that a man of virtues falls into the depths of vices, that a man may learn, in whatever loftiness of sanctity, always to fear the ruin of morals. ⟦s22⟧ Call to mind what manner of man he described above from the mystical tree, and at the same time mark to what end he came at length. ⟦s23⟧ And of the tree indeed thou readest that the birds of heaven dwelt in its branches, and that all flesh fed upon it. ⟦s24⟧ And so it was for the protection of the good, and for the consolation of all. ⟦s25⟧ Because the birds of heaven dwelt in it, and all flesh fed upon it. ⟦s26⟧ Behold, this same man after all things rages against all with tooth and claw, because his portion also was with the wild beasts, and [1341D] he received claws like birds'.

## CHAPTER XLV. *How meanwhile he who before fell from on high sometimes rises again even to a greater glory.*

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ What follows gladdens greatly, when after these things it is subjoined: *Therefore after the end of the days I Nabuchodonosor lifted up my eyes to heaven, and my sense was restored to me, and I blessed the Most High, and I praised and glorified him that liveth for ever; for his power is an everlasting power, and his kingdom in generation and unto generation. ⟦s2⟧ And all the inhabitants of the earth are reputed as nothing before him [n: (Dan. IV)]*. ⟦s3⟧ Behold, he rises again; behold, he returns to his former glory, nay rather to a better, who fell from so lofty a place into so deep a one. ⟦s4⟧ Who would not wonder, who would not be astonished? [1342A] Who may suffice worthily to weigh the bowels of the divine piety? ⟦s5⟧ Who, I ask, however great a sinner, would not presume upon the divine compassion, and revive unto the hope of pardon? ⟦s6⟧ After the end, he says, of the days I lifted up my eyes to heaven. ⟦s7⟧ The end of the days is the completion of the divine judgments. ⟦s8⟧ If justice, or human judgments, is a light, why not much more the divine judgments? ⟦s9⟧ *And he shall bring forth,* he says, *thy justice as the light, and thy judgment as the noonday [n: (Psal. XXXVII)]*. ⟦s10⟧ If thy judgment can be a day (nay rather a noonday), how much more the divine judgment? ⟦s11⟧ The judgments of God are days, because they are made in the light of truth, in the light of rectitude. ⟦s12⟧ For indeed the judgments of the Lord are true, justified in themselves. ⟦s13⟧ The number of such days is ended over any afflicted man when the severity of the divine [1342B] judgments is completed in the striking of him? ⟦s14⟧ The end of such days is completed when the punishment of the divine severity is changed into mercy. ⟦s15⟧ Nabuchodonosor received the end of these days in his own crushing, when the divine compassion now looked upon him. ⟦s16⟧ For if it had not looked upon him, neither indeed would he have lifted his own eyes to heaven. ⟦s17⟧ To lift the eyes to heaven is to think upon heavenly and eternal things, and, the care and solicitude of worldly things put away, to have spiritual things alone before the eyes. ⟦s18⟧ To look back to heaven, and to have the region of light before the eyes, and to fix the eyes of understanding with the whole intention upon that light of eternity. ⟦s19⟧ He had already fixed his eyes there who said: *I thought upon the days of old, and I had the eternal years in mind* [1342C] [n: (Psal. LXXVIII)]. ⟦s20⟧ But behold, he who lifted his eyes to heaven received back the sense which he had lost. ⟦s21⟧ Mark therefore how profitable it is to lift the eyes to heaven in this manner. ⟦s22⟧ I, says Nabuchodonosor, lifted up my eyes to heaven, and my sense was restored to me. ⟦s23⟧ And so the sense of the flesh is the animal's own. ⟦s24⟧ But the sense of the mind is man's own. ⟦s25⟧ The animal's sense is that by which it perceives white and black; but man's own sense is that which discerns between good and evil, true and false. ⟦s26⟧ This sense, along with Nabuchodonosor, they have lost, or never had, who call good evil and evil good, putting bitter sweet and sweet bitter, putting darkness light and light darkness. ⟦s27⟧ This sense they receive in great part who judge between day and [1342D] day, but more fully they who judge every day. ⟦s28⟧ *One,* says the Apostle, *judgeth between day and day, but another judgeth every day [n: (Rom. XIV)]* [var: Vulg. *alius iudicat diem plus inter diem*, "one esteemeth one day above another"; the exposition here and above turns on Migne's *inter diem et diem*]. ⟦s29⟧ And so he recovers his own sense who at some time awakens to the judgment of discretion. ⟦s30⟧ But his sense is not said to be given, but restored; whence also it is plain that it was first had, but lost. ⟦s31⟧ And so the lost sense is then restored to a man when that grace of discretion which he had been wont to have is repaired in him. ⟦s32⟧ But he receives his sense while he lifts his eyes, because the spirit, while it thinks upon eternal and heavenly things, awakens to the judgment of discretion. ⟦s33⟧ For while it strains vehemently toward heavenly things, it sees how nothing is whatever earthly and transitory thing it loved. ⟦s34⟧ He therefore who pants after the grace of discretion, let him strain toward heavenly things, that he may receive the light of his own [1343A] eyes, and, when he has received it, may cry out to his Lord and say: For what have I in heaven, and besides thee what have I desired upon earth? ⟦s35⟧ But how not in vain Nabuchodonosor received his own sense, he shows in the words that follow.
