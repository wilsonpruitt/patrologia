## CHAPTER XLIX. *How a cast-down mind ought to examine itself, and to renew itself unto its former fervour.*

Nabuchodonosor still adds and says: *And my nobles, and my magistrates sought me, and I was restored unto my kingdom, and a greater magnificence was added to me [n: (Dan. IV)]*. Behold by whom he is sought, and into his kingdom restored. And my nobles and my magistrates. What is best in a man but good intentions? And who ought to exercise mastery, [1345C] or to dispose that inward kingdom, but discreet deliberations? In the nobles, therefore, the mind's intentions. But in the magistrates we understand the mind's deliberations. And what is it for nobles and magistrates of this sort to seek the lost head of the kingdom, and to restore him unto his own place, but with the utmost intention and deliberation to investigate how, after the ruin, even late, one may be able to reform the lost vigour of the spirit unto its first integrity? But the soul ought sometimes diligently to inquire what sort of thing of this kind it was, how constantly it acted, how much of perfection it obtained. And it ought consequently to deliberate how it may be restored unto its former state; and this will be, as I judge, to seek that royal dignity of the spirit by intention, and to be willing to restore it [1345D] unto its own step. They were seeking this royal dignity in another's heart, and desiring it to be restored unto its own place, because they thought it cast down, who said: *Where is thy fear, and thy fortitude, thy patience, and the perfection of ways? [n: (Job IV.)]* [var: Vulg. *timor tuus, fortitudo tua … perfectio viarum tuarum*, "the perfection of thy ways"] But not only they who fall headlong into evil, but also they who grow lukewarm from good, ought to examine themselves, and to renew in themselves that former fervour of religion. Hence it is that it is written to the angel of Ephesus in the Apocalypse: *I have somewhat against thee, that thou hast left the first charity. Remember from whence thou art fallen, and do penance, and do the first works [n: (Apoc. II)]*. [var: Vulg. *Sed habeo adversum te, quod caritatem tuam primam reliquisti. Memor esto itaque unde excideris*, "But I have against thee, that thou hast left thy first charity. Be mindful therefore"]

Yet it is soundly to be noted that this king is said to have been sought by his own men, but not to have been restored by the same. But who, I ask, restored him, save he unto whom he lifted up his eyes, [1346A] whom he blessed, whom he glorified? For even if it now perhaps lies within our power to seek by deliberation the state of our mind, of what sort it now is, or once was, yet it does not equally lie within our power to repair at will the declines of our virtue. But if we zealously carry out that which we have already received of the divine gift the power to do, perhaps that also will be added over which we have not yet been able to do. And I was restored, he says, unto my kingdom, and a greater magnificence was added to me.

## CHAPTER L. *How from the virtue of humility the spirit is strengthened in good, and made solid unto perseverance in good.*

A greater magnificence is added after the ruin, unto full reparation, because out of his very fall a man [1346B] is often instructed unto true humility. And then is magnificence truly enlarged, when among the great things of the virtues the virtue of humility also is unmovably kept. But from the virtue of humility the spirit's advance is both strengthened in good, and made solid unto perseverance in good. It stands, therefore, that magnificence is then truly enlarged, when unto the consummation of the good the grace of persevering is given also. The amplification of magnificence, therefore, is the virtue of perseverance. The amplification of greatness, from the virtue of humility adjoined, is divinely the grace of confirmation. This amplification of magnificence the Prophet was asking, when in psalmody he prayed: *Restore unto me the joy of thy salvation, and strengthen me with a principal spirit [n: (Psal. L)]*. An amplification of this kind we ask daily, [1346C] when we say: *Confirm, O God, this which thou hast wrought in us [n: (Psal. LXVII)]*. Thou weighest from these things, as I judge, that many fall, by God's dispensing, unto this end, that they may rise again the stronger; and they who before, prior to the fall, had only the assisting grace, after the reparation of the ruin receive at the same time the confirming grace also. Truly, according to that which is written: *The Lord killeth and maketh alive, he bringeth down unto hell and bringeth back [n: (I Reg. II)]*. Truly it is he who humbles and lifts up, he who strikes that he may heal [n: (Job V)].

## CHAPTER LI. *How, or with what profit, one ought to praise God.*

*Now I Nabuchodonosor praise and magnify and glorify the King of heaven, because all his works are true, and [1346D] his ways, judgments, and them that walk in pride he is able to humble [n: (Dan. IV)]*. [var: Vulg. *Nunc igitur ego*, "Now therefore I"] Above, as we have already heard, he blessed, praised and magnified. Here again he praises, magnifies and glorifies. Above he praised when he received his sense, and because he was not ungrateful for the divine benefits, he merited to be advanced unto greater things. For he received the lost kingdom with an amplification of magnificence. But the kingdom once received, he praises again, magnifies again. Whence also it came about that he should not again ignominiously lose what he both received when humbled and now possessed with devotion. When a benefit has been received, praise thou also, that thou mayest merit to advance unto greater things; and when promoted unto loftier things, praise none the less, that thou mayest merit to persevere in them. If after the likeness of Nabuchodonosor thou hast received thy sense, if thou hast been able to understand the things [1347A] that are to be done, praise, glorify, that what thou hast received of revealing grace, to be able to understand, may be given thee of the grace that breathes upon thee, to be able to fulfil. If after the likeness of the same Nabuchodonosor thou hast received thy kingdom, if thou hast been able to have dominion over thy passions, if thou hast received the power to exercise good affections in good, praise, magnify, glorify, that thou who hast received the grace of advancing mayest equally receive the grace of persevering also. Gladly, therefore, praise him who is supremely good, glorify him who is supremely blessed, magnify him who is immeasurable in majesty, infinite in eternity. Because all his works are true and his ways judgments. His works are his ways. Ways, I say, by which he comes unto us; ways by which he presses himself upon our understanding and makes himself known. For from his works we advance unto the knowledge [1347B] of him. All his works, therefore, because all his ways are judgments; and all his things are true, because done in truth. We often err in our judgments, whence it often comes about that we absolve the guilty and condemn the innocent. But without doubt he from whom nothing can lie hidden cannot err in his judgment. But since it is written: *All the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth [n: (Psal. XXIV)]*, are those things also judgments which are done by him mercifully? But without any contradiction that divine goodness, in all the things which it mercifully bestows, forsakes not the bounds of equity. [1348A] We, when we are moved with pity, often transgress the limits of equity, and often in that very thing wherein we believe ourselves to be bestowing mercy, we act against justice. But the supernal goodness so dispenses the works of its mercy that in all these it does nothing against justice, commits nothing unjust. His ways therefore are judgments, because they depart not from the tenor of equity and the rectitude of justice, and them that walk in pride he is able to humble. What, I ask, does he say that this alone he is able to do, of whom it truly stands that he is able to do all things? But because one must believe him who has had the experience, perhaps he wished to express that thing more especially which he, a third, proved in his own self by his own trial. Beforehand, perhaps, he wished to impress upon our senses before all things that which it is most expedient to retain in memory? [1348B] But we know that pride is the beginning of all sin [n: (Eccle. X)]. We know that before ruin the heart shall be exalted [n: (Prov. XVI)]. [var: Vulg. *ante ruinam exaltatur spiritus*, "the spirit is exalted"] He therefore who fears the ruin of morals ought before all things to be watchful against pride. But it avails most for the crushing of pride, and for the keeping of true humility, always to retain in the spirit that he who is wont to resist the proud is able, when he will, to humble them that walk in pride. This Nabuchodonosor taught them by word, this he taught us by example: that howsoever or whensoever he will, he is able to humble them that walk in pride.
