## CHAPTER ONE. *Of the threefold vice of mutability, and of the inconstancy of the mind.*

[1347C]

*I saw in my vision by night, and behold, the four winds of heaven strove upon the great sea. And four great beasts came up out of the sea, diverse one from another. The first was like a lioness, and had the wings of an eagle. I beheld till its wings were plucked off, and it was lifted up from the earth. And it stood upon its feet as a man, and its heart was given to it [n: (Dan. VII)]*. [var: Vulg. *cor hominis*, "a man's heart"] Let us now see what this great sea is, in which the prophet Daniel saw this battle of the winds, whatever it be. But who knows not how unstable and restless the sea is, which is ever moved at every state and impulse of the winds? Rightly therefore, if I mistake not, is the mutability and inconstancy of the heart figured in it, in that [1347D] it is truly like a wave of the sea, which is moved and carried about by the wind. But this evil is threefold. For there is a mutability of purpose, and there is a mutability of promise, and likewise a mutability of vow. A purpose is what each man determines with himself; a promise is what each man determines with his neighbour; a vow is what each man determines, but as it were with God. Of the mutability of purpose is that saying: *The sluggard willeth and willeth not [n: (Prov. XIII)]*. Hear also how the mutability of promise is reproved in that which is said: *Clouds and wind, and rains not following, a boastful man, and not fulfilling his promises [n: (Prov. XXIII)]*. He wished us to beware [1348C] of the mutability of vow who says: *If thou hast vowed anything to God, defer not to pay it; for an unfaithful and foolish promise displeaseth God [n: (Eccle. III)]*. A foolish promise is one that is made concerning that which it is not lawful to do, or which God forbids to be done. A foolish promise is concerning that which it is not expedient to do, in this, that a man either exceeds the bounds of modesty, or presumes something against obedience. Of such vows the Scripture says that the vows of fools are to be broken. Yet they who have bound themselves by such a vow ought not to break a bond of that kind at their own pleasure, but are to be absolved at the judgment of those who have received and have the power of binding and loosing. An unfaithful promise is one which repentance follows, or which duplicity of heart accompanies. It is good, therefore, to vow good things, and to vow them well, lest the promise be [1348D] foolish. It is good also fully to discharge what has been well promised, lest the promise be faithless: *Vow ye,* he says, *and pay to the Lord your God, ye that round about him bring presents [n: (Psal. CLXXV)]*. [var: Vulg. *omnes qui in circuitu ejus*, "all ye that round about him"] *Otherwise it is better not to vow, than not to pay the vows [n: (Eccle. V)]*. [var: Vulg. *Multoque melius est non vovere, quam post votum promissa non reddere*, "And much better is it not to vow, than after a vow not to perform what is promised"] In mutability of purpose is noted the vice of an inconstant spirit. In mutability of promise, the scandal of one's neighbour. In mutability of vow, the contempt and the wrong of God. Evil, therefore, is the first mutability, but worse the second, and the third the worst of all. O how bitter beyond the rest is this last evil, how fearful, how troubled it renders the human conscience! [1349A] This is that stormy water of the sea, restless through fearfulness, and salt through bitterness. This is that which we seek, the great sea, in comparison whereof none of the rest is reckoned anything. We hold the sea; it remains now to inquire concerning the winds, and the battle of the winds. But what is this battle of the winds, but the strong impulse and vehemence of temptations. For human life upon earth is a temptation. Whence also it is rightly enjoined, or admonished: *Son, when thou comest to the service of God, stand in fear, and prepare thy soul for temptation [n: (Eccle. II)]*. [var: Vulg. *sta in justitia et timore*, "stand in justice and in fear"] But the diversity of the winds of heaven is the contrary affections of the spirit. For concupiscence breathes in one way, and wrathfulness blows in another. And in one way and another, confidence, or diffidence. But who does not recognize by experience [1349B] how concupiscence breathes and pants, how vehemently it shakes the spirit, how strongly it bends it toward that which it desires? *For every man is tempted, being drawn away and allured by his own concupiscence [n: (Jac. I)]*. But often, amid the strong heats and violent impulses of concupiscence, the spirit returns unto itself, and confutes, and sharply rebukes. It is angry with itself, it is indignant with itself, and wrathfulness, blowing from the opposite quarter, strongly resists and struggles against the blasts of concupiscence. These blasts of wrathfulness, and these recoils, he wished to come about, who said? *Be ye angry, and sin not [n: (Psal. IV)]*. In this manner, therefore, the blasts of concupiscence and of wrathfulness; often clash one with the other, and rise up each against the other. A conflict of alternating blasts of this kind each one of us [1349C] is wont to experience, and must endure, so long as the flesh lusteth against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh. For although concupiscence may seem at some time to rest, at least for a little, it begins again and again to rise up, and to assail more vehemently. *It was silent,* says the Scripture, *the land of Juda, for a few days [n: (I Mach. VII)]*. If the earth, and the land of Juda, can scarcely keep silence for a few days, how much more the sea? For what by the sea, but an unstable heart. and what by the earth (a solid element, that is to say, and an immovable body) do we more rightly understand than a heart firm and stable. If therefore a stable heart rarely rests, and that for a few days, what wonder if a heart restless and slippery, and such in short as is designated [1349D] by the great sea, what wonder, I say, if it is rarely silent, and scarcely for a few moments? That wind, therefore, vehemently burning (concupiscence, that is to say, breathing and boiling), after a momentary quiet often rises the more violent, and drives more strongly than is its wont, and well-nigh overturns the state of the mind, so far that the spirit now begins to grow weary and almost to fail, and to despair of perseverance, and to fall into diffidence. When therefore the wind of diffidence blows, it often befalls that the spirit is more miserably endangered than it could be by any impulses of concupiscence. For it is greatly to be feared lest that vehement wind rush suddenly from the region of the desert, and, shaking the house of conscience, overturn the whole state of the mind. For who shall stand before the face of its cold? Unless perhaps, as is wont to happen, by God's breathing [1350A] contrary blasts rise up from the opposite quarter. For it often comes about that, after much trembling of diffidence and exceeding weariness, the spirit at length at some time returns unto conscience, runs back unto the divine mercy, casts its care upon the Lord, repairs its constancy, and scarcely and late resumes its confidence. From thenceforth confidence and diffidence begin mutually to rebel, and strongly to assail, and to drive with adverse blasts, and by alternate turns, now this, now that, to have the upper hand. This is that battle of the winds, if I mistake not, which the prophet Daniel saw and described in mystical speech, and of what sort it is, experience itself has perhaps taught some of you. And would that I knew and were able to describe it as profitably as it truly stands that I have proved by [1350B] experience of what kind it is. But let us see what is sometimes wont to follow this battle of the winds. Let us see what monsters that great sea, of which we spoke above, chances to bring forth.

## CHAPTER II. *What sort, or how great, a conflict of temptation arises out of the inconstancy of the mind.*

Four great beasts, he says, came up out of the sea, diverse one from another. What, I ask, is it for beasts to come up out of the sea after the battle of the winds, but for bestial motions and irrational impulses, after the violence of temptations, to rise up out of the instability of the heart and to break forth into the open? For he puts on as it were a bestial form who lives bestially. For without doubt, he who passes his life irrationally and bestially, [1350C] in so far as irrationally, so far bestially. What are any vices whatsoever, but certain beasts? What are any depraved desires whatsoever, but beasts? Beasts of this kind tear morals, cut the throat of virtues, choke souls. The onslaughts of such beasts the Psalmist feared, when in psalmody he prayed and in praying sang: *Deliver not up unto the beasts the souls that confess to thee [n: (Psal. LXXIII)]*. There are beasts which kill the body, but have nothing that they can do to the soul. Yet to holy men such as these are not greatly to be feared, and least of all to those who by a spontaneous nod are mortified all the day long, lest they seem to incur that prophetic reproach: *There they trembled for fear, where there was no fear. Do not deliver,* he says, *unto the beast the souls that confess [1350D] to thee [n: (Psal. XIII)]*. [var: Vulg. *trepidaverunt*, "they feared"; and at Psal. LXXIII, printed a few lines above, *Ne tradas bestiis*, "deliver not up unto the beasts", plural] He says not the body of them that confess to thee, he says not the flesh of them that confess to thee, but deliver not up unto the beasts the souls that confess to thee. Those, those beasts assuredly are truly to be feared, which are able to kill souls. The onslaughts of such beasts we quickly run into, when, our deserts requiring it, we lose the light of divine illumination: *Thou hast appointed,* he says, *darkness, and it was night: in it shall all the beasts of the wood go about [n: (Psal. CIII)]*. The onslaughts of such beasts the Lord threatens against them that presume upon themselves and glory in their own merits, when in the canticle of Deuteronomy he speaks by the Prophet: *I will send the teeth of beasts upon them [n: (Deut. XXXVIII)]*. But it now remains to inquire why these beasts are said to be four only, or how they are diverse one from another.
