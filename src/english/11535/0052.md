## CHAPTER III. *That the manner of living bestially is fourfold.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1351A]

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ That I may briefly say what I think, every manner of living bestially seems to me to be fourfold. ⟦s2⟧ Some seem to practise good things, but without mode and measure and apart from all discretion. ⟦s3⟧ And although in them there be perhaps a good intention, this in them is bestial, that they live without measure, and are carried headlong, without the judgment of reason and apart from counsel. ⟦s4⟧ And although they may perchance have a zeal of rectitude, yet they have it not according to learning. ⟦s5⟧ Others, however, practise evil things, but timidly indeed, and therefore secretly; and for that reason, according to the voice of the Psalmist, they will dwell, and will hide themselves [n: (Psal. LV)], because they fear to be reproved, or blush to be caught. ⟦s6⟧ Others again practise good things in work, [1351B] but by hypocrisy indeed, and without any good intention, as loving the first couches at feasts, and the first chairs in the synagogues, and salutations in the market place, and to be called Rabbi by men. ⟦s7⟧ Others again practise evil things everywhere, openly, and freely, and without any fear and shame, so that they seem to be without a forehead, because a harlot's forehead has been made theirs, and they know not how to blush. ⟦s8⟧ Thou seest therefore how diverse among themselves are workmen of this kind. ⟦s9⟧ The first and the third exercise themselves in good things, the second and the fourth in evil. ⟦s10⟧ The first and the third do good things, but not well: for the first indiscreetly, the third in simulation, and by that very thing they are diverse one from the other. ⟦s11⟧ In evil things both the second and the fourth exercise themselves. ⟦s12⟧ But those secretly, these as openly as shamelessly, and therefore are they themselves diverse among themselves. ⟦s13+14⟧ The first are they who walk [1351C] in great things and in wonderful things beneath themselves [n: (Psal. CXXX)]. [var: Vulg. *neque ambulavi in magnis, neque in mirabilibus super me*, "nor have I walked in great matters, nor in wonderful things above me"] The second are they who sit in ambush with the rich in secret places, that they may kill the innocent [n: (Psal. X)]. ⟦s15⟧ The third are they who do all their works that they may be seen of men [n: (Matth. XXIII)]. ⟦s16⟧ But the fourth, they who neither fear God nor reverence men [n: (Luc. XVIII)]. ⟦s17⟧ In the first, therefore, there is an indiscreet religion; in the second, a hidden transgression; in the third, a feigned religion; in the fourth, a manifest transgression. ⟦s18⟧ These are the four states of them who live irrationally and bestially (if indeed they are to be called states, and not rather falls), which ever drive a man from evil to worse.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ And it is to be noted that in these four steps of corruptions the second is worse than the first, the third than the second, and the fourth is the worst of all. [1351D] And perhaps some may doubt of the third, how it is worse than the second. ⟦s2⟧ But in the third state, what else is there than a dissembled equity? ⟦s3⟧ But, on Augustine's testimony, we have learned that a dissembled equity is not equity, but a double iniquity.

## CHAPTER IV. *By what steps of corruption the human spirit is often wont to fall.*

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ In the first state, therefore, there is a foolish justice; in the second, an uncurbed malice; in the third, a feigned justice; in the fourth, an unbridled malice. ⟦s2⟧ In the first reigns singularity, in the second impiety, in the third craftiness, in the fourth cruelty. ⟦s3⟧ In the first, I say, reigns singularity, and of it is said: *Woe to him that is alone, for when he shall fall he hath none [1352A] to lift him up [n: (Eccle. IV)]*. ⟦s4⟧ Woe, I say, to him that is alone, that is, to him who presumes upon himself alone and upon his own counsel, and lives by a certain solitary presumption. ⟦s5⟧ In the second reigns impiety, and of it is said: *Woe to the wicked unto evil [n: (Isa. III)]*. ⟦s6⟧ In the third reigns craftiness. ⟦s7⟧ And the crafty provoke the wrath of God, and of them is said: *Woe to the double heart, and to him that goeth on the earth two ways [n: (Eccle. II)]*. ⟦s8⟧ In the fourth reigns cruelty, and to the cruel it is said: *Woe to thee that spoilest, shalt not thou thyself also be spoiled [n: (Isai. XXXIII)]*. ⟦s9⟧ Cruelty is rapine; rapine is when a man takes away another's goods. ⟦s10⟧ What seems to thee of him who sows discord among brethren? ⟦s11⟧ Impiously indeed he deals with thee who takes away thy money; what of him who takes away thy peace? ⟦s12⟧ Does he act cruelly who takes away thy garment, [1352B] and not much more he who takes away thy charity? ⟦s13⟧ But if it is cruel to take from a man outward goods, goods that shall perish, much more cruel will it be to take from a man inward goods, goods that shall abide unto eternity: *For it is charity that never falleth away [n: (II Cor. XIII)]*. ⟦s14⟧ Without doubt, he who takes charity from a man takes God also away: *Because God is charity [n: (I Joan. IV)]*. ⟦s15⟧ Woe to thee that spoilest, shalt not thou be spoiled? ⟦s16⟧ As much as thou takest, so much dost thou lose; thou takest God away, and thou losest God. ⟦s17⟧ Thou takest heaven away, thou forfeitest heaven. ⟦s18⟧ What is crueller than this impiety? ⟦s19⟧ What is madder than this cruelty? ⟦s20⟧ Behold from whence and whither one comes; behold none the less also by what way one goes down: from whence, sayest thou, and whither? ⟦s21⟧ From the height of singularity, unto the height of cruelty. ⟦s22⟧ But by what way, sayest thou, and how? ⟦s23⟧ Through impiety and duplicity. ⟦s24⟧ For from the first [1352C] step one goes down to the second, from the second to the third, from the third to the fourth. ⟦s25⟧ For without doubt presumption cast down is wont to bring in emulation; emulation despised, simulation; simulation cast down, the unbridling of the mind. ⟦s26⟧ For to the first state belongs presumption, to the second emulation, to the third simulation, to the fourth the unbridling of the mind. ⟦s27⟧ To the first, insolence; to the second, enviousness; to the third, fraudulence; to the fourth, shamelessness; and perhaps these are those beasts which we had to seek.

## CHAPTER V. *Of the vice of insolence.*

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ In the description, therefore, of the first beast we understand [1352D] the vice of insolence. ⟦s2⟧ We say *insolitum*, as it were *non solitum*, "not customary", or "contrary to the customary", and thence we suppose *insolentia*, insolence, to be so named. ⟦s3⟧ Rightly therefore is it called insolence, when at random and rashly something is presumed beyond the customary. ⟦s4⟧ Insolence, therefore, is a headlong vice, and a rash presumption, or a headlong and presumptuous rashness, beyond the customary. ⟦s5⟧ But this evil is manifold. ⟦s6⟧ For insolence now raises itself above the customary, now restrains itself below the customary, now sets itself against the customary. ⟦s7⟧ But let us now see concerning the first beast, of what sort Holy Scripture describes it, or how fittingly it expresses the property of insolence. ⟦s8⟧ The first, he says, was like a lioness, and had the wings of an eagle. ⟦s9⟧ The lion is said to be of such fierceness that, when provoked, he fears not even death, so far that not even for the peril of death [1353A] does he restrain himself from the impulse of his own fury. ⟦s10⟧ *The lion,* he says, *the strongest of beasts, will be afraid at the meeting of none [n: (Prov. XXX)]*. ⟦s11⟧ Thou seest in the lion how foolish a boldness, and how bold a folly. ⟦s12⟧ Does it not seem a matter of lion-like boldness, and done to make oneself a name, to walk in great things and in wonderful things above oneself, to presume all things beyond one's strength, the counsel of teachers being spurned and the precept of elders contemned, to stretch fasts, vigils and observances of this kind beyond measure, and neither to spare the health of the body, nor to shudder at the death of the soul in the contempt of due obedience?

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Note that it is likened not to a lion, but to a lioness, because this sex is found more forward and more obstinate in the vehemence of its impulse. ⟦s2⟧ See therefore how irrational and bestial and truly lion-like a thing it is to rest in no man's counsel, [1353B] in no man's precept, but obstinately to persist in the boldness of one's own rashness. ⟦s3⟧ Do ye wish to hear this also briefly, what I think of the wings of a beast of this kind? ⟦s4⟧ It seems to me that the one is of precipitancy, the other of presumption. ⟦s5⟧ Behold, two wings, and both of the eagle. ⟦s6⟧ Who knows not this of the eagle, that it is wont to fly more swiftly, or more loftily, than almost all other birds. ⟦s7⟧ In precipitancy mark the swiftness of the flight. ⟦s8⟧ In presumption weigh the loftiness of the flight. ⟦s9⟧ It has been said already above that insolence is a headlong and presumptuous rashness, beyond the customary. ⟦s10⟧ That I may therefore gird up into a compendious brevity the things said more diffusely, recognize the lion-like likeness in the rashness of singularity. ⟦s11⟧ Note in the eagle's wings the vice of presumption [1353C] and the vice of precipitancy. ⟦s12⟧ The eagle is called the king of birds, and the lion the king of beasts. ⟦s13⟧ Does not he therefore seem to have something of the lioness and something of the eagle, who presumes, in the active life as in the contemplative, to go before all men, and in both lives to hold a singular and highest place? ⟦s14⟧ On earth he is a lioness, in heaven he is an eagle, who presumes to walk more strongly than all others, to fly more loftily; to walk by action, to fly by contemplation.
