## CHAPTER VI. *By what steps of insolence the human spirit is little by little corrupted.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But let us now see what Solomon thinks of a man of this kind, who does all things precipitately and presumptuously, who in his endeavours keeps neither measure [1353D] nor modesty: *He that is hasty,* he says, *stumbleth with his feet [n: (Prov. XIX)]*. ⟦s2⟧ Hence it is that elsewhere he says: *The inheritance which is hastened to in the beginning, in the end shall want a blessing [n: (Prov. XX)]*. ⟦s3+4⟧ To the same sentence there seems to accord that which is added concerning the aforesaid beast: I beheld till its wings were plucked off, and it was lifted up from the earth and stood upon its feet as a man, and its heart was given to it. [var: Vulg. *cor hominis*, "a man's heart"] Behold how they who presume upon themselves beyond measure lose by degrees that whereby they swell and despise the rest. ⟦s5⟧ First the wings are plucked off, afterwards the body itself of the beast is lifted up from the earth; first the grace of the contemplative life, afterwards that of the active life also is lost. ⟦s6⟧ For what is it to lose one's wings, but to lose those lofty [1354A] flights of contemplating? ⟦s7⟧ For when, after many excesses of fastings and vigils, the body has begun to waste away and the head to ache, willy-nilly one must lay down the wings, return to the earth, and take care of the flesh; and it is better that they be laid down willingly than plucked off from one unwilling. ⟦s8⟧ What is it for the wings to be plucked off, but to lose them with huge grief, and without any hope of recovering them? ⟦s9⟧ The wings are plucked off when the boldness of precipitancy and of presumption is cut short, infirmity urging, necessity compelling. ⟦s10+11⟧ Does not the Lord threaten a plucking off of wings of this kind, when he speaks by the prophet: *If thou be exalted as an eagle, and set thy nest among the stars of heaven, thence will I draw thee, saith the Lord [n: (Abd. I)]*. [var: Vulg. *et si inter sidera posueris … inde detraham te*, "and if thou set thy nest among the stars, thence will I draw thee down"] After the plucking off of the wings it is lifted up from the earth; through the mind's elevation, therefore, and because of the mind's elation, [1354B] it is shaken off from the earth. ⟦s12⟧ And deservedly, as I believe, in that by pride, swelling, bursting, wasting, rotting, they yet at some time become as the dust which the wind driveth from the face of the earth. ⟦s13⟧ And so, after the loss of the wings, the habitation of the earth also is lost, because after the grace of contemplation has been withdrawn, as has been said, the grace of working well also is withdrawn from the proud soul. ⟦s14⟧ And he who before presumed to go strongly before all, let him now begin to follow after all, and at last to be left behind. ⟦s15⟧ And he who before through insolence stretched himself beyond the customary, afterwards remains short of the customary; and thus he now falls from the first step of insolence into the second, with this at last to come, which I fear, that from the second he fall back into the third. ⟦s16⟧ But where, I ask, will the beast already spoken of be henceforth, cast down from heaven, the others [1354C] having been lost, and lifted up from the earth? ⟦s17⟧ Will it perhaps return thither whence it came, and go down again into the sea, it that came up out of the sea? ⟦s18⟧ So indeed, so often what is presumed out of the levity of the heart, afterwards into a great fluctuation of the mind in bitterness. ⟦s19⟧ And this perhaps is to lose the earth: to lose all the stability of one's purpose, and, by bearing one's dejection unworthily, to run into an exceeding bitterness of heart. ⟦s20⟧ Thus from the earth one passes into the sea; thus are the mountains carried into the heart of the sea. ⟦s21⟧ For what is it for mountains to pass into the heart of the sea, but to fall from the summit of a great endeavour into the inmost malice of the heart? ⟦s22⟧ For according to the testimony of the Truth, iniquity abounding, the charity of many shall grow cold [n: (Matth. XXIV)]. ⟦s23⟧ No one therefore, in however great a [1354D] loftiness of virtues, ought to presume anything of his own powers, but all of the Lord, and to sing with the Psalmist: *Therefore we will not fear when the earth shall be troubled, and the mountains shall be carried into the heart of the sea [n: (Psal. XXV)]*. ⟦s24⟧ Then indeed are the mountains carried away, when that saying of John is fulfilled: *They went out from us, but they were not of us [n: (I Joan. II)]*.

## CHAPTER VII. *How from the vice of insolence a man falls into the vice of enviousness.*

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But if no place now remains to this beast except the sea (for it seems to have lost both heaven and earth), it is a wonder how it is said to have stood upon its feet. ⟦s2⟧ Or is it perhaps believed to have stood upon some crag or rock, where it might be girt about on every side by the sea, and unceasingly [1355A] beaten by the waves of the sea? ⟦s3⟧ O how hard a punishment, but worthy of presumptuous singularity! ⟦s4⟧ Behold, it which before presumed to outrun all, and out of its own presumption wearied them, now has not whither to go, nor finds how it may rest. ⟦s5⟧ And upon its feet, he says, it stood as a man. ⟦s6⟧ And would that even late it might cease to believe itself an angel, and at length at some time leave off presuming above man. ⟦s7⟧ Behold, now putting off the angel, it no longer flies in the highest, but walks in the lowest; nay, now it walks not, but stands, having lost the love and the hope of advancing. ⟦s8⟧ Yet it lies not down, but stands, so that it never rests. ⟦s9⟧ Continually therefore it stands, because without intermission it labours. ⟦s10⟧ It wearies its feet without going forward, it labours without fruit. ⟦s11⟧ It stands therefore, but how? ⟦s12⟧ Upon its feet, as a man. ⟦s13⟧ A grand spectacle, but a foul [1355B] wonder, to see a four-footed beast, a lion-like shape at that, raise itself upon two feet against heaven, and stand upright against nature. ⟦s14⟧ What is this monstrous uprearing, but a certain enormous and exceedingly detestable pride? ⟦s15⟧ He rears himself against God who swells against the supercelestial majesty. ⟦s16⟧ Does he not swell against God who reproves his judgments? ⟦s17⟧ But who knows not how presumptuous minds are wont to murmur against the divine scourges, to envy the advances of others, and impatiently to bear the declines of their own glory? ⟦s18⟧ But they murmur not only against the justice of God, but even against the goodness of God. ⟦s19⟧ Against the justice of God in their own evils, against the goodness of God in the goods of others. ⟦s20⟧ For an insolent spirit, and one greedy of its own excellence, when [1355C] through excessive pride it falls into envy, bears almost more grievously the advance of others than its own defect. ⟦s21⟧ O monstrous presumption, which murmurs against the divine majesty for this, that God resisteth the proud, but giveth grace to the humble [n: (Luc. IV)]. ⟦s22⟧ *Is thy eye,* he says, *evil, because I am good? [n: (Matth. XX.)]* But they who are of this kind are wont to weave certain complaints as though reasonable, and to display the causes of their murmuring (though in truth they are irrational) as though just; and this perhaps is for the brutish shape to raise itself unto the likeness of a man, to be willing to put forward as it were certain reasons in an irrational murmuring against God. ⟦s23⟧ To this sentence there seems to accord what is further said of [1355D] this beast. ⟦s24⟧ For when it had been premised, it stood upon its feet as a man, straightway there is added, and its heart was given to it. ⟦s25⟧ Whose, its? ⟦s26⟧ Either the heart of a man, or the heart of the lioness. ⟦s27⟧ But why should the lioness be said to have received its heart, which it is not said to have lost? ⟦s28⟧ It received the heart of a man, and of one savouring carnally, when, God permitting, it fell into the prudence of the flesh; or else it then truly receives the heart of a man when through much experience it understands its own error, and more fully recognizes both its own defect and the advance of others. ⟦s29⟧ But what is divinely granted to a man for his own good, the man himself often abuses to his own evil. ⟦s30⟧ And when his own merits and those of others are known to be unequal, from that whereby he ought to be pricked unto penitence, thence he grows unto a greater envy. ⟦s31⟧ For behold, after the lioness described [1356A] received the heart of a man, there succeeds it not a lamb, but a bear [sic: *ut sus*]. ⟦s32⟧ For often a heart greedy of glory and livid, when the glory of others and its own ignominy have been looked upon, falls into enviousness. ⟦s33⟧ Thus vice succeeds vice: insolence goes before, and enviousness follows; and what is yet worse, insolence does not withdraw.
