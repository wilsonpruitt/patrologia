## CHAPTER VIII. *Of the vice of enviousness.*

Enviousness seems to sound something more than envy. As a man is not rightly called a reader save at that time when he is occupied in reading, so too a man is then rightly called envying when he is employing his spite. And from that which is *invidens*, envying, is *invidentia*, enviousness, named. Envy, therefore, then truly grows out into enviousness, when it so agitates the spirit that it [1356B] now scarcely at any time lets it rest. Enviousness, therefore, is a frequent envy, and one that does not let the spirit rest. But in the second beast, if I mistake not, enviousness is rightly understood. But, that this may grow clearer, let us hear how the prophetic word describes it: *And behold,* he says, *another beast, like a bear, stood on one side, and there were three rows in its mouth and in its teeth, and thus they said to it: Arise, eat much flesh [n: (Dan. VII)]*. Now the bear, as we all know, is an ugly animal, and one that is wont to dwell in remote and hidden places; and in this very thing it bears the form of envy. For envy herself too (as blushing at her own foulness) ever strives to lie hidden. But the bear is a malicious and savage animal, and perhaps such is the vice of envy. Envy is the hatred [1356C] of another's happiness; and what is more malicious? What else, therefore, is envy but a light-shunning and lurking malice? But enviousness is a boiling malice, and one that rages in secret. And the bear is a very restless animal, so that when tied to a stake it scarcely ever ceases either to go round about, or, with its feet fixed, to toss its head now to this side, now to that; and in this it has the form not only of envy, but of enviousness, which scarcely for a moment lets the spirit rest. But it is said of this beast that it stood on one side. Not yet, therefore, in this state does a man utterly forsake good things, nor yet, as in the former state, does he extend himself unto all things. It stands therefore on one side, because in certain good things it still seems to persist. For no one [1356D] becomes suddenly most base, just as, on the opposite side, no one becomes suddenly highest. As it were, therefore, it still stands on one side, since old custom still draws the man to do some good things. Behold that in this state the spirit is not yet unbridled even to open malice, in so far as it is either restrained by fear or checked by shame.

## CHAPTER IX. *Of the evil of enviousness, by what steps it is wont to increase.*

And three rows, he says, were in its mouth, and in its teeth. The Scripture itself determines how there were three rows in its mouth, that is, in its teeth. But what do we more rightly understand by the teeth than the bites of malice, of enviousness? But who knows not how familiar, and homely, and as it were [1357A] properly proper it is to envy to render another's life, and to tear it with the bites of disparagement? But this evil is wont to increase by certain steps. For there are in men some things manifestly evil, and some doubtful, so that it may come into ambiguity whether they are good or evil. But a livid mind is wont always to exaggerate manifest evils the more manifoldly, to turn whatever is doubtful to the worse part, and open goods (since it cannot deny them) to darken by what art it can, and to diminish at least something of them. The first is of speaking-against, the second of derogation, the third of detraction. Speaking-against is when a biting and reproving speech is occupied about that, or is made on account of that evil, which is truly in a man. He who knows what it is *rogare*, to entreat, can from the opposite recognize what [1357B] it is *derogare*, to derogate, since they answer to one another by contraries just as to persuade and to dissuade do. Does it not seem to thee derogation when to one who holds a good estimation of some other man it is said, do not so believe, do not so esteem, thou art mistaken, thou art deceived, it is far otherwise than thou thinkest, thou believest darkness to be light and puttest bitter for sweet. This assuredly has no place in open goods, because it cannot bend the things that are manifest to the other side. But to detract is to be willing to diminish at least something of open goods, when thou canst not utterly empty them out. For he detracts who by barking against draws back something from that whereby he believes a man's reputation to be the more glorious. Speaking-against, therefore, is the disseminating of an evil opinion. Derogation, the emptying out of a good opinion. Detraction, the thinning of a good opinion. The tongue [1357C] that speaks against the Psalmist feared, when he said: *All the day long my shame is before me, and the confusion of my face hath covered me, at the face of him that reproacheth and speaketh against me [n: (Psal. XLIII)]*. [var: Vulg. *a voce exprobrantis*, "at the voice of him that reproacheth"] He touches the derogators in what he says elsewhere: *They were catching at the soul of the just, and were condemning innocent blood [n: (Psal. XCIII)]*. [var: Vulg. *Captabunt … condemnabunt*, "they shall catch … they shall condemn"] He expresses the detractors when he says: *They that render evil for good detracted from me, because I followed goodness [n: (Psal. XXXVII)]*. Behold, thou hast the three rows of teeth in the manifoldness of detractions, derogations, speakings-against.

## CHAPTER X. *How from the vice of enviousness the mind falls into the vice of fraudulence.*

Now it is easy to see how rightly there befits this [1357D] beast that which is set in the last place when it is said: Arise, eat much flesh. For what is it to eat flesh, but to tear another's weak things with a slanderous tooth? For they who gladly gnaw another's life, who zealously serve derogations, who delightedly pluck at the infirmities of their neighbours, what else do they but eat flesh? Such gnawers of flesh the Prophet had already known by experience, when he said: *They that harm me draw near upon me, to eat my flesh [n: (Psal. XXVI)]*. But that which is said, Arise, eat, what, I ask, will it be, a command or a permission, a suggestion or an instigation? But if we think it was said by good angels, let us consequently understand that in their words also only a permission was made. Such is that which is said to Judas [1358A]: *That which thou dost, do quickly [n: (Joan. XIII)]*. But if it stands that it was said by evil angels, we can understand in words of this kind both a suggestion and an instigation. For evil spirits are ever wont to suggest malignant things to malignant minds, and to instigate them unto every malice. How malicious a thing it is, and how pernicious, to serve detractions, and in this for each man to satisfy his own vice, thou canst note from that sentence of Solomon: *If a serpent bite in silence, he that detracteth secretly hath nothing the less [n: (Eccli. X)]*. Does not that accord with this sentence which the Psalmist says of such men: *Their throat is an open sepulchre, with their tongues they acted deceitfully, the venom of asps is under their lips [n: (Psal. V)]*. O evil execrable, and deadly as much to him that speaks as to him that hears: [1358B] Arise, he says, eat much flesh. And what else does the bear, what else does this wild brute know, but either to suck its own paws, or to tear the flesh of others? What other office, I say, does the monster of enviousness discharge, but either to bewail its own troubles, or to pluck at the weak things of others? But would that they tore the flesh only, would that they spared at least the bones! But now they mangle all things, they tear all things, whence it comes about that at length at some time they become hateful to all. For he who speaks, I say, cannot lie hidden. But when they begin to be recognized, they begin likewise to be despised and to be held in hatred. But an envious and proud mind, with how great a grief thinkest thou it is burned, with how great a spite is it tormented, when it sees others advance from day to day unto better things, and itself daily decrease in every opinion, and grow more and more [1358C] worthless? Whence it often comes about that it turns itself to crafty devices, while with the utmost zeal and solicitude it seeks how it may either spread wide its own glory, or becloud the glory of others. It begins therefore from thenceforth to dissemble sanctity, and through hypocrisy little by little to give itself over to every fraudulence. Thus the leopard succeeds the bear, while a slippery mind flows down from vice to vice, and through the evil of enviousness falls into fraudulence.
