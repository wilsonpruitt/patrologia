## CHAPTER XV. *On the vice of shamelessness.*

Give heed, I beseech thee, how bestial, and more [1361B] than bestial, a life it is, when human shame has been cast off, to give oneself headlong to all things, and the divine gaze or the countenances of men, not to blush even at things to be blushed at, and at length at some time to unbridle oneself unto every malice. For what else is shamelessness than a headlong and unbridled malice? Let us therefore now hear what is said of this last beast: *After these things I was beholding,* he says, *in the vision of the night, and lo, a fourth beast, terrible and wonderful, and exceeding strong. It had great iron teeth, eating and breaking in pieces, and treading down the rest with its feet. But it was unlike to the other beasts which I had seen before it, and it had ten horns [n: (Dan. VII)]*. Behold, he says a beast, and yet what beast it is, or to what [1361C] beast it is like, he does not say, as in the foregoing. See therefore what kind of monster among the vices this is, which is now mystically described, to which none among so many monsters of beasts is found comparable. Now we know that the mind which has been corrupted by envy and by shamelessness spares thenceforth no one at all, since it spares neither itself nor others. For envy indeed takes away from a man love and compassion for his neighbour; but shamelessness, moderation and circumspection of himself. And often they that are of this sort bear many labours, many griefs, patiently, nay even gladly, that they may afflict others; and they had rather themselves endure misery than not inflict it upon others. See therefore how wonderful, or rather how horrible a thing it is, that a man, forgetful of humanity, should put off the man, and put on a beastly heart, nay one more than beastly. [1361D] And lo, he says, a fourth beast, terrible and wonderful, and exceeding strong. Is it not strong, and exceeding strong, which, for the sake of inflicting evils upon its neighbours, is so patient in bearing evils? But he is said to have iron teeth and nails who is unbridled unto every cruelty. For iron, which tames all the other metals, rightly enough designates cruelty. He therefore receives iron teeth and nails who rages in words and in works against his neighbours, Of another beast it was said that it devoured very much flesh. But of this one it is said that it breaks all things in pieces. It therefore which breaks all things in pieces seizes, condemns, annihilates not only the weak things of others, but the strong as well. And so it tears all things by defamation, and what it cannot defame it treads down by contempt. But that I may be silent about the rest, [1362A] in this chiefly does this beast seem to be unlike the others, that in this last state the wicked mind knows not how to blush. It was unlike, he says, to the other beasts which I had seen before it, and it had ten horns.

## CHAPTER XVI. *How the mind is hardened through the vice of shamelessness, and first against God.*

O bloody beast, which both by contempt treads down all its inferiors, and with the horns of rebellion assails its superiors! Would that of horns of this kind might be fulfilled that which he who speaks in the psalm profitably threatens: *And I will break all the horns of sinners, and I will exalt the horns of the just [n: (Psal. LXXIV)]*. [var: Vulg. Ps. 74:11 *exaltabuntur cornua justi*, "the horns of the just shall be exalted"; the received text is passive, not a first-person promise] Let us therefore ask more diligently what sort of horns shamelessness has, [1362B] with which it is wont to fight back or to assail. But this we know, that horns grow out of the flesh, and harden above the sensibility of the flesh. Now in ten ways, as it seems to me, the shameless soul grows hard, and loses that sensibility of heart which it is fitting to have. For in three ways the mind grows hard toward God, in three toward itself, and in four ways against its neighbour. Toward God, through arrogance, through self-exaltation, through contumacy. Arrogance is when it ascribes to itself that which it has received from God. Self-exaltation is when it lifts itself above that which it has received: through arrogance it glories in itself over what it has, through self-exaltation even over what it has not. Through arrogance and self-exaltation [1362C] it comes about that the divine grace is deservedly withdrawn, and that it is hardened unto contumacy; and being deserted, it is made a transgressor. But there is never a willing transgression of a precept without contempt of God. Contumacy therefore is when it despises God commanding and contemns him threatening. Through arrogance the goodness of God is assailed, through self-exaltation the truth of God, through contumacy the power of God. For certain we know, and without any hesitation, that it is God who worketh all things according to good will [n: (Ephes. I)]. [var: Vulg. Eph. 1:11 reads *qui operatur omnia secundum consilium voluntatis suae*, "who worketh all things according to the counsel of his will"; *pro bona voluntate* is the phrase of Phil. 2:13] And so from the goodness of God alone is whatever of good is, or can be, in us. He therefore who arrogates to himself that whereby he ought to have glorified God, what else does he do than despoil the goodness of God of its glory? He who lifts himself above that which he is wills that to be believed false which is true, and [1362D] true which is false. But he who leans upon falsehood is convicted of striving against the truth of God. But whatever the wicked mind presumes through contumacy looks chiefly to the contumely of the divine power.

## CHAPTER XVII. *How through the vice of shamelessness, the mind grows hard in regard to itself.*

By three steps of headlong fall, nonetheless, the human spirit is wont to grow stiff in regard to itself: by the binding of the mind, by the hardening of the mind, by the unbridling of the mind. The binding of the mind is when a habitual delight so insolubly entangles it that it cannot, even when it wills, entangle itself from the custom of sin; when it now does not at all cease from sinning, so far as impunity permits it. See how him whom arrogance [1363A] despoils, self-exaltation enervates; and him whom self-exaltation enervates, contumacy casts headlong; and him whom contumacy casts headlong into sinning, the custom of sinning afterwards little by little binds. To such a soul, and so bound, that voice of the prophet cries: *Loose the bonds of thy neck, O captive daughter of Sion [n: (Isa. LII)]*. Loosed from such bonds and free, it can sing with the Psalmist: *Thou hast broken my bonds, to thee will I sacrifice the sacrifice of praise [n: (Psal. CXV)]*. But the hardening of the heart is when he that is of this sort can now be restrained from his own excesses by fear of no punishment, by no severity of vengeance. And, that we may use open examples, the shameful know what condemnation they undergo, or ought to undergo, when caught in their shameful deeds, and yet neither are they able to restrain themselves from the perpetration of shameful deeds. It is one thing not to be able to restrain one's own desire [sic: *desidorium*] [1363B] when impunity allures, and another not to be able to beware even of that which is not committed without the utmost peril. In the former step, therefore, the spirit is insolubly bound to fault; in this one it is hardened, as it were insensibly, against punishment. In that one shame, in this one fear also, is trodden underfoot. Hear what is said of those who in this step are hardened against punishment: *I have struck,* he says, *them, and they grieved not [n: (Jer. V)]*. [var: Vulg. Jer. 5:3 *Percussisti eos*, "Thou hast struck them"; the received text addresses God in the second person, and the same verse is printed at 1276C of this work as *Percussi filios vestros*] The unbridling of the mind is when without any hesitation or drawing back the spirit exposes itself to every transgression, and, the bit of shame and the bridle of fear being shaken off, rushes headlong into all things, and becomes thenceforth as the horse and the mule which have no intellect [n: (Psal. XXXI)]. In the two former the spirit, by its own desires, even when it resists, [1363C] is overcome. But in this step it does not resist at all, but, whatever wind of temptation blows, is snatched in every direction, according to that which the prophet imprecates: *My God, set them as a wheel, and as stubble before the face of the wind [n: (Psal. LXXXII)]*. But as in the three first horns it assails God, so in the three following it fights back against God. For by the binding, the hardening, the unbridling of itself, the ill-fortified mind strongly resists, lest any impulse of divine fear should be able easily to humble it.

## CHAPTER XVIII. *How through the vice of shamelessness, the mind grows hard toward its neighbour.*

Four still remain of the aforesaid horns: the horn [1363D] of abjection, the horn of objection, the horn of dejection, the horn of subjection. By these four steps the wicked mind is hardened against its neighbour, and, what is worse, against those to whom it owes a humble service. For as with iron nails it treads down its inferiors, so with these four horns of rebellion it is armed against its superiors. For the shameless soul, which has once shaken off the yoke of fear, rises up now with wicked rashness against its own mastery. And so it rebels with the horn of abjection, when it now disdains to obey; with the horn of objection, when it strives always and everywhere strongly to resist the endeavours of its own prelates; with the horn of dejection, when it strives to cast down its own prelates; with the horn of subjection, when it now strives even to subject them to itself. Behold how the evil [1364A] rages abroad; behold by what steps the depraved soul is hardened unto the utmost shamelessness; behold in what manner a man is brought even to this, that *he neither fears God nor reverences man [n: (Luc. XVIII)]*. [var: Vulg. Luc. 18:2 *qui Deum non timebat, et hominem non reverebatur*, "who feared not God, nor regarded man"]
