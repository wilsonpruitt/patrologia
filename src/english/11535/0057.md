## CHAPTER XIX. *How the mind, when it has come into the depth of evils, often falls even into despair.*

But the depraved soul often, when it has come into the depth of evils, after all evils loses even the hope of coming to itself again, and falls into despair. This is that last horn, namely the evil of despair; a horn, I say, both little through diffidence, and, beyond the rest, hard through the obstinacy of diffidence. *I was considering,* he says, *the horns, and lo, another little horn sprang out of the midst of them, and three of [1364B] the first horns were plucked up before its face. And behold, eyes as it were the eyes of a man were in this horn, and a mouth speaking great things [n: (Dan. VII)]*. This horn arises out of the midst of the others, because by the binding of the heart, the hardening of the heart, and unbridling, the mind is chiefly cast headlong into despair. For when out of these three a man has begun to lose power over himself, he begins likewise little by little to despair of salvation. But the evil of despair renders the spirit not so much swollen as timid, and in that very thing casts down the horn of contumacy. The evil of despair is wont not so much to lift the spirit up as to cast it down, and therefore the horn of self-exaltation cannot stand before its face. The evil of despair makes the spirit the more disparaging of its own powers, because it always [1364C] compels it to fear more than is fitting: whence it is that the horn of arrogance is not able to stand before its presence. The soul that has once despaired of its own salvation, and is utterly forsaken by divine grace, when it feels that it cannot resist an inveterate custom, and cannot restrain itself from its own depravity, is often wont to show itself excusable, and to pour back its own fault upon the Creator. And this is for the horn of despair to have eyes as it were of a man, and a mouth speaking great things. Man alone among the other living things is strong in reason and shrewd in prudence. When therefore, after the evil of despair, anyone has begun cunningly to weave, and shrewdly to put forward, for the proof of his own excuse, causes as it were reasonable, he seems to have eyes as it were of a man. It must be, they say, as it has been predestined; [1364D] who can resist the will of God, who his ordinance? And such as he willed us, such he made us. For whatsoever he would he hath done, in heaven and in earth, in the sea and in all the deeps [n: (Psal. CXXXIV)]. [var: Vulg. Ps. 134:6 *Omnia quaecumque voluit Dominus fecit in caelo, in terra, in mari, et in omnibus abyssis*: the received text names the Lord as the subject, and does not join the members with *et*] What then can we do, what are we? Can we create our own merits for ourselves? It is not, assuredly, of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that showeth mercy [n: (Rom. IX)]. Why then does he not show mercy? Why does he not work in us that which pleases him, who worketh all things in all as he wills? [n: (Ephes. I.)] [var: Vulg. Eph. 1:11 reads *qui operatur omnia secundum consilium voluntatis suae*; the words printed here are those of I Cor. 12:6 and 12:11, *qui operatur omnia in omnibus … dividens singulis prout vult*] A mouth speaking great things, and things as mad as they are empty. That thou mayest excuse thyself, thou accusest God; that thou mayest justify thyself, thou blasphemest God? Is not this seen to be that which is read in what follows, and he shall speak words against the Most High. [var: Vulg. Dan. 7:25 *sermones contra Excelsum loquetur*; Migne prints *Altissimum* for *Excelsum*] Behold by what steps one descends to the lowest things. Behold whence, [1365A] whither, and in what manner one comes from an excessive levity of spirit to a shameless unbridling of spirit, from much presumption of heart to the last despair.

We have heard how the mind, falling ever from evil into worse, is at last led down into the depth of evils. Let us hear how, or whence, that same mind is sometimes, after many and last evils, reformed to the integrity of rightness. For if we rightly judge the things that go before and that follow after in this vision, I think that in this mystical description we recognize what we read in the Apostle: Since where [1366A] iniquity abounded, grace also did more abound. [var: Vulg. Rom. 5:20 *Ubi autem abundavit delictum, superabundavit gratia*, "where the offence abounded"; the noun printed here is *iniquitas*, that of Matt. 24:12] From this place of Scripture it is easy to observe that no malice of ours can conquer the divine mercy. For what is it that, after the beasts succeeding one another, a throne being set, the Ancient of days sat, unless that after many and frequent declines of the vices, he who is eternal before all times, through visiting grace, fixes the slippery heart in a firm stability of purpose, and establishes for himself a dwelling? And what is it that the power of the beasts is taken away, unless that through visiting grace, the tyranny of the vices being lulled to sleep, the kingdom of sin is destroyed?
