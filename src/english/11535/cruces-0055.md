# Cruces — 11535, chunks 0055–0057 (cols 1358D–1366A)

Richard of St Victor, *De eruditione hominis interioris*, Book III, CAPUT XI–XIX.
This range holds the **third and fourth beasts of Daniel 7** (the leopard; the fourth beast
with iron teeth and ten horns) and the **little horn with the eyes of a man** — and it
**closes the work**.

---

## A. ⭐ The ending of the work — read this first

**The treatise does not close its argument. It breaks off mid-exposition.**

- The last printed words, at **1366A**, are:
  *Et quid est quod bestiarum potestas aufertur, nisi, quod per visitantem gratiam,
  vitiorum tyrannide sopita, regnum peccati destruitur?*
  → "And what is it that the power of the beasts is taken away, unless that through
  visiting grace, the tyranny of the vices being lulled to sleep, the kingdom of sin is
  destroyed?"
- **It is not truncated mid-sentence.** That sentence is grammatically complete and closes
  with the question mark Migne prints. Nothing is broken at the plate's edge.
- **It IS truncated mid-exposition, and demonstrably so from the text's own promise.** The
  final paragraph opens by announcing the restoration half of the argument —
  *Audiamus quomodo, vel unde eadem ipsa quandoque, post multa et ultima mala, ad
  rectitudinis integritatem reformatur* — quotes Rom. 5:20 as its programme, and then
  delivers only **two** of the *Quid est quod …?* questions on Dan. 7:9–12 (the throne set
  and the Ancient of days; the beasts' dominion taken away). It never reaches the Son of
  man, and it never reaches **the kingdom that shall not be destroyed** — which is the
  figure the whole work is built toward, expounded at length from Dan. 2:44 in Book I
  (chunk 0027, col. 1288D) and promised again here. The chapter head standing over this
  material is still `CAPUT XIX. *Quomodo mens cum in profundum malorum venerit, saepe
  etiam in desperationem cadit.*` — a chapter on **despair**, under which the restoration
  paragraph sits with no head of its own.
- **Chunk 0057 is 552 Latin words against a work median near 1,000**, consistent with a
  final chunk that stops early rather than one that fills its columns.
- ⚑ **This is worth a sentence in the site's "On this text" note**: the work as Migne
  prints it ends unfinished, and a reader who reaches 1366A expecting the restoration
  Richard has just promised will otherwise think our text is defective.

**Column-band note, checked and NOT reported as a finding.** The anchors run
`… [1364D] [1365A] [1366A]`, skipping 1365B–D, and only ~55 Latin words stand between
1365A and 1366A. I checked it against runbook false-positive 1 (*column-band arithmetic*)
and the text **does not break mid-word or mid-construction** across the boundary —
`quod in Apostolo legimus: Quoniam ubi abundavit [1366A] iniquitas, superabundavit et
gratia` reads continuously. So: no `[ed:]`, no tei-patch, no plate check. Recorded here
only so a later reader does not re-open it.

- **0021 @1276C:** *Percussi **filios vestros**, et non doluerunt, renuerunt recipere

`[var:]` already covers its own printing, correctly; I have not duplicated its content.

## E. Markers fired for defective type

### E.1 `[sic: *exerescit*]` @1358D — *fraus in fraudulentiam exerescit*
Non-word. **Control run:** across the whole PL TEI (5,276 files, 87M words) *exerescit*
occurs in **one** file — this one, at this site — against **113 files** printing
*excrescit*. That is the corpus's own verdict: plate defect, not a period spelling.
**7a⁗ check:** the marked run is the clause's only verb, so the English says the sense in
plain words beside the marker — "fraud **grows** [sic: *exerescit*] into fraudulence".
Conjecture: *excrescit*.

### E.2 `[sic: *eum*]` @1359A — *Certe **eum** volucres volant*
A real word with **no grammatical slot**: an accusative pronoun in a clause whose verb
(*volant*) is intransitive and which already has its subject. Pattern 12's test 1. The
next sentence is the parallel *Certe **cum** volare volunt*, which makes *cum* certain.
**7a⁗ check, question 3:** the marked run carries the clause's conjunction, so the English
supplies it — "Certainly **when** [sic: *eum*] birds fly". Without that the sentence loses
its subordination entirely.

## F. Vocabulary calls, including one I want ratified for the whole work

### F.1 ⭐⭐ *simulatio* vs *dissimulatio* — the ratified equivalence CANNOT hold in this chunk, and I want a work-wide ruling

`cruces-0020.md` §7 fixed ***simulatio* = "dissimulation"** (beside the brief's ratified
*simultas* = "pretense"), and chunks 0018–0024 ship it that way. **CAPUT XII of my range
breaks it**, because at 1359C Migne sets the two nouns side by side and makes the
distinction between them the whole point of the chapter:

> *Simulatio et dissimulatio est de occultis. Sed **simulatio** de occultis **bonis**,
> **dissimulatio** vero de occultis **malis**.*

Four wings, four vices — *simulationis, dissimulationis, ostentationis, excusationis* — and
*simulatio* is feigning a good one does **not** have while *dissimulatio* is hiding an evil
one **does**. Under the ratified equivalence, *dissimulatio* has nowhere to go and the
chapter's four-fold scheme collapses to three. This is the *testa*/*fictilis* situation
exactly: two words printed side by side, the exposition turning on which is which, so they
must never land on one English word.

**What I did, and why:** in this range ***simulatio* = "simulation"**, ***dissimulatio* =
"dissimulation"**, *simulator* = "simulator", *simulatrix mens* = "simulating mind".
English's own simulation/dissimulation pair carries Richard's distinction precisely
(feigning what is not / concealing what is), which is more than any substitute would.

**The ratification I am asking for (merge-step decision, not mine to take):** sweep
***simulatio* → "simulation" work-wide**, freeing "dissimulation" for *dissimulatio*, and
leaving *simultas* = "pretense" untouched. That touches **six shipped sites**, all rendering
*simulatio* as "dissimulation" — 0018 (1270A), 0019 ×2 (1272B area, 1273B), 0020 (1274A),
0024 (1283B), 0027 (1289C) — plus the *simulatum* / *simulati* participles at 1283D and
1289C, which already read "simulated" and would need no change. The alternative —
leaving the work with "dissimulation" meaning two opposite things in two places — is worse.
If the ruling goes the other way, **CAPUT XII still cannot use one word for both**, and
whatever is chosen for *dissimulatio* here has to be recorded in the brief.

### F.3 Followed the fixed vocabulary against my own judgment
**1363B, Ps. 31:9** — *sicut equus et mulus quibus non est **intellectus***. The brief's
ratified item 3 gives ***intellectus* = intellect** (reserving "understanding" for
*intelligentia*), so the psalm reads "as the horse and the mule **which have no
intellect**". Every instinct wanted the traditional "understanding" here, and the
scripture-echo licence would arguably have allowed it. I followed the table, because the
psalm is quoted **inside the definition of *mentis effrenatio*** — a psychology passage,
which is exactly the seam the fixed table exists to protect. Logging it as a
followed-against-judgment call in case the merge wants the licence applied instead.

### F.4 The four -*jectio* horns @1363D
*cornu abjectionis, objectionis, dejectionis, subjectionis* — Richard builds a four-member
figura on one root and glosses each member on the spot. I rendered them **abjection,
objection, dejection, subjection**, which reproduces the figure exactly. ⚠ "Abjection" is
being used in its **etymological** sense (casting off — his gloss is *quando jam dedignatur
obedire*), not the modern English sense of degradation. The gloss follows immediately in
every case, so the reader is not left to guess; recorded here because a later reader might
otherwise think it a mistranslation.

## G. Passages that will not construe (rendered as literally as the words allow)

1. **1361B** — *…ad omnia se praecipitem dare, **et conspectum divinum vel vultus hominum**,
   nec in erubescendis erubescere…* The accusatives *conspectum divinum vel vultus hominum*
   have **no governing verb**; something like *non revereri* is wanting. Rendered with the
   hole visible: "and the divine gaze or the countenances of men, not to blush even at
   things to be blushed at". **No marker** — nothing here is defective *type*, and a
   `[sic:]` would have to swallow a whole clause, which §"Scripture and garbled type" of
   the brief forbids.
2. **1360C** — *Docet nos **ille illius** Achitopel loquens quid in quibusdam possit
   ambitio auctoritatis.* *ille illius* will not construe with a genitive proper name and a
   participle. The likely intention is *illud illius Achitophel **consilium*** ("that
   counsel of that Achitophel"), which would match the balancing clause *docet nos proditor
   et parricida Absalon*. Rendered as printed: "That man of that Achitopel, speaking,
   teaches us…". Conjecture recorded here only.
3. **1360D** — *majora sunt pene quae hypocritae facere possunt, et faciunt et districtioris
   vitae observantias custodiunt, **quam** hi qui se propter Deum affligunt*. The
   comparative *majora … quam* has two finite clauses shoved between its members, so the
   *quam* clause has no clean term to compare with. Rendered as printed.
4. **1363A** — *ut se a peccati consuetudine **implicare**, etiam cum velit, non valeat*.
   The verb should be *explicare* ("disentangle"); as printed the clause says the mind
   cannot **entangle** itself from the custom of sin, which is the opposite of the sentence's
   point (and the printed *implicat* four words earlier is what *explicare* would answer).
   Rendered as printed — "cannot … entangle itself from the custom of sin". **Declined as a
   `[cj:]`**: see §H.
5. **1362B** — *cornua a carne excrescunt, et **supra carnis sensibilitate** obdurescunt*.
   *supra* + ablative. Rendered "harden above the sensibility of the flesh". Pattern 9
   territory at most; no marker.
6. **1361D** — *…qui verbis et operibus in proximos saevit**,** De alia bestia dictum est…*
   Migne prints a **comma** and then a capital *D* beginning a new sentence. Pattern 8:
   preserved exactly, comma and capital both. The English therefore reads
   "…against his neighbours, Of another beast it was said…". Not our punctuation.

## H. Conjectures DECLINED — census candidates, named as the brief asks

Wilson's `[cj:]` bar (2026-08-15) glosses only a **negation or a correlative**. None of
these is either; all four are named here rather than widened into the running text.

1. ⭐ **1360C — *aspirare **ab** subdiaconatum*.** A real word, wrongly chosen: *ab* governs
   the ablative, *subdiaconatum* is accusative, and the two parallel members that follow in
   the same sentence both print *ad* (*subdiaconos **ad** diaconatum, diaconos **ad**
   presbyteratum*). **Control run:** *ad subdiaconatum* stands in **17** PL files; *ab
   subdiaconatum* in **one** — this. So *ad* is near-certain. I rendered the printed word:
   "those who are of a lower grade **aspiring from** the subdiaconate", which is fluent and
   says the opposite of the sentence's own gradient. **This is the nearest thing to a
   Pattern 18 site in my range** — it is exactly the *munde*/*mundo* shape, a real word
   whose faithful English asserts something the author is arguing against. It is declined
   because a preposition of direction is neither a negation nor a correlative, and the bar
   is not mine to widen. If the census ever reaches for a preposition class, **this site
   should be its worked example.**
2. **1363A — *implicare* for *explicare*** (§G.4). A prefix, not a negation. Declined.
3. **1362C/1363B — *mentis obduratio* announced, *cordis obduratio* delivered.** CAPUT XVII
   sets out the three steps as *mentis obligatione, mentis obduratione, mentis effrenatione*
   and then defines the second as *Cordis autem obduratio est…*; 0057 @1364B looks back at
   all three as *cordis obligatione, cordis obduratione, et effrenatione*. Rendered as
   printed each time — "of the mind" where Migne writes *mentis*, "of the heart" where he
   writes *cordis*. ⚠ **This one matters for the fixed vocabulary**, since *mens* and *cor*
   are separately fixed terms in this work and the plate is inconsistent *inside a single
   scheme*. Not a defect I can mark; recorded so the inconsistency is attributed to Migne.
4. **1362C — *desertus … praevaricator efficitur*, and 1362B *tribus apud semetipsum***.
   Masculine agreement where the subject is *mens*/*anima* (feminine). Pattern 9: Latin
   grammatical gender has **no English exponent**. No emendation to make or undo; flagged so
   a later sweep does not churn it.
