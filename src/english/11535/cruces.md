# 11535 — work-wide conventions, settled at the merge

Richard of St Victor (attrib.), *De eruditione hominis interioris*, PL 196, 1229D–1366A.
58 chunks, twelve agents, two rounds. **This section heads the merged `cruces.md`; read it before
changing any word of the English.** What follows is what was fixed and why, so a later sweep does
not churn it.

## The seam this work turns on

***mens* = mind · *animus* = spirit · *anima* = soul · *spiritus* = spirit.** 11536 flattened
*mens* and *animus* both to "mind" because in that work they never collided. **Here they collide
inside single clauses** — chunk 0030's own chapter head is *Quod quidquid **animum** per
cogitationem pulsat, **mentis** devotionem excitare debeat* — so the flattening was not available
and the four words are kept three ways (*animus* and *spiritus* share "spirit").

*animus* and *spiritus* collide in exactly **two** sentences in the whole work, and both were
handled deliberately, by opposite reasoning that reaches the same English:

- **0005 @1244A** — *ex animi impetu* beside *ex spiritus sui impetu*. Richard's *eodem*/*eamdem*
  assert these are **the same** impulse; rendering them differently would silently deny the
  identity his own correlatives assert.
- **0034 @1309A** — *animum humanum Deo conjungit, et cum eo quasi unum spiritum efficit*
  (1 Cor. 6:17). Here the force is **conclusion**, not identity: what is joined *becomes* one
  spirit, and rendering *animus* "mind" would leave *unum spiritum efficit* nothing to attach to.

### ⭐ *simultas* = pretense — how it was settled, since it overturns a classical dictionary sense

Not by ear. The metals allegory reads *in auro charitas, in argento veritas, in **aere simultas**,
in ferro crudelitas, in testa fictili fragilitas* — a moral descent in which "enmity" sits oddly
between truth and cruelty — and **1258D** prints *semetipsum in omnibus **sine simultate** accusat*,
accusing oneself *without dissembling*, which "without enmity" does not say. Two agents had
rendered the word two ways (0018 "enmity", 0024 "pretense"); the second was right.
⚑ **This also WITHDREW a Pattern 18 census candidate at 1269D.** That site was filed as the
range's strongest candidate on the reasoning that the plate glosses the slot with *simulatio* — but
if *simultas* already carries that sense, **the word is not a defect at all.** One agent's crux was
answered by another agent's vocabulary call, which is what the merge is for.

### ⚠ Two ratifications NARROWED at the merge, and it is worth saying why

Both were mine, and both were solving problems this work does not have:

1. ***intellectus* = "intellect".** Ruled to keep it apart from *intelligentia* = "understanding".
   **The two words never once occur in the same sentence** — zero collisions in 58 chunks — so the
   seam needed no defending, and the cost was real: 0038 read "In the intellects of his hands he
   led them" (Ps. 77:72) and "shall declare intellect" (Sir. 34:9). Reverted to "understanding"
   there. The agent had followed the ruling and reported the cost rather than quietly reverting,
   which is how it came to light.
2. ***scientia* = "learning" vs *cognitio* = "knowledge".** They collide in exactly **one** place
   in the work — CAPUT XVI's own head, *Quibus gradibus mysteriorum **cognitio** proficit, et ad
   quantam **scientiae** perfectionem…* — which is preserved distinct ("the knowledge of mysteries
   … a perfection of learning"). Elsewhere *scientia* keeps the natural "knowledge". A blanket
   sweep of 65 sites would have bought nothing.

**The general rule both cases teach:** a fixed-vocabulary pair earns its rigidity only where the
two words actually meet. Check for collisions before ruling, not after.

### Declared exception: retained Latin is italicized where the plate prints roman

Seven spans in 0052–0054 (*insolitum* / *non solitum* / *insolentia*; *invidens* / *invidentia*;
*rogare* / *derogare*). These are **etymological mentions** — the Latin word is retained in English
prose and glossed beside it — and the plate prints them roman because in a Latin text they needed
no marking. In English a bare Latin word has no exponent at all, which is Pattern 12's own logic
(*normalize the language; mark the page*). **Kept, and declared here rather than done silently.**
⬜ **Wilson can reverse this in one line**; it is the only break from 1:1 italic structure in the
work.

### The oath exception

**1324A** *cum sacramento asseverant* means **an oath**, in parallel with *cum juramento* in the
previous clause; rendered "under solemn oath" beside "with an oath". *sacramentum* = "sacrament"
stands everywhere else, and 0029's drift to "this secret" for Dan. 2:47's *sacramentum hoc* was
reconciled at the merge (0014 prints the same Dan. 2 phrase and reads "sacrament").

### ⭐ The plate is reliable when it QUOTES and unreliable when it RE-QUOTES

**Give the polarity read the re-quotations first.**

## Conventions set here for the whole work (peer agents follow these)

**Heads.** `## LIBER PRIMUS.` → `## BOOK ONE.` · `## CAPUT PRIMUM.` → `## CHAPTER ONE.` ·
every numbered head keeps the plate's own numeral (`## CAPUT II.` → `## CHAPTER II.`, never
renumbered, never normalized to a word). The italic summary is translated and stays italic; the
plate's terminal period is kept where it prints one. The three blank lines the plate leaves between
`LIBER PRIMUS.` and `CAPUT PRIMUM.` in 0000 are mirrored 1:1 (Pattern 8a).

**The name.** ***Nabuchodonosor*, not "Nebuchadnezzar", throughout.** Chapter I turns on two
etymologies of the name — *prophetans istiusmodi signum* (1231D) and *sessio in agnitione
angustiae* (1232A, 1233A) — and an English form leaves both glosses explaining nothing, which is
exactly the case the brief's etymology rule and Pattern-7 §2 (*stylus*/*epistylium*) cover. The
corpus is split (4 shipped works print *Nabuchodonosor*, 7 print *Nebuchadnezzar*), so this is a
per-work call and it is **submitted for ratification**. Other names take their received English
(Daniel, Chaldeans, Jerome, Gregory, David, Uriah, Babylon, Syria).

**Vocabulary added to the fixed table** (proposed for the whole work):
*angustia* = **anguish** — the hook the etymology hangs on (*sessio in agnitione angustiae* ·
*quam sine angustia nosse non poterat* · *cum dolore et animi angustia*) ·
*conjectura* = **conjecture**, kept distinct from *interpretatio* = **interpretation** (Migne prints
both in the Daniel lemma and Richard glosses only the second) ·
*publicare* = **to make public**, never "confiscate" (Douay), because Richard's gloss is built on it:
*Quid autem est sapientium domos publicare, et ad communem omnium usum redigere?* ·
*conatus* = **endeavour** · *instantia* = **urgency** · *pudentia* = **modesty** (see crux 28).

## Markers fired (7 sites)

### `[var: …]` — Pattern 14, scripture divergence (3)

**1. 1233A · Rom. 13:1 · `[var:]` FIRED**
Printed: *Quae autem a Deo sunt,* teste Apostolo, *ordinata sunt [n: (Rom. XIII)]*.
Vulgate: *quae autem sunt, a Deo ordinatae sunt.*
Migne draws the agent *a Deo* into the relative clause and prints the neuter *ordinata* for the
feminine *ordinatae*. **Material**: the Vulgate says the existing powers are ordained *by God*;
Migne's line says the things that are *from* God are ordered — and that is precisely the load
Richard puts on it, the unjust king's *voluntas inordinata* against his *potestas ordinata*. The
preceding *Non est enim potestas nisi a Deo* is printed roman, unmarked, and agrees with the
Vulgate. Rendered as printed; the received reading is in the marker.

**2. 1234A · Dan. 2:2 · `[var:]` FIRED**
Printed: *Qui cum venissent, statuerunt coram rege.* Vulgate: *steterunt.*
*statuerunt* is transitive and leaves the clause with no object, so the faithful English ("set up
before the king") states something the received text does not.
**Control run:** `statuerunt coram rege` occurs **once** in the whole PL TEI (this file); `steterunt
coram rege` also once. But *statuerunt* itself is a common word — **1,005 occurrences** — so this is
a real word wrongly printed, not broken type: Pattern 12 has nothing to wrap. Pattern 18 is excluded
by its own rule that a divergence inside a quoted scripture belongs to Pattern 14.

**3. 1240A · Luke 17:21 · `[var:]` FIRED**
Printed: *Regnum caelorum,* inquit, *intra vos est [n: (Luc. XVII)]*. Vulgate: *regnum Dei intra
vos est.* Migne's own note cites Luc. XVII, so the substitution is on the plate, not in the
reference. **Material**: Richard is arguing that the interior kingdom is a *kingdom*, and
*caelorum* pulls the verse into the orbit of the Matt. 5:3 beatitude he quotes four lines later —
which prints the other spelling, *coelorum*, in the same chunk (crux 39).

### `[cj: …]` — Pattern 18, real word, faithful English misleads (2)

**7. 1231B · *quam* stranded without its comparative · `[cj: *quam*; read *facilius quam*, "more easily than"]`**
Printed: *contemplativae vitae gratia **quam** activae elabitur, sed multo difficilius reparatur.*
As printed the sentence has a *quam* with nothing to compare — it does not construe at all.
**Richard supplies the missing word himself, twice, within eight lines:** *somnii visionem quam tam
**facile** amisit* (1231B) and *a contemplativa **facilius** decidimus* (1231C).
⚠ **Bar note, flagged for ratification.** Wilson's 2026-08-15 bar admits a wrongly-printed
**negation or correlative**, and a stranded comparative correlative is the second class; the 7561
@0648 precedent (*solum* for *non solum*) establishes that a **dropped** word is glossable, because
the gloss opens on the word Migne *did* print. I have not widened the bar beyond that. If the
ruling is read narrower than I have read it, this is the one marker in my range to withdraw.

**8. 1239A · *volebant* for *nolebant* · `[cj: *volebant*; read *nolebant*, "were unwilling"]`**
Printed: *Ignorantiam suam dissimulabant, et aperte fateri **volebant**, et in hoc tempus
redimebant.* The printed reading contradicts its own sentence twice: they *dissembled* their
ignorance, and they were *buying up the time* — which is what one does precisely in order **not**
to confess. Richard's whole chapter is *quam multis modis animus seipsum decipit*. This is the
11547 @0260D defect (*nolebant* for *volebant*) with the polarity reversed, and the word that went
wrong is the negation itself — squarely inside the ratified bar.

## A. The *animus* / *spiritus* collision — 0005 @1244A (flagged in the launch brief)

**Printed:** *…quod meminit se prius per **spiritus sui** impetum statuisse? Multa enim …
quae prius **ex animi impetu**, et quasi praecipitanter definivit … et multa saepe **ex eodem
spiritus sui impetu** homo contra conscientiam suam dictat, et tamen **eamdem animi sui**
praecipitationem … temperat.*

**What was done:** both words rendered **spirit**, per the fixed vocabulary table, with no
disambiguating gloss and no marker.

**Why — and this is the point:** the collision here is **benign, and the table is what makes
Richard legible.** The sentence turns on *eodem* and *eamdem*: Richard is explicitly asserting
that the *spiritus sui impetus* and the *animi impetus* are **the same impulse**, and that the
*animi praecipitatio* is **the same headlong rush**. Rendering the two words differently
("mind"/"spirit", or "soul"/"spirit") would silently deny the identity his own correlatives
assert, and the reader would meet two faculties where Richard has one. So the one-English-word
outcome the table produces is not a loss here; it is the reading. No tension to escalate.

(For the record, the four sites in order: 1244A *per spiritus sui impetum* → "through the
impulse of its own spirit" · *ex animi impetu* → "out of the impulse of its spirit" ·
*ex eodem spiritus sui impetu* → "out of that same impulse of his spirit" ·
*eamdem animi sui praecipitationem* → "that same headlong haste of his spirit".)

⚠ Note for the merge: *mens* is kept visibly distinct as **mind** throughout this range
(0005 *studiosa mens*, *compuncta mens*; 0006 *devota mens*; 0007 *mens studiosa*), and *anima*

## 2. The Vulgate-divergence list — `[var: …]` fired (6)

Every italic span named in my launch message was read word by word against the
received text before the English was written. Six diverged materially.

### 1256A · Dan 2:19 · `[var: Vulg. *per visum nocte*, "by a sight in the night"]`
Migne prints *Tunc Danieli **per visionem** nocte mysterium revelatum est*; the
Vulgate has *Tunc Danieli mysterium **per visum** nocte revelatum est*.
**Material, and material to this work specifically:** Richard's whole exposition runs
on the *somnium* / *visio* distinction (fixed vocabulary table: "keep them apart —
Richard argues from the difference"), and the sentence he is glossing is the one that
gives him *mysticae visionis notitiam*. The received text's *visus* would not carry
that hook. Word order also differs; not separately marked.

### 1256D · 1 Pet 3:15 · `[var: Vulg. *de ea, quae in vobis est, spe*, …]`
Migne prints *parati semper … ad satisfactionem omni poscenti rationem **et ea quae in
nobis est fide, et spe***. The received text reads *ad satisfactionem omni poscenti
**vos** rationem **de ea, quae in vobis est, spe*** — no *fide*, *vobis* not *nobis*,
*de* not *et*, and *vos* dropped. Introduced by *quemadmodum scriptum est* and carrying
its own `[n: (I Petr. III)]`, so Pattern 14 fires squarely.
**Declined alongside it:** the printed *et* for *de* is a real word in a real slot —
*ea … fide, et spe* construes as an ablative of accompaniment ("and with that faith and
hope which is in us"), which is how the English renders it. Under Wilson's 2026-08-15
bar a `[cj:]` is available only where the wrongly-printed word is a **negation or a
correlative**; *et* is neither. Declined, named here as a census candidate.

### 1257B · Dan 2:25 · `[var: Vulg. *annuntiet*, "may announce" …]`
The lemma prints *qui solutionem regi **significet***. The Vulgate has *annuntiet* —
**and so does Richard**, three times, in his own exposition of this very verse at
1257D (twice) and 1259A. The divergence is therefore internal to the plate as well as
external to it, which is what makes it material rather than cosmetic: the word the
commentary expounds is not the word the lemma prints. The English keeps *significet*'s
"may signify" in the lemma and "may announce" in every re-quotation, exactly as printed.

### 1259D–1260A · Dan 2:28 · `[var: Vulg. *tibi, rex Nabuchodonosor* …]`
The lemma prints *qui indicavit **tibi Nabuchodonosor** quae ventura sunt novissimis
temporibus*; the Vulgate has *indicavit tibi, **rex** Nabuchodonosor*. Same shape as the
entry above: Richard's own re-quotation at **1261A restores the title** — *Qui indicavit
tibi, **rex** Nabuchodonosor* — and then builds a whole argument on the verse's other
words (*non ait quae ventura sunt tibi, sed quae ventura sunt novissimis temporibus*).
The same note records the second divergence in the lemma, **Migne's plural *in coelis*
against the received singular *in caelo***; that one is not sense-changing and would not
have earned its own marker.
*Also noted, not marked:* Migne sets *sapientes, magi, **et** arioli, et aruspices*
where the received text has no *et* after *magi*. A list connective; no sense at stake.

### 1261A · Luke 17:21 · `[var: Vulg. *regnum Dei*, "the kingdom of God" …]`
**The clearest divergence in the range.** Migne prints *Regnum, inquit Dominus,
**coelorum** intra vos est* with `[n: (Luc. XVII)]`. Luke 17:21 reads *regnum **Dei**
intra vos est*; *regnum coelorum* is Matthew's formula, not Luke's, and the note names
Luke. Rendered as printed ("The kingdom … of heaven is within you"), because Richard's
surrounding argument is explicitly about *regnum coelorum* — *Neque enim alibi quam apud
electos **regnum coelorum** suscitatur* — so conforming the lemma to *regnum Dei* would
have detached the proof-text from the sentence it proves.

### 1262A · Dan 2:29 · `[var: Vulg. *post haec*, "after these things"]`
Migne prints *cogitare coepisti in stratu tuo quid esset futurum **post te***; the
Vulgate has *post haec*. Material: "what should be **after thee**" is a statement about
the king's succession, "after these things" about the sequence of ages, and Richard's
gloss (*Quia de futuris studiose quaesisti*) leans on the futurity either way.
*Also in the same lemma, not marked:* *in **stratu** tuo* for the received *in strato
tuo* — a fourth-declension form of a second-declension noun, no sense at stake
(Pattern 9's neighbourhood).

### 1262C · Ps 37:11 · `[var: Vulg. *lumen oculorum meorum*, "the light of my eyes"]`
Migne prints *Et lumen **oculorum**, et ipsum non est mecum*, dropping the possessive.
Marked because the English then has to read "the light of **the** eyes, even that is not
with me", which is exactly the reading a translator's ear wants to repair back to "my
eyes" — the 7a″ pull, made visible rather than smoothed.

### 1262C · Job 29:2 · `[var: Vulg. *Quis mihi tribuat, ut sim juxta menses pristinos*, "the former months"]`

## C. Passages that would not construe, and conjectures **declined**

Every one of these is a census candidate. The `[cj:]` bar (a negation or a correlative) is
not met by any of them, so none was glossed in the text; all are rendered as printed.

1. **1275C · *quia quo quisque proximum suum minus amat, eo utique **minus** infirma
   molestius tolerat.*** Two comparatives (*minus*, *molestius*) in one *eo* clause; the
   sense the argument needs is "the more troublesomely he bears weak things," and *minus*
   after *eo utique* contradicts it. Rendered as printed ("by so much the less, assuredly,
   does he bear weak things the more troublesomely"), which is as close as the words allow.
   Declined: not a negation, not a correlative — but note it is *adjacent* to the correlative
   class (*quo … eo*), and it is the one site here I would re-put to Wilson if the bar is
   ever widened.
2. **1275D · *dummodo in alios furit, modo in semetipso torpescit.*** The neighbouring
   *modo* makes *modo … modo* ("now … now") the evident intent; *dummodo* is a real
   conjunction and renders ("so long as"), so nothing is marked. Declined.
3. **1277C · *usque ad digni vel debiti finis **talum** non perducitur.*** *talum* =
   "ankle, heel." Rendered literally, "even to the heel of a worthy or due end." **Corpus
   control:** `finis talum` occurs in exactly one file, this one. Declined nonetheless, and
   deliberately: in a work whose entire subject is a statue read from head to feet, "the
   heel of an end" may well be Richard's own figure rather than a defect. Naming it here so
   that nobody silently emends it to *terminum*.
4. **1278A · *Unde utique est, quod jactantiae argentum quam aurum arrogantiae, quo facilius
   eo et citius atteritur.*** The *quam* clause has no comparative to attach to. Rendered as
   closely as the words allow. Declined.
5. **1279C · *Nonne pro favillarum eventilatione Domino **supplicabit**, cum Domino in
   oratione **proclamaret**…*** Future indicative against imperfect subjunctive, where the
   two flanking sentences both use past tenses (*cum Propheta **dicebat***, *cum …
   **erumperet***). *supplicabat* is the evident reading. Declined — a tense/person anomaly,
   explicitly outside the bar.
6. **1279C · *Vide ergo quia corruptionis **opibus** in favillam redigitur.*** "By the
   riches of corruption" — rendered literally, with no expressed subject.
   **Corpus control:** `corruptionis opus` occurs 2×, `corruptionis opibus` only here.
   Declined.
7. **1280C · *sed non **aequa** accepit qualitatem personae agnoscere.*** Rendered "but he
   did not receive equal things, to recognize…" *aeque* would construe. Declined.
8. **1282B · *Volucribus coeli potenter imperat, **cui** cogitationes suas … occupat.***
   The two parallel sentences on either side both use *cum* + indicative; *cui* leaves the
   relative unattached. Rendered literally ("to whom he occupies his own thoughts").

9. **1276A · *nec propter testeam fragilitatem de suo **corpore** redargui.*** The series

10. **1275C · *Et quidem saepe **quaedam** … praesumpserunt … vivunt … possunt.***

  it were, consults the other, when what he could not attain **through one state of mind**
  he learns through another." The other *animus* in the range is **1282D**, *magnamque
  **animi** devotionem* → "a great devotion of spirit." *anima* is "soul" throughout
  (1274C, 1279C, 1279D); *spiritus* is "spirit" (1278C–1279A, and *afflictio spiritus*).
  **No *animus* / *spiritus* collision occurs in this range** — the brief's two flagged
  sentences are in 0005 and 0034, neither of which I hold.
- ***detrimentum* = decline** at 1279D (*contra similium corruptionum detrimenta*), against
  a strong pull toward "damage" or "loss" in that sentence.
- ***gradus* = step** throughout, including the two chapter heads that use it, where "degree"
  read more naturally.
- ***testa* = clay** and ***aes* = bronze** throughout, including *plantam testeam* →
  "the clay sole."
- ***industria* = effort** at 1276B (*quaelibet humana industria*).
- ***praesumptio* = presumption**: at 1279C, *per veniae **praesumptionem*** is rendered
  "through the presuming upon pardon" rather than "presumption of pardon," because here the
  word is positive (confident reliance) and the English noun alone would have made Richard
  say the opposite of his sentence. The root word is preserved; flagging the wording in case
  a peer prefers strict "presumption."

**Three calls I want ratified for the whole work:**

1. ⭐ ***simulatio* vs *simultas*.** Both occur in this range and they must not collapse.
   1274A/1283B print ***simulatio*** ("the good that is done *ex simulatione*"); 1283B and
   1283D print ***simultas*** in the rhyming triad ***charitas · veritas · simultas***,
   where the sense demanded is plainly the same as *simulatio*, not the classical "grudge,
   enmity." I have rendered **simulatio = "dissimulation"**, **simultas = "pretense"**, and
   **ficta / simulatum = "feigned" / "simulated"**. Rendering *simultas* as "enmity" would
   have been classically correct and would have broken the argument (bronze = *pertinacia*
   and feigned piety everywhere else in the work). **Please ratify or overrule before other
   agents meet *simultas*.**
2. ***intelligentia* = "understanding"** at 1278C (*spiritalis intelligentia* → "spiritual
   understanding"). ⚠ The fixed table assigns **understanding** to ***intellectus***.
   *intellectus* does **not** occur anywhere in 0020–0024, so no collision arises in my
   files, but it will elsewhere. If the work needs them distinguished, *intelligentia* →
   "intelligence" is the natural fallback and this is the site to change.
3. ***theoria*.** 1282B prints *bonis meditationibus **spiritualibusque theoriis***.
   "Theories" is actively misleading in modern English and "contemplations" is already spoken
   for by *contemplatio*, which occurs three times in the same chunk (1280B, 1280D). Rendered
   as **italic Latin, *theoriae***, under the register rule for technical words with no clean
   English equivalent. Ratify or replace.

## F. One structural note

**1281C · the split lemma with the *inquit* inside it.** Migne prints *Gloria,* inquit,

## B. Conjectures DECLINED — census candidates, named with reasons

These are all real printed words that make a false or unconstruable claim, and all fall
**outside** Wilson's 2026-08-15 bar (negation or correlative only). I have not widened
the bar. Each is a Pattern 18 census candidate.

1. ⭐ **1295A (0029) · *inde minus desideriorum turba corrumpatur*** — **the most
   consequential declined site in the range.** The clause as printed says the crowd of
   desires is *less* corrupted by the source that illuminates the understanding. **Migne's
   own chapter head, two lines above, says the opposite**: *CAPUT XL. Quod saepe
   desiderium **corrumpitur** unde intelligentia illuminatur.* The likely reading is
   ***imis***, answering *in summis* in the same sentence (*unde **in summis**
   intelligentia ... illuminatur, inde **in imis** desideriorum turba corrumpatur*) — a
   clean *summis/imis* antithesis that the printed *minus* destroys. Declined because
   *minus* is a comparative adverb, not a negation or a correlative; but it is the
   founding 11081 *munde* shape exactly (real adverb, fluent English, false assertion),
   and if the bar is ever widened this site should be the first one revisited.
   Rendered as printed: "the crowd of desires is the less corrupted."

2. **1293D (0029) · *Quartum quod quasi varium sit*** — the seven-fold scheme is
   os · oculi · aures · **nares** · manus · pedes · guttur (Richard sets it out in that
   order at 1293A), and the fourth item's content is *prudenter discernere*, which he
   has just assigned to the nostrils (*ad nares discretio*). ***narium*** for *varium*
   is near-certain. Declined: not a negation or correlative. Rendered "which is as it
   were a various thing."

3. **1287A (0026) · *Quot timores, quot honores, quot algores hostis, noctis et
   frigoris*** — the three genitives pair off *timores*/hostis, ?/noctis,
   *algores*/frigoris, so *honores* is almost certainly ***horrores***. "Honours of the
   night" is fluent and false. Declined on the bar; rendered "how many honours".

4. **1284D (0025) · *imperium, quia negligentius urgere*** — in a series of
   *quia* + object + *potes* clauses (*bonos disponere*, *malis resistere*, *rebelles
   revincere*), the fourth has a comparative **adverb** where the pattern wants an
   accusative object; *negligentiores* ("the negligent") would restore it. Rendered
   literally, "because thou canst urge the more remissly". Declined.

5. **1289D (0027) · *nemo uritur!*** — the exclamation runs through the 1 Cor. 13 list
   (*nemo aemulatur · non agitur perperam · nemo inflatur · nemo ambitiosus quaerit quae
   sua sunt*) and then prints *nemo uritur*, "no one is burned", where the list's next
   item is *non irritatur*. ***irritatur*** is the obvious reading. Declined; rendered
   "no one is burned!" The negation itself is intact, so 7a is not at issue.

6. **1294B (0029) · *in carnibus commodis*** — the parallel is *in spiritualibus et
   animae lucris* ‖ *in [carnalibus] commodis et temporalibus lucris*. *carnibus*
   (fem.) cannot agree with *commodis* as a noun, but *commodus* is also an adjective,
   so the phrase does construe — absurdly — as "in advantageous fleshes", and that is
   how I rendered it. **No `[sic:]`**, because it renders in place and the absurdity
   leaves the defect visible (Pattern 12's own test). Conjecture: *carnalibus commodis*.

7. **1289D (0027) · *ad debiti finis talum non perducitur*** — *ad calcem perducere* is
   the idiom, and *calcem* would be expected. **But I decline even the conjecture**: this
   is a treatise on a statue read from its golden head down to its feet of iron and clay,
   and *talus* ("ankle-bone", "heel") may well be deliberate. Rendered "to the heel of
   its due end". Recorded here so a later reader does not "fix" it.

## C. Vocabulary: the collisions this range was flagged for

### C1. *animus* / *spiritus* at 1309A (0034) — the second of the work's two collisions

Printed: *quanta sit virtus verae devotionis … quae **animum humanum Deo conjungit, et cum
eo quasi unum spiritum efficit**. *Qui* enim *adhaeret Deo, **unus spiritus** est*.* The
same collision stands in the chapter head (*quae animum humanum Deo conjungit*).

**What I did: rendered both "spirit"** — "which joins the **human spirit** to God, and
makes it with him as it were **one spirit**." The chapter head follows suit.

**Why, and why my site differs from 0005's.** The 0005 agent met *ex animi impetu* beside
*ex spiritus sui impetu* and ruled both "spirit" because Richard's *eodem/eamdem* there
**assert the two are the same impulse**, so differentiating would silently deny an
identity the text states. Here the logic runs the other way: the two words name two
*different* things — a human *animus* and the *spiritus* that results — which are
**joined into one**. So the argument for one English word is not identity but the
**conclusion**: the sentence's whole force is that what is joined becomes *unus spiritus*,
and it is 1 Cor. 6:17 that licenses the claim. If *animus* were rendered "mind" here
(as 11536 would have), the proof-text would be about spirit and the claim about mind, and
the *unum spiritum efficit* would have nothing to attach to. The union is the point, and
one English word is what carries it.
⚠ Note that the same sentence is where the plate has assimilated the proof-text to the
claim (*Deo* for *Domino*, §A1 above). The two observations reinforce each other: this is
a sentence in which quotation and gloss have been drawn together, and both moves are
recorded rather than smoothed.

## D. Passages that would not construe, and other plate observations

**1305C (0033) · *Sed profecto qui hoc loco designantur. Spiritum sanctum habere
creduntur;*** — Migne's full stop falls **inside** the sentence, stranding a relative
clause with no main verb and leaving *creduntur* without its subject. Rendered exactly so:
"But assuredly they who are designated in this place. They are believed to have the Holy
Spirit;". **Pattern 8: the mark is not deleted and nothing is supplied.** The sense the
two fragments carry between them is plain and is not in doubt; only the punctuation is.

**1309A (0034) · *vide quam recte Balthassar per effectum dicitur. Per quem quilibet in
filiorum libertatem adoptatur.*** — the same shape: a full stop breaking a relative
clause off from its antecedent. Kept, and the English breaks in the same place.

**1300A (0031) · *quia per somnium corporis sensus corporeus sopitur*** — *somnium*
("dream") stands where the sense wants *somnum* ("sleep"): it is sleep that lulls the
bodily sense, and the very next clause distinguishes them (*Prius dormimus quam somnium
videamus*), while 1300C of this same chunk prints *post excussum **somnum***.
**Rendered "dream" per the fixed table and Pattern 7** — "Because through the dream the
bodily sense of the body is lulled" — awkward, and deliberately so.
⭐ **The work's own control settles it as authorial usage, not a defect, and I record the
control because I nearly called it one.** 0014 argues the identical point in the identical
words — *Quia per **somnium** sopitur sensus corporeus, recte per illum mentis excessum
intelligimus* — so Richard uses *somnium* of sleep in both places, and the shipped 0014
English renders it "by the dream" exactly as mine does. **No marker, no conjecture, and
NOT a census candidate.** Two agents independently reaching the same rendering on the same
construction is the outcome the fixed table exists to produce.

**1302C (0032) · *quae sensu exteriori percepi possunt*** — *percepi* for *percipi*.
**Control run: `percepi possunt` occurs elsewhere in the PL TEI**, and *percepi* itself in
five-plus files, so the e/i interchange in the passive infinitive is an attested medieval
orthography, not this plate's defect. **Pattern 9: rendered normally ("can be perceived"),
no italics, no marker.**

**1302C (0032) · *Illa autem velut intus sunt qua: nisi interiori sensu…*** — the token
`qua:` is *quae*: it is the ligature **quæ** misread by the digitization, and `qua:`
occurs in **16 files** across the PL TEI, which makes it an OCR artifact class of our
source rather than anything Migne set. **No `[sic:]`** — a defect in our files is never
attributed to his plate. Rendered "which". Recorded here so a later pass can decide
whether it belongs in `data/tei-patches/`.

**1298B (0030) · *vel communi infirmitati deseruimus*** — with a dative and in a present-
tense series (*consulimus … deseruimus*), this is *deservimus*, "we serve", with **u for
v** — the ordinary orthography, Pattern 9. Rendered "serve". *Deseruimus* read as the
perfect of *desero* cannot govern a dative and would reverse the sense of the clause; it
is recorded here so that nobody later "restores" a desertion Richard is not describing.

**1309B (0034) · *qui frequentibus relevationibus divinitus relevari laetificarique
meruerunt*** — *relevatio*/*relevare* are real words ("relief", "to relieve") and render
in place, so they are rendered ("relieved and gladdened by frequent relievings"); but the
whole paragraph is about **revelation** (*divinae revelationis desiderio*, *ex
multitudine revelationum suarum*, *lumen revelantis gratiae*), and *revelationibus …
revelari* is almost certainly what stood. **Conjecture declined** (not a negation, not a
correlative; the printed reading asserts nothing false, only something tautologous).
**Named as a census candidate.**

**1307D (0034) · *quid, inquit, per effectum istum David scelestius*** — *effectum* where
Gregory's sense wants *affectum*; a real word, renders in place ("through this effect"),
declined, named.

**1300B (0031) · *cum divino lumine tactus mentis intelligentia perspiciat*** — masculine
*tactus* against feminine *intelligentia*. **Pattern 9: Latin grammatical gender has no
English exponent.** Nothing to render either way, no marker, entry marked so a later
sweep does not churn it.

Two divergences at one site, and this is the range's most valuable entry.

neither will he threaten unto eternity?" — i.e. **the `?` is carried and the `Non … neque` is

## B. Passages that would not construe — rendered as printed, no marker

**1325B (0041) · *quasi contradictionem suarum portas rebellionum vectibus munire***
The accusative *contradictionem* and the feminine genitive plural *suarum* cannot both be
governed here; the sense the sentence needs is *contradictionis suae portas*, "the gates of their

contradictio pertinax … vectis ferreus, nisi rebellio audax*). Rendered by the sense the printed
nouns supply, "to fortify the gates of their contradiction with the bars of rebellions", and
logged. **No `[cj:]`:** the case anomaly is neither a negation nor a correlative, and Wilson's bar
stands. **Census candidate, declined and named.**

**1326C (0041) · *cum crudelitatis ministris, tabulisque feris partem habere***
⭐ **The range's one genuinely unresolved reading.** *tabulisque* is a perfectly good ablative
plural of *tabula*, so Pattern 12 has nothing to wrap and Pattern 7 governs: rendered literally,
"with the ministers of cruelty, and with wild planks". It is nonsense in place.
**Control:** the string *tabulisque feris* occurs **once in the whole PL TEI — here**; every other

*documentis tabulisque populi*). **Conjecture: *talibusque feris*, "and with such wild beasts"** —
and the sentence's own answering clause is the evidence, *nisi cum **talibus** sociis*, five words
later, with *feris* already established as the chapter's word for the cruel (*Per feras … recte
satis crudeles quique intelligi possunt*).
**Declined as a `[cj:]`**: *talis* is a correlative pronoun, which brushes the bar, but the
printed *tabulis* is not a wrongly-printed **negation or correlative pair** in the sense Wilson
ruled — the correlative is in the *answering* clause and is printed correctly. **Named here as a
census candidate; I have not widened the bar.** If the census reopens it, this is the strongest
site in the range.

## E. Fixed vocabulary — followed, including where it cost something

- ***animus* = spirit / *mens* = mind** held apart everywhere, and they collide in this range:
  1327D–1328A (*Vitiorum … dominatio **animum** paulatim emollit*) sits in a chapter whose whole
  subject is the *mens*, and 1331D–1332A runs *animo … animus … animus* through a passage about
  what the *mens* cannot reach. Rendered "spirit" for *animus* throughout. ***spiritus* and
  *animus* never stand in the same clause anywhere in this range**, so the brief's two-site
  collision is not touched here: *spiritus* appears only as the malignant spirits at 1326A, the
  ministering and unclean spirits at 1328B–1328A, and in 0044 as the Holy Spirit and the Dan. 4

  ratified five-materials rule wins over the familiar psalm cadence, and it has to, because the
  chapter's allegory runs *ferrum → audacia* / *aes → pertinacia* across four columns and back

  gradus saevitiae*) followed exactly; *audacia*, which is not in the scheme, is rendered
  **audacity** throughout so the pair *pertinaciter / audaciter* stays visible.

  against *incrementa malorum* and was refused.

- ***simulatio* = dissimulation** at 1329C (*absque simulatione*) — and note this is *simulatio*,

my own judgment at two sites. At 1337C *divinae pietatatis viscera* and 1342A *divinae

rather than decided here.

## F. Vocabulary calls I am proposing for the whole work

These are new to this stretch of Daniel and will recur in 0050–0057 and in the peer ranges
0035–0044. They need to be one word across the work or the tree allegory loses its hooks.

1. **bestia = beast · fera = wild beast · animal = animal.** Non-negotiable in this range:
   1339C glosses *bestialiter degens et feraliter agens* as two distinct vices (carnality
   and cruelty), and 1340D–1341A turns on *ferae* preying on *animalia* while *aves* prey
   on *aves*. Collapsing any two is the *testa*/*fictilis* error in another key.
2. **ungues = claws** (not "nails"). Carries both the birds of Dan. 4:30 and 1341D's *dente
   et ungue in omnes saevit*, "with tooth and claw", where "nails" would be unreadable.
3. **fenum = hay · herba = grass.** Both are in the Dan. 4 lemma at once (*fenum ut bos
   comedes* beside *in herbis foris* / *in herba terrae*), so they must not share a word.
   "Hay" also carries *omnis caro fenum … quasi flos feni* at 1339D.
4. **germen radicum = the shoot of its roots.** Used at 1335D and 1336B; the peer range
   0040 (1323B–1324A) expounds *germen* at length as *propositum correctionis*, so whatever
   that agent chose must win if it differs — flagged for the merge pass.
5. **decor = comeliness · decorare = to make comely.** Dan. 4:33's *ad honorem regni mei
   decoremque perveni* is expounded at 1344C–1345A as a matched pair with *honor*, so the
   two English words must stay distinct and stable.
6. **aula = hall · palatium = palace.** Richard distinguishes them explicitly at 1337D–1338A
   (*Forte palatium … humilitas vera* against *aula Babylonica humilitas ficta*).
7. **gloriatio = glorying · glorificatio = glorification.** CAPUT XLVII's head is built on
   the opposition and it recurs through 1344A.

## A. Markers fired

### `[var: …]` — Pattern 14 (Migne's scripture against the received text)

Policy applied, stated so it can be checked: **a `[var:]` fires where the English on the
page would assert something different** — a different word, tense, number, direction, or a
dropped word that carries sense. Pure abridgement of a longer verse (clauses elided, no
wording altered) and connective-level substitutions that leave the English unchanged are
logged in §B instead, not marked. Twelve fired.

1. **1345D · Job 4:6** — `*Ubi est timor tuus, et fortitudo tua, patientia tua, et perfectio
   viarum?*` Vulg. *Ubi est timor tuus, fortitudo tua, patientia tua, et perfectio viarum
   **tuarum**?* Migne adds *et* after *timor tuus* and drops *tuarum*: the English loses "of
   **thy** ways" and the verse stops indicting Eliphaz's hearer. Marked; rendered as printed.
2. **1345D · Apoc. 2:4–5** — `*Habeo adversum te aliquid quod primam charitatem reliquisti.
   Memento unde excideris…*` Vulg. *Sed habeo adversum te, quod caritatem tuam primam
   reliquisti. Memor esto itaque unde excideris.* Three divergences at once: *Sed* dropped,
   *aliquid* intruded, *primam charitatem* for *caritatem tuam primam* (the possessive gone),
   *Memento* for *Memor esto itaque*. **Diffed against the work's own other printing at
   1271C (chunk 0019), which reads *Sed habeo adversum te, quod charitatem tuam primam
   dereliquisti* — i.e. much closer to the Vulgate, and with *dereliquisti* for *reliquisti*.**
   The two printings of one verse disagree in four places. Marked here (the more divergent).
3. **1346D · Dan. 4:34** — `*Nunc ego Nabuchodonosor…*` Vulg. *Nunc **igitur** ego*. The
   inferential connective is the hinge of the chapter (Nabuchodonosor praises *because* he
   has been restored), and it is gone. Marked. ⚠ Migne also prints a comma inside the lemma,

## A. ⭐ The ending of the work — read this first

**The treatise does not close its argument. It breaks off mid-exposition.**

- The last printed words, at **1366A**, are:
  *Et quid est quod bestiarum potestas aufertur, nisi, quod per visitantem gratiam,

- **It is not truncated mid-sentence.** That sentence is grammatically complete and closes

## F. Vocabulary calls, including one I want ratified for the whole work

### F.1 ⭐⭐ *simulatio* vs *dissimulatio* — the ratified equivalence CANNOT hold in this chunk, and I want a work-wide ruling

`cruces-0020.md` §7 fixed ***simulatio* = "dissimulation"** (beside the brief's ratified
*simultas* = "pretense"), and chunks 0018–0024 ship it that way. **CAPUT XII of my range
breaks it**, because at 1359C Migne sets the two nouns side by side and makes the
distinction between them the whole point of the chapter:

> *Simulatio et dissimulatio est de occultis. Sed **simulatio** de occultis **bonis**,
> **dissimulatio** vero de occultis **malis**.*

Four wings, four vices — *simulationis, dissimulationis, ostentationis, excusationis* — and
*simulatio* is feigning a good one does **not** have while *dissimulatio* is hiding an evil
one **does**. Under the ratified equivalence, *dissimulatio* has nowhere to go and the
chapter's four-fold scheme collapses to three. This is the *testa*/*fictilis* situation
exactly: two words printed side by side, the exposition turning on which is which, so they
must never land on one English word.

**What I did, and why:** in this range ***simulatio* = "simulation"**, ***dissimulatio* =
"dissimulation"**, *simulator* = "simulator", *simulatrix mens* = "simulating mind".
English's own simulation/dissimulation pair carries Richard's distinction precisely
(feigning what is not / concealing what is), which is more than any substitute would.

**The ratification I am asking for (merge-step decision, not mine to take):** sweep
***simulatio* → "simulation" work-wide**, freeing "dissimulation" for *dissimulatio*, and
leaving *simultas* = "pretense" untouched. That touches **six shipped sites**, all rendering
*simulatio* as "dissimulation" — 0018 (1270A), 0019 ×2 (1272B area, 1273B), 0020 (1274A),
0024 (1283B), 0027 (1289C) — plus the *simulatum* / *simulati* participles at 1283D and
1289C, which already read "simulated" and would need no change. The alternative —
leaving the work with "dissimulation" meaning two opposite things in two places — is worse.
If the ruling goes the other way, **CAPUT XII still cannot use one word for both**, and
whatever is chosen for *dissimulatio* here has to be recorded in the brief.

## H. Conjectures DECLINED — census candidates, named as the brief asks

Wilson's `[cj:]` bar (2026-08-15) glosses only a **negation or a correlative**. None of
these is either; all four are named here rather than widened into the running text.

1. ⭐ **1360C — *aspirare **ab** subdiaconatum*.** A real word, wrongly chosen: *ab* governs
   the ablative, *subdiaconatum* is accusative, and the two parallel members that follow in
   the same sentence both print *ad* (*subdiaconos **ad** diaconatum, diaconos **ad**
   presbyteratum*). **Control run:** *ad subdiaconatum* stands in **17** PL files; *ab
   subdiaconatum* in **one** — this. So *ad* is near-certain. I rendered the printed word:
   "those who are of a lower grade **aspiring from** the subdiaconate", which is fluent and
   says the opposite of the sentence's own gradient. **This is the nearest thing to a
   Pattern 18 site in my range** — it is exactly the *munde*/*mundo* shape, a real word
   whose faithful English asserts something the author is arguing against. It is declined
   because a preposition of direction is neither a negation nor a correlative, and the bar
   is not mine to widen. If the census ever reaches for a preposition class, **this site
   should be its worked example.**
2. **1363A — *implicare* for *explicare*** (§G.4). A prefix, not a negation. Declined.
3. **1362C/1363B — *mentis obduratio* announced, *cordis obduratio* delivered.** CAPUT XVII
   sets out the three steps as *mentis obligatione, mentis obduratione, mentis effrenatione*
   and then defines the second as *Cordis autem obduratio est…*; 0057 @1364B looks back at
   all three as *cordis obligatione, cordis obduratione, et effrenatione*. Rendered as
   printed each time — "of the mind" where Migne writes *mentis*, "of the heart" where he
   writes *cordis*. ⚠ **This one matters for the fixed vocabulary**, since *mens* and *cor*
   are separately fixed terms in this work and the plate is inconsistent *inside a single
   scheme*. Not a defect I can mark; recorded so the inconsistency is attributed to Migne.
4. **1362C — *desertus … praevaricator efficitur*, and 1362B *tribus apud semetipsum***.
   Masculine agreement where the subject is *mens*/*anima* (feminine). Pattern 9: Latin
   grammatical gender has **no English exponent**. No emendation to make or undo; flagged so
   a later sweep does not churn it.
