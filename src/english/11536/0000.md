## THE FIRST TREATISE.



## CHAPTER ONE.

[1073C]

*What ails thee, O sea, that thou hast fled; and thou, O Jordan, that thou art turned back? [n: (Psal. CXIII.)]* This utterance of the Prophet is an exclamation, with wonder; and the wonder is with exultation. For he was seeing a certain divine miracle; and not one only, but a twofold one, each great, each wondrous, each exceedingly astounding. He saw the sea flee before the face of Israel; he saw the Jordan bend the rush of its course back toward its spring; he saw both give way to the Hebrew people and leave a free road to those setting out. Which of these is not new, which of these is not wondrous? He contemplated each thus, and marvelled at each, and rejoiced over each. He cries out, therefore, in the voice of exultation and of confession: [1073D] What ails thee, O sea, that thou hast fled: and thou, O Jordan, that thou art turned back? The first miracle happens at Israel's going out from Egypt, but the second happens at Israel's going out from the desert. Who at length will one day grant me perfectly to forsake the region of unlikeness: who could grant me to enter into the land of promise, so that I might be able to see alike the flight of the sea and the turning back of the Jordan? A fair spectacle it is, and pleasant enough, to see the water of the sea fleeing, to see the water of the Jordan returning, the bitter withdraw, the sweet flow back, bitterness fail, sweetness [1074C] overflow. Whoever thou art that seest these things, I should wonder if thou too didst not cry out: *What ails thee, O sea, that thou hast fled; and thou, O Jordan, that thou art turned back?* Miracles of this kind are never wont to happen except before true Jews, except before good Hebrews.

## CHAPTER II. *On the twofold confession, and the twofold advancement.*

Confession makes the Jew, passage makes the Hebrew. For "Jew" is interpreted *confessing,* "Hebrew" *passing over.* And there is a true confession, and there is a feigned confession. Likewise there is a good passage and there is a bad passage. True confession makes a true Jew, and a good passage makes a good Hebrew. To ascribe to thyself thy evils, to ascribe to thy God thy goods — that is true confession, right confession. The former is of crime, the latter of praise; each of them indeed [1074D] useful, each praiseworthy. Likewise the passage is double; each of them indeed good, each necessary. He who passes from evil to good passes well indeed; and he who passes from good to best, he too makes a good passage. Know, therefore, and confess thy evils, and do not remain in them; but pass beyond them, and the first passage is made, and the first confession. Likewise confess to God concerning thy goods, praise him for his goods, and do not grow sluggish in them: but strive as much as thou canst toward better things, and pass over to the best, and the second passage is made, and the second confession. [1075A] At the first confession the first miracle is wont to happen, and at the second confession the second miracle is wont to happen. For through the confession of crime the flight of the sea is brought about, and at the confession of praise the Jordan is straightway turned back.

## CHAPTER III. *How the confession of crime avails for the extermination of evil.*

For all things are washed in confession: the conscience is cleansed, bitterness is taken away, the sea is put to flight, tranquillity returns, hope revives, in spirits it grows glad. For blessed are they that mourn, since they shall be comforted. What is it to mourn, to be made sorrowful, but to be driven by the storms of the sea? And what is the joy of consolation but the flight of the sea and the absenting of grief? Hear [1075B] the Jew confessing his crimes, and mark the water of the sea fleeing before him: *I said,* he says, *I will confess against myself my injustice to the Lord, and thou hast forgiven the impiety of my sin [n: (Psal. XXXI)]*. He was confessing his iniquity, and the sea was being put to flight, because his impiety was being forgiven. For impiety is a sea, since it cannot make sweet water; but its water is exceedingly salt and very bitter, and its bitterness is a most bitter bitterness. What sweetness, I ask, has envy? What sweet taste, I beseech thee, has wrath; what pleasantness has impatience? Waters of this kind, as I judge, are every one of them bitter, and they make a sea, because they can taste good to none at all, can please no one. Dost thou see, then, what manner of thing this great sea is, and wide of hands, which can [1075C] be called the sea of malice. For there is also another which can be called the sea of misery, having indeed itself water by no means sweet, yet less bitter. But happy is he who has dominion from sea to sea, so that he lies under no fault, succumbs to no penalty, to such a degree that all iniquity may not have dominion over him, nor any adversity oppress him. Happy the man before whose face the sea flees malice withdraws, misery gives way, the conscience grows glad. Can it be that one who is such is unable to sing confidently? What ails thee, O sea, that thou hast fled? If therefore thou also wilt, make of thyself a Jew of good profession; confess from thy heart thy sin, that thou too mayest be able to see such a spectacle, so great a miracle, and mayest not hesitate to sing a hymn of this kind. [1075D] What ails thee, O sea, that thou hast fled?

## CHAPTER IV. *How the confession of praise avails for the advancement of good.*

But now let us consider this, how at the confession of praise the turning back of the Jordan is wont to happen. But this, as I judge, we learn better by working than by disputing; this we prove more quickly by experiencing than by speaking. In short, do thou also busy thyself to confess, to marvel at, to venerate God's power, God's wisdom, God's mercy; and be assured that the more thou shalt praise, the more ardently shalt thou love. Let it not irk thee, then, to praise the divine grace much, that thou mayest love much; to love much, that thou mayest desire much. For the more vehemently thou shalt desire, the more quickly wilt thou obtain [1076A] what thou greatly covetest. Love what before thou didst despise, despise what before thou didst love, and be secure, for the Jordan is turned back. Love God, scorn the world, and I will truly acknowledge that the Jordan is turned back. What manner of turning back of the Jordan is this, I ask: how wonderful, how praiseworthy, how lovable — that the whole rush of love, the whole inundation of love, the whole affluence of delight should run with great haste not downward but upward, should seek the things that are above, not the things that are upon the earth; so that thou mayest truly profess that the Jordan is turned back! This, as I judge, was the reason why it neither irked the Prophet nor shamed him to become a Jew: because he had truly acknowledged that *It is good to confess to the Lord, and [1076B] to sing to thy name, O Most High [n: (Psal. XCI)]*. Hence that firm profession and devout promise: *I will confess to the Lord exceedingly with my mouth, and in the midst of many I will praise him [n: (Psal. CVIII)]*. Did he not will himself to be a Jew, when he said: *Praise the Lord, O my soul? [n: (Psal. CXLV.)]* Did he not forbid the water of the Jordan to flow downward, when he admonished his own soul with a coaxing address? *Hope in God, for I will still confess to him, the salvation of my countenance, and my God [n: (Psal. XLII)]*. Did he not desire the streams of the Jordan to be turned back, when he said: *Turn, O my soul, into thy rest, for the Lord hath dealt bountifully with thee [n: (Psal. CXIV)]*. I should wonder if thou wilt hear this and not thyself seek to become a Jew, and to confess to the Lord, since he is good, since his mercy endureth unto the age, and to be found worthy — one to whom the Lord may deign [1076C] to show so great a miracle, that thou mayest sing with the Prophet: for the Jordan is turned back.

## CHAPTER V. *That the first advancement of virtue lies in contempt of the world.*

But let not even that, I ask, be enough for thee, when thou hast become a Jew, unless thou be made likewise a Hebrew also. Assuredly the Hebrews themselves are the very same as the Jews, although Jews on one account and Hebrews on another. For confession, as has been said, makes the Jew, and passage makes the Hebrew. In short, do thou thyself also, if thou art in Egypt, pass over and forsake Egypt, and thou hast made a Hebrew of good hope, and thou wilt see in the flight of the sea that great and astounding miracle. But if thou shalt take pains to pass over the desert itself as well, thou wilt see things yet greater [1076D] than these in the turning back of the Jordan. The first passage, then, is out of Egypt, the second out of the desert. First pass from the world to thyself, second from thyself into God, Thou hast loved the world, scorn the world, and pass over. Forsake the cares of this world, and take care of thyself, and thou beginnest to be a dweller of the wilderness. Returning from the darkness of Egypt, from worldly errors, to the more secret places of the heart, thou wilt find nothing else but a place of horror and of vast solitude. For this is that land desert, pathless, and waterless — the conscience, namely, long neglected, wholly untilled, beset with thorns and thistles, and full of every horror. Thou must therefore forsake Egypt and seek a wilderness of this kind, if thou desirest to behold with thine own eyes the flight of the sea, as David also went far off in flight and abode in the solitude; fulfil, [1077A] O transgressor, what is enjoined upon transgressors, where it is said: *Return, ye transgressors, to the heart [n: (Isai. XLVI)]*, and the first passage is made, and henceforth thou art rightly called a Hebrew.
