## CHAPTER VI. *That the second advancement of virtue lies in contempt of self.*

Return, therefore, to thyself, and with all keeping keep thy heart. But woe to thee if thou remainest there. Do not remain, but pass over, going on, and passing through into the place of the wonderful tabernacle, even to the house of God. Pass over, then, thyself also; go on into thy God; seek and lay hold of the highest good. Hear her who cries aloud in the streets; hearken to the voice of Wisdom, admonishing us to this second passage: *Pass over to me,* she says, *all ye that covet me (Eccle. [1077B] XXIV)*. Forsake the world, and Israel's passage out of Egypt is made. Despise thyself, and Israel's passage out of the desert is made. The first passage, then, contempt of the world makes; the second passage contempt of self brings about. At the first passage the sea is put to flight. At the second passage the Jordan is turned back. The flight of the sea is the extermination of bitterness. The turning back of the Jordan is the reordering of charity.

## CHAPTER VII. *How through contempt of the world the extermination of evil comes about.*

Wouldst thou hear more openly, know more fully how at the first passage of the Hebrews, in what manner at contempt of the world, the flight of the sea, the extermination of bitterness, comes about. [1077C] Straightway and without doubt, as soon as thou hast forsaken Egypt, as soon as thou hast cast the whole world behind thee: the cause of fear perishes, the cause of grief, the root of bitterness (for cupidity is the root of all evils). For when thou shalt be able fully to escape the world, when thou shalt have begun to covet none of its prosperities, to dread none of its adversities, what will there then be that could disturb thy mind? What, I ask, do we more rightly understand by the flight of the sea than to be wearied by no perturbations? In short, let not your heart be troubled, neither let it be afraid, and the sea, fleeing before our face, has gone away and withdrawn. I think that the sea will be no more when God shall wipe away every tear from the eyes of the saints, when there shall be no more mourning, nor crying, nor any grief at all, since [1077D] the former things are passed away. For what are fear and trembling and sorrow, but waters of the sea exceedingly bitter, and wholly devoid of all sweetness? The Prophet came into the depth of a sea of this kind, unless I am mistaken, when he said: *Fear and trembling came upon me [n: (Psal. LIV)]*. And that other word: *Tribulation and anguish have found me [n: (Psal. CXVIII)]*. A sea of this kind they meet with, as many as forsake Egypt, seek the desert, covet the land of promise. For we must pass through many tribulations into the kingdom of God.

## CHAPTER VIII. *That contrition of spirit is now for a help, now for the support of good.*

A sea of this kind some have before them, but others behind them. Those, however, who have it before them — to them it is for a hindrance; [1078A] those who have it beside them, to them it is for a help; those who have it behind them, to them it is for a support. And so the Israelites, forsaking Egypt, wishing to enter the desert, and sighing after the land of promise, first had the sea before them for a hindrance to their setting out; afterward they had it beside them for a help of defense, when it was to them for a wall on the right hand and on the left; at last they had it behind them for a support of security, their enemies having been drowned or shut off by it. Before us are things to come, beside us things present, behind us things past. The sea before us is the fear of dangers to come. The sea beside us is the labor of the conflicts pressing upon us. The sea behind us is the grief of evils committed; the sea before us, the fear of faint-heartedness; the sea beside us, the labor [1078B] of solicitude; the sea behind us, the grief of repentance. The fear of faint-heartedness hinders; the labor of solicitude fortifies; the grief of repentance protects. One man, considering the life and the zeal of others, when he sees many — even after the vow of profession — return to their vomit and go back to the world, does not so much as dare to begin what is good: this man has the sea before him, but for a hindrance. For what is the trembling of the heart, the hesitation of the mind, but a kind of billowing, as it were, of the sea? For he who has hesitated is like a wave of the sea, which is moved and carried about by the wind. Another manfully tramples this fear, puts the billowings of the sea to flight before him, chastises the trembling of his heart, bravely prepares his spirit to endure whatever perils for the Lord, and for this part does not hesitate to sing, the sea saw [1078C] and fled — inasmuch as all useless trembling flees and departs before his face. But because he is still solicitous how he ought either to beware of negligence in good things or to avoid the stealthy approach of evils, he does indeed suffer the sea, though not before him, yet on either side, inasmuch as he must act constantly on this hand toward good things, on that hand against evils, must labor much, and be solicitous on both sides. But happy are those whom now the exercises of the virtues, the conflicts of labors, do not so much weary as delight — who, though they lament only this, that they have consumed so many seasons of their past life either in iniquity, or squandered them in vanity. He who is of this kind shows that he has the sea behind his back, like him who said: *I will recount [1078D] to thee all my years in the bitterness of my soul [n: (Isa. XXXVIII)]*. But who would doubt that for men of this kind the sea has been left behind their back, for a most safe support, and for the overthrow of their enemies? For the bitterness of penitence is wont not only to blot out by satisfaction the evils committed, but it has also been accustomed to bring this about in us: that what is not lawful should not even please us to repeat.

## CHAPTER IX. *On useful and useless contrition.*

Let us consider this also: how here the water of the sea, the wave of bitterness, is to some outside the way, to others in the way. Who would doubt that this life is the way to the life to come? One man grieves for those things which belong to the present life, another for those which belong to the life to come. He who grieves for those things [1079A] which are in this life, to him the sea is in the way. He who grieves for those which are outside this life, to him the sea is beside the way. In this life, both goods and evils; in that life, either goods alone, or evils alone. And so the things which are nothing differ much from one another, and touch one another by no nearness of likeness, inasmuch as between them a great chasm has been fixed, in which no proportion of a like quality can be found. But this life lies as it were midway between those things which belong to the life to come, the goods but the evils, inasmuch as it approaches both, on this side or on that, by some likeness, since it has goods and evils mingled together. Therefore with the goods of that life, by the goods of its own (although it is joined by a shadowy likeness, as also with the evils of that life, by the evils of its own), it is allied by an imaginary affinity. Outside, therefore, [1079B] this way are those things which come after this life. Outside indeed, and beside — not only by likeness of quality, but by the cohesion of time. As soon as we forsake these, we fall into those without delay. He therefore who fears, grieves and laments for these things, pours out the wave of sorrow, the affluence of bitterness, like the water of the sea, beside the way of its own running down.

## CHAPTER X. *On the twofold compunction of the heart.*

Sorrow for us eternal ones is the sea on the right hand; sorrow for eternal evils is the sea on the left hand. Assuredly, if we are of sound mind, for both we ought to [sic: *time re*], to grieve, and to abound in the bitter tears of compunction. Let us fear to lose these, let us fear to incur those, [1079C] and we have a twofold sea strongly fortifying us, and that on either side. Rightly, as I judge, the sea which meets us in the way, which hinders the journey of our setting out, which blocks the course of our progress, we strike with the rod of just severity, we terrify by consideration of the divine strictness, we remove by remembrance of the examination to come. Let all our dread, then, all our sorrow be removed from the way; and, for a useless life, for the solicitude of worldly life, let our compunction be divided into two, that by the sorrow of fear we may wash away eternal evils, and by the tears of desire may purchase eternal goods. Happy are they who pass through this twofold sea also of a double sorrow, who leave far behind their back every ambiguity and dread of what is to be expected in time to come, and pass over the whole mourning of diffidence [1079D] by the security of sure hope — would that in us too a dread and sorrow of this kind might be swallowed up by the security of a confidence coming upon us! Let a sea of this kind also flee and depart, that we too may be able to sing with the Prophet: What ails thee, O sea, that thou hast fled? Do we not think the sea from beside, that one had fled away, who was saying? *I have fought the good fight, I have finished my course, I have kept the faith: for the rest, there is laid up for me a crown of justice, which the just judge shall render to me in that day [n: (II Tim. IV)]*. I think that he had left every sea far behind his back, nor was there anything whence he then ought to have been made sorrowful and troubled, when he was saying: I am conscious to myself of nothing.
