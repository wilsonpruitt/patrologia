## CHAPTER XI. *On the vain or the true contempt of the world*

But what, I ask, do we say that this is: that even [1080A] today we see so many forsake Egypt who nevertheless do not deserve to behold so great a miracle in the flight of the sea? If they truly forsake Egypt, if they are true Hebrews, inasmuch as they pass over out of the world and make the first passage of the Hebrews, why do they not take experience at least of the first miracle also? Or do they perhaps leave the world by profession, but not also by manner of life — in habit, not in affection? If it behooves me to glory, I can glory for this part only. If indeed they are Hebrews, so am I. But see lest perchance it be not enough for us and for you to have changed place, not spirit: they change the world, not the spirit, who run across the sea. What good is it thus to pass over and not to make a Hebrew? Without doubt, at Israel's going out from Egypt, the flight of the sea shows how rightly each man claims for himself the dignity of this [1080B] name.

## CHAPTER XII. *How difficult it is to attain to perfect contempt of self.*

Let us pass out of Egypt in body; let us pass out also in heart. But let us not think that this can suffice us either. For a great way still remains for us, if we wish to attain to the vision of the second miracle. Far beyond doubt, it is not a matter of the same capacity, or of the same ease, to go out of Egypt and to pass through the wilderness, to leave the world and to conquer oneself and to cast oneself behind. The former is as it were a small thing, which is accomplished in a small time; the latter, after many labors, through many temptations, is scarcely at length and by few ever completed. The former is the work of a single day; the latter we scarcely fulfil after many [1080C] seasons. Hard, difficult, great it is to leave oneself, to look well into oneself and fully to scorn oneself, to approve by the perfect and utterly to disapprove. Here is the labor, here the groaning. Depraved is the heart of man and unsearchable, and who shall know it? Ye sons of men, how long will ye be heavy of heart, with stiff neck and untameable heart — and who shall conquer it?

## CHAPTER XIII. *By what means the mind is trained to full contempt of self.*

O how often it is necessary to change places, how often to pitch camp, how many temptations to endure, to prove all things, to hold what is good, to abstain from every evil appearance! O how often it is necessary to go and to return, and to forget the things that are [1080D] behind, and to stretch forward to the things before, and again, by going back behind one's own back, to take experience of oneself, and through one's own failing to be taught one's own infirmity! O how often it is necessary to fall into so many bypaths of errors, so many shadowed places of ambiguities, so many monsters of vices — to traverse them, to look upon them — before one hold the far bounds of the wilderness, and come to the uttermost of humility, and perfectly attain to the highest contempt of self! O how often it is necessary to flee to God's mercy, and, caught amid so many perils, to seek the divine mercy and to obtain grace! O how astounding the mighty works! O how delightful the miracles! O how wonderful the benefits which — in so many divine revelations, in such visitations, in such consolations — they experience, marvel at, venerate, whom [1081A] the vastness of the wilderness and the greatness of their own infirmity have taught to trust nothing of their own virtue, but to presume the whole of it from God's mercy, to cast their care upon the Lord, and to place their hope in the Lord God! There it is granted to experience, in the sweetness of the heavenly food, how great is the multitude of sweetness which God has hidden for them that love him; to taste and see how pleasant the Lord is, and how good the God of Israel is to them that are right of heart. There they who ascend into the mountain of the Lord and draw near to his feet shall receive of his doctrine, proving and professing that great is his power, and of his wisdom there is no number. And so whoever, in that land desert and waterless, has known this — thus he appeared to him in the sanctuary, that he might see his power and his glory — taking experience of that saying, [1081B] that great is the Lord, and exceedingly to be praised, and of his greatness there is no end. But who could enumerate how innumerable are the alternating vicissitudes of this kind — now of desolations, now of consolations — that must be experienced; how many judgments of human infirmity, how many proofs of the divine mercy it falls to the dwellers of the wilderness and the worshippers of God to see, before they reach the far bounds of the wilderness and lay hold of the bounds of perfect humility; before the wave of the Jordan give way to those who come and, turning back its course, return to its spring; and the human spirit, spurning its own things and despising itself, pass over wholly into God, and run by desire into him from whom is every best gift and every perfect boon, and turn back to [1081C] God, the living fountain, the whole rush of its love.

## CHAPTER XIV *That superfluous love of self is more hardly overcome amid the successes of the virtues.*

O happy soul, thou who art worthy — thou for whom the Lord deigns to work so great a miracle, to whom it is granted to see such a spectacle: the waters which are lower, and whatever streams of desires tend toward the depths, running down and failing, and the waters which come from above, the desires which are from on high, standing together in a single mass — so that she may truly sing: *One thing I have asked of the Lord, this will I seek: that I may dwell in the house of the Lord all the days of my life [n: (Psal. XXVI)]*. O unhappy soul, thou who delayest, who dost not hasten, who dost not greatly [1081D] pant to run and to see, to marvel and to be astounded that the streams of the Jordan first fix their course, stand in one place, and cling immovably in one desire of eternity, and thereafter utterly fail in the lower parts and little by little increase in the higher. What miracle, I beseech thee, dost thou think it, what prodigy, that the Jordan should be driven backward, and so great a rush of the river return to the upper parts — especially if it happen at the time of harvest, when it is wont to fill the banks of its channel? For when that seed which fell upon the good ground has begun to bring forth fruit of itself, one thirtyfold, another sixtyfold, another a hundredfold, then indeed the wave of the Jordan is wont to swell higher; and, as is the way of the human spirit, to esteem great things of itself, to believe itself someone great, when it has seen itself bring forth much fruit and [1082A] come to the harvest of ripeness. Then — no wonder — the greater the things it esteems of itself, by so much the greater love does it judge itself worthy of; and it is a very great thing if it does not flow out even unto the depths of superfluity, and does not overpass the bounds of just equity and the banks of usefulness. How great, then, dost thou think, how great a miracle dost thou reckon it, to grow cheap to thyself, to displease thyself more and more, and that especially at the time when thou beginnest truly to be great and to appear great to all? The waters of the Jordan are indeed wont — nor is this a wonder — at the time of icy cold, or even by the drought of summer heat, to be lessened and to shrink; and any mind, however exalted, amid the cold of malice and the excessive heats of concupiscence, to grow cheap to itself, and, however unwillingly, to acknowledge its own infirmity, and to subside not a little from its own estimation [1082B] of itself. Who, I ask, is so monstrously foolish as not sometimes to displease himself amid wickednesses and outrages? But this will be the great thing: if at the time of harvest, and amid the successes of the virtues, he can shut in the waves of the Jordan within the banks of its channel, and restrain the rush of private love within the bounds of modesty. It will be a great thing if it does not burst out to unlawful things, or flow away to useless ones. It will be a great thing if he does not even love himself except so far as is fitting, or expedient. *But the Jordan,* says the Scripture, *had filled the banks of its channel at the time of harvest [n: (Josue VIII)]*. It had filled, it says, not overpassed. It will not be harmful, then, as I think, to fill the banks, but to overpass the banks.
