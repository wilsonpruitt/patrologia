## CHAPTER XVII. *On the failing of vain love, and the progress of true love.*

Therefore the upper waters ascend even unto Sartan, and the lower waters descend even unto the sea. The former ascend and grow, the latter descend and diminish. The former grow and swell, the latter diminish and fail. A fair spectacle, that the upper waters swell about Sartan and the lower waters fail in the sea. Here give heed and be astounded. Here give heed diligently, and be astounded vehemently. Choose, among things so great and so much to be wondered at, what [1085A] thou shouldst wonder at first, what thou shouldst wonder at more: whether that waters among the mountains do not flow down but swell, or that waters out of waters do not swell but fail. *But the waters,* says the Scripture, *which were the lower ones went down into the sea of solitude, which is now called the Dead, until they wholly failed [n: (Josue III)]*. Those, then, which are lower must needs descend into the sea and fail in the sea. But we know that, however sweet the waters may be, since, when they come into the sea, they cannot keep their own sweetness. Those, then, which descend into the sea quickly grow bitter. *Let your laughter,* he says, *be turned into mourning, and your joy into sorrow [n: (Jac. IV)]* — that is, for sweet water to descend into the sea, and to lose its sweetness, and to turn the sweet into the bitter. Laughter indeed and joy make sweet [1085B] water, and of this kind, howsoever ardently each man thirsts, so gladly also does he drink. But mourning and sorrow cannot make water sweet, nor do men readily wish to drink of this kind. As often as laughter is turned into mourning, so often does the lower water slip into the sea and be changed into bitter. For that laughter cannot belong to the upper waters of which it was said by the mouth of Truth: Woe to you that now laugh, for you shall weep. What, then, is it for the lower waters to run down into the sea and to fail, but to draw carnal and lowest delights into grief, and to extinguish them by repenting? For he who laments and groans over a delight already committed, who shudders at one coming upon him and spits it out, who fears one to come and busies himself to avoid it — what else does he do than turn the sweet into the bitter for himself? This, then, this, [1085C] I say, is what we were wondering at above, what we wonder at still: to turn the sweet into the bitter, to bewail joy, to grieve over delight, and in this manner for the sweet to fail in the sea. Do the waters, then, swell about Sartan; do the waters, then, fail in the sea? Is it thus, O man, that thou rejoicest over tribulation? Is it thus, O man, that thou art troubled over delight? O wondrous, O astounding! In voluntary tribulation joys are heaped up; the delights that come to meet us are exterminated by a grief freely taken.

## CHAPTER XVIII. *How, through the failing of vain love, the perturbations of the mind fail.*

But what, I ask, does this mean, that it is called [1085D] the Dead Sea, in which so renowned a deed came to pass? Why, I ask, did it receive such a name? For that it did not have it before, the Scripture sufficiently intimates in this, that it says: *The sea of solitude, which is now called the Dead (Josue III)*. I think that not every sea of solitude, nor every bitterness of desolation — nay, this very sea of which we are now speaking — is by no means worthily reckoned by such a name; nor ought it be called dead, unless perchance the aforesaid miracle come to pass in it. We know that what is dead cannot move itself at all; perhaps this sea, after the wave falling into it had failed, after it no longer had a flood flowing in and driving it, fell away from its own seething, and lost those swelling and boiling storms of its own billowings. For the inflowing rushes of the Jordan, and its most rapid course, [1086A] by their most vehement gliding-in used to drive it and shake it, and did not at all suffer it to be tranquil and quiet. It went, therefore, at that time swelling and foaming and greatly boiling up, and as it were vehemently indignant at the injury of its own commotion and unrest. But after the inflowing inundation began to fail, and the cause of its commotion to perish, the sea itself also began to rest from that stormy agitation of its own, and, as though dead, to lose the motion of its former unrest, and to change its name. So indeed, so the torrent of carnal pleasure and the vehement rush of the flesh, so long as it is vigorous, is wont to be the cause of swelling and of indignation. It is wont indeed to trouble the whole conscience, to stir up waves of bitterness, and to overturn from [1086B] the bottom that deepest abyss of the human heart, and to agitate that great sea with great tempests. For the mind, fervent with heavenly desire, zealous for purity, and tending toward perfection, so long as it is compelled to feel base delights from the rush of the flesh, must needs struggle against the lowest desires, and against those that flow to the depths. Hence it is that it is angry with itself, that it is indignant with itself, that it seems to rage against itself; and I think that a perturbation and tumult of this kind, and a vehement billowing, makes the sea not dead, but living, but strong, but restless. But after the lower waters have begun to run down and to fail, and the flux of the lowest delights to grow faint and to vanish away, the conscience too begins likewise to compose itself little by little to peace and tranquillity, to lay aside more and more the dread of the judgment to come, [1086C] and to lose those seethings of past indignation and anxiety, and, after the likeness of a dead thing, to have no motion of trembling and of perturbation. In this manner, then, the outflow of carnal pleasure, drained and dried up and utterly consumed, makes the sea weak and feeble and tranquil, and such as by right ought to be called dead. Surely thou seest, as I judge, how rightly the sea changed its own name and took on the name of the dead, in which the running down of the lower waters fell away and failed.

## CHAPTER XIX. *With what caution we ought to blot out the perturbations of the heart.*

But it is to be noted how this sea must in [1086D] some respect remain, and yet in some respect be dead: for it is always necessary to repent of the evils committed, to pursue them with hatred and detestation and to shudder at them, yet not always to bewail them and tremble at them, but, after worthy satisfaction and concupiscence subdued, to receive consolation, and to reform the spirit to the hope of pardon and the quiet of security. O truly happy and greatly blessed is he to whom it is given to check the perturbations of the heart and the tempests of sadness, and to compose to tranquillity those great and wonderful liftings-up of the sea! One thing I know, nor dare I doubt it: that so long as love is scattered upon many things and drawn off to the depths, it is never brought through to full peace, never made serene unto the tranquillity of true security. O unhappy and wretched soul, [1087A] how long art thou solicitous, and troubled about very many things? further, since one thing is necessary, fix at length thy desire, love the one. Happy are the citizens of that city whose participation is in the selfsame. Let the waters that are under heaven be gathered together into one place; do not suffer them to flow downward. Or dost thou not know that he who lets his waters go is the head of quarrels? Why dost thou flow away through the lowest desires? What hast thou to do with goods that will perish, and with joys that must be bewailed? Dost thou not know that every water which runs downward descends into the sea? For all rivers enter into the sea, and every lowest delight ends in grief. For laughter shall be mingled with grief, and [1088A] mourning takes hold of the ends of joy, inasmuch as every sweet water descends into the sea and loses its sweetness: for the world passeth away, and the concupiscence thereof. And we know that what is possessed with love is not relinquished without grief. How much better, then, is it to savor the things that are above, to seek the things that are above, and to turn the streams of the Jordan back into their own spring, that we too may dare to take up that voice of congratulation for the use of our own confession? What ails thee, O sea, that thou hast fled; and thou, O Jordan, that thou art turned back?
