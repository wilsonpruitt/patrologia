## CHAPTER ONE.

[1087B]

But neither do I think that this is to be passed over in this place: by what art, or by what industry, it can be brought about that the Jordan turn itself back of its own accord, and by a spontaneous inclination run back to its own spring. For it is one thing to know what is expedient, or what ought to be done, and another not to be ignorant how thou canst obtain what thou dost not doubt will be useful, or even necessary. Who would not despair of being able to turn the rush of so great a river toward the mountains? Who, I say, would presume that he could check that most rapid natural course of human affection, and that most vehement rush toward the depths, and bend it into the contrary direction, unless he receive an art whereby he may hope to accomplish it? In short, we have often heard, and frequently also [1087C] seen, the course of flowing water hindered, nor do we judge this impossible. For it is one thing often to check consent in necessary appetites and in natural affections, and another to change the affection and to turn the outflow of human concupiscence toward the mountains of the virtues, and, by a certain spontaneous inclination of inmost love, to rise up against custom into the concupiscences of supercelestial desires. I think it of little worth, then, to bring forward my own opinion on this, or any man's: let us learn from him who teaches man knowledge. Let us hear, then, what he sets before us for this business, or what he promises for the issue of the matter. When the priests, he says, who carry the ark of the covenant of the Lord God of all the earth shall have set the soles of their feet in the waters of the Jordan, the waters which are lower shall run down and fail; [1087D] but those which come from above shall stand together in one mass. We must therefore, as we hear, have the ark of the Lord's covenant present, if we covet that the lower waters fail and the upper rise up in one mass. O happy they who can have it present — if not always, yet nevertheless frequently — in whose presence miracles so great and so delightful come to pass. But what is that ark of the covenant, I ask, [1088B] unless perhaps that prerogative of divine gifts, the grace, namely, of contemplation? What is that gilded ark, but human intelligence made splendid by the brightness of heavenly wisdom and irradiated by the light of divine inspiration? What do we more rightly understand by that venerable shrine and repository of divine holy things than the spiritual understanding of spiritual men, which both grasps by intelligence and guards by memory the more sacred of the divine secrets and the hidden things of wisdom? But what do we say to this, that this ark is called the ark of the covenant? Can it be that he is wont to give so great a grace as a sign of love and a pledge of charity — he who loves those that love him, and makes them worthy of his love, so far that they deserve to hear from him: Now I will not call you servants, but [1088C] friends, because he has made known to you all things which I have heard from my Father? [n: (Joan. XV.)] And so this ark is not always had present, because the grace of contemplation is sometimes withdrawn even from those to whom it is granted to have it for a time. We have the ark present, then, when from inspiring grace we receive a ray of contemplation. We carry that ark with us when we diligently apply ourselves to divine contemplation, and vehemently [sic: *interdimus*] in the zeal of saving wisdom through investigation or review.

## CHAPTER II. *How the investigation and review of wholesome things avails to correct the spirit.*

For these, unless I am mistaken, are the bearers of our ark, [1088D] by whom it is wont either to be lifted on high or to be carried forward: namely, the investigation of things unknown and the review of things known. For without these it quickly either slips downward or is driven backward. For without review it is not kept from the lapse of forgetfulness, and without investigation it is never advanced to the progress of cognition. But the office of priests, as we all know, is wont to keep watch over the guarding of salvation and the care [1089A] of souls. Our investigation, then, and our review also, ought to attend diligently and to apply themselves unceasingly to the things of salvation, to the things that bear upon salvation, that they may claim for themselves by merit the dignity of the priesthood, and may defend the honor of the name. O how worthy priests! O how fit bearers of the ark — the investigation of things of salvation, the review of things wholesome! By the office of these priests we carry down our ark to the streams of Jordan, when through the zeal of investigation and of review we turn the eye of our contemplation to the consideration of our own desires. And so at their entering in, and at the approach of the ark, thou wilt see the course of the Jordan, and the empire of dissolution, first slacken, then stick fast, and at last go back again. Beyond doubt, the more perfectly thou shalt have inspected the outflow [1089B] of human affection, the more fully thou shalt have known it, by so much assuredly wilt thou wonder at it the more, by so much the more vehemently wilt thou detest it. Consider frequently, then, contemplate diligently the wantonness of thy heart, and the outflow of thy desires. See, then, thy love — whence and whither it runs, whither it tends by desire, or whence it withdraws by loathing — and thou wilt see how it slips from lofty things to the depths, and is drawn off from the highest to the lowest. Beyond doubt, when thou hast begun to inspect these things diligently and to understand them deeply, thou wilt likewise begin to proclaim with the Prophet: *What have I in heaven, and besides thee what have I desired upon earth? [n: (Psal. LXXII.)]*

## CHAPTER III. *That it is easier to correct the spirit than to penetrate its inmost parts.*

[1089C]

But how great a wonder do we think that worthy of, with how great delight to be embraced: that God does not wish to defer his miracle longer, nor to draw out our expectation at length, nor does he press us to descend even to the depths of the Jordan and to penetrate even to that unsearchable deep of the human heart, before he begins to fulfil his promise. Assuredly, since he himself has known our frame, he does not wish to hold us in suspense by the delay of a long waiting, truly knowing that the heart of man is depraved and unsearchable. For who could worthily comprehend it? who could suffice to unfold how great a folly it is, [1089D] how infinite a misery, of one's own accord to forsake the highest goods, greedily to seek the lowest goods, to loathe eternal joys, ardently to covet transitory ones, and for things that shall perish to lose infinite rewards and to incur eternal evils? The incomprehensibility of this consideration seems to be the reason why we are not compelled by the Lord to penetrate to this deep of immensity — lest he should seem to exact of us not merely difficult things, but even impossible ones. But what says the Scripture? *When the priests,* it says, *had entered the Jordan, and their feet were dipped in part of the water, the descending waters stood still in one place, and, swelling after the fashion of a mountain, appeared from afar [n: (Josue III)]*. Dost thou observe, I beseech thee, how straightway at the first dipping of the feet the Jordan begins to check its course and to be turned backward? scarcely had they touched part [1090A] of the water, as thou hearest, and already the streams of the Jordan were turning their own rush back upon themselves.

## CHAPTER IV. *How much it avails to cleave the longer, with deep wonder, to the consideration of our own infirmity.*

But what, I ask, are those feet of the priests, at whose dipping the Jordan is turned backward? But if by the priest we rightly understand investigation, what is more rightly understood by his foot (which is the lowest part of his body) than the depth of investigation? The priest's foot, therefore, is the profundity of investigation. To set the foot in the water, then, is to penetrate hidden things by deep investigation. A foot of this sort is dipped in the water when the mind marvels at the subtlety of its own finding out, and in its own wonder [1090B] takes delight. For what is it to be dipped, but to be affected? What is it to be affected, but to contemplate, to marvel, to be delighted? And although we do not stretch the feet of our investigation all the way to the bottom of the flood, because we penetrate the wanderings of our desires only in part, yet we dip those very feet deeply, though set only in a part, being greatly astounded at what we scarcely, or only in small part, comprehend. We ought therefore, by searching out the inmost parts of the heart, to descend into the waters at least so far that we may be able fully to dip the feet of our investigation, and to turn our spirit into wonder and stupor over the consideration of our own wandering. For this very thing which we can comprehend concerning our follies and our wanderings, I think can suffice us so far that the Jordan [1090C] of its own accord checks the rush of its course, and by a spontaneous inclination runs back to the parts behind, condemning and detesting the bypaths of its former custom, of its own will restraining the lapse of its own ways, and directing them into the contrary. I think that at that time the Prophet had lifted the ark of his own intelligence over the waters of the Jordan, when he was bringing forth a general judgment, not concerning his own heart only, but concerning every human heart: *All is vanity, every man living [n: (Psal. XXXVIII)]*. He saw whither the streams of the Jordan were running, when he cried out by way of rebuke: *How long will ye love vanity and seek after lying? [n: (Psal. XIV.)]* He saw where it would end its course, and how it would fail in the sea, when he said: *Man passeth away in an image, yea and in vain [1090D] is he troubled [n: (Psal. XXXVIII)]*. He had dipped his feet deep in the water when he burst forth into this voice of wonder: *For what have I in heaven, and besides thee what have I desired upon earth? [n: (Psal. LXXVIII.)]* I think that at this dipping of the feet and this wonder of consideration the Jordan, forsaking the depths of desires, returns to the upper parts and seeks again its spring, so far that it says: *My soul refused to be comforted; I remembered God, and was delighted [n: (Psal. LXXVI)]*.
