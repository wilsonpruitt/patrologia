## CHAPTER IX. *On the twofold imperfection, which we ought always to have before our eyes.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But neither indeed did the divine word wish silently [1095B] to pass this over: by how great a space of the way it ought to go before the following throng. ⟦s2⟧ Let there be, he says, between the ark a space of two thousand cubits. ⟦s3⟧ Wouldst thou know what it is for the ark to be with the throng, or what it is for the ark to go before the throng? ⟦s4⟧ When we attend with diligent contemplation to what sort we are by our deeds or our desires, we have our ark with us and as it were present. ⟦s5⟧ But when we look subtly at what sort we ought to be, we send the ark of our intelligence on before our face, that we may not be ignorant whither we must tend. ⟦s6⟧ Who, I ask, can glory either that he does the whole of what he can usefully do, or that he can do the whole of what ought to be done, if it could be done? ⟦s7⟧ For as they do not will the whole of what they can, so neither can they do the whole of what they ought. [1095C] Therefore capacity reaches further than will, and necessity is far more remote than capacity. ⟦s8⟧ Let us therefore send our ark on before, not only as far as the consideration of our own possibility, but even as far as the speculation of necessary usefulness, that we may attend not only to what better we might do than we do, but also to what better ought to be done than we are able. ⟦s9⟧ He therefore sends his ark before his face, as it were a thousand cubits, who diligently attends, who frequently looks, at where he might have been by merit if he neglected nothing of what was possible to him. ⟦s10⟧ But he advances further, even from two thousand cubits, who contemplates not only frequently but almost unceasingly how innumerable are the things he does not do, and how innumerable also are the things he is not able to do, [1095D] which ought to be done if his own strength did not fail for the fulfilling of them. ⟦s11⟧ When we wish to do something strongly, we stretch out our elbow; and therefore we rightly understand by cubits the exercises of the virtues. ⟦s12⟧ The number a thousand is wont to signify perfection, because our reckoning has no greater number. ⟦s13⟧ Two thousand cubits, then, as I judge, seem to designate a twofold perfection: namely, the integrity of the will and the fullness of fortitude. ⟦s14⟧ But whoever cannot yet do the whole of what he ought, and does not even complete the whole of what he can, lacks both perfections. ⟦s15⟧ What, then, is it to send the ark of one's intelligence on before oneself as far as two thousand cubits, but prudently to foresee how much is wanting to one of the twofold perfection, neither of which can one yet [1096A] fulfil? ⟦s16⟧ But assuredly I will say what I feel: I think that he alone has fulfilled this precept of the Lord; him alone, I say, I reckon to have sent his ark on before by two thousand cubits, who declares of himself that he has not up to now laid hold even of the beginnings of the twofold perfection. ⟦s17⟧ For if he cut off even one cubit from so great a space, by walking, by approaching, by hastening, and arrogated to himself at least the first beginnings of consummate perfection, he would not have seen himself remaining two thousand cubits behind the ark. ⟦s18⟧ O perfect man, founded in the highest humility, who can always both make progress by hastening and hasten by making progress, and always keep this humility for himself: that he may always advance in good, and yet may always believe that he has not even the beginnings [1096B] of perfection! ⟦s19⟧ A great spectacle, to see the ark hasten by going before, to behold the throng quicken by following after, and yet always to keep between them the measure of so great an interval. ⟦s20⟧ The more the ark is advanced, by so much the more does the throng also hasten; and the more the following throng presses on, by so much the more does the preceding ark quicken its own journey. ⟦s21⟧ Assuredly, the better we recognize the grace of perfection, the more ardently do we also covet it; and the more we are kindled to love, the more perfectly are we enlightened unto recognition. ⟦s22⟧ The just man will always find how he may set it two thousand cubits ahead of himself, because in this life willing, being able, and owing can never run together into one. ⟦s23⟧ This belongs to the time to come, not to the present life. ⟦s24⟧ There [1096C] assuredly, as he will will the whole of what he justly will be able to do, so he will be able to do the whole of what he by right will owe. ⟦s25⟧ But here the capacity of acting surpasses the action, and the necessity of what is owed exceeds the possibility of doing. ⟦s26⟧ But it is agreed that the one grows out of the other: because, when our willing grows, our being able grows also; and with the increase of both, our owing grows also. ⟦s27⟧ For the more ardently we seek the good, the more easily we accomplish it; and the greater the things we can do, the greater also are the things we owe. ⟦s28⟧ After this manner the ark must always proceed to the parts before, and the following throng hasten after it. ⟦s29⟧ But it ought to be a small thing to thee, who hastenest to the land of promise, that thou send thy ark on before thy face, unless it go before thy journey by two thousand cubits and show thee whither thou oughtest to follow. ⟦s30⟧ Busy thyself, [1096D] therefore, in the consideration and contemplation of thy own perfection, to penetrate so far into the parts beyond that thou mayest truly believe that thou hast not yet attained even its first beginnings, whether by affection or by effect.
