## CHAPTER X. *An example or form of the consideration proposed.*

Do you wish to hear a man picking his way two thousand cubits behind the ark, and not claiming for himself even the first beginnings of a more perfect life? *While I was yet,* he says, *laying the warp, he cut me off [n: (Isa. XXXVIII)]*. He does not say, while I was yet weaving, but, while I was yet laying the warp. What is it to lay the warp, but to premeditate; and what is it to weave, but to do? Indeed, what else is it to lay the warp of what is to be woven and to weave what has been warped, than to preordain the things to be done and to do the things preordained? While I was yet, he says, laying the warp, while [1097A] I was yet premeditating, while I was yet seeking through meditation what I ought to have done, and could not yet fully find it, he sent forth his hand and cut me off. I had not yet, he says, woven anything, I had not yet begun to do good, it was not granted me to begin the web of an ordered life; because, while I was yet laying the warp, he cut me off. I confess indeed that I had already purposed to do good things, that I was already arranging how they might be done, and that I had already warped my web in great part; but, while I was yet laying the warp, he cut me off, and I could not — I will not say weave through, but not even begin — the web of my purpose. How great, I ask, was that man who thinks so humbly of himself, who claims for himself not even the first beginnings of good, since he believes himself still to be laying the warp? Does he not seem to you to be still laying the warp of that web which he had woven with many, [1097B] who diligently sought that he might find and see what was profitable to the sons of men, what work they must needs do under the sun, in the number of the days of their life? And does he not seem even to himself to be still laying the warp and not weaving, who had long striven to know the reason which his soul still seeks, and could not find it? *Behold this I have found,* said Ecclesiastes, *one and another, that I might find the reason which my soul still seeks, and it has not found it [n: (Eccle. VII)]*. What good thing, what great thing, I ask, would that man believe himself to have done, who confesses that he sought and yet did not find what work must needs be done under the sun in the number of the days of his life? It is plain that he had sent the ark of his understanding on before his face, who was seeking whither he ought to follow by desire or by act. [1097C] But he reckons, as I judge, that the throng of his desires now stands as it were idle, who confesses that he has not yet found the reason he was seeking. Or do you still desire to hear more openly of a man who scarcely ascribes to himself the first beginnings of good, and who then, as it were, picks his way two thousand cubits behind the ark? Hear then what he says: *And I said: Now have I begun; this change is of the right hand of the Most High [n: (Psal. LXXVI)]*. Scarcely at some time, he says, did I begin these same things, in that I who believed myself to have begun scarcely and late began to become good out of evil, just out of unjust. Such as I found myself, such I declared myself to be, nor was I ashamed to confess my imperfection; but I said: Now have I begun; this change is of the right hand of the Most High. He does not say formerly, or from that time, but now. Nor does he deny that he has only now begun, lest you should think that he had remained more than two thousand cubits behind the ark. [1097D] Scarcely, when he records that he has at last laid hold of certain beginnings of good, does he show himself to be following after his ark according to the measure of the divine appointment. But, lest anyone believe that he ascribes even this very beginning of good to himself, let him hear what he joins to this saying: this change is of the right hand of the Most High. He could not be content to say, this change is of God, unless he said, this change is of the right hand of the Most High. The very one to whom he was unwilling to ascribe this work, he preferred to designate by a description. He calls him who did this the Most High, and he named the right hand. As if he said openly: He did this mightily who is accustomed to accomplish mighty things powerfully. It was indeed marvellous in his eyes to commit such a man from evil to good as he remembers himself once to have been. What, I ask, did that other man believe himself to have accomplished of the road of perfection, [1098A] who compared all his righteousnesses to the cloth of a menstruous woman? We have, therefore, how we ought to send our ark before our face to a fixed measure, so that, however much we hasten, we may never dare by presumptuous estimation to diminish anything of the space of two thousand cubits, judging that we have always scarcely attained the first beginnings of perfection. We ought therefore, so long as we are in the desert, to gather up the road of our furtherance in the furtherance of our ark, and by another consideration to know beforehand whither we must follow through zeal for a good manner of life.

## CHAPTER XI. *On those things which belong to meditation or to contemplation, and how much the grasp of such things avails for the advancement of virtue.*

[1098B]

But it is not of the same ease to arrange the order of the present life and to estimate the glory of the life to come, to examine the quality of merits and to weigh the quantity of the eternal rewards. For the things that are on earth we shall estimate with difficulty, and the things that are in view we shall find with labor; but the things that are in the heavens, who will be able to investigate? To find, therefore, the things that are on earth is difficult; but to investigate the things that are in the heavens is impossible. Hence it is that the Apostle admonishes: *Be not,* he says, *high-minded [n: (Rom. XI)]*. Rightly, therefore, is the ark of the Lord brought in only after the entrance of the Hebrews into that land of promise, so that where our understanding is not allowed to break in by the steps of its own searching out, it may be led in by the hand-leading of divine revelation [1098C]. Nor yet do we deny that there are some things — nay, many and great things — concerning the quality of the life to come which we can both seek by investigating, and find by reasoning, and construct by arguing. For the invisible things of God, from the creation of the world, are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made. By no means, therefore, are those things to be neglected which can be found by investigation, just as those things which exceed our understanding and human reason are not to be presumed upon out of our own strength. The former are according to reason, the latter are above reason. The former we ought diligently to inquire into by deep searching out; the latter we ought humbly to await from divine revelation. The former belong to meditation, the latter to contemplation. [1098D] Never is the mind fully kindled to the desire of eternal goods, unless it zealously premeditate of what sort and how great they are. And just as it never deserves to be lifted up to the contemplation of them without immense desire, so indeed it is not perfectly inflamed into longings for eternal things without vehement zeal. Hence it is that before Israel's entrance into the promised kingdom scouts are sent ahead, and yet the ark is not brought in until after the entry of the throng. For what is it to send scouts ahead before one's own entrance, and to wish to know beforehand by sagacious searching out the quality of that land into which we hasten to enter, but to direct the thoughts of one's mind, as scouts of the heavenly goods, into the searching of invisible things, and to appoint one's meditations to that work, [1099A] so that by investigation one may find whereby he can urge the throng of his affections into love of them? Let us therefore send our scouts ahead before our face, just as those Israelites once sent theirs, and not once only, but some after others and second after first. As often as we subtly seek anything concerning the goods to come that are to be hoped for or to be merited, we send messengers and scouts ahead before our very selves.
