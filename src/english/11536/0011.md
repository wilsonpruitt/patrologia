## CHAPTER XIV. *That the speculation of merits consists in two things.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But that we may say something more at large concerning the second, it suffices in that scouting to seek sufficiently those things which can suffice for that prophetic admonition: *Turn away from evil, and do good [n: (Psal. XXXVI)]*. ⟦s2⟧ It suffices, I say, shrewdly to provide how cautiously we may beware of evils, how discreetly we may accomplish goods. ⟦s3⟧ One premeditation is in the evils to be avoided, another premeditation in [1101D] the goods to be fulfilled. ⟦s4⟧ But although each is other than the other, yet neither ought to be without the other. ⟦s5⟧ Let them go together, let them run together, and let them succor one another in turn; and without the help of the one or the other they never fully complete their business. ⟦s6⟧ These, as I judge, are the two scouts — provident caution against evil, provident shrewdness in good — whom we ought to send ahead before our face, and by whom we should try out beforehand the roads of our progress: *Go,* says Joshua, *and consider the land and the city of Jericho [n: (Josue II)]*. ⟦s7⟧ Consider, he says, the land and the quality of its regions; inquire diligently into the suitability of the places, that we may know beforehand in what place we can more suitably pitch camp, whither in a moment of necessity it may be expedient to turn aside, whence we must seek the things necessary for daily uses. ⟦s8⟧ Consider therefore and inquire into whatever things are useful, whatever [1102A] are suitable and necessary for our uses. ⟦s9⟧ But consider not only the goods, but the evils also, the advantages and the disadvantages, the fortifications and the dangers. ⟦s10⟧ Consider therefore the land and the city of Jericho. ⟦s11⟧ Consider the city, the strength of its fortifications and the multitude of its enemies, that we may be able to know how we must order the siege, in what manner to assault it, against what sort of men to wage war. ⟦s12⟧ Look therefore upon the land and the city. ⟦s13⟧ Look upon the land which God made, and the city which human invention established. ⟦s14⟧ Examine the good which God created for our good, and the evil which man invented to his own evil. ⟦s15⟧ Consider the land and the city, nature and custom. ⟦s16⟧ Distinguish the good of creation and the evil of corruption, the good of our making and the evil of custom. ⟦s17⟧ If we know the good of nature, [1102B] we know what we ought to cultivate, what to nourish, what to multiply. ⟦s18⟧ If we discern the evil of our own habits, we do not doubt what enemies we must beware of, what adversaries we must assault, what sort of foes we must slay. ⟦s19⟧ Behold what manner of scouts we ought to send ahead before our entry, that we may know against what evils to be on guard and unto what goods to be done we ought to keep watch. ⟦s20⟧ He who sends such scouts ahead before his face will by no means dread the byways of errors.

## CHAPTER XV. *What belongs to meditation, and what to contemplation.*

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But that army of virtues advances securely, and has no diffidence about the certainty of the way, which the sagacity [1102C] of the scouts instructs. and the ordered precedence of the ark directs on its journey. ⟦s2⟧ Let it be too little for us to dispatch scouts before the throng of the Hebrews; let us make our ark also go before our face in the tracks of the scouts. ⟦s3⟧ First it is to search out and find the reason of the things to be done by the scouting of our meditations; and afterwards at last it must be brought into contemplation, that we may be able to have it known, and ready, and familiar. ⟦s4⟧ Contemplation belongs to the carrying forward of the ark, as meditation to the scouting. ⟦s5⟧ One thing belongs to contemplation, another to meditation. ⟦s6⟧ It is meditation's part to search out hidden things; it is contemplation's part to wonder at things clearly seen. ⟦s7⟧ For meditation is the zealous investigation of hidden truth. ⟦s8⟧ Contemplation is the glad wonder at truth clearly seen. ⟦s9⟧ But the things that are hidden [1102D] are wont to come forth into manifestation, now from one's own meditation, now from divine revelation. ⟦s10⟧ Where, therefore, we have the grace of revelation present, we do not need the office of meditation. ⟦s11⟧ But where divine revelation is wanting, the human mind — and no wonder — of necessity has recourse to the zeal of meditation, and takes refuge, as it were, in the offices of the scouts. ⟦s12⟧ But whatever the human mind can come to know concerning the instruction of morals by the scouting of meditations, it ought again to bring into contemplation, and to strengthen the mind in the certainty and the desire of those things. ⟦s13⟧ But when the discovery of meditation is brought into contemplation, what else is it than that the way previously scouted is retraced for the carrying forward of the ark, so that the troop of virtues may follow after securely without suspicion [1103A] of straying? ⟦s14⟧ By meditation, therefore, it is brought about that whatever ought profitably to be done is not unknown. ⟦s15⟧ But contemplation too is wont to accomplish this: that the truth discovered may cleave the more tenaciously to the memory, and the more keenly inflame desire. ⟦s16⟧ By meditation [1104A] therefore we are instructed unto good, and by contemplation we are made solid in good. ⟦s17⟧ This is the reason, as I judge, why we ought to try out beforehand the way of our progress both by the scouting of meditation and by the going forward of the intellectual ark.

## THIRD TREATISE. *Thus far concerning the advancement of good; henceforth concerning the confirmation of the same.*



## CHAPTER ONE.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1103A]

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ It is pleasing to consider this also: what it is that the Lord commands — to gather stones from the midst of the Jordan, from the place where the feet of the priests stood [1103B], and to set them in the place of the camp, and to lay others, gathered from anywhere at all, in the former place. ⟦s2⟧ Let us ask, therefore, what that place is where the feet of the priests halt at the time when the miracle is to be wrought, or in what place likewise the camp is to be pitched after the miracle has been celebrated. ⟦s3⟧ Who would doubt that it is above human power that the Jordan should go back, and that a heap of waters should rise up into one mass? ⟦s4⟧ Who, I ask, unless he were out of his mind, would presume upon so stupendous a miracle from his own power? ⟦s5⟧ Where, then, ought the mind to fix its hope under the expectation of so great a thing, save in trust in the divine magnificence? ⟦s6⟧ A safe place of standing, and one of great security, is presumption upon the help of the divine mercy. ⟦s7⟧ But there is another place very unlike this place, namely the stealing in of diffidence and of despair. [1103C] A place indeed dreadful, full of fear, full of solicitude, and such as it befits at last to fortify with camps, to gird with a rampart, to surround with watches, because on account of impending dangers it is not safe to go forward into what lies beyond. ⟦s8⟧ The former we run into at the time of the divine visitation, the latter at the time of testing. ⟦s9⟧ For the dispensation of the divine mercy is wont to comfort a man with its visitation, and again to test him with various kinds of temptations: *Thou visitest him,* says Job, *early in the morning, and suddenly thou provest him [n: (Job VII)]*. ⟦s10⟧ Behold, we have assigned two places, one of confidence, the other of diffidence; the one where divine miracles are wont to come to pass, the other where the camp must be pitched. ⟦s11⟧ They that trust in the Lord shall change their strength, they shall take [1103D] wings as eagles, they shall run and not faint. ⟦s12⟧ Is it not wholly above man to change one's strength, to fly after the likeness of an eagle, to run and not faint? ⟦s13⟧ Yet these things they alone can do who presume upon the mercy of the Lord and not upon their own strength. ⟦s14⟧ So Peter was able to run upon the waves so long as he held the place of confidence with the foot of hope [n: (Matth. XIV)]. ⟦s15⟧ But after he began to forsake this place of his security, the wave could no longer hold him poised upon its surface. ⟦s16⟧ You see how a strong wind coming upon him cast him down from the place of confidence to the place of diffidence, because the Lord, after his manner, had visited him early in the morning and suddenly had tested him. ⟦s17⟧ So the children of Israel, after the miracle had been wrought and seen, came to the place of the camp.

## CHAPTER II. *On the confirmation of the mind in good and the hardening of the mind in evil.*

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1104A]

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1104B]We have assigned the difference of the places; let us see also the two kinds of stones. ⟦s2⟧ I think there is one kind, of good stones, and I think there is another, of evil stones. ⟦s3⟧ To the good stones belongs every confirmation of the mind in good; but to the evil stones, every hardening of the mind in evil. ⟦s4⟧ What do you think? ⟦s5⟧ Solidity, stability, firmness, weight, and many things after this manner — do they not seem to you to draw to themselves a certain likeness of stones, and by the right of that likeness justly to claim such a name for themselves? ⟦s6⟧ What do we say of hardness of heart, of tenacity or obstinacy? what of harshness, austerity, and cruelty? ⟦s7⟧ Do not those things claim for themselves the name of stone which draw to themselves the likeness of stones? ⟦s8⟧ But the former belong to the good stones, as these latter to the evil stones. [1104C] But I think it is not of the same ease to find good stones or evil ones, because it is more difficult to find good stones than an abundance of evil ones. ⟦s9⟧ For everything precious is rare. ⟦s10⟧ The good, therefore, are gathered from the midst of the Jordan, but the evil from any places whatsoever. ⟦s11⟧ Concerning the former, Scripture plainly designates where they must be sought or found; but concerning the latter it judges it as it were superfluous to determine the place of their discovery.
