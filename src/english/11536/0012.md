## CHAPTER III. *On the evil of presumption or of despair.*

But this seems wonderful and worthy of great wonder: that we are bidden to lay good stones, gathered from a good place, in an evil place, but to heap up evil stones [1104D] gathered from anywhere in a good place. The good place is the presumption of confidence, the evil place the despair of diffidence. But nevertheless we ought to gather evil stones in the place of confidence, and to gather good stones in the place of diffidence. In the day of good things be not unmindful of evils — and you have gathered evil stones in a good place; and in the day of evils be not unmindful of good things — and you have gathered good stones in an evil place. For what is it to gather good stones from the midst of the Jordan (that is, from the place where the prodigy was wrought), but diligently to go back over the state of the mind, such as it was at the time of the divine visitation, and subtly to investigate of what firmness it stood in each several virtue, and, by committing this to memory, to raise up as it were a certain cairn of testimony? But we never have an abundance of such stones [1105A] unless we have first received the grace of the divine visitation and inspiration. He testifies to this who says: *Without me you can do nothing [n: (Joan. XV)]*; therefore neither is it possible anywhere to gather good stones save by his working. But we easily find an innumerable multitude of evil stones, which we gather abundantly and ready to hand out of our whole life. What need, then, was there to designate a place for these, which we can gather plentifully from anywhere? Let each one, therefore, consider of what hardness, of what obstinacy he was, as often as divine grace forsook him and left him to himself: and in doing this he has raised a cairn of evil stones. In this manner, therefore, we can raise cairns and set them up, different ones on different sides — on this side one of good stones, on that another of evil stones. He set up for himself a heap of good stones [1105B] who said: *I will never forget thy justifications, for by them thou hast given me life [n: (Psal. CXVIII)]*. He had raised a cairn of evil stones when he said: *For I know my iniquity, and my sin is always against me [n: (Psal. L)]*.

## CHAPTER IV. *How presumption must be repressed out of the recollection of our evils.*

So do you too: when you have perceived that you yourself hold the place of confidence, and have observed that you presume upon things higher than your merits, remember at once what you once were, and, turning your eyes back to the heap of evil stones, you keep yourself in humility. It is indeed good [1105C] to presume, yet not upon your own strength, but upon the mercy of the Lord. Let the gathering of evil stones in a good place avail each man for this: that through the memory of his own evils he may never presume upon his own powers. That each one, then, may learn to presume not upon himself but upon the Lord, let the recollection of his own evils always meet him in the place of presumption: that trust of presumption, surely, appeases the Lord and obtains great things, which humility commends. They that trust in the Lord are as mount Sion. So, on the contrary, that trust of presumptions is abominable which pride begets. For a king is not saved by his own great strength, and a giant shall not be saved by the multitude of his own strength. Cursed therefore is the man that trusteth in man, and maketh flesh his arm [1105D]: for he shall be as the tamarisks in the desert, and shall not be satisfied when the summer heat is come, how useful, then, do we suppose, will be in a place of this kind that gathering of evil stones which we have prescribed, and that recollection of vices, which will be able to keep us from this execrable type of pride, and to protect us from a curse of this kind.

## CHAPTER V. *How despair must be driven back out of the recollection of our goods.*

So indeed, when danger is at hand and temptation comes upon you, when you have perceived that your mind is touched by despair, and cast down to the place of diffidence, and rolled down to the appointed place of the camp, you ought to have the cairn of good stones present, and to turn your eyes back to the memory of virtues [1106A] past, and out of the recollection of them to refashion the trust of your hope. So Job, stripped of his goods, bereft of his sons, smitten with a sore, when he saw himself caught in the place of the camp, and perceived himself beaten by the evil of despair, in order to temper the evil of diffidence had recourse to the pile of good stones, and out of the recollection of good things fortified his camp on every side, and bravely drove back the assaults of despair, when he says: *I was an eye to the blind, and a foot to the lame; I was the father of the poor, and the cause which I knew not I searched out diligently. I broke the jaws of the wicked man, and out of his teeth I took away the prey [n: (Job XXIX)]*. So too Hezekiah, in a moment of necessity, had recourse to fortifications of stones of this kind, when he despaired of his life; and out of the setting forth of his own good acts [1106B] he called forth the mercy of the Lord, and by the obtaining of his prayers drove off the perils of diffidence: *Remember,* he says, *O Lord, how I have walked before thee in truth and with a perfect heart, and have done that which is good in thy eyes [n: (IV Reg. XX)]*. And so we too ought to be held ready and familiar at every time of temptation, to keep present the memory of the goods done before; and when we shall have brought this habit into continual use, we have, as it were, placed good stones — and stones chosen according to the divine instruction — in the place of the camp. For it is nothing else to gather chosen stones into one place than diligently to go back over the qualities of the divine confirmation and corroboration and to commit them to memory [1106C]; and it is nothing else to have fixed them in the place of the camp than to have firmly made a habit of such memory at every moment of temptation.

## CHAPTER VI. *On the [sic: duodceim] principal virtues in which the mind must be made solid.*

And these we gather from the midst of the channel of the Jordan, dried up by the power of God, and to the number of the twelve tribes, when we diligently examine the state of our mind, such as it was at the time of the divine visitation, and subtly search out what sort of men we were at that time in each of the principal virtues, in some measure. For this is for single men from single tribes to take up each his own stone: to have truly found of how great firmness one once stood, in some measure, with respect to each several virtue [1106D] through the grace of the divine breathing. But what we think concerning the twelve sons of Jacob, from whom these twelve tribes draw their origin, we have already said in another place. By Reuben and Simeon we understand fear and compunction; by Levi, hope; by Judah, charity; by Issachar, gladness; by Zebulun, severity; in Gad is understood abstinence; in Asher, patience; in Dan and Naphtali, circumspection and speculation; in Joseph and Benjamin, discretion and the contemplation of invisible things. But the reason of these we now wish to set forth in this place, concerning which we have already said elsewhere what seemed to us more correct. But let him who trusts that his seed from such offspring has been multiplied into twelve tribes learn what sort of stones he ought to have taken up from the midst of the Jordan, [1107A] when he shall have advanced this progeny of virtues as far as its crossing. Let him therefore call to mind what strength, what fortitude or firmness he felt himself to have drawn in at the breathing of the divine inspiration; and this is to have taken up chosen stones from the determined place. But that this choosing of stones may be made by single men from single tribes, let him exercise this labor of his consideration through each of the virtues which we have distinguished, and extend the number of his stones as far as the number twelve. Let him therefore consider, whoever desires after the turning back of the Jordan to fulfill this precept of the Lord; let him consider, I say, of what vigor he was, in some measure, in each several virtue at the time of the divine visitation. Let him consider how great solidity he had at that time in fear, how great weight in compunction, [1107B] how great stability in hope, how great integrity in charity, how great maturity in gladness, how great harshness in severity. Let him consider how austere he was in abstinence, how robust in patience, how solicitous in circumspection, how assiduous in speculation, how certain in discretion, how quiet in contemplation. But when he shall have fully discerned all these things and committed them to memory, he has raised such a cairn as was needful. It would be a business without end, and one which no one at all could have fulfilled, if the Lord had bidden all men from all the tribes to take up each his own stone, because no one would suffice to weigh himself worthily — what he is with respect to all things, I will not say in every virtue, but in one only, whichever it be. It suffices, therefore, to set apart some one [1107C] notable and signal desire of his own ardor through all these virtues which we have determined, and to establish the use of such a memory, and in recollection of this kind, under every peril of diffidence, to fight against the evil of despair. For he easily restores his own mind, however cast down, however beaten by temptation, to the hope of the divine compassion and to the state of confidence, who calls back to memory what manner of man he once was by the divine working. Altogether and without doubt he gladly exercises himself in such a pursuit, to whom it has been given to know what it avails, or that this zealous collecting of stones of this kind is useful.
