## CHAPTER XIV. *On the strength of patience.*

After these stones there follows the strength of patience: which [1110A] stone I recall that I reckoned in the eighth place. Behold a stone most strong, and the hardest of all. This stone that standard-bearer of the heavenly warfare, the protomartyr Stephen, holds in his hands, and it became for him a tower of strength against the face of the enemy. In this stone he bravely endured the blows of the stones that pressed upon him, and he feared neither the hands of them that stoned him nor the onset of them that gnashed with their teeth at him [n: (Act. VII)]. O stone strong and hard, yet full of sweetness! O breast most robust, yet full of mercy! You wonder, whoever you are that hear, how in his patience he possessed his soul; but you ought rather to have wondered how his mind, under so great a bitterness of persecution, did not lose its own sweetness. By his enemies he was pressed, and for his enemies he prayed. He was enduring human savagery, and [1110B] he was looking up to the divine glory: *Behold,* he says, *I see the heavens opened, and Jesus standing at the right hand of the power of God [n: (ibid.)]*. On earth he was pressed, and from the heavens he was consoled. He was afflicted by the misery brought upon him, he was moved by the mercy grown within him, he rejoiced at the glory beheld. Behold, at one and the same time he saw below that whereat he might have compassion, he saw above that whereat he might be glad. Behold how from the same breast there flowed mercy with gladness, gladness with mercy. Behold how from the same vessel there flows oil with honey, the oil of mercy with the honey of gladness. What, I ask, is this unmingled confusion and unconfused commingling of oil and honey in that stone? I marvel if you do not marvel how that strong fighter at one and the same moment sucked honey out of the rock and oil out of the hardest crag. [1110C] Marvellous is the virtue of patience; and who may worthily weigh the price of this rock, which in so great abundance distils oil with honey? How great an abundance of oil and of honey, I ask, is found to be within, where mercy is diminished by no malice, where sweetness is thinned by no grief? Nay, in a wondrous manner delight grows out of grief, and compassion out of suffering. The martyr indeed, when he was being stoned, had more pity upon his enemies than upon his own limbs. For kneeling down he prayed, saying: *Lord, lay not this sin to their charge, for they know not what they do [n: (ibid.)]*. Behold, at the placing of his knees the oil was poured out, and thereafter his name was made as oil poured out. For at the pitying of his enemy the pouring out of the oil was made, so much so that [1110D] the young man who sat at a distance was sprinkled with the odor of the ointment. For when the body was bowed and the head bent down, the oil began to flow forth, and it was made as ointment upon the head, and it ran down to the hem of the garment of him who kept the garments. Rising up therefore swiftly, he who had long sat in dark places and in the shadow of death began to run after the odor of the ointments; and exulting as a giant to run his way, whithersoever was the onset of the Spirit, thither he went, and thereafter he became the good odor of Christ in every place.

## CHAPTER XV. *On the solicitude of circumspection.*

The ninth rock I find in Hezekiah; I find it also in the friend's bride and the bridegroom's friend, to whom [1111A] it is said in the Canticles: *Arise, my friend, my beautiful one, and come, my dove, in the clefts of the rock, in the hollow of the wall [n: (Cant. II)]*. So too Hezekiah, after the likeness of the bride choosing quiet and seeking after solitude, and sitting in the clefts of the rock, or even in the hollow of the wall, cried out thus as the young of a swallow, and meditated as a dove: Oh who will give me wings like a dove's, and I will fly away and be at rest, if perchance I may be able to fly forth into that place where the sparrow has found herself a house, and the turtledove a nest where she may lay her young! Where, I ask, will anything of safety be found, if it be not found in the clefts of a rock of this kind, where zealous premeditation foresees whatever dangers before they come to pass, and, forestalling the things to come, gathers the future out of the past, and often knows beforehand the undoubted [1111B] outcomes of doubtful matters? For what else is circumspection, but the solicitous and sagacious searching out of the evils that stand round about? To this belongs the going back over past evils, the consideration of present ones, the premeditation of those to come, so that according to its own name it may truly be circumspection. The assiduity of this makes a man cautious, and, so far as is possible, renders him safe.

## CHAPTER XVI. *On the assiduity of speculation.*

The tenth stone has been called the assiduity of speculation. Jacob, turning away from evil and giving place to wrath, while he sets out abroad in haste, lest his flight should be in the winter or on the Sabbath, found this stone upon the road, [1111C] and having found it placed it under his head, lest he should not have where to lay his head while he slept upon the way [n: (Gen. XXVIII)]. Upon this stone, in peace, in the selfsame he sleeps and rests, and upon the road he found that rest which they cannot find even in chambers and on purple couches who are clothed in soft garments and are in the houses of kings. And so, Jacob's head being laid down upon this stone, and the eyes of concupiscence being closed (which we read were open in our first parent), while upon this headrest he is lulled in deep quiet, while he gladly forgets all outward things, his mind wakes up to inward things, and sees what before, while waking and seeing, it could not see. A ladder reaching up to heaven is raised before his eyes, and the Lord is seen leaning upon the ladder, so that through invisible form and the figure of invisible things [1111D] the mind of the beholder may be lifted up into the understanding of intelligible things. Happy the man for whom the knowledge of visible things becomes a ladder for knowing invisible things. A beautiful spectacle it is to see the rungs of this ladder set in fitting manner and in due proportion, and carried up to the topmost point by the continuity of an ordered distinction, so that through the manifold difference of creatures and the gradation of dignities, ordinately distinguished and distinctly ordered, the mind may little by little advance itself to higher things, and by the steps of its furtherance climb ever to loftier things, until the invisible things of God, from the creation of the world, are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, and the Lord is seen leaning upon the ladder. Who, I ask, would see this, save one sleeping upon this headrest? who while awake would bring it about that he should stretch out the ladder of his ascent [1112A] as far as heaven, and join the speculation of invisible things to the speculation of visible things? What of this, that upon this path of ascent the play of an angelic meeting offers itself unbidden, so that divine revelation may come to meet human investigation? And so the angels are seen upon this ladder now to ascend, now indeed to descend, and now to instruct the mind toward the highest things, now toward the lowest, so that in things divine and human it may come to know from divine revelation many things which it could not know by itself. And indeed the angels could, without the ministry of a ladder, at the choice of their own desire either descend into the lowest places or fly forth to the highest; nor would there be need for the divine inspiration, in any manifestation of its revelations, to seek the instrument of any bodily likeness, unless it willed that we should see first through [1112B] a glass and in a dark manner, and not at once face to face. And so the angels ascend and descend upon this ladder of likenesses, and some after others now approach, now depart, so that while of the manifold revelations one is brought in and another withdrawn, the mind of the beholder may be illumined in manifold fashion, in order that what it could not grasp all at once, by reason of the slenderness of its understanding, it may understand successively and one by one through intervals of delay. Behold what they find who fall asleep upon this stone. Happy they for whom that is turned into a ladder which for others is wont to be unto ruin. Happy they for whom the knowledge of outward things becomes a ladder of ascent, not a ruin of casting down. Happy they for whom that knowledge which puffs up is not unto puffing up, but unto the inflaming of her that buildeth up. Happy they for whom the beauty of temporal things becomes [1112C] an incitement to eternity. It behooves therefore, as you perceive, him who desires to find this ladder of ascent to lay his head upon the stone, and to harden his mind in the desire of this speculation, and persistently to press upon this pursuit.
