## CHAPTER XVIII. *On the quiet of contemplation.*

The twelfth stone, and the last of all, we can find, as I judge, at the Lord's sepulchre. It has been called, as was said above, [sic: *lapis is*] the tranquillity of contemplation. Of this kind of stones that Joseph of Arimathea hewed out sepulchres for himself; but Jesus, dead, rested in it, because the quiet which prudence seeks for itself through meditation and describes by definition, wisdom finds through contemplation [1113C] and lays hold of by experience. True prudence always seeks, and always ought to seek, that peace which Christ taught, that it may not be troubled nor be afraid. It always strives after where it may find such peace, always endeavors to defend true security for itself; but it always finds something to grieve over out of the past, something to fight against in the present, something to beware of and to dread for the future. The mind, therefore, can shrewdly seek this peace through prudence and subtly investigate it through meditation; yet it will never be able to find it save through wisdom and the grace of contemplation. But when the mind has begun through pure understanding to go beyond its very self, and wholly to enter into that brightness of incorporeal light [1113D], and to draw from the things which it sees in its inmost parts a certain savor of inmost sweetness, and from that to season its own understanding and to turn it into wisdom, then so far, in this excess of mind, is that peace found and obtained which is neither troubled nor afraid, that there is made a silence in heaven as it were for half an hour, so that the mind of the contemplative is troubled by no tumult of contending thoughts: you will find nothing at all which it may either seek through desire, or reprove through disgust, or accuse through hatred. And so he is composed unto the highest peace who is buried in this stone, who is wholly gathered in and shut up within the tranquillity of contemplation. For this stone is not, like that of Jacob, set under the head alone, nor is it, like that later one, spread beneath the feet, but it is fitted and applied to the whole [1114A] body. And so this stone encircles the whole body, encloses the whole, and comprehends it on every side, in that that peace which surpasses all understanding utterly absorbs every human sense, and by a happy transfiguration turns the purer part of the soul into a certain divine habit. Here the body lies without sense and motion in this monument of the Lord; sensuality does nothing, imagination does nothing, and every lower power of the soul is meanwhile clothed with its own office.

For this stone monument does not (as that craggy headrest of the patriarch Jacob does) take up a living body, however lulled asleep, nor does it receive a body unless it be mortified. It is one thing to sleep, another to die. It is one thing to gather one's whole spirit into itself, [1114B] and another to lift it above itself and to forsake oneself. It is one thing to have curbed appetite and to have cut away from the heart the care of outward things, and another that the heart itself should come into forgetfulness of itself. It must be, therefore, before it is permitted to enter into that secret place of inmost quiet and that hidden chamber of the highest tranquillity — it must be, I say, that there take place that very grave and truly wondrous thing: not the dissolution of soul and body, but another far more wondrous and far more glorious than this, that one, in short, of which this is a type, namely the division of soul and spirit. And this, as the Apostle testifies, that living and effectual word of God is wont to work in us, more piercing than any two-edged sword, and reaching unto the division of soul and spirit. What, I ask, anywhere among creatures is discerned more wondrous than this division, where [1114C] that which is essentially one and undivided is cleft into itself, and that which stands simple in itself and without parts is divided and separated from itself? For in one man the essence of his spirit is not one thing and that of his soul another, but altogether one and the same substance, and of a simple nature. For in this twofold term a twofold substance is not understood; but when a twofold power of the same essence is set down for the sake of distinction, the one, the higher, is designated by "spirit," the other, the lower, by "soul." In this division, therefore, the soul and what is animal remains below; but the spirit and what is spiritual flies forth to the heights. What is gross and dreggy fails like a body deadened, and falls back into itself [1114D] and beneath its very self; what is subtle and purged ascends like a spirit breathed forth, and transcends within and beyond its very self. O deep quiet, O sublime rest, where everything that is wont to be moved after a human manner loses all motion, where every motion that then is comes about divinely, and passes over into God! Here that Spirit breathed forth and commended into the hands of the Father does not need a ladder, as did that dreamer Jacob, that it may fly forth to the third — not to say the first — heaven. Why, I ask, should he need a ladder whom the Father bears between his hands, to snatch him to the secrets of the third heaven, so much so that he glories and says: *Thy right hand hath upheld me. Thou hast held my right hand, and by thy will thou hast conducted me, and with glory thou hast received me? [n: (Psal. XVII.)]* That Spirit, therefore, [1115A] taken up out of the midst, has here no need of the office of a ladder, nor does it need in that ascent of its own subtlety to be supported by the shadowing forth of any bodily likeness, where it sees face to face, not through a glass and in a dark manner. I lie, if they themselves who are of this kind do not assert the same concerning themselves: *But we all,* they say, *beholding the glory of the Lord with unveiled face, are transformed into the same image from brightness to brightness, as by the Spirit of the Lord [n: (II Cor. III)]*. You surely see what it does, and you understand, as I judge, what that division of soul and spirit avails, of which we have already spoken above. The spirit is divided from the lowest things that it may be raised to the highest. The spirit is cleft from the soul that it may be united to the Lord. For he who cleaves to the Lord is one spirit. Happy division, and separation to be longed for, where that which [1115B] is discerned to be passible, to be corruptible, meanwhile dies to its own passions, so far that nothing of passibility, nothing of corruptibility is meanwhile felt; where also that which is spiritual, which is subtle, is raised up even to the speculation of the divine glory, and is transformed into the same image. The lower part, therefore, is composed unto the highest peace and tranquillity, but the higher part is raised up unto glory and gladness. So we have recognized that the face of Moses (the higher part indeed of the body) was glorified from fellowship with the Lord, so that the children of Israel could not look upon his countenance because of its brightness. Who, I ask, would suffice worthily to tell, who to unfold, what of perfection the spirit acquires in the glorification of its own excess, [1115C] although it does not extend the prolonging of its pilgrimage as far as the third day, even if it does not draw out the delay of silence as far as half an hour, [1116A] granted that it goes and returns after the likeness of flashing lightning? So Moses brings back from the fellowship of the divine converse, together with a glorified countenance, horns also, showing what of power, what of strength he had drawn from companionship with him who gives power and strength to his people, the blessed God. That spirit therefore at last returns and comes back to itself which had gone far beyond itself; and what it had laid aside as passible and corruptible it takes up again as it were impassible and incorruptible, by comparison with its former state, and rises again into newness of life. What does it seem to you, to grow cheerful at an injury inflicted, not to blush at a contumely cast in one's teeth, to rejoice over tribulation? is not this to walk in newness of life, and after some manner to show oneself impassible, and not to lie subject [1116B] to one's own passions? Behold how far they advance who rest in this stone. There are many things which could have been said concerning this matter, if they ought to have been said in this place and did not exceed the measure of a moderate digression. For I think this last kind of stones to be the most worthy and most precious of all. Yet we ought to despise none, but to seek at least single ones out of the single kinds, and to gather them into one.

It is indeed to be noted that this is the first work which is commanded to be done in the land of promise, so that an eternal memorial of the divine works may be established first of all. For without this mound of stones that promise of the Lord of an eternal inheritance is never bravely acquired, never securely possessed. For he who forgets the benefits [1116C] divinely received does not deserve to be advanced to the obtaining of greater things.
