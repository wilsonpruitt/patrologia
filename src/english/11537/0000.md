## A SERMON ON PALM SUNDAY.

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1059A]

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ We keep a twofold Pasch; a twofold Pasch, surely, we ought to celebrate. ⟦s2⟧ The first is that which is commonly wont to be called the flowering Pasch, of which we are now constrained to say something suited to the season. ⟦s3⟧ But whom, I ask, [1059B] would it not delight to speak of such a season? ⟦s3⟧ Whom would its loveliness not rouse to its praise, or inflame to its love? ⟦s4⟧ O lovely season, acceptable season, flowering season! ⟦s5⟧ A lovely season, which is marked out by the manifold variety of flowers; an acceptable season, which is besprinkled round about with a pleasing fragrance. ⟦s6⟧ To whom, I ask, would it not be pleasing or acceptable, when it can now be truly said: *For now the winter is past, the rain is gone and departed: the flowers have appeared: the flowering [sic: Vinae] have given their odor? [n: (Cant. II.)]* Whom would it not please to cry out in its praise that word of the Apostle: *Behold, now is the acceptable time; behold, now is the day of salvation? [n: (II Cor. VI.)]* An acceptable time indeed, which is adorned with flowers; and no less healthful, since from it fruit also is hoped for. ⟦s7⟧ Without any ambiguity, if the flowering season does not [1059C] go before, we look for fruit in vain and with an empty hope. ⟦s8⟧ Let the flower-bearing Pasch, then, go before, that the fruit-bearing Pasch may follow. ⟦s9⟧ This twofold Pasch we surely ought to keep as a feast, and to receive with all solemnity. ⟦s10⟧ Do you wish to know more plainly what it is that we mean to say? ⟦s11⟧ You know, as I judge, that *Pasch* is interpreted *a passing over*, and that the Pasch is named from the Lord's passing over. ⟦s12⟧ What, then, do we call the flowering Pasch, save an honorable and pleasing passage? ⟦s13⟧ And what the fruit-bearing Pasch, save a glad passage, full of all joy? ⟦s14⟧ Take, then, a twofold passage, both parts of it pleasing, both honorable. ⟦s15⟧ The first is that we pass over from wickedness to righteousness; the second, that we pass over from misery to glory. ⟦s16⟧ Brethren, what do we say to these things, or of what sort do we think such [1059D] passages to be? ⟦s17⟧ What, I ask, will it be to pass over from iniquity to goodness, to pass over from calamity to blessedness? ⟦s18⟧ The first passage belongs to the first Pasch, the second to the second Pasch. ⟦s19⟧ You see, I beseech you, how gladly and how thankfully we ought to keep such a season as a feast, and to celebrate it solemnly. ⟦s20⟧ And that second Pasch, indeed, we all love in common and alike aspire to. ⟦s21⟧ But is that first one received with the same devotion, or celebrated with equal zeal? ⟦s22⟧ Would that all were as zealous to be good as they aspire to be blessed — then indeed they would be as blessed as they are good! ⟦s23⟧ Would that all aspired to the beginnings of the just as much as they long for their last ends! ⟦s24⟧ *Let my soul die,* says Balaam, *the death of the just, and let my last end be like to theirs (Num. [1060A] XXIII)*; but let those who wish to have the like of the last ends of the just be zealous to have the same beginnings. ⟦s25⟧ Let them flower meanwhile through righteousness, and they shall take fruit from it: life everlasting. ⟦s26⟧ From a righteous life, eternal life; from a good life, a blessed life; from such a flower, such a fruit; from a perfect life, [1060B] a perennial life. ⟦s27⟧ Every tree is known by its own fruit. ⟦s28⟧ That is no evil tree which makes good fruits. ⟦s29⟧ If the tree is good, its fruits also are good, and its flowers pleasing. ⟦s30⟧ But it is always necessary that the flowers go first, that the fruits may follow; otherwise, without preceding flowers, they are hoped for in vain. ⟦s31⟧ Let the flowering Pasch, then, go before, that the fruit-bearing Pasch may follow; let us meanwhile pass over from dishonor to honor, that afterward we may pass over from unhappiness to happiness. ⟦s32⟧ If we are still evil, let us pass from evil to good; if good, let us pass from good to better, and through the better to the best — and we have kept the flowering Pasch.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Why, I ask, do men come together at this flowering Pasch, unless to make a procession according to custom! ⟦s2⟧ Is not the very going in procession a passing over and a keeping of the Pasch? [1060C] And we then keep that Pasch as a flowering one, if we pass over in due order and to more honorable things; and we make a fair procession indeed, if we make progress in good. ⟦s3⟧ How fair, how comely a procession do we think it to go from virtue to virtue, to forget the things that are behind and to stretch forth to the things that are before? ⟦s4⟧ O what flowers are the true virtues! ⟦s5⟧ Let us therefore strew our way with such flowers, and pass over through the flowering fields of the virtues; let us go in procession purely and honorably, that we may make our procession fair and pleasing, and may accomplish the flowering Pasch with a worthy solemnity. ⟦s6⟧ O how pure, how glad it will be to go through flowers and to pass through the lovely places of the virtues: through the crocus of charity, through the lily of chastity, through the rose of suffering and the violet of humility! ⟦s7⟧ O how praiseworthy, O how lovely, to scatter a pleasing fragrance on every side, [1060D] and to spread abroad round about the sweet smell of one's good name, and to draw to the glad spectacle of one's procession the hearts of those who run up from every side, and to summon the hearts of the beholders to the example of one's own uprightness! ⟦s8⟧ This, then, is the first procession, and it belongs to the first Pasch; for there is another also, and that one belongs to the second Pasch. ⟦s9⟧ The first procession is an advancement to the perfection of goodness; the second procession, an advancement to the fullness of blessedness. ⟦s10⟧ Christ made a twofold procession, and surely willed us to make a twofold one: *I came forth,* he says, *from the Father, and came into the world; again I leave the world, and go to the Father [n: (Joan. XVI)]*. ⟦s11⟧ His going forth is from the highest heaven — this is the first procession; and his circuit even unto the height thereof — this is the second [1061A] procession. ⟦s12⟧ First he descended, afterward he ascended. ⟦s13⟧ The first procession is of humiliation, the second of exaltation. ⟦s14⟧ If we are truly Christians, we ought to imitate Christ. ⟦s15⟧ For he who says that he abides in Christ ought himself also to walk even as Christ walked. ⟦s16⟧ First let us be humbled with Christ, that afterward we may be exalted with Christ. ⟦s17⟧ Be humbled meanwhile under the mighty hand of God, that he may exalt you in the day of judgment, in the time of the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ. ⟦s18⟧ Let us therefore follow Christ meanwhile by the way he went on earth, that at length we may deserve to follow by the way he has gone in heaven: *The eyes of wisdom are in his head [n: (Eccle. II)]*. ⟦s19⟧ If you are wise, you who have Christ for your head ought to look to him. ⟦s20+21⟧ Mark the footprints of his journey, and strive to follow with what strength you can. See therefore how he went in procession, or by what way he went before you — [1061B] surely this paschal procession of today's festivity, which generally may be out of the remembrance or the imitation of his works, proceeded. ⟦s22⟧ Mark, then, what procession of his the evangelist describes for you, or what he sets before you to be imitated. ⟦s23⟧ Let us see, then, whence he began; let us see whither he went on; let us see by what way he passed; let us see how he went in procession. ⟦s23⟧ This is what I mean to say, this is what I warn you to mark: whence, and whither, by what way and how Christ made his procession. ⟦s24⟧ Whence did he begin? ⟦s25⟧ From Bethany [n: (Joan. XII; Matth. XXI; Marc. XI; Luc. XIX)]. ⟦s26⟧ Whither does he make his way? ⟦s27⟧ To Jerusalem. ⟦s28⟧ By what way did he pass? ⟦s29⟧ By the Mount of Olives. ⟦s30⟧ How did he come? ⟦s31⟧ On an ass. ⟦s32⟧ From Bethany, then, he began his journey, and went on to Jerusalem, sitting on an ass and passing by Olivet: this is the procession of Christ, which Christians ought [1061C] to imitate. ⟦s33⟧ Behold in what order, behold by what journey, we ought to go to Jerusalem; behold to what end we ought to tend in everything that we do. ⟦s34⟧ Keep, brethren, the unity of faith in the bond of peace, and you are in Jerusalem, and you have completed such a procession as Christ taught. ⟦s35⟧ For Jerusalem, as you know, is interpreted *vision of peace.* ⟦s36⟧ Behold, brethren, what it is to which we mean to exhort you: that we seek peace, that we lay hold of peace, that we guard peace. ⟦s37⟧ Turn away from evil and do good, and you have begun such a procession as befits a Christian; seek after peace and pursue it, and you have come to Jerusalem, and have completed the procession. ⟦s38⟧ In fine, a fifth thing is in us: let us have peace with all men, and we are in Jerusalem; [1061D] we have, surely, some who are above us, some below us, and some our equals. ⟦s39⟧ Let us therefore have peace with our superiors, peace with our inferiors, peace with our equals; and we have peace with all, and have come even to Jerusalem. ⟦s40⟧ The first thing, surely, and in every way most necessary, is that we not disturb the peace of our prelates, to whom it was said by the Lord: *He that heareth you heareth me, and he that despiseth you despiseth me [n: (Luc. X)]*. ⟦s41⟧ Let us be ready at every hour for every obedience, and without doubt we have full peace with our prelates. ⟦s42⟧ In what, I ask, can they be disturbed toward us, if we refuse everything that they refuse, and will everything that they will? ⟦s43⟧ If therefore we wish to make for Jerusalem and to obtain true peace, we must begin from obedience, and [1062A] begin the journey of peace from Bethany. ⟦s44⟧ For Bethany is interpreted *house of obedience.*

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But neither do I think that this is to be passed over here, though I mean to mention it only briefly: that some are above us in power, others in dignity; the former by office, the latter by merit. ⟦s2⟧ The former preside by certain grades of honor and power; the latter excel by certain increases of wisdom and of holiness. ⟦s2⟧ Human election perfected the former; divine ordination raised up the latter. ⟦s3⟧ Consider, then, what a thing it is to reverence those whom human appointment has set in front, and to be unwilling to venerate those whom the divine disposition has singularly magnified. ⟦s4⟧ If those who preside over us for a time are to be venerated with due honor, with how great honor are they worthy who by the merit of holiness excel us forever in eternal exaltation? ⟦s5⟧ Hence it is that [1062B] good prelates venerate with great honor such subjects as these, over whom nevertheless, as has been said, they rule by certain grades of prelacy. ⟦s6⟧ First therefore and chiefly must we take pains to have peace with all who are above us; but neither ought this to suffice, unless we are zealous to keep the bond of peace with our inferiors and with the weaker also. ⟦s7⟧ Our superiors we ought to soothe by compliance and obedience; our inferiors, by having compassion on their weakness and by coming to their aid. ⟦s8⟧ To superiors obedience is to be shown; upon inferiors mercy is to be expended. ⟦s9⟧ The journey, then, is to be begun from Bethany, but the passage is to be made by Olivet. ⟦s10⟧ For ὄλεον among the Greeks sounds *mercy*, and oil itself designates the works of piety. ⟦s11⟧ And so we pass by the Mount of Olives when we expend the labor of piety upon whatever weaker brethren there are, [1062C] when we have compassion on their weakness rather than grow angry or indignant. ⟦s12⟧ Assuredly you see how much this avails for obtaining peace with the weak, and for possessing the tranquillity of your own soul. ⟦s13⟧ For he who is troubled and grows indignant at every offense that he sees — when, I ask, will he find peace? ⟦s13⟧ When, I beseech you, will he obtain true tranquillity of soul, or at least hope to obtain it? ⟦s14⟧ He will always find in weaker men something that he may rightly blame, something that ought to be mercifully borne with. ⟦s15⟧ Otherwise those who had nothing of weakness would not be weak. ⟦s16⟧ Let the advancement or procession of our progress, then, be made by the Mount of Olives, and let our piety grow immeasurably, so that by right of its greatness it may justly [1062D] be called a mountain; so that we may learn to bear with tranquillity of soul not only the least failings of the weak but even the greatest, and to sustain patiently and with piety our neighbors' weakness, not only bodily but spiritual also. ⟦s17⟧ Assuredly, if we do these things, we are already drawing near to Jerusalem, and we reach the fullness of peace the more nearly as we lay hold of the perfection of obedience and of mercy the more highly.
