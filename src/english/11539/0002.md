## On Judiciary Power in the Final and Universal Judgment (cont.)

For, to say nothing of the rest: when shall we be able to distinguish by groping between white and black, between the comely and the misshapen, which by the light of a lamp we discern [1184B] at once, at the very first glance? And what are the hearts of the perfect, illumined by the splendor of divine wisdom, if not kindled lamps? Of these lamps the Lord threatens where he speaks through the prophet: *At that time I will search the houses of Jerusalem with lamps [n: (Soph. I)]*. Jerusalem means the same as *vision of peace.* The houses of Jerusalem, then, are secure consciences: for these are they which seem to have inward peace. What, then, will become of the houses of Babylon, when even the houses of Jerusalem are to be searched with lamps? What will become of foul and desperate consciences, if the presumption of confidence is taken away even from those falsely secure? The houses of Jerusalem, then, are said to be searched through by the Lord, because in those consciences too which [1184C] seemed secure to themselves nothing hidden, nothing unexamined will be left that is not convicted by the light of truth. And this searching of the houses with lamps the Lord declares he will make, because he will execute the judgment even of the most hidden thoughts and wills by the ministry of those whom the highest understanding of truth has inwardly illumined for the examination of every judgment. A certain splendor of the divine light, therefore, will radiate from the hearts of the perfect, which will lay bare to each one, by manifest vision, all the good or evil things that lay hidden in his heart. Consider, then, how now, at the pouring-in of this corporeal light, one at once surveys all the things that are in one's house, and detects not only the quantity but the quality of things in one simple glance. But it is agreed that that [1184D] incorporeal light is much more excellent, much more efficacious in its own kind, than this one is or can be in its kind. It is too little, then, that I said that that incorporeal ray fully shows individuals to themselves — indeed, both to themselves and to others. We see how, at the first ray of the emerging sun, all things which blind night had taken away from the eyes of all it restores again to the eyes of all — how at once all things lie open that before lay hidden. Scarcely yet do the peaks of the mountains receive the ray of light, and already, from the reverberation of the light which they pour back into the valleys, all things that are there, all things that are done there, are laid open to all. So indeed, so the highest and perfect men, founded in perpetual stability, [1185A] magnified with stupendous sublimity and truly eternal mountains, receive immediately the ray of divine wisdom, and from the received, so to speak, reverberation which they spread on every side, bring into the light, in a moment of time, all things that must be made manifest for the reckoning of judgment. Hence is that which you hear in the Psalms: *Thou dost enlighten wonderfully from the eternal mountains [n: (Psal. LXXV)]*. Now, as I judge, you weigh in part how this illumination from the eternal mountains is, without any ambiguity, wonderful. For from that light of divine splendor which they receive immediately, by pouring it back to lower things, they show to any of those to be judged, with no doubtful vision, the quality both of merits and of recompenses. Certainly we who are still in this life, when we wish to enlighten some, cannot enlighten many [1185B] as to the same thing, but different men as to different things, at one and the same time. But if we cannot at once and at the same time instruct different men as to different things, how much less one and the same man as to many and greatly differing things? Consider, then, how wonderful is that illumination where, under one vision, in the greatest multitude, any individual is enlightened at once and together as to countless things. But this dilation of the mind, by which the human soul is dilated in the light of God to comprehend so many things under one vision — because we cannot for the present prove it by experience, we believe with difficulty. Here occurs that which the venerable Pope Gregory writes of blessed Benedict [n: (lib. II Dialog., cap. 35)]. For he relates of him that at a certain time, [1185C] while he was engaged in nocturnal prayers, suddenly, light being poured from above, the whole world, as though gathered under a single ray of the sun, was brought before his eyes. But let us hear what Gregory himself adds to this, as though by way of exposition: « But that the world is said to be gathered before his eyes, he says, does not mean that heaven and earth were contracted, but that the mind of the beholder was dilated. » If, then, the mind of one still placed in this world could be so dilated in the light of God — one who was surrounded with mortal flesh and set thus far under the condition of this life — what ought we to believe of that condition of the life to come, where we shall mutually see one another's consciences? But if at the pouring-in of corporeal light countless things can fall under one glance, how much more, at that pouring-in of incorporeal light, is it to be believed that incomparably [1185D] more things can be comprehended under one simple vision? If, then, according to the likeness of its own property, we grant to incorporeal light what by experiment we prove of visible light, I do not know what should prevent that last judgment from taking place with all speed. Or did the Lord perhaps wish to express this very thing by a likeness when he said: *As lightning comes forth from the east and [1186A] appears even unto the west, so shall be the coming of the Son of man? [n: (Matth. XXIV.)]* Lightning surely, when it appears, appears suddenly, and with all speed disappears. Of that light, then, which uncovers the consciences of the wicked, and shows what men deserve what things, must we not believe that it suddenly appears to them and suddenly disappears, although the memory of the things they have seen does not perish at all? For perhaps not without cause did he compare his coming to lightning rather than to the ray of the sun: for the sun too reaches from the east even to the west, but lightning never appears except for a little while; whereas with the ray of the sun it is wont to happen far otherwise. It will appear, then, like lightning, and reaching from east to west, from end to end, so that with wondrous speed it may bring forth into the light all things that everywhere on earth are, will be, [1186B] or have been, and, as far as pertains to the reckoning of judgment, all things may come forth at once and together to manifestation: *We shall all,* says the Apostle, *rise again, but we shall not all be changed. In a moment, in the twinkling of an eye,* etc. [n: (I Cor. XV)]. He who will be able to restore the bodies of all with such haste — will he not, with the same speed, be able to illumine the hearts of all as to whatever he will, as far as it pleases him? If we do not think that a ray of such brightness as we described above radiates from the hearts of the apostolic men, what shall we say of that heart in which dwells that brightness of the eternal light, in which are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge, in which dwells the fullness of that light which enlightens [1186C] every man coming into this world? There is surely none of the faithful who would dare to doubt that a light sufficient for all things can radiate from the heart of Christ; and, as follows from this, none who would presume to deny that the apostolic men receive immediately the rays of so great an illumination, and pour them back upon others, so that what is written may stand: *Thou dost enlighten wonderfully from the eternal mountains.* The apostolic men, then, will without doubt, above all other men — indeed, above all the malignant spirits — be not only more perspicacious in the understanding of truth, but also more supereminent in the prerogative of power, to such a degree that of all the rest no one at all will be able either to refute their sentence or to resist their violence. For the tormentor demons too, who before dragged men to torments, [1186D] will now, the turn being reversed, be disposed through the places of punishment according to the decrees of the men who judge. Behold, now it appears, as I judge, what is that prerogative of judiciary power conferred upon the apostolic men by the one Lord of all. Behold, what before was thought to be of no moment has been found to be of such great sublimity as can scarcely be believed by the less learned.
