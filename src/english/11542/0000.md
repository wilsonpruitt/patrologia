## On the Sacrifice of David the Prophet

[1031C] *I will offer up to thee burnt offerings full of marrow, with the burning [1031D] of rams; I will offer to thee bullocks with goats [n: (Psal. LXX)]*. Let us see, if you please, what sacrifices of this kind mean — the flesh of the ram, the flesh of the ox, the flesh of the goat. For I think that all these things are spoken in figure. Otherwise, if in all these things you have a carnal understanding, if you attend to the letter alone, attend to what is said to you: *Shall I eat the flesh of bullocks? or shall I drink the blood of goats? [n: (Psal. XLIX.)]* In the carnal sacrifices of the Jews, therefore, let us understand nothing carnally, but let us examine spiritually those things which according to the letter savor of flesh. For the Prophet would never have promised the Lord the flesh of ram, of ox, or of goat as though it were something great, had he not perceived that in these something great, and exceedingly pleasing to the Lord, was foreshadowed. The ram is an exceedingly meek animal, which before its shearer is dumb, and openeth not its mouth. In [1032C] this very thing, then, it bears the form of meekness, which [1032D] amid oppressions and insults keeps patience. Do you wish to hear how great the power of meekness is, or what the recompense of this gift is? *But the meek,* he says, *shall inherit the land: and shall delight in abundance of peace [n: (Psal. XXXVI)]*. How great a thing is it, I ask, to receive an eternal inheritance, and to possess it with peace? But with what kind of peace? They shall delight, he says, in abundance of peace. It was too little for him to say, they shall delight in peace; but they shall delight, he says, in abundance of peace, because of that peace there shall be no end. Now the ram is the leader of the flock. He, therefore, who wishes to be the author of this gift must, through the virtue of meekness, show himself the pattern of the whole congregation, so as to be an example to all the rest. To have, then, a meekness that is proved and imitable by all — this is to offer a ram; and proved virtue rebukes [1033A] the foolish. So much for the ram; let us now see concerning the ox. The ox, as we know, of its own accord bows its neck to bear the yoke. This is the form of obedience, which is always wont, and ought, to subject itself to another's will. As, therefore, in the ram proved meekness is understood, so in the ox voluntary subjection. O what a sacrifice, and how great, wholly to set aside one's own will, and to expose oneself entirely to another's judgment, reserving nothing of oneself for oneself! O how confidently will he stand beside the Crucified who for his sake subjects himself to every obedience! With how great a security of conscience will the truly obedient man draw near to the throne of grace of him who was made obedient to the Father unto death, even the death of the cross! With how many trophies will he go forth from the racecourse of this life who for Christ's sake [1033B] utterly casts himself away, and makes himself over to another's service, according to Solomon's saying: *An obedient man shall speak of victories [n: (Prov. XI)]*. Assuredly he speaks that which he works. How great a victory, to conquer sin! How great a victory, to conquer oneself, to conquer the world, to conquer the devil! Thus he conquers all things who overcomes his own will, who willingly puts his neck under the yoke of obedience. To offer an ox, therefore, is willingly to display voluntary subjection. So much for the flesh of the ox. Let us now see what is to be thought concerning the flesh of the goat.

We know well enough what that virtue is which makes a man cheap in his own eyes, and, as it were, foul in his own nostrils. The goat indeed has a fetid odor, and we offer the Lord as it were a goat through voluntary self-contempt. [1033C] As, then, voluntary subjection is figured in the ox, so voluntary self-abasement is aptly figured in the goat. To expose oneself, therefore, to every meanness and abasement for the Lord's sake, and humbly to acknowledge one's own weakness — this is to offer the Lord the flesh of a goat. The less flesh of this sort is to the taste of the human palate, the more it is to the taste of the divine. For the Lord is high, and looketh on the low things: and the high things he knoweth afar off. See, we have now said what sacrifices of this kind mean — the flesh of the ram, the flesh of the ox, the flesh of the goat. The flesh of the ram, proved meekness; the flesh of the ox, voluntary subjection; the flesh of the goat, voluntary self-abasement. These are the sacrifices full of marrow, sacrifices exceedingly pleasing to the Lord. Marrow lies hidden within the bones, and fattens. The marrow of the virtues is good intention; [1033D] the marrow of the virtues is inward devotion. For from good intention, from the inmost devotion of the soul, the virtues are watered, and are made solid unto the strength of perseverance. Sacrifices full of marrow, therefore, are devout services. Let our meekness then be devout, let our obedience be devout, let our humility be devout, that every offering of ours may be full of marrow. See, we have what sort of victims we ought to offer the Lord. See, we have what sort of banquet we may set before him, whenever we will. I think that if from the animals designated we set three dishes before him, we shall be able to furnish him a supper exceedingly pleasing: *Behold I,* he says, *knock at the door: if any man shall open to me, I will come in to him, and will sup with him [n: (Apoc. IV)]*. Let us meanwhile bring him in to our supper, that one day we may deserve to be brought in to his. [1034A] But if there are among you men of riches for whom it is too little to set before him only three dishes after the likeness of David, let them be eager, after the example of Abraham, to add a fourth and a fifth. For Abraham offered the Lord a fivefold sacrifice, as he received it from the Lord's own instruction; for when Abraham had received the promise concerning the land of the Canaanites which he was to possess in his heirs, by the exceeding boldness of that familiarity which he had with the Lord he humbly sought of the Lord a sign to this very effect: *Lord God,* he says, *whereby may I know that I shall possess it?* And the Lord to Abraham: *Take,* he says, *me a cow of three years old, and a she-goat of three years, and a ram of three years: a turtledove also, and a dove [n: (Gen. XV)]*. Abraham did what by divine instruction [1034B] he had received, and, fire having been received from heaven upon his victims, he obtained the sign he had desired. That he received fire from heaven was assuredly a sign of great love, and rightly afforded great confidence for hoping greater things: for lesser benefits freely bestowed give, beyond doubt, the boldness to presume upon greater. So we receive earnest-money, that we may hold the certainty of the agreement unshaken. That fire, then, sent from heaven, which consumed his victims, was for Abraham as it were a certain earnest of the divine promise. I think that we too, if we have received the heavenly fire, the fire divinely sent, shall thenceforth be wholly unable to doubt any more concerning the divine promises: *Fire,* he says, *I came to send upon the earth; and what will I, but that it be kindled? (Luc. [1034C] XII.)* But when Jesus brought this fire from on high, was fire then lacking on the earth at that time, or was it perhaps not good? Beyond doubt he found an earthly fire, but he brought a heavenly one. The one he came to extinguish, the other to kindle vehemently. All love is a fire, but not all love is good. An evil fire is worldly love, the love of the flesh, the love of baseness. A good fire is spiritual love, the love of truth, the love of virtue. It is good, therefore, to extinguish the one, and no less good to kindle the other, and diligently to feed it. A good fire is the fire of charity, a good fire and one to be fed vehemently. This is the fire which came from the heavens, which descended upon the disciples in tongues of fire: *The charity of God,* says the Apostle, *is poured forth in our hearts by the Holy Spirit who is given to us* [1034D] [n: (Rom. V)]. Behold the fire that is sent from heaven; behold the fire that is given only from God. Beyond doubt, he who has received this fire will not be able to doubt any longer concerning the promise of the eternal inheritance. Assuredly, where there is abundance of charity, there can be no place for mistrust. For perfect charity casteth out fear.
