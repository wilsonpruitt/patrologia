## On the Sacrifice of David the Prophet (cont.)

But do you wish to know more plainly that those who receive this fire cannot waver concerning the promise of the eternal inheritance? *Charity,* he says, *beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things [n: (I Cor. XIII)]*. For how should that charity mistrust the divine promises which believeth all things, which hopeth all things? I think that the Prophet had received this heavenly fire when he said: *I believe to see the good things of the Lord in the land [sic: interra] of the living [n: (Psal. XXVI)]*. And to Abraham [1035A] indeed the land of the Canaanites is promised, and, an earnest as it were having been received in the fire sent down from above, he is made firm in the [sic: *cercitudine*] of his hope. Since, therefore, the promises were spoken to Abraham, if we wish to become co-heirs of promises of this kind, let us take pains to obtain from the Lord by prayers the fire we have designated. Let us consider what it is to receive as an inheritance a land flowing with milk and honey, and to wrest it by force from the hands of the Canaanites. A great matter surely, and exceedingly difficult. The patriarch knew this, and knew it well; and therefore he sought that his confidence in so great a promise be confirmed by a sign. But what, do we suppose, is this land which flows with milk and honey, which wholly overflows with such sweetness? Where, I ask, is this land? Where is it to be sought, or where is it to be found? Concerning the kingdom of heaven the catholic [1035B] faith has most certainly acknowledged that it is within us. But that heaven which is not within — why should it not have a land of its own kind, just as that heaven which is seen has its own land? If the kingdom of heaven is within us, why should not the land of the living also be within us? Assuredly that inheritance which is promised to the spiritual is proved to be spiritual. If you will, it is a land; if you will, it is a heaven. A land because of its stability, a heaven because of its sublimity. If we are spiritual, if we have a spiritual understanding, let us seek this land flowing with milk and honey within, not without. Let us ask, then, what this land is, which refreshes its tillers with a wondrous sweetness, gives them sweetness to drink, feeds them with sweetness. Now we know that, whereas the other elements are always in motion and can never be at rest, the earth alone [1035C] abides in one place, and settles itself in utmost quiet. We know none the less that fear is wont always to shake the mind, but security to steady it and settle it to quiet. Security of conscience, therefore, bears the form of earth in this very thing, that it has always been accustomed to gather the human heart to quiet. This land flows with abundant sweetness indeed, because security of conscience prepares the heart's gladness. The land of promise is a secure conscience; the honey-flowing land, a glad conscience. Rightly is such a land taken away from the Canaanites; rightly is such a land conferred upon the Jews. The worst race of all the men who at that time were upon the earth was the race of the Canaanites. The best race of all the men who at that time were upon the earth was the race of the Hebrews. [1035D] You see that this land belongs either to the very evil or to the very good. Assuredly none can, or are wont to, have security of conscience save either the very evil or the very good. Do you wish to know more plainly how the very evil are wont to possess such a land? There are the wicked, who are as secure as if they had the works of the just [n: (Eccle. VIII)]. For the wicked man, when he is come into the depth of sins, contemneth [n: (Prov. XVIII)]. We know none the less that perfect charity casteth out fear, and straightway lets security in. For if our heart do not reprehend us, let us have confidence toward him. You see clearly that what makes security of conscience is either a great perversity or a great perfection. A conscience evil and secure, in the very evil; a conscience good and secure [1036A] in the very good; a conscience evil, but not secure, in those not very evil; a conscience good and not secure, in those not very good. So long, therefore, as the conscience is secure and evil, the Canaanites are still in the land. But when the conscience has begun to be secure and good, you may know that the Jews now dwell in the land. For nothing at all is wont to produce security of conscience save either a great enormity of vices or a great perfection of virtues. This is that inheritance which must altogether be wrested from the hands of the Canaanites, and purged with the utmost zeal from every stain of vices. This is that inheritance which flows with milk and honey, because security of conscience is wont to fill the human mind with gladness and exultation. He willed us to be refreshed with the sweetness of milk and honey who said: *Be glad in the Lord, and rejoice, ye just (Psal. [1036B] XXXII)*. A false security makes a false joy; a true security, a true joy. O what and how great a joy the testimony of a good conscience has! *Our glory,* he says, *is this, the testimony of our conscience [n: (II Cor. I)]*. In the milk and honey understand a twofold joy, of a conscience secure and good. A whole conscience indeed brings forth a twin joy: joy over the beatitude of the body, joy over the beatitude of the mind. From this twin recompense we rejoice meanwhile in hope, but in the time to come we shall rejoice in the reality; milk flows forth from flesh, honey comes down with the dew from heaven. The milky sweetness is indeed drawn from flesh, when from the beatitude of the body joy and exultation arise. The honeyed sweetness flows down as it were from heaven, when from the incorruptibility of the mind a certain inestimable [1036C] rejoicing arises. That sweetness too is milk which we shall drink in the contemplating of creatures; but that is honey which inebriates the mind from the contemplation of the Creator. Will not this also be to suck milk — to rejoice in the humanity of Christ beheld? Will not this also be to be refreshed with the honey of the highest sweetness — to glory beyond measure in the divinity of Christ beheld? In milk there is indeed sweetness alone, yet not the highest; but in honey sweetness both alone and highest. For what is sweeter than honey? In contemplating the humanity of Christ there is indeed great gladness, yet not the highest. But in contemplating his divinity, a gladness both alone and highest. It pertains to the beatitude of the outer man to see with outer eyes the human nature in God. But it pertains [1036D] to the beatitude of the inner man to see with inner eyes the divine nature in the man. In milk, therefore, understand whatever of beatifying sweetness a corporeal substance supplies to the bodily senses. In honey take whatever of glorifying gladness an incorporeal substance distils into the corporeal senses

What else, then, will it be to receive as an inheritance a land flowing with milk and honey, than to possess for ever the eternal security of a twofold beatitude? If, therefore, we desire to inherit [sic: *promissionnes*] of this kind together with faithful Abraham, let us, for the certainty of our hope, ask as it were for a sign that heavenly fire which we designated above. For, as we taught above, the heart which has fully glowed with the fire of charity no cloud of ignorance, no torpor of mistrust [1037A] will be able to enfold. But let him who desires, after the likeness of Abraham, to receive fire sent from heaven, be eager likewise himself to offer a like sacrifice. Abraham is therefore commanded, as has already been said, to take a cow of three years old, and a she-goat of three years, and a ram of three years, a turtledove also and a dove [n: (Gen. XV)]. Let us now see what this sacrifice of Abraham means, or in what it seems to differ from that sacrifice of David already spoken of. What is it, I ask, that the Lord requires of the great patriarch not an ox, but a cow? Does he then in this kind of victim love the female more than the male? Perhaps it is so. For he would not exact from so great a friend what he himself loved the less. If things mystically spoken are examined by a mystical interpretation, I think it becomes plain that what [1037B] is said is found to hold good: We know of the ox that it can bear the yoke, but cannot give milk. But it falls to the cow to do both — both to give milk, and to bear her master's yoke. O what a sacrifice, both humbly to bear the yoke of obedience, and amid whatever hardships of obedience to keep the sweetness of the heart, and to distil through one's words nothing other than a milky sweetness! Many, when hard things are enjoined upon them, take it ill indeed, and bear it not so much sweetly as bravely. Now we know that the male sex is stronger than the female; yet it cannot give milk. A brave obedience, then, makes the ox; but a sweet obedience the cow. To offer an ox is to obey manfully; to offer a cow is to obey graciously. We offer an ox by obeying steadfastly; we offer a cow by obeying [1037C] gladly. The flesh of the ox, a sturdy obedience; the flesh of the cow, a cheerful obedience. Let him, therefore, who can, offer the flesh of the cow; he who cannot do this, let him offer the ox's. He who can do neither of these, let him at least offer a calf from the herds. The calf indeed is reared for the bearing of the yoke, yet meanwhile is not put to it. But that meanwhile it does not bear it comes not from the fault of kicking back, but from a lack of ability. If, therefore, you do not profess obedience, not because you will not, but because you cannot, you vow a calf and pay it. For what do we understand by the calf but the purpose of obeying, just as in the ox and the cow the practice of obedience? If, then, you cannot offer a cow or an ox, offer at least [1037D] a calf, which, whenever you fully will it, you can have at hand. Assuredly, if you cannot have the office or the practice of obedience, you can have the purpose of obedience, you can have the desire of obedience; and whenever you will, you can offer a calf of this kind. Those who are joined in matrimony cannot at once profess obedience when they will. For those joined in matrimony cannot be separated from one another without the leave of either party, and therefore neither can they profess obedience. Let them, then, offer a calf who are of this sort, who cannot undergo the yoke of obedience at the judgment of their own mind. Hence it is that in the Law the anointed priest is commanded to offer a calf for a sin of ignorance. The anointed priest is a prelate endowed with charity. The anointed priest is a prelate loving toward his neighbor and devout toward God. [1038A] Such a priest, the more truly he loves those subject to him, the more vehemently does he pursue and hate their faults. But often, while he strikes at their faults with too little discretion, while he knows not how — or neglects — to weigh the strength of those he strikes, by striking too incautiously he extinguishes charity in them. Thus he sins through ignorance, when he heaps up the evil which he ought to have cut away. Let the priest, therefore, who by acting indiscreetly offends through error while he intends the good, offer a calf for his sin, and let him henceforth have the desire rather of obeying than of commanding — he who by experience now proves that he cannot fully dispense, as he ought, the office of rule entrusted to him. For what else is it for him to offer a calf, than now ill, and to desire to be under rather than over, and to render obedience [1038B] to others rather than to exact it from others. He, therefore, offers a calf from the herds who now desires to become as one of those subject.
