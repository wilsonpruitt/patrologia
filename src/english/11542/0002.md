## On the Sacrifice of David the Prophet (cont.)

It is indeed to be noted that the priest who sins through ignorance is commanded to offer for his sin not an ox, but a calf, lest he dare rashly and inconsiderately to cast off the pastoral care which he once received. He, therefore, who is of this sort ought not so much to enter upon the place of subjection as to seek after it, not so much to snatch at it as to desire it; and this will be to offer not so much an ox as a calf. Let him therefore cut away from his affection the love of prelacy, yet let him hold the office of prelacy for the fear of God and the profit of his neighbor, until he find a fitting and reasonable way out; and let him meanwhile, for his sin, through the desire of subjection [1038C] offer a calf. But what do we say this to be, that David promises not an ox but oxen, whereas from Abraham's hands the Lord willed to require not cows but a cow? If it is more to promise and to pay oxen than an ox, why should it not equally be greater to present cows rather than a cow in the sacrifice of God? But ought we to believe that in Abraham's sacrifice anything could have fallen short of the perfect? To whom, then, is perfection rightly ascribed, if it is denied to the greatest of the patriarchs? And who would say either that the Lord did not instruct so great a friend unto the best things, or that so great a man had not a mind ready for the instructions of perfection? But that I may say briefly what I think concerning the ox or the oxen, this, as it seems to me, is to offer an ox: bravely to stretch out one's hand to [1038D] the full paying of the obedience that is owed. This victim is doubled when the hand of our working is manfully stretched out both to the obedience that is owed and to that which is not owed. Almost all of us, when we come to religion, promise obedience with a limitation, for we profess obedience according to our order. It is not, therefore, in the prelate's power forcibly to compel his subject to those things which are above the order of his profession. For that is owed which is enjoined according to the order; that is not owed which is above the profession. We therefore offer oxen with the Prophet, since we wring from our own mind both the obedience that is owed and that which is not owed, and steadfastly display it. But we truly offer cows when we pay both obediences not [1039A] only with equanimity, but even with delight. How, then, is a single cow required of Abraham, or how is one only paid by so great a man, as has been said? For neither does the unowed obedience alone suffice for the paying of what is owed, nor the owed alone for perfection. I will surely say — I will say what my mind suggests to me on this matter; let each judge as he will. You can, if you will, make no obedience unowed for yourself; and then I do not see how God can require of you more than one cow. If you promise before your God, or profess before your prelate, obedience unto all things, then henceforth no obedience will be unowed by you, and for the debt of obedience it suffices you to offer a single cow. Better, as it seems to me, is one single cow offered after this manner, [1039B] than when your victim is doubled after the manner aforesaid. No obedience indeed will seem to you heavy or unbearable, if you perfectly emulate the example of him who was made obedient to the Father unto death, even the death of the cross [n: (Philipp. II)]. We therefore offer a cow in the Lord's sacrifice after Abraham's example, if we promise full obedience unto all things, and pay it with joy. And let us be prompt and ready, so far as in us lies, to display such obedience not only to our betters or our equals, but even to our inferiors; and it will be established beyond doubt that, according to the Lord's precept or teaching, we have offered a cow of three years old. He willed us to offer such a victim, assuredly a cow of three years old, [1039C] who said: *Be ye subject to every human creature for God's sake [n: (I Petr. II)]*. So much for the cow; now let us see concerning the she-goat. But it is easy, unless I err, to gather from what has gone before why the Lord preferred to require of Abraham a she-goat rather than a he-goat. In the he-goat or the she-goat voluntary self-contempt is understood. Now the she-goat is wont to furnish milk, not the he-goat. To offer a she-goat, then, is to bear the contempt shown one with great sweetness of heart. Now the male sex, as was said above, is stronger than the female. A he-goat, therefore, and not a she-goat, is offered when the contempt of oneself is by anyone not so much joyfully received as bravely borne. He, therefore, who cannot offer a she-goat, let him offer at least a he-goat; he who can offer neither of these, let him offer at least a kid. The kid is a tender thing, and one which without its mother's milk [1039D] can scarcely, or in no way at all, live. He therefore offers a kid who bears the contempt shown him with difficulty indeed and with great trouble, yet, refreshed by the milk of another's consolation, wrings patience from himself. While, therefore, one bears the contempt shown him patiently, another bravely, another gladly, the first is seen to offer a kid, the second a he-goat, the third a she-goat. If, then, you embrace patience under contempt, you offer a kid of the goats. You offer a he-goat by contemning the contempt shown you. You offer a she-goat by growing cheerful at the injury inflicted. A she-goat, unless I err, was the oblation of those of whom you have it in the Acts of the Apostles: *The apostles went from the presence of the council rejoicing, because they were accounted worthy to suffer reproach for the name of Jesus [n: (Act. V)]*. But if you are a poor little man, and are not equal to the oblation [1040A] of a she-goat or a he-goat, offer at least a kid of the goats. With the flesh of kids assuredly did Jacob satisfy his father, and received the blessing owed to his brother. You hear of a blessing; attend also to what sort it is: *God give thee,* he says, *of the dew of heaven, and of the fatness of the earth, abundance of corn, and wine, and oil; and let peoples serve thee, and tribes adore thee. Be thou lord of thy brethren, and let thy mother's sons bow down before thee. He that curseth thee, let him be cursed; and he that blesseth thee, let him be filled with blessings [n: (Gen. XXVII)]*. He, therefore, who desires to inherit such a blessing, let him hear the counsel of Rebecca: *Bring me,* she says, *two of the best kids, that of them I may make meats for thy father, whereon he feedeth willingly [n: (ibid.)]*. You see clearly that he must prepare two kids who pants after so great a blessing [1040B]. Assuredly, when you act badly, you are justly despised. And what else than the flesh of kids do you offer, if you embrace patience? But he who is despised even amid good works, and yet embraces patience, is seen to double his victim. To offer twin kids, therefore, is to bear patiently contempt both just and unjust. And anyone is then seen to offer a twin he-goat, when he is found ready to bear bravely both just and unjust contempt. But to offer she-goats is to grow cheerful in mind at either contempt, and amid whatever injuries, however unjust, to retain the heart's sweetness in the heart. But as for him who can truly say with blessed Job: *For neither doth my heart reprehend me [1040C] in all my life [n: (Job XXVII)]*, I know not how in this kind of sacrifice his victim can be doubled.

For how, I ask, can he incur just contempt whom his own conscience bites in nothing? Rightly, therefore, did the Lord seek of Abraham one she-goat only, whose life was beyond reproach, and who could be despised by others only undeservedly. Rightly did David in this part double his victim, who amid the many good things he did also committed certain most grievous evils. He had, therefore, in his life both that for which he could justly, and that for which he could only unjustly, be reprehended or despised; whence it was also necessary that he should double his victim in the Lord's sacrifice. Happy is he who so lives that there is nothing for which he can rightly be reprehended by others. [1040D] For him who is of this sort it suffices to offer one he-goat, and to bear unjust contempt with equanimity. But the she-goat is then the oblation of the perfect man, when, unjustly spurned, he receives it with joy. We are despised indeed now by superiors, now by equals, now by inferiors. But we ought to receive the mockeries and unjust reproaches of any whatsoever willingly, nay even gladly; and this will be, as I judge, to offer a she-goat of three years. So much for the cow, so much for the she-goat. It remains still to consider the oblation of the ram. The sheep is a meek animal, and therefore, as we have already taught, an animal of this kind can figure meekness. You offer, then, a beast of the flock when you display meekness. But it is in your power whether you will offer a lamb, or a ewe, or a ram, and set it in the Lord's sacrifice. The lamb, as [1041A] we know, does not so much pour out milk as suck it. But on the contrary the ewe does not so much suck as pour out. The ram indeed is wont to do neither of these, but rather to strike with his horns. He therefore makes a lamb who from the sweetness of another's consolation advances to the grace of meekness. But he presents a ewe in the Lord's sacrifice who by the zeal of gentle consolation and loving solicitude advances others to the grace of meekness. But we truly offer a ram then, if in the spirit of mildness we rebuke offenders, and, being zealous for the Lord, rage against transgressors with the horns of a loving severity. In the lamb, therefore, we understand a trembling meekness; in the ewe, a caressing meekness; in the ram, a zealous meekness. In the lamb a fearful meekness, in the ewe a loving meekness, in the ram [1041B] a severe meekness. You offer a lamb by humbly acquiescing, a ewe by devoutly soothing, a ram by lovingly rebuking. Therefore, with meekness receive the ingrafted word, which is able to save your souls — and you have offered a lamb. Strengthen the weary hands and make firm the feeble knees — and you sacrifice a ewe. But, according to the Apostle, reprove, entreat, rebuke [n: (II Tim. IV)], the virtue of meekness being everywhere preserved — and your oblation will be, as I judge, a ram. I think you no longer wonder why the Lord required the male sex of Abraham in this kind of victim, who in the other kinds of victims chose out the female. It is no wonder, nor unusual, that a mind founded in meekness should keep the heart's sweetness in the heart, and, after the likeness of the ewe, [1041C] pour out the sweetness it has conceived as a kind of milk, and distil it through its words. But this is truly great, this truly to be wondered at, when a meek and loving heart wrings from itself things zealously pursued for the Lord, and to strike offenders with the horns of rebuke.
