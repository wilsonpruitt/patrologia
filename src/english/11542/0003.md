## On the Sacrifice of David the Prophet (cont.)

So far as concerns me, I wonder at, or rather prefer, one ram — after Daniel's dream, butting against the West, and against the North, and the South — more than many ewes abounding in milk. O what and how great a sacrifice it will be, for the testimony of truth not to spare even oneself, but out of zeal for uprightness willingly to provoke against oneself the fury of persecutors. O what a sacrifice, to suffer one's own injuries with meekness, willingly to forget them, to be vehemently indignant at injuries done to one's Lord, and, by raging with the horns of rebuke, [1041D] bravely to avenge them! This is, as I judge, to offer a ram, and to set it in the Lord's sacrifice. But rightly in this place also, after the manner above, it is asked why one ram only is required of Abraham, whereas David the prophet seems to propose or to promise something greater, in that it is said: *I will offer up to thee burnt offerings full of marrow, with the burning of rams [n: (Psal. LXV)]*. He does not say, with the burning of a ram, but with the burning of rams. But, as we taught in the immolation of the she-goat that we are sometimes justly, sometimes unjustly despised, so indeed we are afflicted now justly, now unjustly, and by turns suffer a just or an unjust persecution. He, therefore, who bears with meekness a just persecution as often as he incurs it — such a one immolates as it were one ram in the Lord's sacrifice. [1042A] But he who, while being zealous for the Lord, is afflicted now justly, now unjustly, and suffers either persecution without loss of his meekness — he assuredly is seen to double his victim. But those who are without complaint, and live beyond reproach — these indeed have nothing whereby they either ought or are able to double their victim. I think that for those the oblation of one ram only sufficed unto perfection, concerning whom we have the testimony from the Gospel: *And they were both,* says the evangelist, *just before God, walking in all the commandments and justifications of the Lord without complaint [n: (Luc. I)]*. You perceive, as I judge, that it is greater to offer one ram after this manner than to double one's victim after the manner aforesaid. But he immolates to the Lord a ram of three years [1042B] who assails with the horns of free rebuke not only those less powerful or equally powerful, but even the more powerful, as often as zeal for uprightness demands it, or the rule of truth dictates. See, we have now said what we ought to think concerning the cow, concerning the she-goat, and finally concerning the ram. From these things, therefore, as I judge, you perceive what sort of dishes you ought to prepare for the Lord's table. But if we wish to set a rich table before the Lord, we ought, after the likeness of Abraham, to add still a fourth and a fifth dish out of the turtledove and the dove. To the pursuits of perfect action, therefore, let us join also the pursuits of contemplation. The former indeed pertain to the four-footed beasts, the latter to the winged creatures. Four-footed beasts live upon the earth; winged creatures [1042C] lift themselves on their feathers into the heights. By action we dwell as it were upon the earth; by thought and desire we are suspended among things sublime. We offer the Lord, therefore, as it were winged creatures, as often as, out of the duty of devotion, we fly on the wing of contemplation to the consideration of things to come. By the turtledove and the dove understand a twofold contemplation: in the turtledove, the contemplation of eternal evils; in the dove, the contemplation of eternal goods. Now these birds are wont not so much to sing as to moan, and have moaning for their song. As in the feather the grace of contemplation, so in their song or moaning understand the power of compunction. We ought, therefore, on either side to meditate upon eternal evils or goods, to contemplate them, and in the consideration of them to be pricked with compunction; and this will be to offer the turtledove and [1042D] the dove, and in part to double our victim. The turtledove loves solitude, and goes about wandering alone, and in this bears the form of fear, which is always seeking a byway. The dove flies in flocks, and thereby displays the form of love, which always reconciles hearts, and binds them into one concord of peace. In the turtledove, therefore, the compunction of fear is regarded; in the dove, the compunction of love. If you meditate upon eternal evils, and in the consideration of them are not pricked with compunction, you have the turtledove's feathers, but you have not the turtledove's song. If you are fixed fast in the contemplation of eternal goods, and burn in the desire of them and pour out tears, you assuredly show that you have both the dove's flight and the dove's song.
