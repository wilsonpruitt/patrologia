## *Purge out the old leaven, that a man may be made a new lump.*

I confess, I can no longer bear with an even mind the importunity of your exaction, who compel me to resolve not only the questions proposed to me by you, but also those proposed to you by me. But if we wish to explain them fully, [0258B] it will be necessary to expound that whole apostolic lesson of the paschal solemnity: *Purge out the old leaven, that ye may be a new lump, even as ye are azymi.* That we may be able more fully to understand this passage of the Apostle, there is need, as it seems to me, of a manifold inquiry: first, what leaven is, what "a little leaven" is, what "old leaven" is, what the manner of purging is, what *azyma* is, what "a new lump" is, and what the difference between bread and lump is; and lastly — as I have already said above — why we are admonished by the Apostle to be made a new lump, and not rather bread without leaven. What bodily leaven is, and of what efficacy it is, we know; and by that very thing we weigh what we ought to think of spiritual leaven. Spiritual leaven, then, seems to be that which corrupts [0258C] the integrity of a spiritual and rational substance. Spiritual leaven, therefore, we say to be every sin that corrupts it, whether in respect of sense or in respect of affection. In respect of sense it is corrupted by falsity; in respect of affection, by impurity. So long, then, as we can truly say — for if we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us — rightly is it said to us, and deservedly ought it to be said: *Purge out the old leaven.* But if every sin is deservedly called leaven, it follows that by "a little leaven" is understood venial sin, which is both easily committed and easily remitted. And I say "a little," not that it is to be lightly esteemed, [0258D] but that it is little in comparison with criminal sins. But you say, perhaps: If, according to the Apostle, a little leaven can corrupt the whole mass of merits, we cannot understand by "a little leaven" anything but something criminal. And indeed that, you say, against which the Apostle disputed in what precedes — although a great sin — was nevertheless, according to the same [Apostle], a little leaven; otherwise, to what end does he consequently add: *Know ye not that a little leaven corrupteth the whole mass? [n: (I Cor. V.)]* So perhaps it seems to you. What then? Did he who had his father's wife contract but a little of leaven? If a little leaven works a little corruption, and much leaven a great corruption, shall we say that he was corrupted but a little, [0259A] and not much, who committed such an outrage? I rather understand it to have been said by the Apostle — that a little leaven corrupteth the whole mass — so that those who heard might understand how great a corruption so great and so abominable an outrage brought in, [and] with how great zeal and haste it behoved to be purged out, since even a little leaven is wont to corrupt the whole mass. If, according to the apostle John, *Every sin is iniquity [n: (I Joan. III)]*, what will criminal sin be but a great iniquity? We shall say, then, that a great iniquity is a little corruption; for this seems to follow, if a little leaven be called criminal sin. But how, you say, can venial sin corrupt the whole mass, according to the Apostle? [0259B] Hear what another Scripture says, and from it weigh how this ought to be understood: *He that neglecteth small things falleth away little by little [n: (Eccli. XIX)]*. And we know that from a little spark a fire blazes up. We often see, while the head of the household sleeps, how great [a fire] blazes up from a little one, and in a single night or hour consumes the whole substance of the possessor — and sometimes even the dweller himself together with his dwelling. So indeed concupiscence, little in the beginning of temptation, if it be despised, begins little by little to grow, and by degrees to soften the powers of the mind, and, its strength exhausted, to drive on to crime, and after this manner to corrupt the mass of our merits. So indeed, so — if it be neglected — from a little leaven no little mass is often corrupted; for if we do not in the least neglect [it], though however basely [0259C] fallen, [but] strongly press on in purging out the leaven of whatever crime, we shall be able to preserve the mass of our goods from its corruption. Hence it is that, after his special admonition, taking occasion, he rises to general doctrine, when he says: *Purge out the old leaven.*

Do you suppose he teaches us to press on in purging out the little leaven alone, or the great alone, and not rather both the great and the little? *Purge out,* he says, *the old leaven.* That leaven is deservedly called "old" whereby the soul is infected and, in the infection, corrupted. For that leaven seems recent which as yet strikes the soul only through thought. What is in thought alone strikes, as it were, outwardly at the doors; what already holds the affection [0259D] occupies and defiles the inward parts. The one, then, is to be repelled; the other, to be purged out. And the manner of purging is carried out in manifold ways: by reproving, by praying, by confessing. Of the first you have it in the psalm: *The things which ye say in your hearts, be ye pricked with compunction upon your beds [n: (Psal. IV)]*. Of the second and third you have it in the apostle James: *Confess your sins one to another, and pray for one another [n: (Jac. V)]*. Of the fourth there is that saying of John the Baptist: *Bring forth,* he says, *fruits worthy of penance [n: (Luc. III)]*. In the purging of criminal [sins] there is need, assuredly, of confession and satisfaction; for compunction and prayer are sometimes wont to suffice for the purging of venial [sins]. Criminal [sins] must be purged out fully, both in respect of guilt and in respect of act. In respect of guilt, that you make satisfaction for things past, [0260A] and for the rest presume nothing of the kind. But because we can never, so long as we live in this corruptible flesh, wholly root out venial [sins] from our life, we ought always to press on in the purging of them, and to renew our zeal toward daily increases of purity. *That ye may be,* he says, *a new lump, even as ye are azymi.* It seems, indeed, that *azyma* can be said both of bread without leaven and of a lump not yet leavened; for *azyma* sounds the same as "without leaven." Which of these, then, ought we to understand in that which he says, that they are *azymi*? But if they were already a new lump, there was no need to admonish that they be what they were. In that, then, which he says [they are] *azymi*, he wished — as it seems — bread without leaven to be understood. What then? Did he wish to admonish them of this, that they should [0260B] cease to be bread without leaven and become a new lump? Who would say this? For neither is a lump wont to be made from bread, but rather bread from a lump. It is understood, then, that in respect of something they were *azymi*, and again in respect of something they were not *azymi*: in respect of that whereby they were not without leaven, he wished them to be made a new lump. For many are *azymi* according to faith who are not *azymi* according to work; and many seem to be *azymi* according to work who nevertheless are not *azymi* according to intention, as hypocrites. And there are those who have the *azyma* of sincerity in intention, who nevertheless do not fully lack leaven in affection. And therefore, without doubt, so long as we, consenting in mind indeed to the law of God, see another law in our members warring against the law of our mind, and [see] captivity [0260C] into the law of sin — so long, I say, as we see this in ourselves, beyond doubt we have need of the purging of leaven, [and] deservedly is it said to us: *Purge out the old leaven, that ye may be a new lump, even as ye are azymi.* That which he says, "a lump" — not any lump whatever, but "new" — seems to be worth as much as if he said "not leavened." For a lump, even when leaven has been cast in, is not leavened without an interval [of time]. After it has already been leavened, then, it ceases to be recent and loses its newness. As, then, in respect of something they had already been made as it were bread without leaven, so in respect of something he wished them to be made a lump without leaven. In both cases he did not wish them to have unleavened bread; in both, to be without corruption — both in that which they already were, and in that [0260D] which they might yet be. Let us now see what there is between bread and lump. Bread, as has already been said, is made from a lump, but not a lump from bread. A lump is a matter tender and soft and capable of receiving diverse form. Bread is a thing already formed, and firm, and solidified by fire. Unleavened bread, then, is made by an affection firm and robust in the good. A new lump is made by the recent purpose of the soul, but as yet rude and little exercised. A lump is formed between the hands, afterward baked by fire and solidified, and thus is made bread. So indeed, so, the promotion of our merits, the renewal of our advances, is begun from deliberation, moderated through discretion, consummated in love.

When, therefore, to increase the soul's purity we determine something new, and press on in an intention of this kind [0261A] with much deliberation, what else do we do, in purging out the leaven, than make a new lump? But because not all can do all things, nor do all things alike profit all, when we learn our measure and the good manner of acting in whatever purpose is to be carried out, and are trained through our own experience unto discretion, so that we accomplish all things with moderation, what else do we do than shape loaves to be baked? For then is the bread as it were baked at the fire, when the good purpose is turned through use into delight, and is solidified through love. It is one thing, then, [to have] the promotion of good, and another [to have] the confirmation of good. It is one thing, in fine, [to have] the beginning of good, and another the consummation of the same. The latter [0261B] pertains to bread, the former to the new lump. Because, then, no one becomes perfect of a sudden, nor is at once confirmed in every beginning of good, the Apostle, speaking to men in human wise on account of their infirmity, admonishes that they be made a new lump, not bread without leaven — although without doubt he tended to this end, that, being made a new lump, from the new lump they might at some time become even *azymi* loaves. For no one makes a lump for any other cause than that from it bread may be made. Deservedly, therefore, does the Apostle admonish that they become that which they could meanwhile become, and by that very thing intimates to what end they ought to tend — that is, that at length, at some time, they may wholly become *azymi*, and advance to the consummation and confirmation of the true good. Behold, now you have why they are admonished to be made a new lump, [0261C] not bread without leaven. But this is a marvel, and no less to be investigated: why they who are called *azymi* are invited to the eating of the lamb. For to eat is not [proper] to *azymus*; but to eat or to be eaten is not [proper] to a lump. When, then, it has been said that they were *azymi*, [and] enjoined that they become a new lump, what consequence, I pray, is there that he should consequently add: *For our pascha hath been sacrificed, even Christ; therefore let us keep the feast [n: (I Cor. V.)]*
