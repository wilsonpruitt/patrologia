## An Explanation of Certain Difficult Passages of the Apostle

[0665A]If the things that are said about the law by the Apostle in various places are compared with one another, much contradiction seems to be in them. For he seems to be contrary [0665B] to himself, contrary to reason, contrary to truth, and — what is greater than all these — contrary to the judgment of the Lord himself. Hear him then crying out and protesting: *That by the works of the law no man shall be justified [n: (Rom. III)]*. How is he not in this contrary to himself, when he himself protests elsewhere that: *Not the hearers, but the doers of the law shall be justified [n: (Rom. II)]*. Again in another place: *The law,* he says, *is holy, and the commandment holy, and just, and good [n: (Rom. VII)]*. Again elsewhere it is said by the same man: *The law worketh wrath [n: (Rom. IV)]*. What seems more against reason than that a commandment which is called holy, just and good should, with a just and merciful Lord, work wrath rather than grace? But elsewhere he says that: *The law leads no one [0665C] to perfection [n: (Hebr. VII)]*. How, I ask, could this be truly said, or how was he in this not contrary to the truth, since the time of the law had so many just men, and men so perfect? Was Elijah not perfect, in whose spirit and power, according to the Lord's testimony, there came he than whom none greater has arisen among those born of women? [n: (Matth. XI.)] What shall we say of the other prophets, what of the just men and the kings, who wished to see what the apostles saw, and could not? If they were transgressors of the law, could they be just? If they were observers of it, were they not just thereby? But he seems to hold what is contrary to the judgment of the Lord in that he says elsewhere: *For if a law had been given which could justify, truly justice would be from the law [n: (Galat. III)]*. But to the lawyer [0665D] asking what he should do to possess eternal life, and, at the Lord's questioning, putting forth to the Lord the first and greatest commandments of the law, the Lord answered: *Do this, and thou shalt live [n: (Luc. X)]*. What then? Can justice not be obtained from that whence eternal life is obtained? Or can eternal life be had without justice? Who would say either of these, or presume to say it? *Do this,* he says, *and thou shalt live.* Thou shalt live, without doubt, by loving thy God with the whole heart, with the whole soul, with the whole mind, and thy neighbor as thyself. There are those who say that the works of the law are those that are done out of fear, but the works of the Gospel those that are done out of love. And to serve without love cannot justify a man. Whence also, according to them, it is rightly said that by the works of the law no man *justicabitur*. And in testimony [0666A] of their assertion they bring forward what they read elsewhere in the Apostle: *For you have not received again the spirit of bondage in fear, but you have received the spirit of adoption, wherein [0666B] we cry: Abba (Father* ) [n: (Rom. VIII)]. It is agreed then, they say, that those served under fear who had received the spirit of fear, not of love. Hence it is that they further add that the law is a commandment without grace, but the Gospel a commandment with grace. I confess, I do not recall having read where the law enjoins that one serve out of fear alone, where it forbids that the commandments be fulfilled out of true love. Shall the works, I ask, which the law itself enjoins not be works of the law, while the works it does not deliver shall be its works? But they say that those who were under the law received the spirit of bondage. I say boldly, and affirm without hesitation, that of those who lived in the time of the law and of those who have lived in the time of the Gospel until now, some received the spirit of fear, but others the spirit of love. [0666C] Did those alone not keep the law who, with charity abounding and casting fear out of doors, observed it out of love alone? It belongs certainly to the law, and to the Gospel, to set forth teaching; but to the Lord, to give grace or to withdraw it, and to pour in the spirit according to his good pleasure. What then do they mean who say that the law is a commandment without grace, but the Gospel a commandment with grace? How often do we see in our own times that unskilled hearers have greater grace in carrying out the commandments than certain most skilled preachers of the Gospel! Is it not the Gospel that they teach who most learnedly preach the apostolic doctrine, and yet neither have themselves the grace of fulfilling it nor are able to give it to others? Were those men of old living without the law who had received the teaching of the law together [0666D] with grace?

There are likewise those who say that those alone are rightly called works of the law which were begun with the law and ended with the law. But I say meanwhile what I think, and answer these things with a brief succinctness. Those works of the law which were begun with the law and ended with the law either justified when fulfilled, or they did not. If therefore their fulfillment could justify, how will it be true that no one shall be justified by the works of the law? There are perhaps those who say — and if they do not say it, they could not unfittingly have said it — that a distinction ought rightly to be drawn between natural, legal and evangelical works. For rightly are those works to be ascribed especially to the Gospel which received their institution from the Gospel, such as regeneration, confirmation and the communion of the Lord's body [0667A] and blood, and the new institution of whatever else. But those alone are to be called legal in a certain special way which, as has already been said, were begun with the law and ended with the law. And those ought rightly to be called natural which could have been understood by men through the natural law, even if the law and the Gospel had never been instituted. According to this distinction, at all events, someone will perhaps say that by the works of the law we ought to understand those legal institutions alone of which we hold for certain that by their fulfillment alone no flesh shall be justified. But by the name of the law, as it will perhaps seem to someone, we must understand both natural decrees and legal institutions, inasmuch as both are written in the law. For the Apostle says that *the doers of the law*, not the doers [0667B] of legal works, are justified. But what he affirms of the law, he denies of the works of the law. *By the works of the law,* he says, *no man shall be justified.* He does not say from the law, but from the works of the law no flesh shall be justified. And likewise in another place: *We account a man to be justified by faith, without the works of the law [n: (Rom. III)]*. There he says that a man cannot be justified by the works of the law. Here he says how far he can be justified without the works of the law. If anyone thinks that the Apostle meant it thus, I ask that he answer me upon this: whether the Apostle says this of the present state, or rather of the former state? If he said this of the time of the former state, the text of that discourse will seem to be at odds with the testimony of the law and the prophets. Did not the law cry out daily that a sinner, and one repenting, should according to the manner and [0667C] the magnitude of the sin offer this or that, now for a sin, now for a trespass, and it shall be forgiven him, it says, and the Lord will be merciful to him [n: (Num. XXX)]? How, I ask, is a man not justified by that whereby his sin is remitted to him and the Lord is made merciful? But if he wished what he said of the works of the law to be understood of the state of this present time, what great thing, I ask, did he say? For if we still sacrifice animals, without doubt we either judaize, or after the manner of the nations we put forward the rites of idolatry. And so I say concerning these men what the Apostle says of the sacrament of circumcision: that if we still persist in sacrifices and the other ceremonies of the law, Christ will profit us nothing. But do you wish to know more plainly what I say? Certainly the things which were immolated in the law for the expiation [0667D] of sin were sacrificed as a figure of things to come. For they prefigured the passion and death of Christ that was to come for the redemption of sins, and by that very fact they had the power of expiation and sanctification. If therefore we still sacrifice in a figure of things to come, we serve falsehood and not truth. For, *Christ rising again from the dead dieth now no more, death shall no more have dominion over him [n: (I Cor. XV)]*. If we sacrifice apart from the figure of our redemption, we put forward, as has been said, the form of idolatry, and in that very act we set the sacrifices of animals beside Christ, and (what is more wicked!) prefer them to him. For if we offer those alone, the divine sacrifice being despised, we without doubt prefer them to Christ. If we offer them together with the divine victim (as though seeking salvation from both), [0668A] we without doubt set them beside Christ, and judge the victim of Christ to be as it were less than sufficient. What great thing therefore is it for the Apostle to say that no one shall be justified by legal works, when by the practice of them anyone will be reproved rather than justified?

But to these things perhaps you answer, and say: On account of certain *legiszelatores*, whom he desired to win rather than to scandalize, he spoke indeed more sparingly, but nevertheless he spoke true things. For as regards the present state, legal institutions defile rather than sanctify. But as regards the former state, without the natural decrees the legal enactments alone were not able to suffice for men's salvation. Hence it is that he does not say from the law, but *exoperibus of the law shall no [0668B] flesh be justified.* And again: *For we account that a man is justified by faith without the works of the law.* Without the works of the law, he says, not without the law — wishing under the name of works to understand the legal enactments alone, and under the name of the law both the legal and the natural, inasmuch as it contained these and those. Hence it is that he says that *the doers of the law* shall be justified. For what can be holier, what juster, what finally more perfect, than to love God with the whole heart, the whole soul, the whole mind, and one's neighbor as oneself? The law therefore could confer justice, and not any sort whatever, but full and perfect. To these things I myself briefly reply and say: If the Apostle so thought, that the law could confer perfect justice, why does he say elsewhere that *the law brought no one to perfection?* If justice is conferred from the law, [0668C] how does it *work wrath?* Does it work wrath by conferring justice, and can it confer justice by working wrath? As far as it seems to me, whoever wishes to understand the apostolic sense will have to seek an exposition other than those aforesaid. As far as it seems to me, in all these matters those questions have not yet perished in which, as Augustine writes, the text of the apostolic discourse once seemed to him to be at odds with the testimonies of the law and the prophets. For he who says that a man cannot be justified by legal works — does he not seem to be at odds with Moses writing, nay rather with the Lord commanding: Let the man who has come to know his sin offer this or that, and it shall be forgiven him, and the Lord will be merciful to him.
