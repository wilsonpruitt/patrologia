## An Explanation of Certain Difficult Passages of the Apostle (cont.)

[0668D]Your importunate request compels me to say what I think about the matters that have been set forth, and I confidently hope that where John seeks, grace will not be wanting, both for thinking truly and for setting it forth usefully. It is not our custom, most of all in great and very deep matters, to presume anything out of our own understanding, or as it were to establish or affirm anything on our own authority. And so, so far as the Lord shall grant, let us draw out from the testimonies of Holy Scripture itself that whereby we may be able to loose the knots of our doubt. But this is most certain, and no one can doubt it: that the law was either such as could justify when fulfilled, or such as could not justify even when fulfilled. But if it could not justify when fulfilled, in no way would the Apostle say [0669A] that the doers of the law would be justified. To the same judgment runs what the Lord said to the lawyer concerning the two greatest commandments: *Do this and thou shalt live.* See how a sure authority proves that the law, when fulfilled, justifies. Again I say that the law was either such as could be fulfilled by those to whom it was given, or such as could not be fulfilled by them. But let us prove this very thing that we are seeking, again by apostolic testimony: *What was impossible,* he says, *to the law, in that it was weakened by the flesh, God sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh, and of sin condemned sin in the flesh, that the justice of the law might be fulfilled in us who walk not according to the flesh, but according to the spirit [n: (Rom. VIII)]*. You weigh, as I judge, from these things how impossible it was for the law to justify, and how necessary it was for us [0669B] to have such a remedy — that God should send his own Son, that the Son of God should empty himself, and come in the likeness of sinful flesh, and condemn sin of sin, so that at least in this way the justice of the law might be fulfilled in us. But was everything that belonged to the law also impossible? By no means, but in that part in which the law was weakened by the flesh. Do you wish to hear this too, how it was weakened by the flesh: *The flesh,* he says, *lusteth against the spirit [n: (Galat. V)]*; and elsewhere: *I know that there dwelleth not in me* (that is, in my flesh) *good. For to will is present with me, but to accomplish the good I find not [n: (Rom. VII)]*. If therefore we diligently examine the testimonies brought forward, we shall find beyond doubt that certain precepts of the law were such as justified when fulfilled; but certain others, though fulfilled, in no way [0669C] justified. Nonetheless certain of them were such as could be fulfilled, and certain others such as without doubt could not be fulfilled. But those that could be fulfilled without doubt could not justify, and conversely those that could justify could not be fulfilled. Rightly therefore does the Apostle cry out: *That by the works of the law no flesh shall be justified.* If no flesh, then no man, inasmuch as every man is flesh; because he is carnal, sold under sin. How, I ask, can one who has been made flesh and become carnal be justified, either from what he can do and which cannot justify, or from what can justify and which he cannot do? For the law teaches what a man ought to do, but it does not equally teach how he may be able to fulfill [0669D] what is commanded. From the law alone, therefore, there is knowledge alone, and not fulfillment as well. Hence it is that, when the Apostle has first set down that by the works of the law no flesh shall be justified, he at once adds and joins to it: *For by the law is the knowledge of sin [n: (Rom. III)]*. See, you have both what is not from the law, and what is from the law. By the law the knowledge of sin — would that there were the abolition of it too. For if abolition, then without doubt justification too. And so I say: If no one is justified from the law, then he is justified from elsewhere. For who would dare to say that no one is or has been just? Hear what the Lord says to the apostles: *Many just men and prophets have desired to see the things that you see, and have not seen them [n: (Luc. X)]*. If just, then certainly justified as well. *For all have sinned, and do need the glory of God. Being justified freely by his grace [n: (Rom. III)]*. For he it is *who justifieth [0670A] the circumcision by faith, and the uncircumcision through faith (ibid)*. See, from these things it is plainly known whence a man is justified. Without doubt through faith, and not through the law. For *the justice of God is through the faith of Jesus Christ [n: (ibid.)]*. See, you plainly have it that justice is from faith.

But it remains to be asked whether it is always from faith alone, or sometimes from faith alone without works, and sometimes from faith together with works. Let us seek, if you please, upon this very point the testimony of the Apostle himself: *We account,* he says, *that a man is justified by faith without the works of the law.* See, again you plainly have it that a man can sometimes be justified from faith alone apart from the work of the law. But is it always from faith alone, and without every work of the law? By no means. For if he has time for working, *faith without works is dead* [0670B] [n: (Jac. II)]. For not the hearers of the law, but the doers shall be justified. Without doubt, so long as time for working is granted to a man, there is required of him by God, and not undeservedly, a faith that works through love. From the testimonies brought forward, therefore, it is plainly gathered that a man is indeed justified sometimes from faith alone, sometimes from faith together with the work of the law, but never from the law alone. From these things, therefore, as I judge, it can be weighed that the Apostle is not contrary to himself in his sayings. For in that he says in one place that by the works of the law no flesh shall be justified, he plainly gives us to understand that for human diligence the teaching of the law alone, without the faith of Christ and grace, does not at all suffice: *By the grace,* he says, *of God I am in what I am [n: (I Cor. XV)]*. But again, lest the law [0670C] be thought to be such that it cannot justify when fulfilled, therefore he says of the same law in another place: *For not the hearers, but the doers of the law shall be justified.* For whether it be granted or not granted that to any man such grace has been given, or is to be given, that he can fulfill it, nonetheless it is true that the fulfilling of it is the justification of men. The Apostle therefore teaches that the law by itself alone never confers justice upon a man, nay — what is heavier still — that it also works wrath. But whence this comes about the Apostle himself makes plain by adding: *For where there is no law, neither is there transgression [n: (Rom. IV)]*. For it works wrath from this, that from the teaching of the law alone, without grace joined to it, the hearers of the law are made not so much doers as transgressors. [0670D] For, lest the commandments of the law should be thought foolish rather than impossible, therefore it is said by the Apostle in another place: *The law indeed is holy and the commandment holy, and just and good.* Perhaps you think this was said of the moral commandments alone. I boldly say it of any institution whatever of the law, that in its own time it was holy, and just and good, as being instituted by God, and divinely enjoined upon man.

What, I ask, were the sacrifices, if not a figure of the passion and death of Christ, and by that very fact sacraments of the same? There was therefore in one and the same work both a figure of our redemption and a due obedience. Certainly, if Abraham had wished to slay his son without a divine precept, it would have been the utmost impiety and a horrible outrage. If therefore it was holy, and just, and dutiful [0671A] to sacrifice his son at the Lord's command, why should it not in the same way be called holy, and just, and good to offer and immolate to the Lord, at the Lord's command, a beast that can be killed without sin? That the law therefore worked wrath came not from the law's imperfection or disorder, but from man's weakness and imperfection. Do you wish to hear this very thing not from me, but from the Apostle himself: *We know,* he says, *that the law is spiritual, but I am carnal, sold under sin. For that which I work, I understand not. For I do not that good which I will, but the evil which I hate, that I do [n: (Rom. VII)]*. See, now you have whence transgression comes; see, now you hold that it is not from a fault of the law, but of man. Nothing therefore is said against reason in that he cries out that the holy and just commandment works wrath. [0671B] According to the Lord's testimony it is true, nor can we deny, that before the coming of Christ there were many just men and prophets; nevertheless what the Apostle said was not contrary to the truth, that the law brought no one to perfection. For that they were just, and whatever of perfection they were able to have, this was conferred on them not by the bare teaching of the law, but by divine grace joined to it. For *it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that showeth mercy [n: (Rom. IX)]*. For they were by no means justified without the faith and grace of Christ. For unless they believed and hoped that Christ would come, in no way would they have desired his coming for so long a time. For what else does it mean that the Lord says to the apostles: *Many kings and prophets have desired to see the things that [0671C] you see, and have not seen them, and to hear the things that you hear, and have not heard them? [n: (Luc. X.)]* Their justification, therefore, is ascribed not so much to the law as to the faith and grace of Christ. Now let us consider this, how what the Apostle thinks of the law is not contrary to the Lord's judgment when he says: *For if a law had been given which could justify, truly justice would be from the law.* You see that, according to the Apostle, the law can neither give life nor justify; but why is this, if not because it cannot be fulfilled? For a man cannot refrain from coveting, just as he cannot love with the whole heart, the whole soul, the whole mind — than which nevertheless nothing is holier, nothing at all is juster. Certainly, if such a law had been given to men ignorant but sound, and strong for accomplishing it, it could without doubt have both justified those willing to obey and by that very fact sanctified [0671D] them. But it was given, for certain, to the weak, and to the very unskilled, and to those less fit for fulfilling it. Likewise, if it taught the ignorant what they ought to do, and the weak in like manner how they could accomplish what was commanded, without doubt — as must be confessed — it could have both justified and given life. But the law teaches only what ought to be done, and does not teach how it can be fulfilled by the weak. But the Apostle denies that from knowledge alone, or the teaching, of what is to be accomplished; he denies, I say, and rightly, that a man cannot be given life. But what the Apostle denies of the knowledge of the commandment alone, the Lord affirms of the fulfilling of the commandment: *Do this,* he says, *and thou shalt live.* He does not say, if thou knowest this, thou shalt live; but, do this, and thou shalt live. What therefore the Lord affirms, the Apostle does not deny; and what the Apostle [0672A] denies, the Lord does not affirm. For, as has been said, the Apostle speaks of the knowledge of the commandment alone, but the Lord of the fulfilling of it.
