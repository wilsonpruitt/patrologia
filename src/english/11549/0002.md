## An Explanation of Certain Difficult Passages of the Apostle (cont.)

Here it is not undeservedly asked by what reasoning it is imputed to them, or how it works wrath in them, that they did not fulfill the law who could not fulfill it; or what it profited them to have received such a law as they could in no way fulfill. Certainly, and not undeservedly, they ought to receive such a law who presumed everything upon their own strength and not upon divine help, according to what they confidently said to Moses: *All things that the Lord hath spoken we will do [n: (Exod. XXIV)]*. What seems right to you concerning those who, after so great and so steadfast a promise, utterly neglected it? Can so great a negligence excuse them? Or [0672B] those who, pressing on strongly but failing through their own defect and through manifold experience, could not be humbled — can so great a pride excuse them? Or those, I ask, who through much experience acknowledged their own weakness, but were slothful in obtaining the grace that helps — can so great a sloth excuse them? Who would say this, or dare to say it? From these things, therefore, you can easily weigh how inexcusable the transgressors of the law they received are. For those indeed who, striving according to their strength and failing now through their own defect, now through another's, acknowledged their own weakness and impossibility, and thereafter placed their whole hope in divine help, deserved to be justified, [0672C] grace having been obtained and received from God. The law that was given, therefore, profited some, and to others profited nothing at all. It certainly profited those living well, but harmed those using it ill. But where it harmed, it was not a fault of the law, but a fault of the one abusing it. For to this end the law had been given, which ought to have been fulfilled and could not be fulfilled: that those striving and failing might understand how vainly they presumed upon their own strength for what they could not fulfill without divine grace.

Perhaps you still press on, and say that whatever has been said of the law can also be said of the Gospel. For as the teaching of the law does not suffice for justice without divine grace, so without doubt neither does evangelical teaching without divine grace; neither can be fulfilled, neither can justify. Why then should not what is said of the law be said of the Gospel, that by the works [0672D] of the Gospel no flesh shall be justified? Does not that also bring no one to perfection, of which it is agreed that it cannot be fulfilled without grace? By the same consideration we can reckon that it cannot at all give life to anyone, just as it cannot justify. But to these things I reply and say that the teaching of the law is single, the evangelical threefold. For the Gospel teaches what ought to be done, how it can be fulfilled, and how what has not been fulfilled can be made good. But the law teaches only, as has been said, what a man ought to do, and does not add how he can fulfill it, and for that reason it can justify no one. But evangelical teaching, that it may be perfect in every respect, teaches those three things already named. What you ought to do you are taught when you hear from the Lord: [0673A] *This is my commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you [n: (Joan. XVIII)]*. But lest anyone presume the same upon his own strength, he is taught by the same master, when it is said elsewhere by the same: *Without me you can do nothing. As the branch cannot bear fruit unless it abide in the vine, so neither can you, unless you abide in me [n: (Joan. XV)]*. In obtaining grace, therefore, whereby we may be able to fulfill what we will — what, I ask, will be the manner of obtaining it? *If you shall ask the Father anything in my name, he will give it you [n: (Joan. XIV)]*. If you lack grace, ask as you ought, and you shall receive. But how do we ask in his name if we believe in him? This is what he wished to teach you, when he says: *You believe in God, believe also in me [n: (ibid.)]*. Let him therefore who cannot fulfill what he ought believe in Christ, [0673B] and in Christ ask the Father who can do all things. Let him then ask for that Spirit who works in us to will and to accomplish according to good will; that Spirit, I say, who works all things in all, dividing to every one according as he will. If you ask him with the faith that is required, without doubt you shall receive. For if you, being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your heavenly Father give a good spirit to them that ask him? [n: (Luc. XI.)] See, you plainly have, as I judge, what ought to be done by you, and how, or by what reasoning, it can be fulfilled.

But you still ask to be shown whence what has not been fulfilled — through negligence, or in any other way whatever — can be made good. Say first how much you owe your Lord. A hundred measures of oil, perhaps? A great debt, [0673C] and hard to pay. It is a great debt to love God with the whole heart, the whole mind, the whole soul. See, a debt of ten thousand talents. No wonder if you greatly fear and tremble; but hear briefly what you should do. Forgive the hundred pence that are owed to you, and every debt shall be forgiven you; and with confidence you will be able to pray for yourself and those like you: forgive us our debts as we also, and so forth. What, you say, if nothing is owed to me — how shall what I myself owe be forgiven me? By the same order, from the same fountain from which you drew grace in performing, draw also the indulgence of compassion in showing mercy. From these things you can certainly see that nothing is wanting to the Gospel for the perfection of teaching. You have in it whereby you may be justified, you have from it whereby you may be given life. *He that eateth,* he says, *my [0673D] flesh and drinketh my blood, hath everlasting life [n: (Joan. VI)]*.

What more do you still seek? Could that lamb of the law do any such thing? See, the Gospel sets forth that whereby a man can be justified, which legal teaching cannot do at all. Certainly under the law many were perfect; certainly in the time of the Gospel many are imperfect. Yet the perfection of the former is not ascribed to the law, just as neither is the imperfection of the latter to the Gospel. For divine grace conferred this on them, and not the teaching of the law.

*By the works of the law no flesh shall be justified [n: (Galat. II)]*. It is one thing to be just and another to be justified. Since God indeed is just, and yet not [0674A] justified. But it is agreed that sin makes a man unjust. What therefore is it for a sinner to be justified, if not to be freed from sin? For from sin a man becomes liable both to vice and to punishment. In the one he is bound to guilt; in the other he is bound to penalty. In the one he incurs the debt of servitude, but in the other the debt of eternal damnation: *He that committeth sin is the servant of sin [n: (Joan. III)]*. But he who is a servant needs redemption that he may be able to go out free. But the law neither teaches whence a man ought to be redeemed, *nex* does it set forth whence he may be able to be freed. The teaching of the law, therefore, does not free from sin, and (as follows from this) by it no flesh shall be justified. What then does the law do? It teaches what a man ought to do, it teaches what he ought to beware of, but it does not teach how he can fulfill it. [0674B] It forbids coveting, it commands loving God with the whole heart; it commands ordered love, it forbids disordered love. You have heard what it does; hear what it effects. What it forbids is done by the flesh at every hour; but what it commands is never fulfilled by the flesh. Whence also by its works no one is justified. For whatever it teaches is either such as he cannot do, or such as cannot justify. From these things, as I judge, you have understood what the frequency of sinning is. Now attend to what the violence of sin is. It is commanded in the law, for certain sins, that of the transgressors one be stoned, another burned, another die by any other death whatever. And yet many crimes of this kind were committed by the hearers of the law. It can therefore easily be weighed how great the violence of sin is, [0674C] which in some could not be restrained even by the peril of death itself. The law having been given, therefore, and frequent and manifold transgression following, it became plainly known how great is the frequency of sinning, and how great the violence of sin. Hence it is that, when it has first been set down that by the works of the law no flesh shall be justified, it is at once added by the Apostle: *For by the law is the knowledge of sin [n: (Rom. III)]*. It is plainly given to be understood, therefore, or he gives it to be understood, that by the law there is only the knowledge of sin, and not the abolition of it as well. For if abolition, then certainly justification. Someone will perhaps wonder why it is not said rather the knowledge of the commandment than the knowledge of sin; for that too could equally truly have been said. For as by the law there is only the knowledge of sin, and not [0674D] the abolition of it, so by the law there is only the knowledge of the commandment, and not the fulfilling of it. But as far as it seems to me, if he had first set down that by the works of the law no one was just, or had justice, he would more fittingly add: For by the law is the knowledge of the commandment, than if he said, For by the law is the knowledge of sin. For what is more rightly called justice than the observance of the divine commandment? A man's justification is the same as his liberation from sin. Since therefore he has first spoken of a man's justification, it is easy to see how fittingly he adds: By the law is the knowledge of sin, rather than the knowledge of the commandment.

*For we know that the law is spiritual, but I am carnal, sold under sin [n: (Rom. VII)]*. It is rightly asked in whose person the Apostle says these things, [0675A] most of all on account of those things which are subsequently joined to them.
