## An Explanation of Certain Difficult Passages of the Apostle (cont.)

It is asked, therefore, whether it is said in the person of the weak or of the imperfect, or rather in the person both of these and of those. For as far as it seems to me, it stands clearer than light what, concerning the criminal, these things ought neither to be expounded, nor can they at all be rightly understood. For who thereafter will be able reasonably to convict any criminal of his crime, if he can bring forward the apostolic testimony in his own excuse, and boldly answer the one convicting him: It is not I who work it, but the sin that was in me? For what is it to expound these apostolic words in such a way that they can also fit the impious? What, I say, is this other than to teach the workers of iniquity to incline their heart to words of malice, to make excuses in [0675B] sins? But this seems to be the thing to be asked in these words of the Apostle: whether it is said under the form of the perfect, or of the imperfect? But how can it fit the Apostle, or any perfect man whatever: but I am carnal, sold under sin? For who would deservedly be called spiritual, if Paul, or men like Paul, are believed to be carnal? If what the Apostle sets out in this place fits only the weaker sort, without doubt it does not accomplish what he strives to prove by these words. For he had said of the law that it cannot confer justice, but rather that it works wrath. But lest this be believed to be from a fault of the law and not of man, therefore he consequently adds and says that the law indeed is holy, and the commandment holy, and just, and good. But here the Apostle [0675C] plainly shows that it is from a fault of man that he cannot be justified by the law, since he is by no means sufficient for the fulfilling of it. It must therefore be known first of all that according to different respects we take the placing of words in different ways. We judge otherwise, therefore, concerning man according to the present state, and otherwise according to that state which was before sin. According to the present state some are called spiritual, others carnal. But without doubt, in respect of the first condition, however religious a man may be, he is not undeservedly called carnal. But as regards the present state, those are called carnal who go after their own concupiscences and are slaves to carnal desires; and spiritual, those who strongly resist the desires of the flesh, [0675D] and vehemently press on with spiritual pursuits. It belongs to the carnal to feel the concupiscences of the flesh and to consent to them. It belongs to the spiritual to feel them and not to consent, but to resist, but to fight back, but to mortify them. But if we look back to the state of the first condition, in this very thing we are carnal, that we feel the concupiscences of the flesh, and foul and disordered delights. For if man had not sinned, he would neither have loved carnal things more than was lawful, nor spiritual things less than was fitting; but in loving both he could have both known and observed the bounds of moderation and the measure of equity. But after man of his own accord deserted the uprightness of his state, being now once sold under sin and made carnal, he is sufficient for neither: for in manifold ways he both errs in judgment and is unbridled [0676A] in desire. How true therefore is that prophetic saying: *In thy sight no living man shall be justified [n: (Psal. CXLII)]*; how true also is that apostolic one: *If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us [n: (I Joan. I)]*. Just as truly can it be understood of any religious man, but I am carnal, sold under sin; and whence and in what respects he is carnal he shows when he adds: For that which I work, I understand not. I do not that which I will, but the evil which I hate, that I do. See, three evils in which our carnality consists. What we do we do not understand; we do not do what we will; we do what we do not will. But the Apostle joins the two latter, since both descend from one fault. For from the fault of concupiscence [0676B] both descend: both that we are lukewarm toward the good, and fervent toward the evil: *That which I work,* he says, *I understand not [n: (Rom. VII)]*, and this in him certainly from the fault of ignorance. I do not, he says, that which I will, but the evil which I hate, that I do. From this twofold fault, certainly, it comes to us that we are carnal so long as we live in the flesh.

But it is to be noted that the Apostle adds two things subsequently to the one thing he set down first. He set down first that he was carnal, and by the two things joined he shows whence this was in him. The first sentence must therefore be repeated, and joined to each of the two clauses in this manner. I am carnal, sold under sin. For that which I work, I understand not. I am carnal, I say, [0676C] sold under sin, for I do not that which I will, but the evil which I hate, that I do. Anyone can prove in himself how truly it is said, that which I work I understand not. Did he understand what he was working who said to the Lord in prayer: *I give thee thanks, O Lord, because I am not as the rest of men? [n: (Luc. XVIII.)]* Did those also well understand what they said: *All things that the Lord hath spoken we will do? [n: (Exod. XLII.)]* For the words seem to be words of great devotion, and they are truly words of great presumption. Often, from that whereby a man thinks to stir up devotion, he nourishes elation and feeds presumption. But whence is this, I ask, if not because he does not understand what he does? Often a man is proud, and knows it not. Often he presumes, and meanwhile does not understand it. For both kinds of [0676D] ignorance divine grace is wont to be withdrawn. For while he acts presumptuously and knows it not, grace is withdrawn lest he accomplish what he desires. While he is proud and knows it not, he is often permitted to fall into that which he blushes at and hates, and he who is of this sort can say: For I do not that which I will, but the evil which I hate, that I do. But however spiritual a man may be, he errs not only in discerning but also in deliberating, when what he thinks works together with him unto good works together with him unto evil, and what he believes works together with him unto evil works together unto good. But whence does this good come about, if not because he does not understand what he works? But what wonder is it if we do not understand what we work, we who know not what we should pray for as we ought? For who could not be deceived in praying, if [0677A] the apostle Paul could be deceived in this very thing? For he thrice asked that that be taken from him which had been inflicted upon him as mercifully as it was usefully, for the keeping of humility.

*As a wise architect, I have laid the foundation. But if any man build upon this foundation gold, silver, precious stones, wood, hay, stubble, every man's work shall be made manifest [n: (I Cor. III)]*. There are those who define the foundation in this place to be faith working through love. I confidently say that no man can lay such a foundation in a human heart. What is to be believed, a man can teach a man. But love a man cannot pour into a man. For the charity of God is poured forth in our hearts, not by man, but by the Holy [0677B] Spirit who has been given to us. As a wise architect, he says, I have laid the foundation. What, I ask, is that foundation which Paul laid, and which could be laid by Paul? Certainly Paul could teach how one ought to believe in Christ, and taught it without ambiguity. This document of our faith is agreed for certain to be the foundation of the spiritual building. First of all it is that a man should know what he ought to believe, or to do, and this a man can know through a man. The preacher of truth ought therefore diligently to teach what one must believe, in what works a man ought to press on, or how by true penance he can blot out the evils he has committed; and this will be for him to lay the foundation. How many there are who both have this foundation of catholic [0677C] teaching, and hold it by memory, but build upon it very different things! For some do good deeds, others sin in the hope of coming to their senses. Both, therefore, have the foundation of true faith, but build different things upon it. Some gold, silver, precious stones; others wood, hay, stubble. Gold, silver, precious stones cannot be consumed by fire. These indeed can pass through the fire, and remain after the burning: *If any man's work,* he says, *abide, which he hath built thereupon, he shall receive a reward.* If therefore by such things we acquire a reward, what else ought we to understand by gold, silver, and precious stones than good works. But in wood, hay and stubble what, I ask, ought to be understood — good works or evil? Wood, hay and stubble the fire is wont [0677D] to consume, and as it were to reduce to nothing. But far be it that that future fire should be able to consume and annihilate good works. In wood, therefore, and hay and stubble nothing is rightly understood except works worthy of burning, and fit for being consumed, not for remaining. What then are these things that are to be burned and consumed by fire, if not evil works? *If any man's work,* he says, *burn, he shall suffer loss; but he himself shall be saved, yet so as by fire.* Who, I ask, are these workmen who are saved indeed not as it were by good working, but so, certainly, as by fire? This we hold for certain, that no evil workman whatever can be purged by purgatorial fire unless he has truly repented of the evils he has committed. For true penance alone brings it about that the perpetration of evils [0678A] can be consumed and blotted out by purgatorial fire. But no crimes are so detestable that true penance does not make them soluble. There seem therefore to be two kinds of insoluble works, but one kind of soluble works: the true goods of those persevering, the persevering evils of those not repenting, and to be blotted out by no term. But the evils of those truly repenting are without doubt soluble, and can without doubt be blotted out by purgatorial fire. In wood, therefore, and hay and stubble we understand any soluble evils whatever. But since they cannot be consumed by fire, but remain forever — not only the goods that are to endure, but also the insoluble evils — what is it that he says, whose work shall abide that he builds thereupon shall receive a reward? But what he adds, if any man's work abide, is to be referred only to those workmen of whom he has already [0678B] spoken before. Of those workmen, therefore, who build not iron, lead and tin, but gold, silver, precious stones, wood, hay, stubble — of workmen of this sort, I say, if any man's work abide he shall receive a reward. if any man's work burn, he shall suffer loss; but he himself shall be saved, yet so as by fire. By gold, therefore, and silver and precious stones we understand, as we have said, enduring goods. By wood, hay and stubble, soluble evils. In gold there is rightly enough understood the contemplation of truth; in silver the word of preaching; in precious stones the exercises of virtue; in wood, hay and stubble understand the works of perversity, superfluity and vanity, [0678C] made remissible through true penance.
