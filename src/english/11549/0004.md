## An Explanation of Certain Difficult Passages of the Apostle (cont.)

It seems perhaps a thing to be wondered at by some, how the Apostle judges that that shameful man who had his father's wife should be delivered to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, that the spirit may be saved in the day of our Lord Jesus Christ. But this cannot come into doubt: that by the same power whereby he could free a man possessed from a demon, he could also (where reason required it) deliver a man to demonic vexation. But if some shameful man is delivered to Satan and is made possessed by a demon, the way of salvation seems in this not to be opened to him but shut off. For the crimes he has committed cannot be blotted out without true penance. But how can one possessed by a demon, who lacks his senses, have true penance? How therefore does the Apostle judge that that shameful man is to be delivered [0678D] to Satan, that the spirit may be saved? It must therefore be known that demonic vexation is of one kind continual, of another intermittent. He suffers continual vexation who is never allowed to rest, but is driven by unceasing furies. But he incurs intermittent vexation who at different times now loses his senses, now recovers them, and amid manifold torments the mind of the tormented man sometimes returns to itself. He therefore who has been delivered over to intermittent infamy has for certain the means of repenting, and by that very fact the means whereby he can be saved. For who could be of a mind so hardened, so obstinate, that in the very intermission of his vexation, when his mind has returned to itself, he does not grieve and inwardly repent of having committed that whence it has befallen him both to incur demonic vexation [0679A] and to lie open to every mockery. See therefore how from the intermission of the vexation it often comes about that the vexation itself becomes for him a cause of repentance, and from that repentance it is brought about that the vexation itself is for him unto purgation. The Apostle therefore plainly gives it to be understood to what kind of vexation he wished that criminal to be delivered, in that he says, that the spirit may be saved. For as by the continual vexation of the demon the way of salvation could be shut off rather than opened, so conversely by intermittent vexation it seemed that it ought to be opened rather than shut off.

But it is to be noted that to be delivered unto the destruction of the flesh — for certain, when a man dies the spirit itself thereafter does not use the services of the flesh at its wish. The flesh of one possessed by a demon therefore dies in a certain way to its own spirit, [0679B] when the malign spirit violently abuses its services, and his own spirit is deprived of the bodily service owed to it.

It seems a wonder to you that it is said by the Apostle: *All things,* he says, *are lawful to me [n: (I Cor. VI)]*. Many things indeed are lawful, yet not all. For many things are utterly unlawful for every man. But what we call lawful we are wont to take in two ways. For we take the same expression sometimes more strictly, sometimes more broadly. We say that is lawful which can be done in an ordered way. Sometimes we say that is lawful which we are free to do, whether or not it can be done in an ordered way. Hence it is that it is wont to be said of a man of crime that he would gladly have done this or that crime, if it had been lawful [0679C] for him to do it; which is to say, if he had had the ability or the opportunity of doing it. Hence it is that blessed Gregory in his *Dialogue* says of certain less religious brothers, who had chosen blessed Benedict as abbot, but now repented of having chosen him because of the discipline of a stricter life — whence they were preparing to do away with him by poison, because they saw that unlawful things were not lawful for them under him. What is it that he says, that unlawful things were not lawful for them, if not that under him they had no license or liberty for doing unlawful things? We know that God has placed it in man's liberty whether he wishes to choose good things or evil. Hence is that saying of the Wise Man about the Lord: *He hath set before thee fire and water; stretch forth thy hand unto which thou wilt. Before man is life and death, good and evil; that which shall please him shall be given him* [0679D] [n: (Eccli. XV)]. Hence it is that the Apostle, having enumerated so many evils, adds and says: *All things are lawful to me,* lawful certainly not according to concession, but according to permission alone. According to concession only ordered things are lawful; according to permission alone even disordered things. For the Lord permits many things to be done which nevertheless he concedes to no one to do. All things, he says, are lawful to me — without doubt as regards permission; but all things are not expedient, not even of those that are done according to concession. All things are lawful to me, but I will not be brought under the power of any — namely of any of those things which are done from permission alone. For what he says, all things are not expedient, looks to that license which is according to concession. But what he says, but I will not be brought [0680A] under the power of any, looks to that license which is according to permission alone. For many of the things that are conceded hinder greater goods, and therefore are not expedient, yet do not consign the user to servitude. But he who commits sin is the servant of sin. If therefore we wish to avoid this servitude, and not to be brought under the power of any, those things which are lawful from permission alone must be carefully shunned. But that not all things are expedient which are lawful according to concession he evidently shows when he adds: *Meat for the belly, and the belly for meats; but God shall destroy both it and them [n: (I Cor. VI)]*. Meats certainly were made for the refreshment of the belly, and are therefore lawful according to concession; yet it is not expedient to indulge them overmuch, and to omit more necessary pursuits. For what does too great an indulgence [0680B] in them avail, when it stands truly agreed that God shall destroy both it and them. But that not all things which are lawful according to permission are lawful according to concession, he shows subsequently when he immediately adds: *But the body is not for fornication, but for the Lord, and the Lord for the body [n: (ibid.)]*. Meats indeed are for eating, and the belly for using. But the body is not for fornicating, but for serving its own Lord. For that we can fornicate often when we will is not the indulgence of one conceding, but the testing, or the desertion, of one permitting. He had said above that fornicators shall not possess the kingdom of God; but because he foresaw that there would be many who would find this hard to believe, and who would not think the evil of fornication so [0680C] damnable, therefore he consequently shows in two things what we ought to think of this evil. For he shows how deep this kind of evil is, and how it is beyond all others inward. For by the work of fornication the members of Christ are taken away, and are made the members of a harlot. Hence one may see how deep this kind of evil is, which casts down from so great a height into this lowest and foul thing. Hear also how it is beyond all others inward: *Every sin,* he says, *whatsoever a man doth is without the body; but he that committeth fornication sinneth against his own body [n: (I Cor. VI)]*. But if we wish more fully to understand the rights of the apostolic judgment, we must raise our consideration higher. Man, as we commonly know, consists of a twofold substance, one [0680D] corporeal, the other incorporeal. The corporeal can die; the incorporeal cannot. The incorporeal can subsist without nourishment, but the corporeal cannot at all. But the corporeal substance, which cannot preserve its own nature of itself, can of itself restore and even multiply it — which the spiritual substance cannot do at all. You certainly see that the prerogative of the corporeal substance is one thing, the prerogative of the incorporeal substance another. The prerogative of the incorporeal substance is that it can subsist forever, and without support. The prerogative of the corporeal substance is that it can produce from its own substance a substance conformed to itself, and by sowing multiply the nature of its own kind. But this conformity of substance we cannot produce from ourselves without [0681A] a drawing away, so to speak, of our own substance. For the begetting of offspring does not take place without a certain cutting off from the begetter. In which this too is to be noted, that this cutting off of the parent for this work is not made, nor indeed can be made, from any member of the body whatever, but is drawn out from the inmost part and as it were from a certain treasury of its own kind placed in the inward parts, and stored away for that office. But although nothing more excellent seems to have been given to corporeal substance than this dowry of nature, it is nevertheless more glorious to cease from this office than to exercise it, in that after the corruption of nature it cannot be fulfilled without lust and foul delight. For that it is lawful to exercise this with one's own spouse, and only for the sake of begetting offspring — this very thing, if you attend well, is a matter of indulgence rather than of chastity. But there is in drink and food [0681B] no small delight, and yet it is not vicious, except for him for whom it has been excessive and passes into surfeit or drunkenness. But in surfeit and drunkenness there is a certain drawing away of another's substance. But in the work of lust there is a certain drawing away and cutting off of one's own substance. There indeed the exaction of viciousness is made upon another's body. But here upon one's own body, nay from the very inmost part of the body itself. Well therefore was it said of the rest, that every sin is without the body. But he who commits fornication sins not only in his own inmost part, but also against his very own body, since he seems to empty out his own body into the uses of foulness, and abuses the prerogative of a divine gift for filth both his own and another's.
