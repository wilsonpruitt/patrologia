## An Explanation of Certain Difficult Passages of the Apostle (cont.)

*If any man think that he knoweth anything, he hath not yet known [0681C] how he ought to know [n: (I Cor. VIII)]*. What, I ask, shall we say to these things? To say nothing of the rest, what are we to say of the Apostle himself? If the Apostle did not think that he knew anything, with what face did he busy himself with teaching the rest by preaching and by writing? And if he does not think that to be true which he declares from the words set down before, why, I ask, does he so steadfastly affirm that very thing? If he did not think that he knew anything, what is it that he set down above, when he says: But concerning those things that are sacrificed to idols, we know that we all have knowledge? But this very thing which he seems to say in this place he seems to contradict in what follows. But knowledge, he says, is not in all. How, I ask, do all have knowledge, if knowledge is not in all, and how can it be wanting to those who have it? [0681D] It must therefore be known that one knowledge is that which all have. Another is that which certain men do not have, but are able to have. But another is that which in this life no one has, nor is able to have. We all have knowledge — behold, of the first. Knowledge is not in all — behold, of the second. But to the third the Apostle looks in what he says: *But if any man think that he knoweth anything, he hath not yet known in what manner he ought to know [n: (Rom. II)]*. The knowledge of discerning between true and false, good and evil, just and unjust — although some more perfectly, others more imperfectly — we all nevertheless have in common: Jews, Greeks, barbarians and Latins. For if that knowledge were not common to the faithful and the unfaithful, in no way would the Apostle [0682A] have said: *For when the Gentiles, who have not the law, do by nature those things that are of the law, these, having not the law, are a law to themselves, who show the work of the law written in their hearts [n: (ibid.)]*. Behold, of the knowledge which all have. But the knowledge which, with grace received, would suffice each man unto salvation, many do not have, and yet are still able to have. Of knowledge of this sort is understood what is rightly said by the Apostle: *But knowledge is not in all [n: (I Cor. VIII)]*. That he wished knowledge of this sort to be understood there is shown by what he had set down before. For, although there are those who are called gods, whether in heaven or on earth, yet for us there is one God the Father, from whom are all things, and we in him; and one Lord Jesus Christ through whom are all things, and we through him. And to this end [0682B] we receive that knowledge which is common to all, that we may be able to seek and to find that which suffices unto salvation. But of this knowledge which is truly of salvation, some have more, others less; and it often happens that those who have less are saved, and those who have more perish. For the greatness of knowledge is for some the kindling of pride, and by that very fact the cause of ruin. This very thing he intimates who says: *Lest the greatness of the revelations should exalt me, there was given me an angel Satan, a sting of my flesh, to buffet me [n: (II Cor. XII)]*. But no one who is in this life can fully know whether the knowledge he has, with grace received, can suffice him unto salvation. This is the knowledge which in this life no one has, no one is able to grasp: *Even if I shall be simple,* says [0682C] Job, *this very thing shall my soul be ignorant of [n: (Job IX)]*. To the same judgment runs what Solomon says: *Man knoweth not whether he be worthy of love or hatred [n: (Eccle. IX)]*. We have from his judgment that man is ignorant of what profits him in his life. We have according to the same: *That man knoweth not his own end, but as fishes are taken with the hook, and as birds are caught with the snare, so,* and so forth. [n: (Ibid.)] From these words, therefore, we understand for certain that we cannot have in this life the knowledge of sure security. If therefore, according to Solomon, all things are kept uncertain for the future, see what you ought to think of your own knowledge. Certainly, even if it seems something great as regards quantity, you do not know whether it be even a little thing, you do not know whether it be anything at all unto your profit. Of what sort, I ask, must that [0682D] estimation of a thing be called, of which you do not know, as far as concerns you, whether it be of some worth or of none. We ought therefore, so far as we can, to advance in knowledge, and yet, however much we shall have advanced, to reckon it as nothing, so long as we cannot have a sure judgment of its end. According to this consideration, therefore, what is said by the Apostle is rightly understood: *If any man think that he knoweth anything, he hath not yet known in what manner he ought to know.*

Every love is either only gratuitous, or only owed, or owed and gratuitous at once. And gratuitous love can be understood in two ways. We rightly call that love gratuitous which is according to grace alone. The fullness of true and highest love is [0683A] when it is so great and of such a kind that no greater or better can be. Where there is not the highest perfection, the highest love is not of avail, nor indeed ought it to be. That is according to grace alone which, at the good pleasure of the one bestowing it, can justly be bestowed or not bestowed. But from the divine excellence [0684A] whatever can be said to be according to grace alone is utterly foreign. Likewise love is not incongruously called gratuitous where to him who bestows all things nothing but love is rendered in return.
