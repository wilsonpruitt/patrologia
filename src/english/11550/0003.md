## CHAPTER II. *How the soul considers the examples both of the perfect and of the imperfect.*

And so the bride, having suffered difficulty and as it were a repulse, in no wise is still from seeking, but girds herself [0412A] more amply for seeking. For she shows her own importunity and constancy, saying: *I will rise, and I will go about the city [n: (Cant. III)]*. She turns herself to humility, that whom she does not find through herself, she may seek through others, if perchance through them she may find him. With much labor she had prepared a bed for herself, first ceasing from sins, and afterwards contending more strongly against vices, and after these things withdrawing from occupations and cares, and at the last being at leisure from harmful things, and truly also from very many good works, so that, made less in her deeds, she might perceive the wisdom which is God. But now she turns to going about and searching out the good works of others, that by this labor she may find the Bridegroom, whom being still she had less found. She rises from stillness, going about, namely, the city. She goes about in thought the assembly [0412B] of good men, that in them she may take examples of a better life and lessons of the virtues: for through humility she reckons that there is lacking to her the adornment of virtues, and cleanness of heart, through which she might deserve to find the Bridegroom, showing the house of her mind, though free from vices, yet less adorned with virtues. Which house, that she may adorn it more becomingly, going about and traversing the virtues of the saints, and exploring the special grace of each single one, and plucking flowers from one of humility, from another of patience, from another of obedience, from another of abstinence, from another of charity, from another of purity. These she strives to transfuse by imitation into her manners, and with them to adorn the house of her mind. For she measures the fabric, that is, she weighs the life of the saints, and considers in them the construction of the virtues, that she may blush in herself for her own imperfection. [0412C] And, as is read in Job, she looks upon men, she attends to the holy [n: (Job XXXIII)], that she may reckon herself a sinner in comparison with them; whence, humbled by their examples and kindled to advancement, she gives her effort anew to the cleansing of her heart and to the cultivating of the virtues, that with her house more fully cleansed and adorned she may become worthy for the finding of the Bridegroom. The bride herself goes about the city (good men, namely) that she may search out their virtues and holiness: *And she seeks the beloved through the streets and the broad ways.* The streets are narrower, and the broad ways wider. Whence by the streets are designated those who, leaving all things, and naked following the naked Christ, have chosen for themselves a narrow way of living. But by the broad ways, which are wider, we understand the perfect of secular life, who, walking by a wider way, [0412D] and not leaving all their own things, but using their own well, have pleased God by hospitalities and alms and the other good works. Who, turning aside from the broad way which leads to death, have held the strait way of the commandments and of life. She seeks therefore the beloved first through the streets, and afterwards through the broad ways.

For because she is borne by the desire of perfection, she seeks examples first from the perfect. She considers by how strait a law of living they bound themselves, and how slender was their food, how poor their clothing, how brief their sleep, how heavy the labor of the body, how strict the discipline of the heart, how in all things they cut away the delights of the body, drew out the wills of the heart. She weighs after these things how great was in them the loftiness and the liberty of mind. And how, dwelling in the flesh, they had their converse [0413A] in the heavens, how they passed beyond all earthly things and trod them down. Some of these were sustained by a little bread and water, some lived not on bread but on herbs or roots, and some received their food from heaven, some led their life almost without any food at all, some dwelt without any clothing at all under the naked air. Who, casting away not only the acts of the world, but even all its matter, were as it were outside the flesh, and by as much as they passed beyond the world and went beyond the vices of the flesh, by so much, filled with grace and swallowed up by the spirit, they searched out the hidden things of the Godhead, and knew them. And so the soul, made fervent, runs through these so heavenly ones, filled with so great grace, and considers them, and what she has less in herself, in them she loves. Through these she seeks God, that in their [0413B] examples her love, being nourished, may at some time rise up to God. For since in them the image of God, and in great part the likeness, shines out, and by as much as she has been able to imitate them and to be made like them, by so much does she deserve to be united to God, and by so much does she receive God into herself, as she has taken on his likeness. For nothing is so near to God as his character and likeness. But when the soul has been made just, true, merciful, wise through grace, which God has by nature, by as much as she has formed these virtues in herself, by so much will she reform in herself the likeness of God. Wherefore, when the bride attends to the perfect, and labors to be made like them, and walks by their examples that she may find the beloved, through the streets she seeks him whom her soul loveth. She seeks [0413C] him also through the broad ways, namely in certain seculars or even in married persons. For nowhere does she hold the examples and incitements of salvation to be despised. And so many of this sort, although they held a lighter and wider way of converse, nevertheless spread abroad the odor of a good report of themselves, and afforded examples of imitation. What examples of sanctity are there in Abraham? What in Job are not found, by which a soul seeking God might be kindled and built up? Who were filled with so great grace, and endowed with so great love of God, that the one of them offered his only son to death at the Lord's command, and the other bore with even mind the death of all his children, and rendered thanks to God. The one deserved to be blessed by the Lord for his excelling merit, the other, in the loss of all that he had had, [0413D] blessed the Lord. The one deserved to enjoy the converse of angels, whom also he received in hospitality, and fed. The other heard the Lord himself speaking, and saw him with the eyes of his heart illumined. With how great a light of divine grace was he illumined, whose mind the night of so many adversities and of the enemy's temptations did not darken, and whom his whole quiet bed, turned about, did not overturn? Not undeservedly, therefore, does the bride run from her night, and from the bed of her stillness in which she did not find the Bridegroom, to this man, whom she knows to have found an ampler grace in his own night and in his troubled bed.

Moses and Samuel, among the other virtues in which they shone excellently, set forth that surpassing example of charity, that they devoutly interceded for their enemies. The one indeed for the people that was stoning him, the other [0414A] for the people that was casting him out from his princedom. The one with so great charity that he asked of the Lord either that their sin be forgiven them, or that he himself be blotted out of the book of life. The other declares that he prays no less devoutly for those casting him down. Who would not wonder at the humility of David, the man, namely, whom God found according to his own heart? Who after so great and so glorious deeds did not lift himself up into pride, but rather called himself a dead dog or one single flea. Who manifests that he had had both outward and inward humility by these words, in which he says: *I will play and become viler than I have been, and I will be humble in my own eyes [n: (II Reg. VI)]*. For he did not refuse outward vileness, and inwardly he was abject in his own judgment. How many such were there under the law, or even in the time of grace, who afforded of themselves wholesome [0414B] examples of the virtues? Through these, therefore, the bride also seeks the beloved, and through them kindles herself into the love of him.
