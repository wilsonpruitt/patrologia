## CHAPTER III. *How the examples of good men, once considered, humble and kindle.*

*I sought, and I found him not [n: (Cant. III)]* [var: Vulg. Cant. 3:1, the place cited, reads *quaesivi illum, et non inveni*; the wording printed here is that of Cant. 5:6, which the previous chapter cites as (Cant. V)]. The bride sought the beloved through the streets and the broad ways, and did not find him to her wish. For she has not yet felt his presence, has not yet received the grace desired. Nor must I doubt but that in her going about she acquired much, and increased much grace in herself. Since indeed from the knowledge of good men and of their virtues the desire of justice was kindled in her not a little. For she came near [0414C] to those burning with this fire which the Lord Jesus sent upon the earth, and willed that it be vehemently kindled. To whom, while one lukewarm draws near, he grows warm, and one fervent pardons the more [cj: *ignoscit*; read *ignescit*, "catches fire the more"]. But not because she has acquired grace is she still from seeking an ampler grace: her desires have been kindled, not fulfilled, stirred up, not stilled, and by as much as they have grown, by so much do the things she has received suffice her less. The increase of grace has stirred up hunger, so far that by as much as she has received more, by so much does she desire more. The bad or the lukewarm man, because he does not love or desire the good, knows not his own defect, nor to how great things he is held. Whence, though he be needy, he reckons himself rich, because he knows not the spiritual riches. For he says, "I am rich, and I need nothing," when he is wretched and miserable, and poor, and blind, and naked; but the good man and one advancing [0414D] does not attend to what he has, but stretches himself always to the things before. And because he thinks that he has never laid hold, he is always humbled out of desire. In one advancing, therefore, with grace growing, humility also grows, and by as much as he is rich, by so much does he believe himself needy, because grace, once tasted, kindles him the more. And so the bride advances: for by as much as she is kindled to love, by so much is she humbled. She believes that she has not yet found grace, while she attends to the virtues of the perfect, whence she says: *I sought and I found him not.* She says that she has not found him whom she found so perfectly in others. So Antony, the greatest of monks, after he had found Paul whom he had sought, judged himself to be nothing in comparison with him, saying: "Woe to me a sinner, who bear the false name of a monk." This [0415A] the finding of Paul afforded him, that he who before had stood forth as the greatest of monks now said that he was not worthy even of the name of a monk. Which is wonderful in imitation, since one highest in the merit of sanctity became by his own judgment the lowest, and one so lofty before God became so cheap to himself. But how lofty do we think this vileness of himself made him? I know not whether any virtue in Antony, however lofty, exalted him before God as much as that casting-down of himself, and that annulling of all the merits which he had had before. And so he believed that he had not yet found Christ, whom he beheld dwelling so excellently in Paul. Behold how much the finding of Paul, and the knowledge of his sanctity, conferred upon Antony. So the bride believed that she had not found the beloved, while by searching out she came to know the virtues of the perfect. Whom [0415B] she seeks also not only in the more perfect, but in her equals, or even in her inferiors. Since indeed these too can likewise be called streets and broad ways, the good, namely, and the middling. In these she explores and considers the gift of some grace which they have specially received, and venerates it, and strives to imitate it. For he who fears God neglects nothing; he holds nothing to be despised whereby he may receive an advancement of salvation. By how much therefore she is great, she humbles herself in all things, not refusing to be amended even by the lowest. For in these she sometimes finds examples of imitation and of advancing, which she may profitably set before herself. For good men are wont to kindle their virtues in turn, and the one to take from the other advancement and the incitements of humility [0415C] alike. Whence it comes about that, while this one considers that one's patience, and that one this one's obedience; this one that one's charity, and that one this one's cleanness, always the one takes from the other whereby he may grow into good. For they are much girded to the zeal of amendment when they see in others the good which they themselves have less. And while in turn they think each other their superiors, and the one reveres and loves the other's good, by loving it he makes it his own, and keeps himself in humility. So Paul did not attend to it that he had labored in preaching more than all the apostles, but looks upon the innocence of others, and because he himself had persecuted the Church of God, he calls himself the least of the apostles [n: (II Cor. XV)]. But Peter does not consider that he is the first of the apostles, but wonders at the wisdom in Paul, [0415D] and in a certain place commends this concerning him [n: (II Petr. III)]. For neither was Peter less than Paul, who bore him up with praises; nor was Paul less than the apostles, who judged himself the least. But it was necessary that they who were great before God should seem small to themselves. And they who were adorned with all the virtues ought to be graced also with the virtue of humility. For it is the greatest beauty of the soul, if after the perfection of all the virtues it possess humility, and, being of great merit, know not that it is great. Blessed Job knew not that he was great, who said: *If I shall be simple, of this very thing shall my soul be ignorant [n: (Job IX)]*. For he esteemed his own goods small, because he was always fervent toward greater ones. And so the perfect, that is, the truly humble, know and judge their own reprehensible things, and, dissembling the evils of others, [0416A] rather weigh their goods, being strict judges of themselves, but merciful of others: whence, when they behold some small goods in others, they hope for greater in them. But when they themselves have done many things, they reckon themselves useless servants. And so it comes about that they [think] well of others, but grow cheap to themselves, and believe themselves inferior to their lesser ones. So the bride, not considering her own merits, nor setting them before her eyes, fixes her consideration upon the grace conferred on others. She rejoices together with those receiving the gifts of grace, she rejoices together with God the bestower, she is reminded of the beloved through his members, and through his members is the more inflamed toward the beloved. And when she considers the gifts of his liberality, which he has bestowed on divers persons, she kindles her own desires in herself anew: whence she repeats her devout complaint, saying: *I sought and I found not.*
