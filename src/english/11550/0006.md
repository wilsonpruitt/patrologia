## CHAPTER V. *How the soul, after the contemplation of the angels, passes over to the contemplation of God and to discretion.*

*When I had passed by them a little, I found him whom my soul loves [n: (Cant. III)]*. In his threefold creation God can be searched out and known: in the world and in the things created for its adornment, in man, and in the angel. These the soul that thirsts for God goes round one by one, and passes by, that she may find him whom she seeks. From the visible appearance of the world, once beheld, she considers the invisible Creator. For these things, when beheld, give her to know that the Lord himself [0419B] is God, and that he made this world, and not the world itself. But this vision is common to all, that is, to the evil as much as to the good. For in this manner all men see God, as is read in Job, so that every one beholds him afar off [n: (Job XXXVI)], because God is seen outwardly, and not in himself. In this manner the philosophers saw him, who nevertheless did not find him through love. Whence from this vision she passes over to man, who also passes by in the image, although he has lost the likeness, and in whom God is more familiarly seen, inasmuch as in the image of his soul, which represents him in itself through reason and understanding. But she does not pass over to all men, but only to the good and the spiritual. For she does not direct herself to those walking the broad way, which leads to death, but to those who straiten themselves for the sake of the [0419C] kingdom of heaven, whom above she calls streets or lanes, and by whom she designates the less perfect or the more perfect. In these she finds not only the image of God, but in part the likeness, in so far as they have advanced in the virtues, or have climbed up to perfection. But because she does not find in these a full likeness (although she finds some), in order that she may comprehend more fully and contemplate more nearly, she passes over to that which contemplates more nearly, namely to the angelic creation, in which she considers the likeness fully, inasmuch as it is the seal of the likeness, and in which the likeness signed from the beginning perseveres. Men, however good, however holy, nevertheless see in the night, that is, in obscurity, but the angel at noonday, that is, in full knowledge. For as [0419D] blessed Job says, the stars are darkened with the gloom of this night [n: (Job III)], because even those who shine with virtues and with holiness of life are darkened by the shadows of human blindness. The angel is suffused with the light of perfect contemplation. For he is the brightness of the eternal light and the mirror without stain of the divine majesty, who, as he is in no part distant from God, so seeing God perfectly and as he is, represents him also in himself, since he is a clean and transparent mirror to him who directs the eyes of his heart upon him, eyes cleansed, however, more fully from sins and vices, and even from the images of them. For vices darken, and their images also beat back, and hinder contemplation and hide knowledge: he therefore who looks into this mirror, so as to consider the cleanness of these, sends clean [0420A] eyes of the heart into it. In this mirror the likeness of God shines back to him. For they are clean as that One is clean; they are holy, as the Lord their God is holy. Finally, from that clean one he passes over to the author of cleanness, and from the holy to the Holy of holies, from the contemplating to him who is to be contemplated, and at once he finds the beloved. For these are so near to him that there is scarcely any middle between these and him, and after these there is nothing to find but him. Therefore, the beloved having been found, congratulating the angels and calling to mind the benefit obtained through them, she says accordingly: *When I had passed by them a little, I found him whom my soul loves.* Because straightway, as soon as I passed these by, I found the beloved; these I passed by that I might come, and through these I passed over that I might find: for by their meeting and their help I obtained what I longed for, [0420B] who both provoked and kindled my desire, and obtained the meeting of the beloved. The finding of the Bridegroom, therefore, is the experience and revelation of his presence.

To the soul that seeks, indeed, and does not cease from her intention, he shows himself at last and comes to meet her vows. And with him present the soul is made new, and, as it were cleaving to him, she feels the sweetness of an inward taste, spiritual understanding, the illumination of faith, the increase of hope, the incitement of charity, the affection of compassion, the zeal of justice, the delight of the virtues; she has in prayer a familiar colloquy with God, feeling that she is heard and for the most part graciously heard, speaking face to face with God, and hearing what the Lord God speaks in her, in prayer constraining God, and at times convincing him. Illumined by this [0420C] grace, she begins to see the darkness of her own heart, to know herself, to discern in what manner vices hide themselves under the appearance of virtues, or secretly ally themselves to them. For a new day arises in the mind after the night of ignorance, and there comes the daybreak in which she rises to offer the holocaust of contrition for each of the virtues, lest perchance they have been imperfect, or less pure, and to burn up whatever of the carnal has been mingled in them. For the spirit of man is at times carried toward another good to which something carnal is annexed, which delights his humanity and secretly draws him, but he does not know it. Again, how often does someone believe himself to be moved by the spirit to do something, when he is moved by carnality, or by natural things, or by levity, or by some cause [0420D] or occasion. Finally, at times a man does something before the time, or too late, or without discretion. These shadows and hidden evils she detects and judges, and the daybreak of compunction shows the man to himself, namely that he is less in good than he believed, that he has received only the beginning of grace, that he has lain in the night, that he has come to daybreak [sic: *diliculum*], and not to the full day. Therefore, just as, being illumined through the grace received, he knows himself, so through that same grace he understands that what he has found is but little. Elias, after he had heard the whistling of a light air, in which he learned that the Lord was, covered his face with his cloak, and stood in the mouth of the cave [n: (III Reg. XIX)]. So a man, grace having been perceived and, through it, known in his perfection [cj: *in perfectione*; read *imperfectione*, "his imperfection"], covers his face with his cloak; because he recognizes with how great ignorance he is covered, and he stands in the mouth of the cave, [0421A] because after the night of his blindness now known he desires to go out from the vice of corruption and to come to the day of grace that is dawning upon him. The slack and the blind man neither sees himself nor loves grace, because he is wrapped in his own shadows, and is ignorant of the light of grace. The devout soul, illumined by grace, just as she sees herself, so, thinking little of herself, is humbled; and just as she understands grace, so she is the more inflamed to desire it. Humbled therefore, and desiring, and pleading with herself about her poverty, she recalls that she has found the beloved a little, not perfectly, nor to her vow.
