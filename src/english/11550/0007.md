## CHAPTER VI. *How the soul receives Christ into herself, and on the true love of God.*

*I held him, nor will I let him go, until I bring him into the house of my mother, and into the chamber of her that bore [0421B] me [n: (Cant. III)]*. The soul, although she has received this grace, is nevertheless not forgetful of her imperfection, nor overmuch confident of the gift of God, and she recalls that she has many times been carnally affected by her own will and borne by the spirit, that she has not yet perfectly corrected her manners, nor laid hold of the virtues of Christ. She desires therefore that her effect be purged, her will mortified, her own spirit fail, and that she put on Christ, as the Apostle says: *Put on the Lord Jesus Christ [n: (Rom. XIII)]*. For although she has found the beloved, she does not yet bring him into her lodging, and what she has received was as it were outside, not within, in desire and not in effect. She understands that she has been visited and consoled, but that she is not yet such a one with whom the beloved would tarry, and have a dwelling until she perfectly fail from herself. To [0421C] this therefore she asks to be helped, and to advance so far that Christ may be formed in her, and that she may now not herself live, but that Christ may live in her. But until she comes to this state, she declares that she will not rest, nor cease from insistence in prayers, saying: *I held him, nor will I let him go,* etc. God is held by the devotion of prayer, by desire, by importunity, by memory, by entreaty, by faith and by the expectation of being heard, and he is not let go if there be no ceasing from the intention, nor any further turning of the face to other things. And so she holds the beloved, although it be the dawn, and she does not let him go until he shall have blessed her. She wrestled with him all the night in which she slept, but her heart kept watch, because, that is, she ceased from outward occupation and labored in seeking the beloved. Also, [0421D] although through the night she has come to the dawn, she does not cease from the wrestling, namely from insistence in prayer, nor does she let the beloved go unless he give a blessing, that she may go from virtue to virtue, and see the God of gods in Sion, that is, in beholding through a mirror and in a riddle him whom she cannot see as he is while she lives in the body. This blessing she seeks, and the changing of her name, that she may now be called not Jacob, but Israel, and that she may have no more need to supplant vices. For Jacob is interpreted *supplanter*, but that, the vices being overcome and driven out, she may enjoy the purity of the virtues and contemplate God; because Israel is interpreted *seeing God*. For to purity of heart God is promised as to be lived [cj: *vivendus*; read *videndus*, "to be seen"], as it is written: *Blessed are the clean of heart, for they shall see God [n: (Matth. V)]*. This blessing [0422A] having been received, she will limp on one foot, because she who walked with double desires, feeling indeed carnal things but consenting to spiritual, will now, the vices being more perfectly mortified and carnality withering away, be borne by spiritual desires only. Sighing therefore for this purity and perfection, she says: *I held him, nor will I let him go, until I bring him into the house of my mother.* Our mother is the grace of the Spirit, which spiritually regenerates us. Whose house is the human mind, in which that same grace is received. Into this house she desires to bring the beloved, that as she has found him, so she may abide and dwell with him. For she desires to retain in herself the grace received, to pour it into her manners, and to be transformed in her manner of life. Christ abides in that soul which has possessed his virtues, [0422B] humility, meekness, patience, obedience and charity. And likewise she who keeps his commandments, and as he walked so she also walks: *If any one loves me,* he says, *he will keep my commandments [var: Vulg. *sermonem meum servabit*, "will keep my word"], and my Father will love him, and we will come to him, and will make our dwelling with him [n: (Joan. XIV)]*. Such a one therefore both truly loves, and brings Christ into the house of the mind. That affectionate love, by which we said above that the love of the soul is signified, at times more affects him who loves less and is less more perfect. For each one does not love so much as he feels this affection, and so much as in that state he seems to himself to love, but so much as he has been grounded in the virtues and in charity, and is held faithful in keeping the commandments. A sweet affection toward God is in a certain way carnal, [0422C] and deceptive, and at times rather of humanity than of grace, of the heart than of the spirit, of sensuality than of reason; so that it sometimes goes rather toward the lesser good, and less toward the greater, and toward something which has more savor than is expedient. By this affection the disciples went astray, and loved God carnally, whose presence they were unwilling to be without. Whence they were even charged with not loving; what of it, that they embraced what delighted rather than what was expedient. Thus some carnal and imperfect person is at times affectionately affected toward God, not because he greatly loves, but because he tastes the sweetness of grace, which helps just so long as it lasts; as long as the sweetness lasts, so long the love also lasts. But a friend will not be known in good things. For in that day the Lord [0422D] indeed commands his mercy, but in the night of temptations and labors he declares in his precepts how much each one loves. By the visitation of grace God consoles our faintheartedness, helps our infirmity, arouses our will. And what wonder if the infirm man be anointed with grace, when even the evil man confesses to the Lord when he has done well to him? And so it happens that the fainthearted man, greedy of love, is more moved by this love, and he in whom sensuality and carnal appetite rather than reason bears rule. At times he feels it the more who is light of heart and poor in grace: for he is more easily moved who has been the lighter, and one who is without consolation receives more delightfully what is offered. The cause therefore of this sweet affection is at times not an abundance of grace, but a poverty of mind. For small things gladden [0423A] the poor man. Not every one who says: Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven. Not every one, because again and again he says: Lord, sweetly (namely, affectionately) shall enter, but he who shall have done the will of the Father, who shall have kept the commandments. *Call me and I will answer thee [var: Vulg. *Vocabis me, et ego respondebo tibi*, "Thou shalt call me, and I will answer thee"],* says blessed Job [n: (Job XIV)]. God calls through visiting grace, and man will answer through the fulfilling of the commandments; the calling does not make perfect, but obliges; the answer through the commandments justifies. For sometimes this sweet affection comes from an evil spirit, so that while too much is believed of it, and while it greatly delights it is clung to, a man is brought to weakness of heart. Again, so that through occupation with it he may be called away from more useful work. And again, so that someone, relying on the abundance of it, may believe himself perfect, and be less exercised toward the perfect. [0423B] Sometimes too a man of himself abuses grace, by receiving it in vain, or by omitting some more useful work, or by setting in it the end of his own advancement. For as the spirit of the prophets is recorded to be subject to the prophets, so this grace is made subject to man, that he may use it either well or ill: through it therefore he offends who does not know its use, just as virtue too is turned into [sic: *id*] vice, if it be exercised too much, or out of season. The bride therefore sought the offence [cj: *delictum*; read *dilectum*, "the beloved"] affectionately and likewise longingly, but when he was found she was no less solicitous to bring him in to herself. For a fuller grace is to be sought diligently, and there must be no ceasing, but progress must be made through it, until one comes to [sic: *ac*] perfection. *Before I eat I sigh,* says blessed Job [n: (Job III)]. [0423C] Before anyone sighs he does not eat, because unless he desire and seek this grace, he does not receive its refreshment. Nor, unless he advance through it, does he attain to perfection. This grace is food, because it refreshes; it is light, because it illumines. For it reveals to a man his own evils, and strengthens him that he may be able to correct them; his own peril having been known, he receives it, who neglects to correct himself through it and to advance to inward things. But the fervent soul, lest she receive it in vain, labors always to make progress, and diligently tills her own field by bettering her manners and by cultivating the virtues, that she may build the house of the mind into which she may bring the beloved.
