## CHAPTER NINE. *On the spiritual assent of the soul, and on discretion and fervor.*

*Who is she that goeth up by the desert, like a little rod of smoke from the spices of myrrh and frankincense, and of all the powder of the perfumer? [n: (Cant. III.)]* The bride goes up by the desert, that is, by the lovers of this world, who are a desert, who are deserted by God, who desert him, [0429C] when by their deeds they say to them: *Depart from us, we desire not the knowledge of thy ways [n: (Job XXI)]*. Therefore they are deserted by him, and their house is left to them desert, so that they may not have God as an indweller within themselves. By these the bride goes up, because, dwelling with them in body, in mind she transcends them, seeking the things above; and spurning their lowest delights, she stays among carnal men, and does not imitate their perverse manners and life. The good, whether set far off or near, she heeds; to them she draws near [sic: *aceedit*] in mind, and is joined to them in thought; she considers their examples, and conforms herself to their manner of life. Thus she is saved from this perverse generation, when she is separated from it, and passes over and transcends to the generation of those who seek the Lord: for it is difficult to be saved among the perverse, unless in this manner they are transcended [0429D] and deserted, because their perverse manner of life draws and attracts, and their conversations and their talk corrupt. For to that which is frequently seen and heard the mind is easily conformed, because, as is commonly said, beside whomever a man sits, from him he also learns. To be set apart, then, from the fellowship of the evil, and to pass over to the good by love and to imitate them, this is to ascend, because it is to be raised on high spiritually. For as much as a man loves the good, so much does he become good, and so much does he ascend into God. The evil descend daily, because they fall headlong from sin into sin, and, being in filth, they grow yet more filthy. Again, the tempest of water, that is, sin, drowns them, and the deep swallows them up, that is, their iniquity enwraps them, and the pit shuts its mouth upon them, that is, the depth of iniquity, [0430A] when not only are they sunk in sins, but they also lose the entrance of confession. But the good ascend, because they go from virtue to virtue, and daily advance in charity, because charity is increased in them through interior goods, through the will and through works. While therefore the bride advances among those deserted by grace, and failing and descending, she goes up by the desert. This world is also called a desert, either because it is deserted and despised, or because it deserts itself and fails in itself. For daily, with time, the joys of time pass away, and as many as the days are, so many joys of the days go by and fail. As many days as a man has received for living, so many are cut away from him from living and from rejoicing; this, then, is for a man to live and to rejoice: to fail from life and joy. And he who enjoys [0430B] a transitory joy is to be said not so much to rejoice as to learn joy [cj: *discere*; read *deserere*, "to forsake"]; because his joy passes and perishes together with time. The world, therefore, is a desert, because it deserts and loses its own joys. Whence, in that the soul perfectly despises these joys and stretches toward the joy above and true, she goes up by the desert. This desert not only deserts its own delights, but is also barren and dries up, and has no fruit; for it cannot bear fruit to Christ, because it cannot receive the Spirit which is of God, because it has not known him, nor does it know him. But the devout soul, while she labors for the true joy, bears fruit in the barren and dry desert, and among things withering and slipping away brings forth fruit that may remain. Therefore, in this desert and in this land desert, pathless and waterless, to bear fruit and to bring forth [0430C] that which remains, this is to go up by the desert. And the bride goes up like a little rod of smoke from the spices of myrrh and frankincense, and of all the powder of the perfumer.

By myrrh we can understand the mortification and the contempt of earthly love or consolation, and by frankincense the desire of the things above and prayer. Every man is bound to this, that he mortify his members which are upon the earth, fornication and uncleanness, evil concupiscence and avarice, and the other deadly things, and likewise that he keep the commandments: for these belong to eternal life, and this is the justice which, if a man do it, he shall live in it. But the soul that wills to climb to a loftier degree [0430D] ought also to beware of the multitude of venial sins and of the lesser concupiscences, that she may deserve to perfect and to receive a fuller grace. For she ought to avoid carnal and vain thoughts, and likewise thoughts of suspicion, of anger and of bitterness, and the like; and earthly consolations too, namely in food and drink, in conversation, in seeing and hearing, in curiosity, in clothing, in sleep, in any liberty whatsoever; because, as much as she mortifies and spurns these delights and refuses to be consoled carnally, so much will she deserve to be consoled spiritually and to be quickened in grace. As much as grace finds the heart empty of these things, so much will it fill. As much as a man despises these falling things and steps over them, so much does he ascend, because so much does he pass from the lowest things to the things above. And so, after the mortification of the greater [0431A] sins, the soul that is advancing, and that wills to climb to the spiritual life, must mortify and tread down the lesser ones also. So blessed Job, after he had girded the loins of the flesh, was bidden also to gird the loins of the mind, that is, evil thoughts, by the Lord [n: (Job XXXVIII)], when he was to have converse with him, and was to make his command manifest to him. With blessed Job, therefore, the devout mind must gird these loins: for this myrrh, that is, mortification, belongs to the advancing or the perfect soul. With this she ought also to burn frankincense, because she ought not only to mortify earthly delights, but also to desire the things above, and to seek them with devout prayers. Assuredly the smoke of mortification rises together with that of desire and of prayer; [0431B] because the one is propped up by the other, so that neither by itself can rise or please God. For we cannot desire the things above unless we despise earthly things, and we do not despise earthly things unless we are drawn by the desire of the things above. For the heart cannot be without delight, but it must therefore be drawn by some delight, because each man's own pleasure draws him: whence, when one delight is withdrawn from it, it is the more easily inclined to another, and because it is empty, it is the more disposed to take hold of something. So much the more, then, does it admit spiritual delight, as it is both without share in earthly consolation and does not desire to be delighted in anything.

Again, as much as we know eternal things, so much do we despise and condemn transitory ones: for this is that precious pearl, and he who finds it willingly forsakes all the things which he had before [0431C] loved. The soul, therefore, burns myrrh when she despises earthly delights; but frankincense, when she desires and seeks the things above. But when with myrrh and frankincense the smoke of all the powder of the perfumer is declared to rise, he does not say "of all the pigment," but "of all the powder of the perfumer." For a pigment [sic: *Pigmen*] is any virtue whatsoever or any work of virtue whatsoever, which is ground into powder when it is subtly examined and discerned, lest anything of evil lie hid in it, or it be done indiscreetly. The perfumer is the man who grinds this powder, that is, who examines his single works; for provision must be made that good things be done without an admixture of evils, because the greater evils slay good things, the lesser stain a man. Manifest evils, therefore, [0431D] must be guarded against. But because we cannot know all things, a general and hidden contrition must be had for these. For sometimes there is some sin in a man which lies hid from him, or which he does not know to be mortal. Let contrition, then, be the first pigment which we make and offer to God, because a general contrition blots out all deadly sins, hidden and manifest, and restores charity, if it has been lost. Whatever we do outside charity will by no means please, nor, however much we grind or crush it, will it be fragrant before God. Therefore, that all our works may be done in charity and that we may grind a powder pleasing to God, let us in the first place be contrite. Then the single works must be examined, alms, vigils, prayers and the other labors of the body: whether they are done purely for God's sake; whether something else is in [0432A] the case, principally or secondarily; whether they are done with discretion and with due measure; whether the end is advancement and perseverance; whether there is the possibility of completing them; whether the time is one at which it is expedient that this be done; whether the manner, by what means and how much and how often, and so forth. Again, when something is to be left off, and when it is to be pressed more vehemently. Again, whether it be done with scandal to others or with loss; whether levity, inconstancy, curiosity drive to it; whether praise is sought, or steals in secretly; whether human delight, whether earthly advantage is to be examined; lastly, whether the virtues are pure, whether they are perfect; whether God is loved above all things; whether anything is set equal to his love or before it; whether love of one's neighbor is had; whether it is shown sincerely for God's sake; whether the friend is loved in God; whether this love is bestowed spiritually and [0432B] not carnally; whether the person is not received; whether the cause is considered.
