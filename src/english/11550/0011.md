## CHAPTER NINE. *On the spiritual assent of the soul, and on discretion and fervor.* (cont.)

For sometimes a man is moved by love, but by a carnal one, or he is led by a private love, so that it is not purely shown forth in God, or a man loves more that which is less to be loved, or the contrary. Again, it must be seen whether humility is true and not feigned or imperfect; whether a man is so cheap in his own eyes within that he does not bear proudly or impatiently an injury or a contempt inflicted from without; whether he so presses down and humbles himself outwardly that he is not proud within of this, but holds a lowly judgment of himself with himself, and truly feels himself within to be such as he shows himself without. Again, whether he is humble there where humility is honored, and shuns humility there where it is held in contempt. Again, whether he practices humility when he ought freely [0432C] to set himself against injustice, and holds to patience when rather, being impatient of evils, he ought to be kindled with the fire of divine zeal, and to fail, not being able to bear that justice be despised and perversity prevail. For we ought not to endure it if any man devours, if any man is lifted up, if any man strikes us on the face. If he is lifted up and grows insolent against justice, or he who strikes us on the face with reviling, so that we are the cause, in our own fault or vice. If, sinning and buffeted, we bear it, we have no reward; but if not sinning, this is a patience pleasing to God. Again, it must be considered whether a man so shows patience outwardly that he does not grieve within, nor think to repay the evil; and likewise whether in the very hour of the injury received he answers words of impatience or of reviling, [0432D] or after that hour keeps a heart of envy or of rancor. Again, a man ought to judge whether he keeps obedience for some advantage of his own, or for gain, or purely for God's sake and for the sake of the precept; whether he obeys in adversities, whether promptly, whether devoutly. Again, he ought to heed whether he rebukes modestly, whether he corrects temperately, and whether in keeping discipline he keeps gentleness, and avoids vehemence, anger, bitterness, indiscretion. And whether he is perhaps more inclined to rigor than to compassion, and whether he shows remission where rigor is to be exercised: in these things the mind is often deceived, and what it does carnally and viciously it believes to be a work of virtue. Zeal too is false if we avenge our own injury rather than the divine, and if against those who are ungrateful to us or less dear [0433A] we are the more inflamed. Again, the bestowing of alms is less acceptable if a man gives late or grudgingly; but it is stained if it is done with pride or with reviling. Again, a man is deceived if under the occasion of sustaining the body he serves delight, or withdraws from himself the sustenance that is necessary. Again, continence is defrauded if a man is chaste in body and in mind, and yet under the appearance of usefulness or of counsel it is suggested to him that he have familiarity with certain women; for to that to which he is led by a pious intention nature also is prompt and easily acquiesces, and in that business vice secretly joins itself, whereby also by use it brings him to this, that a man does not understand himself, and does rather that which delights than that which is expedient. So it happens that sometimes a man is borne to [0433B] this by a carnal appetite rather than a spiritual one; and from the peril of that temptation each man will be so far free as he shall have been able, with a free and unsaddened mind, to withdraw from this familiarity. Vigils and prayers are useless to thee if thou so practice them that thou sleepest and growest torpid at that time at which thou oughtest to pray and to watch; if thou holdest a certain singularity in them, through which thou omittest the common things, and those to which thou art the more bound: to admonish thy neighbor, to console him who grieves, and the other works of charity which please God. And yet it happens that they are not always done purely in God: for sometimes a man is prompt to these things because there is a laboring under sloth, and he is affected with weariness, and it is delightful to him when he is entangled in outward things, or he pleases the persons to whom he speaks, or he is pleased with himself in these things, and glories in such [0433C] favor. Because upon those who lead even a still life torpor or error sometimes steals, so that either through torpor they become useless, or through too much examination of spiritual things they fall into error.

And likewise in this life a fault is committed, if for the sake of stillness the outward care of one's neighbor is utterly abandoned. For to examine all these works of the virtues subtly, and to reconsider whether they are truly good, is to grind all the pigments into powder. And when the man who is a perfumer mixes this powder with myrrh and frankincense, that is, with mortification and with devotion, he composes a precious spice, which, when it has been burned with the fire of charity in the censer of the heart, will give forth from itself upward a smoke of sweet odor. But this smoke ought to rise like a little rod, which is straight and grows on high; [0433D] so directly and not crookedly ought grace to stretch upward, so that it seeks nothing from the side, nothing carnal or earthly. The more it seeks anything earthly, or is delighted in anything, the less does this smoke rise, because it is drawn crookedly and from the side; whence it is deservedly compared to a little rod, that the swiftness of ascending may be signified: for those rods which thicken as they grow are not drawn too high, but those rods which grow more swiftly and upward are slenderer and smaller. This smoke, therefore, of mortification, of devotion and of discretion ought to rise swiftly after the manner of a little rod, that we may not be slothful in doing good things and in the zeal of advancing, but eager, so that we may not thicken through slowness and idleness, but may grow slender and lofty. And through the fervor of the mind [0434A] let us stretch upward in haste; for in haste must the ascent be made, and manfully must we press on, because if a man tries slackly to ascend upward, he is pressed down by the weight of the body and of human weakness. For the body that is corrupted weighs down the soul, and unless she advances, she must needs fail, because nature draws downward, and always seeks the lowest things. For we see in heavy men that if they wish to climb a tree and to creep upward, whenever they go about the climbing more slackly, they are at once weighed down and fail; but swiftness of climbing and persistence overcomes the heaviness, and carries them through. In this manner those who eagerly and carefully stretch toward what is perfect, and fulfill the commandments, attain the summit of perfection; but the slack fail, and daily become more inclined to a fall. The bride, then, is compared to a little rod [0434B] because of the purity of her intention and the swiftness of her advancing; but because of all the foregoing it is deservedly said of her: Who is she, that is, how much to be marveled at, how worthy of praise, etc.
