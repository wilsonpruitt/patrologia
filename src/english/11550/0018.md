## CHAPTER XIV. *On interior and exterior humility, and on its threefold distinction.*

[0447D]

*Behold thou art fair, my love, behold thou art fair [n: (Cant. IV)]* [var: Vulg. Cant. 4:1 *Quam pulchra es, amica mea, quam pulchra es*, "How fair art thou, my love, how fair art thou"; the wording printed here is that of Cant. 1:15, whose second clause also closes with *es*]. Christ commends in the perfect soul a twofold beauty, namely that she is fair both without through holy manner of life, and within through a simple intention and humility of mind. And so this exterior beauty of the soul is when she lives temperately in all things, when she uses slender food and clothing, when she guards against all things superfluous or blameworthy, and practises all things just and honourable. Again, these things adorn her, when she is composed in work and equally in speech, when she shows herself companionable and lovable toward all, hurting none, circumventing none, having compassion [0448A] on all and succouring all according to her power. And again, when she is prudent, chaste, and mild, and meek, and adorned in all things with the other good qualities, the soul rightly has beauty outwardly when she has honourableness in habit, and gravity in bearing, cheerfulness in countenance, ears that do not itch, eyes not lofty or curious, a tongue instructed, and tempered from light or useless words, and bringing forth things good and wholesome. It also beautifies her when she is not disturbed by anger, or by impatience or rancour, when she does not contend, does not detract, does not judge, nor lends an ear to those who deal in such things, when she converses with all not only without complaint, but even with grace. She is likewise fair, when she is fervent in good work, in God's service, in the occupation of brotherly charity, strenuous, fervent and discreet in all things [0448B] to be done. There is another, interior beauty, when in all things that she does praiseworthily she has a simple eye, that is, intention, neither affecting nor caring to be praised by men, but seeking the praise which is from God alone and desiring only the things above. And so, although she be fair without through works, she is more adorned, and pleases within to the divine gaze, through purity of intention and humility, and through that sense of mind whereby she both does great things and always grows cheap in her own sight. Which cheapness of mind is the more precious, the more it proceeds from the fount of love and the root of fervor. For to one who loves and is devout, what he has never suffices, nor does he reckon great what he has done, because whoever always pants with desire after greater things judges himself needy.

[0448C]There is therefore a humility that is devout, one that is voluntary, one that is constrained. It is constrained when a man, calling back before his eyes his sins, his infirmity and his imperfection, makes his mind, as it were unwilling and holding its own good things rather before its eyes, to fall dumb and to grow cheap in its own sight. In these things, even if it is constrained, yet it is brought to completion, so that the mind attains it in some measure. If, however, through negligence the mastery of constraining should cease, it will at once slip away from it, and will be led back to its former pride without remission. But in some it cannot even be constrained, the mind always stretching upward to high and accustomed things, nor is there anything that can press down and subject the mind through the lowest things of humility. For to the proud humility is high; and if at some time they attempt to climb up to it, they at once slip back. [0448D] To which they therefore do not climb up, because they stretch toward it with a weak desire. For they will and will not, as Solomon says of the slothful man: *The slothful man willeth and willeth not [n: (Prov. XIII)]*. Humility is voluntary when from the leading of reason a man sees how infirm he is and how much a sinner. And that, according to Job, there is no help for him in himself; and the will consents to this reason, and loves this judgment, and chooses to be despised, because it knows that so to know oneself to be is so to be profited. And so willingly he humbles himself, because, being enlightened through grace, he now loves humility as also the other good things. But this humility is not constrained; because, time being now shaken off and the passions of the vices weakened, the soul comes to this liberty of spirit, that the good delights her and is savoury to her.

[0449A]There is likewise another, more sublime humility, which is not constrained, not drawn by the leading of reason, nor only voluntary, but fervent and devout: this the perfect soul has, who is the more humble the more devout she has been, and as much as she grows in spirit or in knowledge, so much does she advance in humility. For true grace does not exalt, but humbles; because it shows a man more fully to himself, and, understanding its sublimity, he sees how scanty is that which he has been able to grasp of it. As much as anyone eats of spiritual banquets, so much also does he hunger: which, because they can never be comprehended to the full, never suffice for one who desires: whence they always humble her, because her delights always abound less than they are desired. And so grace tasted humbles such a soul, and equally the examples of the good [0449B] considered, the consideration of her pilgrimage and of the manifold misery of humanity, the love of the felicity above and the desire of liberty, namely because she will be delivered from the servitude of corruption into the liberty of the glory of the sons of God. Such a soul, purged of earthly delights, knows how to desire only spiritual things, and passes over wholly into the love of them and into the affection of the virtues; the love of Christ humbles her, who was humbled for her; the love of that virtue itself humbles her, which she therefore embraces because Christ especially loved and had it; whence it is also singularly called the virtue of Christ: all humble things please her, because she understands them to be sublime. For she knows that as soon as she is humbled she is exalted within, because she is made like to Christ, who was the least, that is, the most humble, [0449C] in the kingdom of heaven, that is, in the present Church, although he was great and the Son of the Most High. The Spirit of Christ, who kindles in her love of him, instructs her also unto his humility, whereby, as much as she is full, so much does she choose to be abject, and however blessed she is, so poor is she in spirit. For neither can she who has the Spirit of Christ not love what Christ loves and has. And so great is the agreement of the love of Christ and of humility, that whoever has had the one of these is proved necessarily to have the other. And as much as he has had the one, so much may he have the other; so that if thou truly lovest, thou art truly humble; and if thou art less humble, thou art convicted of loving less. The perfect soul therefore, because she loves, is humbled, and on account of this humility, which proceeds from the root of love, she is deservedly called beloved and [0449D] fair. Whose twofold beauty is commended, namely of exterior work and of interior humility. And deservedly is he praised who both does great things and thinks humbly of himself; who is not high-minded, but consents to lowly things, and in all things bears a humble mind.
