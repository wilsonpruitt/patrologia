## CHAPTER XVII. *On the discretion of thoughts, and how one comes to it.*

[0455C]

*Thy teeth are as flocks of the shorn, which have come up from the washing [n: (Cant. IV)]*. The teeth are the examinations of discretion, by which spiritual meditations and the thoughts of the heart are sifted and broken small, and are discerned, whether they be pure and truly good, whether they be from God, or from an evil spirit, or from the human heart. For the evil spirit knows how to suggest certain thoughts which seem spiritual, and the human spirit at times feigns and lies that it is the Holy Spirit, as is read in Job: *Behold, my belly is as new wine [n: (Job XXXII)]*. Which happened also to Elias himself [cj: *Eliae*; read *Eliu*, "Elihu"], who brought forth words through the swelling of his own spirit which he believed himself to speak from the Holy Spirit and from zeal for God. For by the belly is understood the bosom of the heart, by the new wine the fervor of the Holy [0455D] Spirit, by which Eliu in Job supposed himself to be stirred, when yet he was moved by the impulse of his own heart alone. In many such things a man follows the motion of the heart, or even the impulse of a vice, and yet judges himself to be moved by the Holy Spirit. The demons too at times hide evils under the appearance of good, they stir men up to certain good things, and, that they may lead them through to evil, they even announce things to come. For certain things to come they are able to foretell, because at times they know them; certain things they search out through the subtlety of their nature; certain things they learn from the holy angels revealing them; certain things they conjecture from the outcomes of causes, certain from the natural things of man, certain from the disposition of the air. And for a long while they declare some things as they are, that at last they may deceive the credulous [0456A] mind. They deceive also by false devotion, because they produce in the soul certain prayers and meditations, a sweet affection, even tears, that they may lead either the mind into error, or into elation, or the body into weakness. At times they suggest, by a false interpretation, that something of Scripture is to be understood otherwise, or that from it something is to be done otherwise, than the truth holds; they make some men ill solicitous about the salvation of others, whom they stir up and kindle to the conversion or the edification of others set far off, so far as to cast them down from the stillness of the mind and from their own profit. Which business, painting it in the soul as a kind of day, they insinuate as more healthful and more useful than their former occupation.

When therefore something is suggested to us to be done, we ought to weigh whether any indiscretion, [0456B] in some part of itself, mingle itself in, or the guile of the enemy thrust itself forward; and fully to examine it: whether it be done with due fear and caution; whether not so much our own profit as also that of others consist in it; whether human praise or ostentation creep in; whether any vanity or lightness impel; whether it dissent in nothing from Scripture or from the examples of the saints. But whatever is from an evil spirit will be able to be caught out as false in some part, or its peril hangs upon the future and will quickly be laid bare; because the enemy, in uncovering his own fraud, will suggest more openly what he had hidden, whereby he will be known. The Holy Spirit also works in the soul in divers manners, whence his operations must be known and discerned. For at times he converts the mind by fear of punishment, at times he ascends to the love of God, nor yet does he afford much [0456C] knowledge. He moves the mind, and stirs it up to the good and to God, nor yet does he at once make the man perfect. For in these there is not full knowledge, but a beginning and an incitement to the good, or a consolation; because these things one who is weak and imperfect sometimes perceives, and one who attains less to knowledge and to spiritual understanding. But when he shall have made progress in knowledge, and thereafter ascends to the degree of contemplation, if he perceives something of God, and then seems to himself perfectly to comprehend it, he does not understand it as it is in God; whence Elias, who saw the Lord pass by, because he understood him to be incomprehensible, says that the Lord is not present in spirit, and in commotion, and in fire, but in the whistling of a light air; because whatever is able to be perfectly comprehended is not there, but rather there something subtle [0456D] is perceived of God, and that which could not be fully comprehended. To one still beginning and weak grace is given, that being enlightened by it he may know himself, and being strengthened may be able to correct himself, and so may come more fully to acquaintance. For acquaintance of this kind is not attained unless, the manners being bettered, a greater purity of heart be obtained. The more perfectly each man shall have cleansed the eyes of the heart, the more fully will he be able to see God; and the more fully he sees him, the more does he understand him whom he sees to be incomprehensible. Whence he who has made progress in acquaintance perceives perfectly concerning God, and understands whatever he can grasp of him to be a small thing. For the nearer he approaches to wisdom and to acquaintance, the less does that seem to him to be which he can grasp; because, according to Solomon, the more [0457A] a man is made wise, the more does wisdom itself withdraw from him [n: (Eccle. VII)], that is, he understands only this much, how incomprehensible it is. Such knowledge does not press upon the soul, but kindles and humbles her, and shows her own blindness to her more fully. In this each man can know that he savors soundly, and that his eye then sees God, if he himself reproves himself out of humility, and hungers the more for the wisdom he has perceived, and understands that what he has grasped of it, or can grasp, is the least thing. Between the Holy Spirit therefore and his own a man ought carefully to distinguish, and to examine himself, and, when he is moved to some good work, whether some human delight, or a vice, or a temptation impel him to the carrying out of it. For there are in the soul certain impulses from the disposition of the man, or from some cause, [0457B] or occasion, or curiosity, or ambition, or lightness, or vice, or temptation. Those which are from vice or from temptation must be altogether guarded against. Those which are from our humanity and from the rest before named we can tolerate, and use well, namely that we never follow them, but suffer them at our side, for ministry and not for harm, that is to say, that from them we may have a greater eagerness for carrying out that work which we intend to do. For where natural things work together with us without another cause, our spirit is the readier in these, and more eagerly carries out what it intends.

For it must be seen, and altogether guarded against, that no carnal or human delight impel us principally; nor let us follow such an impulse, even if we suffer it, [0457C] but where we know the impulse of the Holy Spirit to be, there let us walk, and let us do what he suggests. Likewise it must be considered that in a soul still weak and imperfect the carnal motions are more prone than the spiritual. Whence when anyone is moved easily to some good, and with a certain lightness of heart, it is to be feared that lightness of this kind comes from carnality, especially if something be annexed there which might be able to delight [sic: *possis*] his humanity. Likewise the eagerness of the heart is suspect, when a man is impelled to something with a certain vehemence and impatience of delay; because the Holy Spirit is modest and tranquil, and his motions are tempered. Lightness of heart, natural kindliness [sic: *benegnitas*], prosperity, or some arisen cause of gladness also at times works together with devotion, and mounts it more fully. [0457D] And likewise a sadder disposition, or some adversity, and a cause of sorrow, presses it down and even hinders it. Whence in prayer some simple man is at times falsely consoled, or distrusts, while according as he feels the eagerness of his spirit he conjectures the signs of being heard, prophesying out of his own heart, and judging that what his own heart speaks the Lord speaks. The enemy also knows how to deceive by false consolations, or by false revelations, him whom he catches credulous in such things. Whence the discretion of these spirits must be known; all which things, while the devout soul judges and examines them, she grinds and breaks small as it were with certain teeth of discretion. These are the teeth of the bride, which are likened to flocks of shorn sheep; the shearings are harmless thoughts, cutting away outward things, [0458A] namely the love of earthly things, and the desire of having the prudence of the world. For the love of earthly things blinds the eyes of the heart, weighs down and presses down the mind, that it may not be able to rise up to the knowledge and discretion of spiritual things. Worldly wisdom also does not grasp God, nor does the animal man perceive the things that are of the Spirit of God. For God has made foolish the wisdom of this world, so that the more a man savors earthly things, the less he savors God; and the more widely the heart is stretched out, the more is it straitened in the love of divine things. For thus the light of outward things and of carnality so beclouds a man that he apprehends the less the purity of discerning spiritual things; and the more he is scattered abroad to many things, the less does he gather himself to the one thing and most necessary. Inwardly also something at times clings [0458B] to the soul whereby the discretion of spiritual things is greatly hindered, namely her own will. The more this seeks its own, the less is it joined to God; and the more it abides in itself, and does not ascend to God, the less does it apprehend the things that are of God. Likewise he who loves his own will is not able to perceive discretion; because, while he follows what delights and not what the truth judges, as he withdraws from the truth, so is he separated from true knowledge, and thus from discretion. This is the fleece which must be cut off, namely at the end of delight, the weight by which, while the soul is warmed and burdened, she is hindered from spiritual things. So the shorn sheep (namely these harmless thoughts) more freely grasp and discern spiritual things, and write wisdom in the emptiness of these. These thoughts come up from the washing, [0458C] that is, from compunction and the tears of penitence. In this washing they are cleansed from the filth alike of sins and of vices, and are unburdened of their weight, that they may be able to come up, and to climb to the acquaintance with spiritual things. For compunction fully consumes sins and vices, and cleanses the soul from all their filth, and at the same time from the dregs of carnality; it opens also the pores of the heart, and pours forth the rains of the virtues, by which she may be able to fly to these things and to be lifted up. The soul, suffused with these, rises as at daybreak to discern her single thoughts, and to see whether any has strayed, or has been impure, or has worked uselessly. So discretion, while after innocence and the casting off of worldly things, and the mortification of her own will, she is cleansed through the grace of compunction, and [0458D] is instructed unto the understanding and examination of spiritual things, is deservedly declared to come up from the washing.
