## CHAPTER XVIII. *On the discretion of natural things, and of things of grace, and on the divers motions of the soul.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *They are all with twin offspring, and there is not a barren one among them [n: (Cant. IV)]*. ⟦s2⟧ Great is the virtue of discretion, by which a man searches out in what part he is prone to evil, or disposed to good, and how much the goods of nature and the gifts of grace can support him; he discerns virtues and vices, the motions of the heart also, the causes arising from without or from within, works more perfect or imperfect, the consolation of grace and the knowledge of these things; in short, he is eager to know his inward and outward [0459A] state. ⟦s3⟧ For discretion, applied to the word of God, is more piercing than any two-edged sword, reaching unto the division of the soul and the spirit, of the joints also and of the marrow, and a discerner of the thoughts and intentions of the heart: this shows a man how there is a twofold affection in him, namely a carnal and a spiritual; and that, so long as the carnal is not yet tamed, the soul cannot perfectly be delighted in spiritual things; and although she exult in the Lord, yet the flesh cannot exult, according to what the Prophet says: *My heart and my flesh have exulted in the living God [n: (Psal. LXXXIII)]*. ⟦s4⟧ So long as a man is so diverse and divided, he does not fully rise up to spiritual joy, nor to the carrying out of any laborious work. ⟦s5⟧ For one still imperfect and carnal is without strength for [0459B] any laborious work, for the doing of which he has nothing of his own, that is, in which no will of the heart draws him, nor delight of the flesh, nor any human cause or occasion, with which he very greatly prevails to do spiritual things for which, out of grace alone, he would be less ready. ⟦s6⟧ All the works and desires of such a soul are imperfect, because partly she is borne by the impulse of the flesh, partly she is moved spiritually. ⟦s7⟧ Of such a one it is to be suspected that in every work in which he is found frequent or ready, or in which he perseveres for a long time, grace alone does not help him, but along with it some human delight. ⟦s8⟧ But if the motion of venial sins impelled him more frequently than that of reason or of charity, unless he blotted them out through penitence, [0459C] it would be to be feared that he would quickly fall into a mortal one, and be buried under the sand of such things. ⟦s9⟧ Yet so long as a man is not so blinded that he turns aside from charity, what he does will not be fruitless, because whatever is done out of charity does not lose its merit. ⟦s10⟧ If finally the enemy stir up one established in charity to some good work, that he may turn him away from a more useful one, or in some way deceive him, even this will be meritorious to the man who does not know it and who intends it well, because it is done out of charity. ⟦s11⟧ For so far do the camps of the devil serve God as a demon, with evil intention, stirs a man up to some good. ⟦s12⟧ For the enemy does not always assail openly, but at times under the appearance of good joins himself to a man that he may deceive him. ⟦s13⟧ The very nature also of man and his disposition is, in some good work, contrary [0459D] and without strength; in another so ready that its eagerness could seem to be devotion: for natural things are exceedingly powerful in man, so that in an imperfect man there proceed from them more frequently the motions of thought, of speech and of work, and likewise of joy or of sadness, of eagerness of heart or of weariness, and of the other affections of the soul. ⟦s14⟧ Even in the most devout man, and in one who more perfectly rules all his motions inward and outward, yet the things he does are formed according to his own disposition, and these natural things help or hinder.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ For as is commonly said, that which has been in some vessel savors of the vessel; whence it happens that an irascible man, even though he be moved by a good zeal, natural bitterness mingles itself in. ⟦s2⟧ If anyone shall have been light or slack more than he ought, the one will pursue rigor strictly, the other [0460A] clemency loosely. ⟦s3⟧ Likewise a man sad or rigid from natural disposition shows less kindness; one benevolent and pleasant, less of the rigor of discipline. ⟦s4⟧ In these things and the like we must keep ourselves from her who sleeps in our bosom, namely human frailty, so that even the most devout man be wary for himself against those motions by which he feels his nature disposed and prone to evil. ⟦s5⟧ All these things discretion knows and judges, and in what manner it must deal with them that they may become meritorious. ⟦s6⟧ It understands also in what manner the nature of man, or another cause, helps or hinders the works of the virtues, namely of humility, patience, obedience, meekness, charity, and all the rest. ⟦s7⟧ And that a man merits not according as he is ready or slow in any of these, but according [0460B] as he is rooted in the virtues and has an intense charity. ⟦s8⟧ For at times, according to the natural things of a man or another cause, one has a greater charity while having less eagerness in some work of virtue, and one having less charity has greater eagerness. ⟦s9⟧ For the virtues, since all, are equal, cannot all be exercised equally, because according to natural disposition one man is more prone to exercise this one than that; which yet are all equal, because they proceed from the same root. ⟦s10⟧ If therefore anyone be more prone and more disposed to compassion than to patience, yet patience is as perfect in him as compassion, because both virtues proceed from the same charity. ⟦s11⟧ For out of as great a charity as he has compassion, out of [0460C] so great is he patient; because from an equal charity, and by both virtues, he merits equally, because an equal charity obtains an equal reward. ⟦s12⟧ And although a man exercise this one more laboriously than that, if his motion is as much intended in carrying out this as in that, he merits as much; nay rather, he who strives and labors merits more, because here the battle is greater, and in him charity is the more increased. ⟦s13⟧ A like judgment holds concerning devotion; because, if two be in equal charity, and the one have the grace of exercising himself in devotion, while the other labors under temptation, or wages war against some weakness or imperfection, this latter merits as much by the motions and the striving of the battle as the former by devotion and eagerness of mind; nay, he will be able to merit more if he has striven [0460D] lawfully. ⟦s14⟧ Paul declares, in these words in which he says, that a man is able to merit most in the labor of temptation, and that in it charity is increased: When I am weak, then am I stronger and powerful [n: (II Cor. XVIII)] [var: Vulg. *cum infirmor, tunc potens sum*, "when I am weak, then am I powerful"]. ⟦s15⟧ For vehement weariness and bitterness of heart exercises the virtues, it does not overthrow them. ⟦s16⟧ Whether therefore a man labor under sloth, and dryness of heart, and some temptation, or whether he enjoy devotion to the full, he can merit as much in that adverse state as in this prosperous one, if his motion and striving be as much intended in the struggling.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But it happens that a man is moved more in that virtue to which he is better disposed, and so merits more; whence for the weak the state of devotion is for the most part more fruitful, but it belongs to the robust and the constant [0461A] to merit more fully in the adverse state. ⟦s2⟧ Yet it happens that the weak man is made equal in the spiritual battle to the strong; because the more without strength he is than he, the more he labors in resisting; whence what the one acquires by virtue, this the other acquires by a greater weariness. ⟦s3⟧ But if rather, because both the weak and the strong slip more frequently into venial sins, because he labors alike in resisting, it happens the more that to the punishment owed for venial sins there answers the punishment of resisting, and that punishment blots out punishment. ⟦s4⟧ Nevertheless in him who commits fewer venial sins charity often grows the more. ⟦s5⟧ Discretion understands all these things, and knows how to use well the adverse state as well as the prosperous. ⟦s6⟧ In this manner therefore it makes a twin fruit, while it moderates both states, and profits from it. ⟦s7⟧ And likewise, while it both guards against the evil of indiscretion and prudently [0461B] does the good. ⟦s8⟧ For if anyone intend well and act less prudently, he offends; because, itself [cj: *se*; read *si*, "if"] thou offer rightly, and divide not rightly, thou hast sinned, says the authority; likewise it makes a twin fruit, while it both rules the thoughts within and prudently disposes the work without. ⟦s9⟧ Not only does it make this twofold fruit in itself, it makes it also in others, whom it amends as much by the admonition of prudence as by the example of working rightly. ⟦s10⟧ Rightly therefore are these goats (namely discreet examinations) said to have a twin fruit, which both in themselves and in others bear fruit and beget spiritual offspring, and there is not a barren one among them. ⟦s11⟧ For no labor of examining these things, no motion of pious intention, nothing finally that is done by charity will be able to be barren, but it is fruitful and meritorious.
