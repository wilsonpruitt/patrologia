## CHAPTER XIX. *On the keeping of speech, and on sweetness.*

[0461C]

*Thy lips are as a scarlet fillet, and thy speech sweet [n: (Cant. IV)]*. The lips of the bride are compared to a fillet, because as a fillet binds together the hair, lest it flow down loosely, so the devout soul binds and restrains her lips, lest they speak evil or useless things, and flow down and be dissolved unto her own ruin or that of others. And this fillet is called scarlet because by scarlet is understood charity, wherewith these lips are bound. For through the love of God and of the neighbor words are contained and repressed, namely that nothing be brought forth which either offends God or harms the neighbor. This *dilectio* may be called *Dei lectio,* a reading of God, or *Dei electio,* a choosing of God. For she reads God in the school [0461D] of the heart; there she learns to know God, to savor God. She also chooses God in love, and God has chosen her, and has chosen her beforehand; whence also before others she reads in him, and learns what she thinks, speaks and does. He who is one Spirit with God, as he does not perceive the things that are foreign to him, so neither does he speak them. He who is allied to goodness is not disjoined from it through hurtful things. He who cleaves to truth is turned away from vanity, because truth and vanity do not dwell together in one dwelling; and whoever has been devout does not flow away through useless words. The lips therefore of the bride, because they compress words lest they flow loosely, are compared to life [cj: *vitae*; read *vittae*, "a fillet"]. But because they are dyed through the love of God, they are likened to scarlet. Fillets are wont, while they bind the hair, also to adorn the head, and to show that very adornment in minds. [0462A] So the keeping and discipline of speaking adorns the mind, which is the head and the chief part of a man, and outwardly shows the soul beautiful. For words loose and ineptly brought forth deform outwardly, and inwardly scatter the rectitude of the mind. For with as many superfluous words as a man uses, in so many ways is he led outside himself, and scattered from his own keeping. But where words shall have been composed and brought forth with discipline, they give testimony of constancy of mind, of prudence and of discretion. The lips therefore of the bride are compared not to any fillet whatever, but to a scarlet one, which is more precious; because so great a discipline of speaking is the more sublime and the more worthy in her, inasmuch as it is not had from human wisdom, or from natural disposition only, but from divine love. But one man is naturally of a good disposition [0462B] and of modest speech, another moderating his words out of natural prudence, according to what Solomon says: *He that moderates his lips is most prudent [n: (Prov. X)]*; but that which is had from grace and charity is more pleasing, and edifies. Whence it is added that the speech of such a soul is sweet. Deservedly is that speech sweet which proceeds from grace and from divine love; whence, because it arises from such a root, it delights outwardly and edifies. What is brought forth out of a dry and empty heart, because it does not pour out of a full one, does not edify or refresh. He who is needy in himself cannot enrich others, nor pour out again that with which he has not been poured in. For he who brings forth words out of a full heart, and fulfills in work what he says, his words have effect, they have savor and are sweet. Deservedly therefore is the speech of a devout soul sweet [0462C] and delights, is pleasing and edifies, because it proceeds from charity and flows forth from the fountain of grace. For she tastes that the Lord is sweet: whence she pours out again the sweetness which she experiences, and belches forth the fullness which she enjoys; for because the heart is filled with spiritual delights, it belches forth outwardly a good word of sweetness, of consolation, of instruction, of admonition; the storehouses of the heart are full of these riches, belching forth from this gift of grace into that, from this spiritual meditation into that, from one fullness into another. Out of the abundance therefore of the heart she has refreshment, out of the taste sweetness. Likewise she has sweet words, because her conscience has been cleansed from the bitterness of vices and from the dregs of carnality. Whence they do not savor of rancor, not [0462D] of indignation, not of anger, not of envy, not of detraction, nor finally of anything that is hurtful, or dishonorable, or of a vice or an imperfection lying hidden in the soul, whence she might bring forth judgment. The words of such a soul have kindness or charity, meekness, patience, humility. In the bitterness of an injury received they keep sweetness, because the soul retains charity. Nor is she only patient, but also kind, so that she grieves more over him who wounds her than over her own wounding, that she may grieve together with him. She is more prone to pardon than to anger, to compassion than to indignation. For as is read in Ecclesiasticus: *In the commandments of God is wasting and death [n: (Eccli. XXVIII)]* [var: Vulg. *tabitudo enim et mors imminent in mandatis ejus*, "for wasting and death threaten in his commandments"], that is, compassion, whereby a man wastes away through compassion over the sins of his neighbors, [0463A] and death, that is, the mortification of vices; because he has mortified these in himself, when provoked he knows not only how to pardon, but even how to suffer together with the other. For while he knows his own misery, and himself seeks mercy from God, that he may be pardoned, he has mercy, that God may have mercy on him: *Remember the testament of the Most High,* says Ecclesiasticus, *and despise the ignorance of thy neighbor [n: (ibid.)]*. The testament of the Most High is, that is, the covenant, that if we forgive, it be forgiven us. He who wills that it be forgiven him is mindful of this testament, and forgives that it may be forgiven him; he also despises the ignorance of his neighbor, that is, he contemns and does not care for the folly of the man who reviles or wounds him. For he is foolish who wounds and does not know what he does, because he harms himself, and renders a service to him whom he wounds. For the fool serves the wise man (namely unto salvation) [0463B] while he afflicts him; and he who does injury holds also the place of a sick man, while he is unable to restrain his own motions; but the patient and long-suffering man bears the manner of one strong and unharmed, while he is not overcome by evil, but overcomes evil in good; he would be overcome if he succumbed through impatience, but he overcomes while he bravely bears; nor does he envy, nor does he answer a banquet [cj: *convivium*; read *convicium*, "a reproach"], nor does he in any way harm the man who has reviled him; therefore he is not overcome by evil, lest he be evil together with him; nor does he answer the fool according to his folly, lest he be made like to him; but by a soft answer he breaks the anger of the man stirred up, and soothes the viper. Such a soul knows how to excuse before herself the man stirred up, and to ascribe what he has said rather to weakness or imperfection than to malice, and to reckon this with herself, that after a little while it will repent him of having so gone beyond bounds in words. [0463C] For she does not attend to the present state, and to the things which he did when stirred up, but rather to his former tranquillity, or even friendship, and that after a little while the man will return to himself. Not only therefore does she set a keeping to her mouth when the sinner stands against her, but at all times she keeps the ways of her words, that she may not offend in her tongue. Because if the vessel of the mouth have not a lid (of silence and of keeping), it will be unclean. And with as many superfluous words as she shall have contracted stains, so much will she dry up of the sweetness of grace. Through the keeping of the mouth therefore she preserves the cleanness of the heart, and out of the cleanness of the heart she forms her words outwardly. Christ therefore commends this keeping in the bride, and compares it to scarlet, because she is informed by charity, and knows how to speak for God's sake and not for human [0463D] praise or seemliness; therefore he proclaims her sweetness, because it does not proceed from flattery, or from a feigned kindness of words, but from grace and purity of heart.
