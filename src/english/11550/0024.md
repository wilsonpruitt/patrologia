## CHAPTER XX. *On the beauty of the penitent soul, and on humility, and on her hidden merits.*

*Thy cheeks are as a piece of a pomegranate, besides that which lies hidden within [n: (Can. IV)]*. In the cheeks, which he says are as a piece of a pomegranate, shame is designated; because, while we blush, we are red in the cheeks. The pomegranate is red without, and white within, and is full of grains, whence also it is called a granate apple; to which are likened the cheeks of the devout soul, which is red without, that is, blushes at the memory [0464A] and recollection of past sins, at the daily and shamefaced consideration of shameful thoughts or of temptations, at her weakness also and imperfection: these very things she sees unceasingly, that God may not see them, to whom every heart lies open; and she judges all things also, that God may not judge them. For what she shall have had before herself will not be before God, and what she judges, God will not judge, who does not judge twice for the same thing; if only she shall have judged sufficiently her sins and all her reprehensible things, and shall have had before herself the things which displease God and could be objected against her by him, and paints for herself an image of these, and is red for them, that is, blushes; she is displeasing to herself that she may be pleasing to him, she becomes worthless to herself, and appears base in her own eyes; but as much as she blushes for herself, as much as she seems base to herself, so much is she made beautiful before God. For she sets apart [0464B] the useless from the useful, she winnows the chaff and separates it from the grains, and burns these with the fire of confession and of penitence; the grains she hides within by this humility, by this redness of confession and of confusion, and from her sins she is made clean, and comely before God. This redness therefore both consumes sins and adorns the face. This rind of humility guards and preserves good things. For humility is the keeper of the virtues. This redness of the cheeks adorns the soul, kindles God to love of her, provokes him to look upon her. For the heavenly Bridegroom looks upon the shamefacedness and humility of the devout soul; a lowered countenance pleases him, not an upraised one, eyes not lofty but cast down; and so far does he love and gaze upon this redness, that he does not see the redness of sin wherewith she was red like a little worm, but wholly hands it over to oblivion. [0464C] Prudently therefore the bride covers and wipes away this redness with the other, and being made judge of herself for God's sake she exchanges redness for redness, and offers to the divine gaze what is pleasing, and hides what is hurtful. For the lover of men gratefully receives this exchange, who wills not the death of sinners, but their conversion.

Well therefore does the soul divide with God, leaving indeed her own good things to the divine judgment, but herself reproving and judging her evil ones, converting the hurtful redness and the healthful one, and blushing through penitence, she who was red through fault, so that thus she who was defiled may also be beautiful, and there may be comeliness in place of baseness, a sweet odor in place of stench. Whence these cheeks, that is the redness, are compared to a granate apple, because it has redness without, and within [0464D] the whiteness of the grains. But it is not said to be like a whole granate apple, but a piece, which partly hides the grains, partly uncovers them: for although good men hide their virtues, that they may be known only to the Lord, yet sometimes they are in part uncovered, and become known to men. For no devout man can so lie hidden but that sometimes, from some work of virtue, it is caught what he is. Likewise where the granate apple is broken, there the grains are seen, which are not seen where it remains whole; if the rind be opened, even there the whiteness and virtue of the others lying hidden is found. So in a perfect soul the virtues lie hidden, but while she is beaten with adversities, it is uncovered. She is known to have patience, while she bears an injury, and does not envy, nor is overcome by anger; humility, while she bears contempt with an even mind, [0465A] and is not caught striving after exaltation; obedience, by the fulfilling of it in adversities; charity, by the love of enemies; love of God, by the observance of the commandments; compassion for the neighbor, by succor in his need. So by works is perfection proved. And according to Job the bones which had been covered are laid bare [n: (Job XXXIII)], that is, the virtues which had lain hidden are made manifest. The soul has also the redness of the pomegranate in her cheeks, when for Christ's sake she does not flee the blushing of reproach, of poverty, of contumelies, of afflictions and of labors. For in these she is pleased with herself for Christ's sake, and is willingly suffused with this redness for love of him, who in his passion was made red with his own precious blood poured out for her. So after the likeness of the pomegranate good men are red without, but within are white with divers [0465B] gifts of graces. But their affliction is compared not to a whole apple, because they have not borne things like to the passion of Christ, nor were they full of grace as he was, but out of his fullness all have received. For Christ in his suffering can be likened to a pomegranate which is full and whole, because he was full of grace, which was not given to him by measure. And he was wholly red in his punishment, because he bore for us not small things, nor a part of sorrows or of labors, but things so great and so grievous that never was there any sorrow like his sorrow. It was more bitter, and exceeded the sorrows of all men which they were to suffer from the beginning of the world unto its end. But it behooved his sorrow to be the more bitter, that he might bear the sorrows of all, [0465C] because he was a victim sufficient for all, but not [sic: *efficians*]. For he did not make all safe, which yet rests upon those very men who are not saved. For from the hour of his birth he labored under want, flight, persecutions, hunger, thirst, many labors and adversities. In his heart also he suffered, because he was incomparably afflicted by this, that he knew the bitterness of his passion before he undergoes it, and knew most certainly that he was to suffer it. In his passion also he was tormented not in a part of the body, but in the whole body. For not only were his limbs transfixed with nails, but the body itself also bore stretchings, punishment and scourges; and the head, besides the blows and the veiling of the eyes, bore also the prickings and wounds of thorns. Because therefore Christ suffered so grievously and in so manifold a way, [0465D] therefore every affliction of the elect, which they have borne for Christ or after Christ's example, can be compared only to a piece; because in respect of those things which he bore, the things which they were able to suffer will be small, and only a slight part of them. Whence while they consider and know these things, they esteem lightly all that they can suffer; and while they think upon his loftiness, and the greatness of the things he bore, and that he suffered this, as blessed Job speaks, without the iniquity of his own hand [n: (Job XVI)], and that he was crushed for our wickednesses, and by his bruise we are healed [n: (Isai. LIII)], they always make little of what they themselves can do. It delights them to bear adversities for him; they are kindled to greater things out of love of him, and always remain humble in themselves; without they are red with labors and afflictions, [0466A] within they are white with virtues; they suffer evils, they do good, and they become worthless to themselves. To a piece of a pomegranate are the cheeks of the devout soul compared. And besides these she has within divers merits and gifts of the Spirit, the perfection of the love of God, and the desire of dying for him.

Which if it is not granted in the body, she makes for herself a martyrdom in the mind. She mortifies carnal appetites, her own will, the vices of the heart, depraved manners; she wrestles with her own flesh, and with the enemy unceasingly assailing it. The martyrdom of abstinence also she has invisibly in the mind, while she represses the earthly delights of the heart, and the motions of sensuality, while she refuses all consolations which delight carnally. She suffers spiritually, while she is fried in the frying-pan of the mind with zeal for souls, while she is tormented over [0466B] the contempt of justice, over the prevailing iniquity of evil men. The martyrdom of passion also she obtains, while she is afflicted for those who sin, while she suffers together with the sick, while she condescends to those laboring under any necessity whatever. Likewise it lies hidden, nor does it appear without, how all her glory is from within, and that she is the daughter of the heavenly King; in what manner she serves him, not with penal fear, but with filial love; that she does the justifications of God, not only for the sake of retribution, but willingly sacrifices to God and confesses, that is, praises him because he is good, loving the good for the good's sake, and is delighted with the good itself and with the good pleasure of God. Finally she glories in God only, not in the world, nor in any thing, without outward or earthly advantage. Likewise she has the testimony of a good conscience: nothing that gnaws at her conscience, [0466C] nothing that she believes, in doing, to be against God, nothing intending otherwise than according to God. In manifold ways also she merits secretly in the heart; because, with God alone as witness, she intends or does many things inwardly. For she merits by the motions of desires, by the contemplation of things above, by compunction of heart, by delight in the love of God, by compassion for the neighbor and by prayer, by the will of accomplishing something which she is not able to do; because before God she nevertheless does as much as she intends; rightly out of as great a charity as she intends, so much does she merit, because she is judged to have done so much. She has also hidden merits in the heart, which although they lie hidden are known by God; because, with him alone as witness and privy to them, many things which are merits are done in secret. [0466D] How many are the ways in which she secretly humbles herself for God's sake in heart or in work, or, being humbled by others, willingly bears it; and many times also, being injured, keeps silence, when she could reasonably answer, lest she desert the virtue of patience? At times also, when the injury shall have been greater and the cause of excuse better, she does not defend or excuse herself, knowing that then virtue the more profits in her, and merit grows. Since her friends are wordy, her eye drops toward God. For these who do injury are friends, because out of them she makes gain, and they do not harm but render service: for because they make a din with words, with the eye of her intention she drops toward God, whom she knows to be the witness of conscience and the rewarder of hidden things. She knows, according to place and time, now to be silent, now to speak, now to set herself against certain [0467A] causes, now to bear, now to do something, now to leave off; wherein she is not led by negligence, but uses discretion and a pure intention. These are the things which lie hidden within, and commend the bride in the eyes of the inward judge, who sees hidden things.
