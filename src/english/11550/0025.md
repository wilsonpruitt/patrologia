## CHAPTER XXI. *On progress, and on the strength of the good, and on the teachings of Scripture.*

*As the tower of David is thy neck, which is built with bulwarks [n: (Cant. IV)]*. [var: Vulg. *aedificata est*, "has been built"] The neck joins the head and the members. Whence by the neck are signified those who by preaching or by admonition convert others, and join them to Christ the head. These are as the tower of David, strong in grace and lofty before God: for nothing so pleases God as zeal and the gain of souls. [0467B] This tower is a spiritual building, namely the construction and heaping up of good works, the increase of grace, and perfect love [sic: *dilectis*] of God and of neighbor. In these, by the grace of Christ, both preachers and other devout and perfect men are made solid, according to their measure and their capacity, urgently intent upon the conversion of others. This is the tower of David, who is interpreted *strong of hand,* and signifies Christ, who strengthens us, that in Christ we may be able to do all things, and in whom every building, being framed together, grows and advances. This tower is said to be not so much theirs as Christ's, from the fact that it is completed by the power of Christ rather than by their own. Not I, says Paul, but the grace of God with me [n: (I Cor. XV)]. And Job: Behold, there is no help for me in myself [n: (Job VI)]; [0467C] whence elsewhere too he makes plain that he had no confidence in himself, saying: I have despaired [n: (Job VII)]. Who, by this that he despaired of himself, grew strong the better in divine hope. As much as the faithful distrust themselves, so much do they trust in the help of divine hope; and as much as they are made weak in themselves, so much are they strong and mighty in God. For as that which is sown unless it first die, so he who wishes to make progress is not strengthened unless he be first made weak in himself, and be humbled thereby; nor does he advance in God if he does not first fail in himself, and know his own failing. It is impossible therefore that anyone be able to triumph over himself, until he has learned by experience that he cannot obtain the triumph by his own power. And so to those who know their weakness, and who humble themselves thereby, and who do not trust in themselves, Christ affords [0467D] strength, that they may cease to be strong carnally and may grow strong spiritually so, while they despair of themselves and hope in Christ, they change their strength, and take wings as eagles and fly. For he who had been strong in seeking earthly things, let them become strong in desiring the things above and the heavenly: *My soul hath chosen hanging,* says blessed Job, *and my bones death [n: (Job VII)]*. Because while by his intention he hung himself toward the things above, he mortified carnal strength in himself. But this carnal mortification is not obtained at once, nor without labor. For no one becomes highest suddenly, nor is the top of a ladder climbed by flying, but by ascending step by step. It is necessary therefore that everyone making progress strive to conquer himself, and to break his own wills, and to beware not only of greater sins but also of lesser delights. [0468A] For as much as he is conquered by his own will, or by delight, or by carnal consolation, even in the least things, so much does he fall away from strength, and become weak in mind. For by whom a man is overcome, he is made his servant also; and as much as he is overcome, so much is he led into this servitude. Again, as much as he succumbs to the small suggestions of the enemy, so much does he make him strong against himself, as Job says in the person of such a one: He rushed upon me like a giant [n: (Job XVI)], so that now he is not able to resist him to whom, by frequently yielding, he was giving strength against himself. He therefore who by living softly and dissolutely has afforded the enemy strength against himself, let him, conversely, that he may be strengthened against him and resist him, begin from the least things, and often try to prevail in such things, so that by the practice of conquering he may be strengthened, and from the victory over lesser things [0468B] may little by little grow strong for conquering greater. He ought also to accustom himself to decline the delights and comforts of the body, and to break the wills of the heart. As much as he shall have prevailed in these, and shall have broken himself, so much will he be unbroken and strong spiritually. As often therefore as he resists either the carnal motions of the heart or the suggestions of the enemy, so often is strength adjudged to him against vices or temptations. He ought also to shun faint-heartedness and womanish softness, and to accustom himself to fitting labors and afflictions of the body. For if he is conquered by faint-heartedness, he will little by little fail, and will be unnerved from the strength of the spirit.

By these aforesaid means the soul which desires to build in herself the tower of the [0468C] spiritual building ought to grow strong out of weakness, and to pass over into manly strength. And although this tower is called the strong hand of Christ, because it is completed by his help and power, it is nevertheless needful that all a man's industry and labor be applied as well. The perfect have conquered all these things, because they have both compelled the body to serve the spirit, and have subjected the unlawful motions of the heart to reason. To a tower therefore are compared preachers, or other devout men who are eager to attend to the conversion of others, because they who are eager to raise others to the things above and the heavenly ought themselves to be firm and lofty by merit. Now this tower has bulwarks (namely the diverse teachings of Scripture) whereby it may defend itself against enemies, and from which it may assail and strike them. For in Holy Scripture it sees the snares and cunning devices of the enemies by which they deceive men, now [0468D] by open evils, now by hidden ones, now by other and other ways. Whence the Lord in Job declares that the enemy now fights against the soldier of Christ out of a quiver by ambushes, now brandishes a spear before his face [n: (Job XXX)], because some vices he covers over under the appearance of virtues, and others, as they are open, he casts before his eyes; and if he does not deceive a man by open battle, he sets a shield against him, so that if by striking he does not pierce the breast of the one resisting, he may at least block the way of the one making progress by certain objections, and hinder him with alien thoughts or occupations. For he suggests something that may delight a man, or may seem to have usefulness, that he may turn him away from the more useful thing that he was intending to do. But the Lord established the sacred word, in which he uncovers the arguments of the demons, and sets against them [0469A] the remedies by which he may be met. For as many as can be the arguments of his temptations, so many teachings are found in Scripture against them. The Lord himself also, when tempted, answered the enemy out of Scripture. For when he urged him to change stones into loaves, he confounded him out of Scripture [n: (Matth. IV)]. And so when he suggests to us the eating of bread (that is, of earthly delight), let us consider that we are more blessed if we eat the labors of our hands, that is, if out of the tribulation of our labors we are refreshed by the consolation of hope and by joys. For he who understands out of Scripture what reward he awaits for his labor, and that for single labors a reckoning of a thousand years in eternal happiness, and more, corresponds, is rightly refreshed better by his labors than by earthly delight, and eats this the more joyfully. [0469B] Let us therefore call to mind that saying of blessed Job: *His bread in the belly shall be turned into the gall of asps within [n: (Job XX)]*. Because earthly delight, bitter at the end of retribution, is known to have been the persuasion of demons, while those who have fed upon it are led to the bitterness of eternal torment. These two testimonies of Scripture are as it were two bulwarks, by which we may be defended from the enemy, and may conquer him. Again, we are taught out of Scripture that the enemy urges us to cast ourselves down, when he suggests the affecting of miracles. For we read that weakness of conscience is better than the vanity of signs. Paul also teaches us to leave the things that are behind, and to be stretched forth to the things before [n: (Philip. III)], namely to progress toward virtue, not to the glory of miracles. [0469C] For signs are given for the sake of unbelievers, that through them they may be converted to the faith, but the virtues of the soul are required of the faithful [n: (I Cor. XIV)]: for without these there is no salvation. But signs have sometimes been done by unbelievers, or even by the reprobate, as for instance by Alexander of Macedon and Judas the traitor, and by many others, who did these things unto damnation. By some they were done for the faith or the profit of the bystanders. Sometimes too signs were done through demons, as it is written, that there are certain spirits of demons working signs. And so among the faithful, and especially among spiritual men, the signs of this time are deservedly suspect, especially those which have ostentation but no profit, namely those which are said to happen to these women, and which are shown to be done rather by demons [0469D] than by God. For there are in these days some, according to the Apostle, who, giving heed to certain spirits of error, turn away their hearing indeed from the truth, but are turned to fables and to the rumors of new things, because they wonder at and give heed rather to certain miracles, whether done by demons or feigned by certain persons to whom they seem to happen, than to perfection of manners and to virtues. For blessed Gregory says that perfection is not indeed to work miracles, but for each one to love others as himself, to know true things concerning God, and to think better of others than of ourselves.

Again he says that each one is perfect in so far as he perfectly feels the sorrows of others. And again, that he is truly perfect who is not [0470A] impatient toward the imperfection of his neighbor. Charity therefore, humility, patience and the other virtues, and perfect manners, make a man perfect, not miracles. And so salvation is in the virtues; upon these the soul is strengthened and stands firm in miracles she is not made solid or grounded, but is lifted up and raised to no purpose and in vain. Those therefore cast themselves down who affect miracles without cause of profit, and they are suspended to no purpose, they do not stand in the solid and the safe, so that they go backward, not forward, that they might go from virtue to virtue. But on the contrary Scripture teaches us not to tempt God in the showing of miracles, but rather to be eager for the perfection of virtues, and to give labor to the fulfilling of the commandments. The Lord also teaches us by his answer to meet the third temptation, when [0470B] he says: *Thou shalt adore the Lord thy God [n: (Matth. IV)]*. For where God is said to be adored and he despises the kingdoms of the world, he calls forth to the worship of God, and teaches us to seek true riches and to despise the false. For if we worship God and serve him we shall possess unending riches, because for single good works a greater reward will be given us than the kingdom of the world is. Finally, of every single one who shall be saved we shall have a singular joy, and by the abundance of each we shall be more enriched than if we ruled over the whole world. These testimonies and examples of Scripture are bulwarks, by which we may defend ourselves from the enemy, and instruct and kindle others, and cast darts against the enemy; whence the enemy himself strives to hinder us in the understanding [0470C] of Scripture, that we may not advance in the learning of it, staining and darkening the purity of spiritual understanding by earthly thoughts or by carnal knowledge. But when our Redeemer defends us from their temptations, then the mind lays hold of this knowledge, as is read in Job: *The Almighty shall be against thy enemies, and silver shall be heaped up for thee [n: (Job XXII)]*, that is, the charity and purity of the divine word. God therefore, while he instructs his own with the understanding of Holy Scripture and refreshes them with its food, makes them at once wary and strong against temptations, as is rightly read in Job: *In famine he shall deliver thee from death, and in war from the hand of the sword [n: (Job V)]*, so that while they are refreshed by the word of God, they may not be overcome in the war of temptation. As often therefore as we resist the temptations of the enemy, and when [0470D] we confute them, being both taught out of the Scriptures and helped by God, so often indeed do we strike and wound him, and the penalty is turned back upon him which would be inflicted on us if we were conquered by him: these teachings and testimonies of Scripture are the bulwarks with which the soldier of Christ fortifies and protects himself.
