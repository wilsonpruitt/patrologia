## CHAPTER XXII. *On true perfection, and on the examples of the perfect.*

*A thousand shields hang from it [n: (Cant. IV)]*. The shields are the virtues by which we are defended against vices or temptations. But by the number of a thousand the perfection of these same is signified. For if the virtues have been founded in the soul, [d: she perfectly despises herself, and is truly humble, is less hurt or burdened,] the vices or temptations have no strength against her, and he who perfectly [0471A] despises himself, and is truly humble, is less hurt or burdened, if contempt be shown him. For he is not pierced by the dart of pride who, being founded in humility, understands the humility of exaltation, and glories in contempt or abjection, as it is written: *Let the humble glory in his exaltation [n: (Jac. I)]*. For he who loves his enemies has blocked the entrance of envy; because envy does not penetrate a breast which it has found fortified by the perfection of charity, nor will he be moved in any degree by injury who, founded in the virtue of patience, rejoices even in reproaches. Again, he cannot seek his own who through charity desires his own things to be common to others. In the other virtues the soldier of Christ is fortified, that he may be able to meet the contrary vices, and on whatever side they assail him, on that side they are repelled by the virtues, and [0471B] being repelled they leap back. And so a thousand shields hang on this tower, when a man shall have obtained all the virtues and perfectly attained them, so that on no side can a place lie open to the vices and to the tempter. For as much as the vices are untamed in anyone, as the carnal appetites, so much the more powerful are they against him, and the vices have strength to assail him. But when they have been overcome and mortified, and illumined by the contrary virtues, then if perchance they tempt or assail, they at once leap back, and cannot prevail. But he who is weak in the virtues, and has not yet subdued the vices, carries in himself that whereby he may be tempted and be weak. Hence it is that the apostle said that everyone is tempted by his own concupiscence [n: (Jac. I)], namely one still implanted in him, and not fully overcome. For as long as virtue and vice contend in a man, [0471C] and virtue does not obtain a quiet place, it is necessary that the man be shaken and wearied by many temptations, and be now the upper, now the lower. But when he shall have possessed the virtues perfectly, then he perfectly presses down carnality, and overcomes and repels its assaults. And so these are the signs of perfection, if he has so subdued the vices that, when tempted whether by the concupiscence of the flesh, or by gluttony, or by his own will, but [cj: *sed*; read *sive*, "or"] by affection for kinsfolk, or by some carnal motion, he feels himself strong against these, and easily presses them down beneath him. Again, the perfect man is ruled by reason, the imperfect man is often led by sensuality, and is weighed down by it. For the perfect man ought to be head to the members, that is, of sensuality, and to have it beneath him and press it down by reason. [0471D] But that he may rule and press it down, it therefore behooves that it be illumined and strengthened by Christ, that he may be able to know the carnal motions and to bridle them when known. For if Christ be the head of the man, and rule the reason, the man too, that is the reason, will consequently be able to rule his own lower part, namely sensuality, and as much as he shall have received the Spirit of Christ, so much strength will he have for pressing it down. He therefore is perfect who by the strength of the spirit powerfully presses down both the wills of the flesh and the will of the heart. Again, these, namely the signs of perfection, if they proceed from a good conscience, not from a false righteousness or from presumption of faith, make a man have death in desire and life in patience, for the sake of God and of his brother's profit. Because therefore such is the soldier of Christ who has attained the aforesaid virtues [0472A] and perfection, he is compared to a tower which is fortified with a thousand shields. In which tower all the armor of the strong is added to be, the armor of the strong is the lofty working of good men, and the examples of their virtues, with this armor the soldier of Christ is eager to fortify himself, who is eager to reform his own life by their example. In these each one sees what labors of heart and afflictions, and what temptations they bore, how manfully they fought it out, and broke their wills, and labored in fulfilling the commandments. How great was in them the cleanness of life, the purity of heart, and every perfection of virtues. How many there were in the deserts or in monasteries who were never filled with bread, never fully refreshed with sleep, never warmed with garments, never delighted with the sweetness of a bed, [0472B] because, while in these things they were needy and afflicted, they were rich in the perfection of virtues and in the fullness of grace, commanding demons, healing the sick, and dwelling in mind in heaven. The examples of all these and the rest are the arms of the strong, which the soldier of Christ has with him, which he uses, with which he is armed and fortified against the enemies of the soul, against the world, against himself. He who shall have these examples with him, who always gives heed to such men, cannot grow lukewarm in idleness, nor perish unarmed and dissolute before the enemy. The evil too have their own arms, namely their members, which they yield as arms of iniquity unto sin. For with their eyes they see to covet evil, they use the tongue for detracting, for reviling, or for speaking vain or useless things; they have itching ears for hearing [0472C] new or hurtful things, their feet run to evil, and their hands work iniquity, whose right hand is a right hand of iniquity. Finally with heart and body alike they war for sin, and through this for the devil; their arms do not fortify, but slay, and the more familiarly one joins himself to such men, the more grievously is he wounded by these arms. For he who associates with the evil and gives heed to them is separated from the good.

For in two ways they do harm, because they corrupt by example, and seduce by false praise. For because they do not know what is good, they proclaim him best who is not, and him good who has been exceedingly evil; and while anyone has been afflicted by their praises, he is little by little deprived of the knowledge of himself, and believes himself to be such as [0472D] he hears himself to be. For he thinks of himself not according to the testimony of his own heart, but according to the judgment of those who suppose this or that of him. Whence very many are moved by a false opinion about themselves. For to the things which are before the eyes the mind is easily inclined, so that it gives heed to them, or thinks according to them. Whence it is necessary that a man rather be eager to look with the eyes of the heart upon the strong and the perfect, and to set their virtues or deeds before himself and give heed to them. If he gives heed to the evil and entrusts himself to their judgment, he will fall and be seduced, and will be rolled down to worse things; if he thinks upon the good, he will be humbled and advanced to good. From the evil therefore, or the dissolute, we ought to be estranged in mind, and to ignore them (although they be placed near us), to turn our gaze away from considering them, but with the eyes of the heart to look upon the good, and to consider their manner of life [0473A] and to be eager for their virtues. Among these we ought to dwell in mind, whose manner of life is in heaven, and so, according to that which is written, to walk with the good. If we are assiduous with such good men, and know them as acquaintances, and join ourselves to them in desire and love, we shall assuredly be made like them, so that with the holy we may be holy, with the robust, perfect. In this way therefore we ought to compare ourselves with them, and to transfer their examples to ourselves, and to place them with us as arms with which we may war spiritually. Thus the soldier of Christ dwells with these, and, as is read in Job, he abides among the stones [n: (Job VIII)], stones assuredly living and belonging to the heavenly structure, firm and squared, their vices and carnality cut and pruned away. With these he walks in heart, [0473B] among these he abides, not among earthly stones, namely carnal men who are of the earth and speak of the earth, and act and think after an earthly manner. Among these he abides who is delighted by their favors or their manners. But the good man abides among the stones with which he has a covenant, as is said to Job: *Thy covenant with the stones of the regions [n: (Job V)]*. Whoever belongs to the building of the city above has a covenant with the stones of the regions, namely that he may imitate the firm and the strong who are or have been throughout the region of the world, and may be made like them. In this tower therefore, namely in the perfect man, is all the armor of the strong, that is, the examples and works of those who manfully contend in God. Their constancy in labors, their patience in adversities, temperance in prosperity, [0473C] charity toward enemies, obedience in hard things, endurance in injuries, devotion of mind, loftiness of contemplation, he places in his memory; these he thinks upon, in these he exercises himself, these he is eager to imitate, and by their example he shows himself devout and strenuous in the spiritual warfare: for the examples of good men build up, instruct and kindle. This strong armor therefore he has with him, and this he uses, that he may be made equal to their merits, and be endowed with their rewards.
