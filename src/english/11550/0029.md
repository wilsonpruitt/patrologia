## CHAPTER XXV. *On the crown of the blessed, and how pollution and certain other things are mortal.*

[0478D]

*Come from Lebanon, my bride; come from Lebanon, come [n: (Cant. IV)]*. Lebanon is a mountain, on which myrrh and frankincense grow. Now Lebanon is called *whitening* or *a making white.* Christ therefore calls the bride from Lebanon, when he invites her, cleansed and made white through the mortification of sins and of carnality and through the devotion of prayer, to the reward above. But that he exhorts her to come not only with a doubled voice, but even with a tripled one, insinuates the immensity of the desire and love which he has toward her, and that the threefold repetition may be an attestation of immensity and of firmness, for a threefold cord is hardly broken. Again, the multiplied repetition of the call indicates also the rejoicing with which he rejoices together at her deliverance from present misery. [0479A] He hints also with how great desire he wishes this to be done the more swiftly. For a call repeated again and again expresses the affection alike of one hastening and of one rejoicing together. Therefore also he calls her so many times, that by the repeated call he may insinuate the greatness of the happiness to which she is called. And that the threefold call may indicate the enjoyment of the Trinity, which she is to receive after labor, which enjoyment is eternal beatitude. And when he calls her, he also foretells to her from what things she is to be rewarded, saying: *Thou shalt be crowned from the head of Amana, from the top of Sanir and Hermon.* Amana is interpreted *restless* or *turbulent,* and signifies the lovers of the world, who are restless and turbulent alike. There is restlessness of labor and weariness in seeking worldly delights, and when a man attains them, he does not find full [0479B] satiety in them. Not had, these things vex; had, they do not satisfy. For while a man desires to satisfy his desire in these things, he kindles it the more. Whence it is said that the remedies of sins are sicknesses. Because whence a man by sinning cures his desire for a time, thence he afterward sickens with a greater desire of sinning. Because therefore it has no satiety, it does not afford stillness, nor does the Spirit of God remain in a mind that seeks earthly things, who rests upon none but the humble and the still. For he is not received by a mind which has been restless through earthly desires, and he does not illumine with spiritual light her who is filled with carnal delight. For where the vessels of the heart have not been empty of carnal things, the oil of the spirit has no place to stand there. For neither does the Holy Spirit come or [0479C] remain in a man when he is flesh, that is, carnal. When therefore the soul is made restless by these desires, she likewise also becomes turbulent, that is, dark and confused and estranged from the knowledge of truth. For earthly desires confound and darken the eyes of the heart, so that a man knows neither himself, nor his own blindness and misery. But the mind of the good refuses to be consoled in these delights, and therefore deserves to be delighted, and to be illumined with grace. For blessed Job condemns and curses the day of carnal delight [n: (Job III)], and turns it by his own judgment into darkness, or night, while he weighs to how great a blindness and misery it will lead. For through the night considered, which he considers to follow this day, he destroys the day itself, and makes it perish in himself, and so [0479D] arrives at the day of grace. Thus he turns night into day, and after the darkness of night which he condemns, he deservedly hopes for light, and receives the illumination of grace. Those therefore whom earthly delight overcomes are Amana, that is, the restless and the turbulent, and their head is the devil, who through these things holds princedom in them. While the devout soul resists his persuasions, and does not look back upon them, she triumphs over this head. But not only from the head of Amana, but also from the top of Sanir and Hermon, does Christ promise that she is to be crowned. Sanir and Hermon are mountains of Judea, in which lions and leopards dwell. These mountains signify those who stink through uncleanness, and who are separated by fault from God and from the fellowship of the good. For Sanir is interpreted *stench,* and Hermon *anathematizing.* But of those who [0480A] are Sanir, some are polluted by act, others by thought, and others, consenting to some temptation or frailty, or resisting less lawfully, do not escape the fault of uncleanness. And the uncleanness of acts indeed is plain enough, nor is it needful that much be said about these. But in thought one sins mortally either by the will of sinning, or by consenting to the delight of the thought, and by delaying in the delight. For if thou consent to the delight of a thought concerning a mortal sin, even though thou be unwilling to commit the act, thou sinnest mortally, especially if thou delay long in such delight after reason has detected it, and it please thee. For if the delight itself has lasted while reason does not consent, or does not notice it, it is not called mortal. Thus sometimes a longer delay is venial, and [0480B] a shorter one mortal, when it is noticed, and is not resisted, but is consented to. But if some pollution happen in the body to one awake, and a flux of seed, it is mortal, unless it happen from some natural infirmity. If therefore it happen from delight, it is mortal, even though it be not voluntary when it happens, if a mortal fault has gone before, of familiarity, of thought, of the glance and the like, from which the lingering delight through which it came about had its occasion. For because these were voluntary, and through them the lingering delight was had, the sin itself is not excused, even though it happen against the will. But if anyone, after a lingering delight, has been contrite for it, if afterward pollution happen to him, and the delight returns again, [0480C] it is mortal, because in the very hour of its happening he in some degree consents to the delight, so that it happens to him. For if he does not consent, it is a venial sin, because no mortal sin happens unless it be voluntary. Either it happens from infirmity, and then it is venial, or it is no sin at all, if there has been no delight in it. For it can happen from infirmity, and yet there is some delight in it which nevertheless is venial. If also it happen to one sleeping from some fault which is mortal, it will be mortal. But if contrition intervene [sic: *interveveniat*], it is venial, although it has its occasion thence, because it has been abolished by contrition.

It is similar concerning the madman, and the ignorant, and the one who omits. For when a madman, set in his madness, kills or fornicates or does something mortal, although [0480D] he does not then have the sense of discerning what he does, yet he sins mortally, if he fell into that very madness by his own fault, and was in the will of sinning mortally, and thus is made guilty from the preceding fault; so for one sleeping, from preceding causes, pollution is mortal. So it is concerning the ignorant; because, although in the hour of knowing what he ought he is unwilling to be ignorant, yet he is guilty, because he earlier despised to know when he was able to know. And concerning the one who omits, when he omits what he ought, because he could have [sic: *pot isset*] shown himself [sic: *exhibui se*] solicitous for the doing, and so, at the time of doing, not have omitted it. Sanir therefore are those who stink, whom the devil pollutes, either in work or in thought, who is read to creep upon breast and belly. But Hermon, which is interpreted *anathematizing,* signifies those who are anathematized, that is, separated from God by their own [0481A] iniquity, and others who for manifest sins are excluded from the society of the Church by the sentence of excommunication. And let us speak of the first, who are disjoined from God through mortal sins. Every mortal sin is committed by acting against the precepts, or against conscience. For whatever conscience should dictate to be mortal, even if it were venial, he who should do this against such a conscience would sin mortally, the Apostle saying: *All that is not done of faith,* that is, against conscience, *is sin [n: (Rom. XIV)]* [var: Vulg. *non est ex fide*, "is not of faith"], and what is done against conscience builds unto hell. The other mortal things that are done are done against the precepts.
