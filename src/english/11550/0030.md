## CHAPTER XXV. *On the crown of the blessed, and how pollution and certain other things are mortal.* (cont.)

But there are certain sins which are always mortal, such as hatred, idolatry, poisoning, sorcery, fornication and the like. But anger, impatience, [0481B] sadness, avarice, gluttony, pride, vain glory, detraction, and many such things are not always mortal, unless, namely, one sin in these through grave contempt, or do something against a precept, that is, against the love of God and of the neighbor. For anger is not mortal when one is moved concerning his neighbor with the will and consent [cj: *voluntate et consensu*; read *sine voluntate et consensu*, "without the will and consent"] of harming him in person, or in property, or in repute, because he incurs only the first motions of wrath. And therefore the sun does not go down upon his wrath [n: (Ephes. IV)] [var: Vulg. *sol non occidat super iracundiam vestram*, "let not the sun go down upon your wrath"], that is, Christ. It is the same with all the other motions short of the will and the consent to act against the love of God and of the neighbor. Through impatience one sins mortally when one so exceeds through it that the virtue of patience is utterly abandoned. Through sadness, when one is so overcome [0481C] that he in no wise consoles himself in God, but consents altogether. Through avarice, when the tenacity is so great that he does not succor his neighbor in straitened need, and holds back what ought to be given. Through gluttony, when by habit or frequently one slips into surfeit or drunkenness, and, having had experience of his own weakness in this, does not beware for himself, nor moderate himself; or when he is overcome, like Esau, through the ardor of concupiscence for food or drink. And again, sometimes in these ways: over-hastily, sumptuously, too ardently, with studied care. Through pride, or vain glory, when he sets himself before others, and puts human favor before the love of God. By detracting also, or by publishing another's sin out of envy, one then sins mortally; or [0481D] when other things are said by which he intends to harm the person, or the repute; or when one strives to corrupt another's good standing. A venial sin also becomes mortal through contempt, as Augustine says, no sin is so far a venial sin that it does not become mortal while it pleases, if, namely, it so please that a man would commit it even if he knew it to be mortal; or a venial sin becomes mortal by occasion, because through a multitude of venial sins one comes to the committing of a mortal one. Whenever therefore one sins mortally, then it is done either against conscience, or through contempt, or against something which is commanded or forbidden. Until, therefore, the first motions go so far forward that something is done against one of these, there is no mortal sin. But when one so consents to them [0482A] that he offends in one of them, then for the first time he sins mortally. But in whatever aforesaid way the perverse sin, they are anathematized, and separated from God through mortal sin. But those who have been excommunicated by name are excommunicated with the greater excommunication, and are to be shunned in prayer, at table, in greeting. For no one can pray in church while such are present, neither secretly nor openly. Innocent, however, says the contrary. He can, however, outside the church, they being present or hearing. For such also one can pray secretly, not openly, or with anyone hearing. In these things, however, we can communicate with them which pertain to their absolution and salvation. And if they should owe anything to anyone, it can be demanded back from them, and they can be sued, not sue. [0482B] The perverse, therefore, are Amana, that is, the restless through love of earthly things; or Sanir, that is, those who stink through unclean life; or Hermon, that is, those anathematized through sin, or through the sentence of excommunication. But their head or top is the devil, who by tempting leads them to this, and through this bears princedom in them; and while each devout man resists his persuasion, lest he be entangled in sins, he is crowned from this head, because he triumphs. The lions also and the leopards are the demons, because now, with open iniquity, raging, they deceive some, now with flattering counsels and various frauds they seduce the upright, just as lions are fierce, and leopards are sprinkled with varied color. The dwelling of these lions or leopards are the wicked, whose perverse manners and sins, while the good [0482C] do not imitate but flee them, and sometimes convert such men to good by example and by teaching. From these mountains and dens of lions and leopards are they crowned, and are endowed with eternal rewards.
