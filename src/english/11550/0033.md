## CHAPTER XXVIII. *On the praise of the twofold compassion, and on sweet teaching.*

[0485C]

*How fair are thy paps, my sister, my bride; thy breasts are fairer than wine, and the odor of thy ointments above all aromatical spices [n: (Cant. IV)]*. The two paps are the twofold compassion, bodily and spiritual. These the soul has when she has compassion on those laboring under bodily need or under any adversity, or bestows consolation and prayer upon those set in some sin or temptation. These breasts she has when she knows how to rejoice with them that rejoice, and to weep with them that weep, to be weak with the weak, to burn with them that are scandalized. Fair are the paps of that soul which has compassion on all out of affection, and cannot pass by any man's sorrows without sorrow of heart. And well does she see spiritually, through one [0485D] of her eyes, that whereby she is also moved spiritually toward her neighbors. For he who loves God embraces his neighbors also out of charity. Because he has drawn near to goodness, he has received the bowels of tenderness, so that he is moved toward others, and in charity, even as he has learned to love God. Likewise, as he has a single regard to his own salvation and to God, so likewise toward his neighbors he has one motion, namely of tenderness, not of commotion or of rancor. Rightly is the soul that has these breasts fair, and pleases the gaze of Christ, having charity which covers her own sins, and bestowing mercy upon the sins of others, that she herself may obtain mercy; of which it is read in Job: Visiting thy beauty, thou shalt not sin. Our beauty is our neighbor, in whom we look upon what we are. [0486A] When we so visit him through compassion, we do not sin, that is, we are free from sin, because through this tenderness we are cleansed from sins. The soul also becomes fully clean from sins which not only draws no sin from her neighbor through some rancor, or rash judgment and suspicion, but moreover, by having compassion, receives pardon for what she has committed. Rightly therefore is she commended who through tenderness is both cleansed from sin and at the same time adorned with merits. Whose breasts are said to be fairer than wine. In the wine we can take the rigor of justice, which pleases God greatly and adorns the soul; yet the bowels of mercy commend and adorn her more. For as oil floats upon the other liquids, and puts forward its own brightness, so mercy exalts itself above judgment, and is [0486B] preferred to it, and is the stronger; so much so that justice ought not to exercise any right, or any judgment, until mercy has more fully fulfilled her own, nor can justice work anything except in what mercy leaves over. Rightly is mercy so far pre-eminent and worthier, that she can exercise her office, if discretion be present, without fault; but justice cannot exercise her own, save with mercy mediating. Rightly therefore are the breasts in the bride proclaimed fair and commended, because by this she advances toward the likeness of God, whose property it is to have mercy and to spare, in the measure that he himself knows how to have compassion perfectly and to succor. And the odor of thy ointments above all aromatical spices. The fragrance of this sweetness, and of the spiritual unction whereby she is so kindly affected toward all, has compassion, groans, prays and [0486C] desires that succor be given, is in the nostrils of the Bridegroom above; sweet with all the virtues, she is fragrant in her works, and is known to please. For charity is the mother of all the virtues; whence not undeservedly does this virtue, which proceeds from her root, deserve to excel judgment, and to set before it the fame of a good odor. Such is the bride in heart as she is also in mouth and in work.

There follows: *A honeycomb dropping are thy lips, bride; honey and milk under thy tongue, and the odor of thy garments as the odor of frankincense.* A honeycomb dropping are the lips of the devout soul, because they flow with the liquor of spiritual sweetness, and pour it out to others for edification. This honeycomb the soul gathers from the various flowers of Scripture; these she searches out, upon these she sits, out of these she draws and elicits the sweetness [0486D] of spiritual delight. The knowledge that puffs up she sets apart and despises; edification she rather seeks out; she chooses sweet-smelling sentences, not pompous eloquence. The examples also and the spiritual sayings of the saints she investigates and surveys with her mind, who were assuredly flowers, and flourished like the palm. As their life was holy, so sweet and pleasant were the things which they brought forth, or taught, to flowers of this kind she flies; from these she gathers spiritual honey, but chiefly from that one singular flower, that is, the flower which came forth from the rod of Jesse, the flower of the field and not of the garden. The odor [sic: *ordor*] of this flower is as the odor of a full field, which the Lord hath blessed. Full is this field, in which dwells the fulness of the Godhead, in which are all the treasures of wisdom and of knowledge, in which the just flourish, and [0487A] in which they bear fruit both in good life and in sacred knowledge. This is the flowering field, nay, full of flowers, in which there are as many flowers as there are just men bearing fruit in good, and outside which there is no flourishing, but withering. To this flowering and full field the bride flies like a prudent bee; in the odor of this flower she runs, to him she goes forth with desire, to him she clings with love and with faith, from him by the importunity of prayer she sucks the honey of grace, of his fulness she receives grace, through his spirit charity is poured abroad in her heart, and she receives the breasts of tenderness, and this grace is poured abroad in her lips, so that she may breathe out the savor of the sweetness above and give others to drink of it. Honey also and milk she has under her tongue, that is, the refreshment of the strong and the consolation of little ones, the solid food of the perfect and [0487B] the milk of tender teaching for the weak. For she has fore-tasted the sweetness of the life to come, where souls shall be refreshed by the enjoyment of Christ's divinity and by the milk of his humanity. She has tasted the foretaste of that beatitude, because she is refreshed by the streamlets of the river of honey and butter, of which it is read in Job that the hypocrite and the feigned man shall not see them [n: (Job XX)], that is, shall not understand or taste them. This twofold sweetness, like the stream of a river, gladdens the city of God above; but it visits with its streamlets the good who are still pilgrims and exiles, and gently pours in and refreshes them. Out of this abundance the soul has breasts by which she may bring forth the sweetness she has received, which savor and edify the more, in the measure that it has been given her to learn these things in experience, and to teach them from her own judgment. And the odor of her garments [0487C] as the odor of frankincense. The garments of the soul are the works which are, or are done, in charity, clothed with which, and not naked, the elect are found before God. Which garments are as the odor of frankincense, because good working sends forth from itself a good odor even as devout prayer does. For if anyone break it off for the sake of a useful occupation, that occupation itself will be reckoned in place of prayer, so that, according to the Apostle, he prays without ceasing who always works well.
