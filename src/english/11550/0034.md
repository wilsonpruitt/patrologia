## CHAPTER XXIX. *On the twofold enclosing of the soul, and on hidden martyrdom.*

*A garden enclosed, my sister, my bride, a garden enclosed, a fountain sealed; thy sendings-forth are a paradise of pomegranates with the fruits of apples* [0487D] [n: (Cant. IV)]. The garden is the soul, in which are cultivated the seedlings of the virtues, and the twin [seedlings] of spiritual studies. This garden is then digged, while in her the vices are rooted utterly out, and manners are converted. And it is then digged the more deeply, when a man is zealous to know the nature and the origins of the vices. For never will a lukewarm worker, ignorant of the nature of the vices, be able to mortify the vices well. It must be digged, therefore, with the memory of the divine passion; and those nails with which the wicked digged the hands and the feet of the Lord pierce it through, while we hold the memory of these things, and grieve together with this sorrow, and thereupon grieve for our own sins, we who recall ourselves ungrateful for so great benefits. Such sorrow therefore drives out and represses the vices, so that they cannot prevail in us or harm us, while, these things [0488A] being considered, we grieve vehemently. Which sorrow, when the love of God accompanies it, from this twofold striking compunction is generated, which, while it softens the heart, also makes the virtues to advance. This garden is enclosed with the bar of silence. For through silence she cultivates in advancement and in justice, because the cultivation of justice requires silence. In which she restrains herself not only from harmful things, but also from idle and superfluous ones, because through the least things one comes to the greater, and through idle things the mind is deceived, and is led outside itself, as Gregory says. By as many superfluous words as the mind is scattered from the strictness of its own silence, by so many streams, as it were, is it led outside itself. That therefore it may remain within itself and see itself, know itself, correct itself, it restrains and tempers itself from idle and superfluous words. Hearing also and sight she encloses, lest they draw inward [0488B] what may disquiet or stain the mind. And upon these windows she sets bars, because through them death is wont to enter in. For she takes care lest she be overcome by curiosity of seeing or of hearing, because through the delight of these the spirit is unnerved from its strength, and one becomes the servant of sin in the measure that he is overcome by sin. For death does not always rush in through these windows violently or suddenly, but even little by little, and creeps in through a small negligence in the mind. For neglected bilge-water sinks a ship as surely as an inrushing wave. For these things, by use, heap dust upon the mind, whereby it is defiled and darkened, and little by little it is overcome by them, so that it fails from its own vigor, and at last willingly endures what it has negligently endured. So he that neglects small things falls away little by little [n: (Eccli. XIX)]. The care also of outward things [0488C] burns the heart, and chokes the shoots of the virtues so that they do not come forth; and the multitude of earthly thoughts, if it has broken into this garden, tramples it down like a beast.

These things therefore the devout soul shuts out, and against such things she encloses her own garden. But because this garden is called enclosed a second time, we ought to consider another enclosing of it also. For not only does she fortify and shut it from outward perils, but within she hides her own good things, and conceals them from men, knowing that when good works become known and are praised, they loosen and unnerve the mind. For it is difficult for one to be praised and venerated, and not in some measure to be delighted with his own praise, or even to glory within. It is difficult for one to reckon himself an unprofitable servant [0488D] while by the testimony of good men he is proclaimed good; nay, it is wont to happen that, deceived by praise, he believes report rather than conscience. Who, deprived of knowledge of himself, is sometimes led, blinded, to this pride, that he believes himself to have what he has not. So while a man is called blessed, he is seduced, and the way of his steps is scattered, so that henceforth he does not strive toward what is perfect, but believes himself to have finished. But the devout soul hides her good things and shuts herself against praises, that she may not hear them, deaf to them like the asp that stops her ears lest she hear the voice of the charmers. For the soul is charmed by praises, so that she does not see her own evils, and deceitfully exalts herself for her justice. Thus, strong, she shuts and fortifies herself; but within she makes ready for grace, and opens her heart to it, as it is read in Job: *My root is opened above the waters* [0489A] [n: (Job XXIX)] [var: Vulg. *secus aquas*, "beside the waters"], that is, the intention of the heart lies open, through desire, to receive the grace of the Holy Spirit. Which she draws the more swiftly and the more fully, the more she lies open to nothing else. For her love is directed to God the more freely, the less it is hindered by the delight or the hindrance of praise. One thing she asks, and this she seeks after, that she may dwell in the house of the Lord not made by hand, but eternal; that she may see the will [var: Vulg. *voluptatem*, "the delight"] of the Lord, by the fulfilling of which she may come to it, and may visit that temple in which all its citizens declare his glory. This temple she visits by meditation and by desire. Its happiness and joy she contemplates, and by the contemplation of it she easily spurns the joy of praise, and shuts it out from the entrance of the heart. This garden then, thus enclosed, a sealed [sic: *signarus*] fountain waters; understand therefore the fountain sealed [0489B] to be the spiritual understanding, stamped with the seal of the Holy Spirit, and fortified with the teachings of Holy Scripture and with the examples of the Fathers. For what such a soul savors and feels is neither corrupted by an evil spirit, nor discordant in anything from sound doctrine, nor is it found contrary to the examples of the saints, nor is it vitiated by harmful novelty or by presumption. With this fountain the seedlings of this garden are watered, because by this knowledge the virtues and good pursuits are formed, and advance, and are made discreet. By a sober draught of this fountain a man is quickened in soul, and obtains peace of heart. For to savor according to the spirit is life and peace. Whose sendings-forth are a paradise of pomegranates with the fruits of apples. Red pomegranates signify the martyrdom which takes place in the peace [0489C] of the Church, namely, whereby the vices and the sins that are bloody and red as a little worm are mortified. It is martyrdom when we mortify our members which are upon the earth, fornication, uncleanness, evil concupiscence, avarice and the other evils. Now the pomegranate contains within many and white grains. So too the soul has in herself many works of martyrdom which make her clean and white. For they are works of martyrdom, that one afflicts himself through compassion for his neighbor, that he pardons him who hurts him, that he loves his enemy, that he chastises the flesh with abstinence, with watchings or with other labors, that he labors against various temptations, that he struggles against his own will, that he strives against the pleasures of the flesh. In the affliction [0489D] and labor of this he drinks the chalice of the passion, and daily takes up his cross. These are the grains of the pomegranate, which assuredly have whiteness, because they not only adorn the soul with merits, but cleanse her from the punishment owed for the sins of which she is penitent. For because they are laborious and penal, therefore they are said to blot out punishment rather than other works that are not penal. In another way also they adorn the soul, because through them charity is believed to be the more kindled, and so merit to be increased. The fruits of apples which this garden puts forth at the same time are the other works, fruitful and meritorious of eternal life, and fragrant with the odor of good example and of good repute. For it sends forth cypress, with spikenard and saffron. The seed of the cypress is said to be white as the seed of coriander, as is read of the manna. Whence rightly, through the whiteness which the manna has, [0490A] it signifies the refreshment of heavenly grace. But the spikenard, with which the Lord's body was anointed, signifies the unction of the love of God in the heart, because we are so anointed that we are not overcome by the fire of tribulation. When grace refreshes and fills us, it makes us strong against adversities. Saffron, which brings coolness to the burning limbs of the sick, signifies this virtue of charity, which tempers the mind from the heat of carnal pleasures, and inflames it to desire the joys above. Her sendings-forth also are the sweet cane and cinnamon with all the trees of Lebanon, myrrh and aloes with all the chief ointments: the sweet cane, which is also called cassia, is of a stout bark, and is of a purple color, and is said to avail for curing the troubles of the bowels. And rightly, by reason of its shortness, it signifies humility: [0490B] for the humble, as though clothed in purple color, are always mindful of the Lord's passion, always ready to suffer for the Lord. This virtue is especially wont to repress the inward motions of sin, and wantonness, as it were the pains of the bowels, when, considering again that the Lord suffered for us, we acknowledge that we suffer less than we deserve. Cinnamon holds the figure of the same virtue; whence by this, that he joined cinnamon to the sweet cane, it can be understood that we ought again and again to press on to the advancement of humility, and to search out the ways of this virtue and the modes of humbling ourselves. And because through it grace is chiefly obtained, so through it perfection is chiefly attained. Now this tree is of a blackish [0490C] or ashen color, signifying this, that the humble know themselves to be sinners, dust and ashes, and judge that it behooves them to do penance in embers and ashes: *The sweet cane,* he says, *and cinnamon with all the trees of Lebanon.* As the sweet cane and cinnamon show the humble gatherings of the just, so the trees of Lebanon show their lofty working; these grow in the garden of the good, while they both do great things and are always humble and little in their own eyes. Myrrh and aloes express the continence of the flesh; and the nature of these aromatical spices is such that the bodies of the dead anointed with them do not putrefy at all. But the chief ointments are those of which Paul says: *Be zealous for the better gifts [n: (I Cor. XII)]*. Fairly also are myrrh and aloes joined with all the chief ointments, because [sic: *quis*] [0490D] while we restrain the flesh from wantonness, it follows that we receive the greater gifts of the Spirit. This paradise of trees she brings forth and sends forth from the root of the heart of the devout soul. A garden well tilled, namely the virtues and the shoots of spiritual studies. This is the paradise in which the soul dwells, with whose delights and pleasantness she is delighted. This paradise she possesses on earth, being about to pass from it to the heavenly one. The first parents had a [sic: *terestrem*] paradise, being about to have the heavenly one after it if they had kept the commandment. But instead of the food of the trees of paradise they earned thorns and thistles through disobedience. She, the thorns of the vices being plucked out, receives the fruits of the Spirit, and a paradise of virtues: this paradise is justice, peace and joy, delight in the Holy Spirit, a taste of the virtues and an experience [0491A] of the divine goodness, a foretaste of the beatitude to come, the hope of eternal life. This paradise the garden of the devout soul sends forth, and out of this garden she is sent forth to the enclosed garden of the heavenly city Jerusalem. Whose bars of the gates have been strengthened, all misery shut out, and all happiness and everlasting beatitude shut in.
