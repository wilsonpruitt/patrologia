## CHAPTER XXXI. *On the consolation of temptation, and that an ampler grace follows it.*

[0493D] *Arise, north wind, and come, south wind; blow through my garden, and its spices shall flow [n: (Cant. IV)]*. [var: Vulg. Cant. 4:16 *et fluant aromata illius*, "and let its spices flow"; jussive subjunctive, where Migne prints the future indicative *fluent*] Though the virtues are in a man, they are sometimes pressed down by some temptation or adversity, so that they do not appear outwardly though they are held within. When, therefore, someone labors under the adversity of temptation, in his garden the north wind blows, a wind, namely, icy and dry. This wind binds tight the waters of grace and fetters the exercise of the virtues; whence the north wind is called *aquas ligans,* the binder of waters. Yet although anyone in such a state, if he bears himself well, merits no less, he is nevertheless less able to exercise himself freely or gladly in grace or in the virtues. For the waters of grace are hindered by the cold of this wind, so that they cannot run freely. For the Lord, lest a man be lifted up if he should continually enjoy this consolation, by dispensation [0494A] permits him now to be troubled, now kindly consoles the same man, and works all these things for his profit. He turns this captivity as a torrent is turned in the south wind, and melts the ice of the mind with the clear weather of his grace. He bids the north wind arise, that is, withdraw, and the south wind blow, that is, that the adversity cease and the consolation of his Spirit come. He commands this wind and the sea, that is, he makes the disturbance cease, and makes a tranquillity not middling but great. For he makes the south wind, that is, the Holy Spirit, not only blow in the garden of the soul devoted to him, but also blow through this garden, that is, fully take possession of it. Then, the north wind withdrawing and the ice of the body being scattered and the Holy Spirit blowing, the waters of grace flow through the garden of the soul. The south wind was indeed blowing in this garden, [0494B] because the soul had charity, which is poured in not otherwise than through the Holy Spirit; and although these spices flowed less, they were not held less. For no one is less in the virtues when he is possessed by temptation. For although the virtues are pressed down, yet they are not extinguished, and as often as anyone resists, so often is he crowned. And it comes about that the soul which has labored more harshly under temptation is more fully rewarded with perfection, and the adversity of the fight becomes the occasion of an ampler grace. In himself Christ permitted the enemy to rage for a time, that he might destroy him; and he likewise allows his own to be tempted, that they may overcome him, and that on that account he may reward them with a greater grace. For when the enemy is conquered, his strength is unnerved, and the victors are spiritually strengthened and endowed [0494C] with grace. Then the south wind, that is, the Holy Spirit, blows through the garden of the soul, because he blows perfectly in it and endows the soul with perfection. Then the spices of the virtues flow with such abundance indeed that a man reckons any adversities to be prosperity, and turns outward losses into the gain of the soul. Then patience becomes glad, charity kind, humility devout, obedience ready. The enemy is loved not so much by the leading of reason as by the affection of the mind. The carnal motions of the heart are weakened, because the spiritual ones are strengthened. The soul has taste according to the spirit and is moved; she is affected with spiritual sweetness, she is kindled with zeal and with love of every good and with charity for the salvation of the brethren. In short, she has the virtues not so much in desire as in effect. Then the spices of the virtues so flow that she embraces all men with the bowels of tenderness, [0494D] and both corrects others by example and scatters abroad from herself the odor of a good report. The south wind so blew through the garden of a certain monk that these spices flowed from it, whose manners and manner of life I have thought should be inserted here. For he was truly meek and humble in heart, so reckoning himself within, by his own judgment, vile and nothing, as outwardly he humbled himself for God's sake. For he judged himself a man ever of no usefulness to be fed in the house of God. And although he flourished in the other virtues, he nevertheless obtained the grace of gentleness in a special way. Therefore he showed himself lovable and affable to all. So circumcised was his mouth that scarcely ever was there heard from his mouth the sound of detraction. a word of scurrility, speech of vainglory, the voice of envy; he was not caught judging others, or consenting [0495A] to those who judged. Not only would he have guarded against speaking empty things; nay rather, whoever spoke such things feared him. Set among those who laughed, lest he become burdensome, he showed himself cheerful, but he did not admit an entire laugh. If ever he was called to counsel for the sake of some business, he persevered sad and clouded, until he was restored to the fellowship of the brethren. Very rarely was he found to have been absent from the solemn vigils, very rarely from the hours of those chanting in choir. In the refectory using none but the common foods, if anything were by chance set before him beyond the usual he did not take it; scarcely ever, save when obedience compelled, did he enter the infirmary, and scarcely, when he had entered, could he be kept there. If anyone was struck by the multitude or the greatness of temptations, he heard from his mouth both the root of the temptation and the remedy of its healing. [0495B] For he so ran through all the corners of the conscience of the one who was sick that he who was confessing could believe that he had seen all things, had been present at all. He was of such charity, and had so put on the bowels of tenderness, that he excused all, interceded for all, they for whom he spoke not knowing of it, and was no accepter of persons but of necessities. In short, the foot which he had set on the paths of the Lord Jesus he did not draw back until he had finished the course of the journey. Whence the south wind, that is, the Holy Spirit, had blown through such a garden and had taken possession of the whole of it, so that no place in it lay open to vice or to carnality, but its spices flowed and ran back to others also.
