## CHAPTER XXXII *On the coming of Christ into the soul, and that through mortification one comes through to perfection.*

[0495C]

*Let my beloved come into his garden, that he may eat the fruits of his apples [n: (Cant. V)]*. The bride, advancing in the virtues and her conscience more fully made calm, yet glorying for this not in herself but in the Lord, prays that the Bridegroom come, although he rejoices over the fruit of the virtues acquired by her. For she knows that he rejoices together at our advance, and that the fruit of salvation delightfully refreshes him. He remains, however, in the devout soul: for without him she could not be devout, nor bear fruit unless he remained in her; but he is said to come into the soul when he increases grace and rejoices with her at her advance. For daily he comes into the devout, because he renews them and heaps up grace in them. And so she says: *Let [0495D] the beloved come,* etc. As if she said: Not by the boldness of presumption, but trusting in a good conscience and in faith not feigned, I have applied myself to cleansing my heart and to preparing it more fully for thy coming. Gladden me with thy visitation, refresh me after labor, take delight and be fed with the fruit of my labor. Whence she calls the beloved her own in a singular way, and the more confidently indeed the more perfectly, adorned with virtues, she has prepared for him a more pleasing dwelling. Whom also she asks to come into his own garden, and to receive his own fruit. For the fruit is his, the garden is his; he is the gardener, hedging it about with his keeping and giving it increase; because neither he that plants nor he that waters is anything, but God who gives the increase. Therefore she attributes not to herself but to God the fact that she advances, not to her own strength or industry, but to the divine tenderness: [0496A] for she knows that without him she can do nothing, and that unless he keeps this garden, watch would be kept over its keeping in vain. For if the divine keeping ceased, the wall would be destroyed and it would be given over to plundering, nor would the plantings advance. And so she invites him to the eating of his apples. The apples are the works of the virtues, which have refreshment and odor. For they refresh the mind, and send forth from themselves to others the odor of good example. The beloved eats these apples when he is fed with those works, and is delighted as much with their advance as with ours. Drawn therefore by the bride's desire, and delighted by the beauty of her virtues and the cleanness of her heart, the Bridegroom shows forth his presence, saying: *Come into my garden, my sister, my bride.* And that same cleanness [0496B] and perfection which he found, nay rather which he himself worked in her, he enumerates, saying: *I have reaped my myrrh with my spices. I have eaten my honeycomb with my honey, I have drunk my wine with my milk [n: (Cant. V)]*. O my sister, because thou art the bride and joined to me in love, I have found in thee my garden enclosed and well tilled, that I may eat in thee and refresh thee after thy labor. Thou hast borne the affliction of penance, the fight, temptation, the labor of the correction of manners, and thou hast come to the harvest of the perfection of the virtues, from a laborious life to the more tranquil state of contemplation. And so I have reaped in thee myrrh with spices, because together with the bitterness of labors the spices of the virtues [0496C] were compounded and perfected in thee, and with the commandments kept wisdom advanced. For when carnality and one's own will have been mortified, then the spices of the virtues flow, and through the consummation and the triumph of this labor one comes to the cultivation of perfection. But Christ reaps the myrrh when he perfects in the soul the labors set forth above, by which she reaches through to the virtues and to perfection, and gently strengthens her so that she does with delight what before she shuddered at, as it is read in Job: *The things which before my soul would not touch, now, for anguish, are my food [n: (Job VI)]*. So the apostles grew strong out of weakness; so Paul could do all things in him who strengthened him [n: (Philip. IV)].

There follows: *I have eaten the honeycomb with my honey, I have drunk my wine with my milk.* The honeycomb is the sweetness of good [0496D] merits which the devout soul has within; the honey is the work which she shows forth outwardly, which is deservedly compared to honey, because sweet is that which is repaid for it in the time to come. Christ eats the honeycomb when he is fed with the sweetness of good manners, when for him whose place is made in peace a place is prepared by manners amended and made peaceful. He is refreshed with honey when someone does good works, when to him whose delights it is to be with the sons of men, who stands at the door and knocks, it is opened. For it is opened to him when through works charity advances, grace is increased and preserved. Therefore by working more we open to him more, that he may enter to us and sup with us. Happy the banquet, and blessed the soul with which and in which Christ feasts. Christ also drinks wine and milk in the soul when at her twofold [0497A] contemplation, namely of his divinity and of his glorified humanity (which are designated by the wine and the milk), he rejoices and takes delight. Hence we can also fit the food and the drink to the soul, and expound these things from her own person. For she herself eats the honeycomb when, adorned with good manners, she is delightfully refreshed by their sweetness. For those who are sweet in manners have great sweetness and tranquillity of mind, as on the contrary there is no peace for the wicked and the insolent. Again, while they so keep themselves from sins that conscience bites them in nothing, they always feast, they always enjoy security, as it is written: *A secure mind is like a continual banquet [n: (Prov. XV)]*. Then the soul eats honey when she is delighted with good work, when she eats the labors of her own hands, [0497B] when from that which she works outwardly she is fed within by the hope of reward, when what she does for the sake of the delight above becomes sweet to her, and the days of labor seem few to her before the greatness of that happiness. For she sighs to see God, whose sweetness, unless she had tasted it in part, she would by no means desire. Whence there follows:

*I have drunk my wine with my milk.* Then the soul drinks wine when she perceives the savor of his divinity, and is so made drunk with this cup that she gives over all earthly things to forgetfulness and cleaves through love to the things above. She receives milk when she thinks upon the humanity of Christ glorified in heaven. By this contemplation she is refreshed, and in this hope and remembrance she grows cheerful and is consoled. This is the twofold happiness, of angels as much as of men, in that life: the enjoyment, namely, of the divinity and the contemplation [0497C] of the humanity. Which happiness the devout soul experiences in part in this life, but will comprehend perfectly in the life to come, when she shall see God as he is, and shall contemplate him face to face. But first the honeycomb and the honey are eaten, and afterward the wine and the milk are drunk, because it is necessary that amended manners and worthy work go before, sooner than the grace of contemplation be granted. Leah is put under first, before one comes to the embraces of Rachel. And so one must labor, and the eyes of the heart must be cleansed that God may be seen. For God is promised to be seen to cleanness of heart, as it is written *Blessed are the clean of heart, for they shall see God [n: (Matth. V)]*. That thou mayest therefore see God, cleanse the eye of thy heart; otherwise thou mayest see the world for thyself in place of [0497D] God or of an angel of light, and receive an angel of darkness, and mayest not drink the wine of God and sweet honey, but the gall of dragons be thy wine, and the incurable venom of asps. For the sight of one who is going blind is often deceived, so that the worse he sees, the more he seems to himself to see; and when one thing appears to his eyes, he believes rather that he sees two. So certain men, when they see certain vain things and divine, lies, they see deceitful visions, because they believe every spirit, and have less cleansed the eyes of the heart for seeing spiritual things, and for discerning.
