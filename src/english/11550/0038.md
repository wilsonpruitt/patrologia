## CHAPTER XXXIII. *On spiritual refreshment and the use of grace.*

*Eat, friends, and be made drunk, dearest ones [n: (Cant. V)]*. [var: Vulg. Cant. 5:1 *Comedite, amici, et bibite, et inebriamini, carissimi*, "Eat, friends, and drink, and be made drunk"; Migne's setting lacks *et bibite*, "and drink"] The good eat while they receive spiritual consolation, [0498A] while they are fed with the love of Christ, while they are enlightened in faith, while they are consoled in hope, while they are kindled with charity, while they are delighted with justice, truth, chastity and the other virtues. For every virtue feeds the mind while it refreshes it with its own delight. The food of the good is also Scripture, while it is rubbed, while it is chewed, because there lie hidden in it spiritual senses which instruct the mind and feed it. But in this Scripture the evil receive not food but disease, nor do they take from it edification but inflation. For they weigh out the argument [var: Vulg. Isai. 55:2 *argentum*, "silver"] of it not in bread, and their labor not in fullness [n: (Isai. LV)]. But the good have in it as many *fercula*, as many refreshments, as they have spiritual meanings. The good hunger for it; through it they escape dangers, as it is written: *In famine [0498B] he shall deliver thee from death, and in war from the hand of the sword [n: (Job. V)]*. For while they hunger for the word of God and refresh themselves with it, they are delivered from the death of sin and are fortified in the war of temptation, so that, strengthened and instructed by its food, they are neither cast down nor led astray. But the good are wont now to be cherished with consolations, now to be filled with bitternesses, now to abound in grace, now to labor under listlessness. But they have been taught from Scripture that temptation is a testing, and they are wont to ascribe the very burden of listlessness to temptation, when, namely, the flesh struggles against the spirit and wishes to be made equal to the heart, or even to be preferred. They know too that sadness itself is sometimes of more profit to thee than for thee to be without it: *For the Lord is near to those who are troubled in heart,* says the Psalmist: *And the humble of spirit he will save [n: (Psal. XXXIII)]*. The tribulation [0498C] which they suffer makes the Lord a neighbor to the good, and the very failing through which they are the more humbled. Sometimes the Lord so forsakes his own that he as it were slays them, and they grieve that all the things they seemed before to have are extinguished; but while they are thus slain they hope, as it is read in Job: *Although he should slay me, I will hope in him [n: (Job. XIII)]*. They hope while they are thus slain, and they live, because the little fire of charity preserves them, by which, thus destroyed and laid low, they keep their intention of living rightly for God and of in no way sinning mortally. And so while the good tell tales, the Lord is near to them; while they are slain, they hope. But if he should laugh toward them, they do not believe him, as Job likewise speaks concerning God: *If ever I laughed toward them, they did not believe me [n: (Job XX)]*. [var: Vulg. Job 29:24 *non credebant*, "they did not believe"; Migne's setting adds the object *mihi*, "me"] And so [0498D] the good do not quickly or rashly believe God when he laughs, because God knows both how to furnish the consolation of grace, and yet is wont to condemn those who are lifted up over their own good, and are ungrateful, and neglect the commandments. The good know indeed how to be consoled in adversity, they know how to be humbled in prosperity, they know how to be filled and to hunger, to abound and to suffer want. He who has been just will sometimes not lift up his head, filled with affliction and misery. The wicked, to whom in the time to come there shall be woe, are here sometimes refreshed even with inmost gifts: *The enemies of the Lord,* he says, *have lied to him, and he fed them with the fat of wheat, and filled them with honey out of the rock [n: (Psal. L)]*. They are therefore called enemies, and yet they are filled not so much from the wheat, whereby [cj: *qua*; read *quam*, "as"] from the fat of the wheat, receiving even mystical and inward things. And indeed God does what belongs to love, what belongs to friendship, [0499A] even to the unworthy. But he who receives grace from him and does not in turn show himself a friend by answering to the grace and by working worthily from it, makes himself an enemy, and turns it to the heaping up of his own damnation. And so grace refreshes the good; it refreshes the reprobate also. A man has food also from nature herself, because a more cheerful man feeds with the food of sweetness. He has it also from accidental causes: for prosperous things refresh while they turn out well. That food is not the spiritual food with which Christ refreshes. And yet for the most part it is the food of the imperfect, and the herb of the weak. And often this herb usefully feeds the weak, as Paul says: *He that is weak, let him eat herbs [n: (Rom. XIV)]*. For it lightens and in part cures the disease of listlessness which the mind suffers from the want of grace. O how frequently the imperfect and those ignorant of grace [0499B] are moved by carnal joy or by natural briskness, and think themselves moved by spiritual consolation! For often a man feels something from the enemy or from his own heart, and believes this to be from the Holy Spirit. And what wonder if the enemy mingle himself in devotion and speak false things, who spoke even in the prophets themselves. Who also sometimes said from their own spirit what they believed themselves to say from the Holy Spirit. A man ought not therefore to follow the suggestion or the experience of his own heart, since he knows that in matters of this kind certain prophets were sometimes deceived. Nor is it only in the state of sin that these dangers must be guarded against, but also in grace itself it must be prudently attended to what is to be done. *Drink wine with wisdom,* says Solomon, [0499C] signifying grace by the wine [n: (Eccli. IX)]. And again it is written: *That the Holy Spirit will withdraw himself from thoughts that are without understanding [n: (Sap. I)]*. God therefore grants grace, but the use of it lies under man's charge, because one man works through it more usefully and more healthfully than another. He ought therefore to consider the manner of the grace by which he is affected.

For the Holy Spirit is called single and manifold, because, though he is one, he nevertheless visits and affects minds in diverse ways. The soul therefore ought to hear what and how the Lord God speaks in her. And when he speaks, in the midst of the hearing he must be asked to lead her as she hears, and to teach how the meditations are to be directed. For then spiritual meditations offer themselves, so that the mind sits as it were a king, with an army of them standing round about it. And so it must rule these [0499D] and discern which it should chiefly admit, in which it should exercise itself more, how long it should cleave to each one, when it should pass over from that one to this. For every meditation is a *ferculum*, because it feeds the mind; but that devotion itself may be kindled and that thou mayest feast the longer, when thou hast been refreshed with this *ferculum* and its savor has begun to cease, thou usefully takest another. Turn thyself therefore to another meditation which shall have offered itself more nearly, and which thou shalt know to be like the preceding one and not different from it. For although all spiritual meditations are useful, such nevertheless is their manner that they build up in a diverse, nay sometimes even in a contrary way, and now those, now these suit better and have savor for the mind. A mind affected thus these build up, and a mind affected thus others build up. But the consolation of the heart and spiritual [0500A] refreshment is indeed the gift of God, yet thou must cooperate with this gift and be prudently exercised in it. For God is the author and bestower of this refreshment; but man is the servant and the cook, so that he can prepare it for himself either well or badly. But when anyone has fixed his contemplation upon God himself, he will have less need of this alternation of thoughts, but will cleave to the highest good as long as it is given. For then Wisdom herself is the servant and the cook, and makes a rich banquet, since she offers herself for food. For there is no need of human labor or of preparation when the divinity brings in the *fercula* and herself serves the refreshment. God therefore feeds his friends with food, as has been said. But the dearest ones he more fully refreshes and gives to drink, nay with a more abundant grace he even makes them drunk; concerning which food some things [0500B] have been said. But of that drunkenness I am afraid to speak, because it has not been given me to speak of it out of my own judgment. For the soul which at the marriage of the Lamb is a wife, or a daughter, knows how to be made drunk with spiritual wine, she who at this banquet sits beside the sides of Christ, or about the table, as it is written: *Thy wife as a fruitful vine on the sides of thy house, thy children as young olive plants round about thy table [n: (Psal. CXXVII)]*. But I, after the manner of a poor man, am set apart not only from the table but even from the banquet; yet would that before the doors I might receive of that pleasant wine in my little cup. For I know that I am not fully cleansed from the filth of sin, and for this I shall not be admitted to the table of the sons and to their drink, nor draw near. I leave therefore the discoursing about this wine and this drunkenness [0500C] to those who have experience of it. But knowing my own folly and weakness, and attending also to the sores of many, I wish to lie (that is, to humble myself) before the door of the rich Christ, and to beg not only drink but food also from the table of my lords, or of my ladies, that is, of devout souls. For although the wife and the sons or the daughters feast splendidly, yet those who are lower and poorer shall eat and be refreshed from the leftovers of the table. I am unwilling therefore to blush to beg the nourishment of life from such, lest after this life I beg in vain with the foolish virgins. There those who beg suffer a repulse; but here, he that asks receives. And because it is given more willingly and more abundantly to acquaintances and to friends, I wish to show myself a friend of the good and to love their good. He who loves in another a good which [0500D] he himself does not have, Scripture says, it is imputed to him as though he had it. Whatever good therefore there shall be in any, I wish to love it, and to pray for the good that they may advance in the good, that they may be kept from evil and come through to life eternal. So do I wish to associate and to place myself with the good, that I may make myself a partner and a sharer in their merits also. I contemplate also that banquet of fat things full of marrow and of wine purified from the lees, that is, of souls carried over to the things above and purged from all the dregs of sin, and feasting at the table of Christ in his kingdom. There the elect are filled with the fat of wheat, that is, with the divinity of Christ; from this table I desire that crumbs be let fall to me by which I may deserve to be refreshed. And I find a reason why I should receive mercy from that table. For I rejoice together at the happiness of the blessed, [0501A] and give thanks that from the miseries of the world they have come through to the enjoyment of God. When therefore I intercede for those who fight and exult for those who triumph, I trust to be helped by the prayers of both as much as to be enriched by their merits. In short I think upon, and gather into one, all the good things of my Lord, namely the merits of Christ and of his members, whether of those reigning with him or of those still pilgrims on the earth; then the sacrament of the Eucharist, and also the other sacraments of the Church, various indulgences and many relaxations; and finally the mercies of the Lord bestowed in manifold ways in diverse places. When by thinking and by loving I turn all these things over, and implore them as much for myself as for many, I trust that in so general a comprehension and desire I am for a great part lifted out of my want, nay even enriched. [0501B] Whence now I desire to impart of the goods acquired to others who are in need. And so I consider the many who sin, and who by sinning die, some passing from the death of the flesh to eternal death. And since the heap of their misery is great, I consider on the contrary the great heap of the aforesaid goods. Then I stretch toward this by desire and by prayer, that I may set these goods against those miseries, and may implore for the miseries. To this manner of praying or of meditating I strive, and I am sweetly still, when I yet reach through to it. And so to the friends who eat, and to the dearest ones who are made drunk, I wish to be a friend, and their daily beggar.
