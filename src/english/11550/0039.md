## CHAPTER XXXIII. *On spiritual refreshment and the use of grace.* (cont.)

*I sleep, and my heart waketh.* The natural motions of the heart are various and innumerable. Whence in manifold ways a man is made sad by his own nature, or [0501C] made glad, is troubled, or is tranquil. He ought not therefore to believe these motions or to yield to them, because according to the measure of human mutability, or of outward causes, the heart is varied, so that it be sorrowful or cheerful, and be affected thus or thus. When therefore some faint-hearted man trembles so greatly that he all but despairs, he ought to believe that this dejection is of faint-heartedness and of a troubled heart, not of despair. For he ought to be confident about his own state, provided only that he be contrite, and have the intention of turning aside from evil and of doing good [n: (Psal. XXXVII)], and of in no way committing a mortal sin. According to this purpose anyone can hope that he is called holy (that is, that he has charity), by whatever burden or temptation he be pressed down. A man ought therefore, [0501D] in an adverse state, to be confident even in the prayers which he is then wont to pour out, and to make for himself the assurance of hope. For confidence hinders the sadness of adversity; nay rather, he is then in a better state than he was before, provided only that he has not fallen from charity through mortal sin, because charity advances as much through adversity as through prosperity. In an adverse state, therefore, and in dejection of mind, let him learn to be confident, and in prosperity not to be led astray by a false hope, but to exult in the Lord with trembling; because, even if consolation is at hand, a man does not know whether he is worthy of love or of hatred. Nor let him attend to the motions of his own heart, or to his experience, so as to measure his state by these, lest he be deceived. Let reason hold rule over sensuality, let it pardon the motions, let it govern the thoughts, let it keep watch, nay let it anticipate the watches, because by forestalling useless [0502A] thoughts it will the more easily repress them. Reason therefore ought to keep watch, and to keep the night watches over the flock of its own thoughts; and when that is neglected, the mind runs into blindness, so that a man knows himself less, sees less what he is, or what he ought to be, how much he is deceived, how much he offends. If therefore it keeps watch anxiously over the flock of good thoughts, so as to nourish these and to repress the bad, the divine grace will flash more largely over him; and while it shines and blazes round about it, he will receive strength against bad thoughts, so as to lull them and make them sleep. When reason thus holds rule and keeps watch, and sleeps, the motions of sensuality and the thoughts, a man can say: I sleep, and my heart waketh. For in that part in which sensuality holds dominion, a man wakes, and he is man, because the things which he then does are of humanity. [0502B] But while he practices spiritual things, he wakes in heart unto salvation, because he returns to his heart, and in heart bears care about inward and invisible things. Then sensuality sleeps, but reason wakes; bad thoughts sleep, and good ones wake; carnal desires and temptations are lulled; a spiritual gladness affects the mind, its understanding is opened, and a greater knowledge is bestowed upon it. With the eyes of the heart it contemplates that supernal joy, namely the praises and the song of the blessed spirits. This is the concert of heaven which God reveals to his own, but makes the reprobate to sleep, that is, hides it from them. And so the Bridegroom does not sleep to the concerts of heaven, because, every disturbance being lulled, he perceives it before the eyes of the heart, keeps watch for it, desires to be present at it, implores it. He prays therefore to the citizens of heaven. The citizens of heaven pray [0502C] for her; she prays to God, and she is roused by him to pray; she speaks to God, and hears God speaking, for he speaks in her peace unto his people, because he moves her and admonishes her to salvation and to the care of her neighbors. And this is what follows:

*The voice of my beloved knocking: Open to me, my sister,* etc. This is the voice of the beloved, that is, the inspiration which he instils in her heart, that, because he is the beloved, for love of him she may love her neighbors also and bear care of them, and study to come to their aid unto salvation. He wills, therefore, that by preaching, or by taking up governance, by admonishing or by consoling, or by praying, she prepare a way for him in others; whence, that he may kindle her the more to this, he calls her sister, that is, coheir; his love, that is, privy to his secrets; [0502D] his dove, that is, illumined by the Holy Spirit; immaculate, that is, cleansed from the filth of sins by herself. As if he said: Because I have bestowed so much upon thee, do thou also answer to my benefits, and about my members exercise the grace with which thou hast been more specially endowed. Because my head is full of dew, and my locks of the drops of the nights. As if he said: From this, that I am God, I abound in mercy and in the dew of grace; and these who cleave to me by faith are filled with the drops of sins and with blindness. For the head of Christ is God, and he himself, according as he is man, is the head of the Church, and received grace not by measure. He bewails therefore our misery, so that, because we are stained in manifold ways with sins, he may show that he mercifully wills to have mercy. For he is Hermon, that is, light exalted, from which descends [0503A] the dew of grace upon mount Sion, that is, upon holy Church, so that where the offense abounds, his grace may superabound. And so he admonishes his beloved to open to him, insinuating also that he wills both to have mercy and to illumine that blindness itself, right.
