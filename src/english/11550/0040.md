## CHAPTER XXXIV. *How grace consoles, and kindles, and on perfection and mortification.*

The bride, having heard the voice of the beloved knocking and admonishing her to the salvation and care of her neighbors, although she loves, and is ready to obey the will of the Bridegroom, nevertheless fears to forsake the consolation of contemplation, and to be entangled again in the acts of the world. Whence she adds: *I have put off my tunic, how shall I be clothed with it? I have washed my feet, how shall I defile them? [n: (Cant. V.)]* The tunic is an earthly [0503B] garment, whence by the tunic earthly action can be signified. She fears therefore to be wrapped in earthly acts which she recalls that she has cast off, and to be torn away from heavenly contemplation, to descend from the highest and spiritual things and to be taken up with the lowest. Recalling too that she has washed her feet, that is, her affections, she is afraid to stain them again; these feet she had set in the courts of Jerusalem, and had made them to stand there. These she washes with living waters, that is, with compunction of heart, and afterward makes them stand by contemplation in things supernal and heavenly; and she fears not without cause to defile them, if she should draw them away from these things and send them into outward care. Nor is it a wonder if she who had fixed her feet in heavenly things fears to practice earthly ones. While therefore she trembles, while she wavers, the beloved consoles her, and visits her with ampler [0503C] grace, penetrating her heart, and illuminating it with the ampler light of grace. And this is what follows:

*My beloved put his hand through the opening, and my belly swelled at his touch.* [var: Vulg. *intremuit*, "trembled"] Which visitation of grace she calls the sending of the hand through the opening. For he pours in grace as through a chink, when he does not flood the whole soul, but visits with a certain measure of grace; and her whom he thus visits in part, he does not fully illuminate. The hand of the beloved can be taken as this operation and gift of grace. But the belly is the mind itself, in which thoughts are cooked, as food in the belly. The beloved also sends his hand through the opening, when through the Holy Spirit he suggests to the soul the benefits which he has bestowed on her: that he redeemed her by his own death, that he conferred on her the gifts of his grace, that he preserved those same gifts by [0503D] keeping them. For he granted her life and mercy, and his visitation kept her spirit. He granted her life by dying for her, his mercy by pouring it in; his visitation keeps her by preserving her from the fall of sin. By bringing these things back to the memory of his beloved, God makes an opening and an entrance to her heart, whereby she may be touched and kindled to love, and may tremble, and fear, if she does not answer to so great benefits according to her power. So therefore, touched and made to tremble, she pardons the more [cj: *ignoscit*; read *ignescit*, "catches fire"] unto the love of God and of neighbor. And this is what follows:

*I arose that I might open to my beloved; my hands dripped down myrrh, my fingers are full of the most approved myrrh.* He rises to open to the beloved who, grace being received, is the more kindled to lay hold of it, [0504A] to love more fervently, to press more studiously upon the care and salvation of his neighbors. For she who had before opened to the beloved is the more kindled toward him, and presses upon the works of charity. She then exercises herself in the love of God or in contemplation, and expends works of mercy for the profit of her neighbors, as their necessity demands and the time requires. For some she prays, others she consoles, to others she gives counsel of salvation, and she prepares to undertake rule or to exercise the office of preaching, if the time should require, while she is thus visited by grace, and strives to answer grace and to conform herself to it. She is kindled also to mortify whatever carnal thing she detects in herself. But although she has mortified sins and vices, yet from the more abundant grace received she pardons the more [cj: *ignoscit*; read *ignescit*, "catches fire"] unto this, and finds the more that she repairs. [0504B] The more illuminated, the more she knows her own defects, and, being made better, she grows the more cheap in her own eyes. She then condemns the small defects which before she tolerated, and understands that she has committed many sins both in words and in thoughts, which before she reckoned as lesser evils. While she presses upon the labor and work of this mortification, her hands drip down myrrh, because this operation is understood by the hands, and by the myrrh this mortification is signified: *And her fingers are full of the most approved myrrh,* because she does all things both discreetly and with a pure intention. For by the fingers this discretion is signified, by which she rightly divides what she rightly offers, that is, she makes discrete what she well intends. But by the most approved myrrh is taken the pure intention in mortifying, [0504C] by which she so seeks God that she mortifies vain glory, and despises outward praises, and her eye, that is, her intention, looks always to God, namely, and to the future and true love. She is said to be full of this mortification also, because she exercises it both in mind and in body. For she chastises the body with abstinence, with vigils, and with the rest of labors, and cuts off delights and earthly pleasures, knowing that wisdom, or the grace of God, is not found in the land of those who live sweetly. But by as much as one is delighted in this sweetness, by so much the less is he lifted up in God. She restrains also sight and hearing from curiosity, which two Solomon declares insatiable, and by this represses the other senses, and holds them back from pleasure. She is not fed with sweet odors; she guards against unlawful touch. For she guards against familiarity [0504D] with women, and even freedom of speaking, because from such freedom one comes to the servitude of sin. For that common saying sometimes comes to pass, which says that through bad keeping the wolf has carried off the wool. Therefore she bridles the vice of gluttony, withdrawing from herself what delight craves, and granting what necessity requires. In want she keeps patience, in abundance temperance, knowing with the Apostle how to abound and to suffer want. There, therefore, she acquires merit where the evil heap up sin for themselves. Who in Job are said to be squalid with calamity and misery [n: (Job XXX)], because in clothing themselves pride is served, and in eating, gluttony and concupiscence. The devout soul therefore, while she takes food temperately, and withdraws from herself something that appetite requires, and pours a libation to God, merits even in eating, and offers myrrh pleasing [0505A] to God; thus she shows forth mortification in the body, and fulfills this also in the mind. She resists also carnal delight, so that if she feel it, she never consents to it. She struggles also against the motions of anger and of envy, and of the other vices. When she endures reproaches, she does not answer; she is not overcome by evil, but overcomes evil in good, loving her enemies and interceding for them; she burns with so great a love of Christ that she desires to die for Christ, or for the salvation of her neighbors. And if this is not granted, she fulfills only a certain substitute for martyrdom: namely, that when something delights her more according to human nature, she the more cuts it off from herself, and severs it, and slays the appetite for it. This is deservedly judged the most approved myrrh, because with such great perfection she immolates herself to God. [0505B] But the fingers of the Bridegroom are full of the most approved myrrh, when, keeping her heart with all watchfulness, she discusses innumerable thoughts and motions of the heart, and, repelling the harmful, receives the useful. Because therefore she approves the pure and reproves the impure, and with the sword of contrition cuts off and mortifies sins, while she distinguishes and accomplishes these things with perfect discretion, she is declared to have fingers full of the most approved myrrh.
