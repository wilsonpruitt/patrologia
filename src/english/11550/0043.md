## CHAPTER XXXVII. *On the quality of the members of the beloved.*

*His locks are as the upraised boughs of palms, black as a raven [n: (Cant. V)]*. The upraised boughs of palms are the *spatulae*, that is, the longer branches, ever tending to lofty things. The bride has expressed what the beloved is in himself; now she begins to set forth what he is in his members. Whose [0509C] locks she calls those who cleave to him in faithful service, and these she declares to be like the palm and the ravens alike. The palm below is rough, and wrapped about with tighter bark; above it is beautiful and ample, both in sight and in fruit. So the members of Christ below do hard things, and suffer adversities, and above, in the eyes of God, are beautiful and fruitful. For through the hard things of the body they purge themselves, and advance to the beauty of the soul and to spiritual sublimity. For whence they consider the supernal beauty, thence they gladly labor below. For their eyes are in their head, that is, in Christ, and in his golden head, that is, his divinity; thither they desire to reach, thither to tend, to be refreshed by his comeliness and to be irradiated by his splendor. For they sigh after that vision, after that beginning once seen, and [0509D] the days seem to them few for the greatness of love, and the tribulation light in comparison with the weight of glory. For the sufferings of this time are not worthy of the future glory of that vision. The consideration of glory and of the reward stirs up desire in them, and desire makes labors light, and carries upward. Whence therefore they consider and covet the supernal things, thence they are strengthened below; and whence they are strengthened below, thence they triumph over labors, and grow into the heights of the virtues. For the palm advances greatly by ascending on high, and holds forth victory by its own signification. The locks of the beloved are as the upraised boughs of palms, because the elect, while they ever lift themselves up to the heights of the virtues, at length through God's grace bring themselves through to victory. [0510A] In the lowest things their life is wrapped about as with many barks, while it is straitened by innumerable tribulation. But in the highest it is spread out, as with leaves of beautiful greenness, by the amplitude of the reward. Who are also called black as a raven: the blackness of the good is that they reckon themselves sinners, and bear the shame of their sins in their countenance. For they choose to be looked down upon, they bear contempt with an even mind, and mean works and a sordid habit they not only do not shun, but even seek, knowing that in this humility a precious treasure lies hid. This is the blackness of the good, in which they make themselves like to ravens, that they may be made like to Christ also. For humility is the virtue of Christ, and whoever has it will have also the likeness of Christ; and by as much as he is the blacker through it, by so much will he be the more like [0510B] to Christ, and the more humble in his own eyes. For the good are black, and despised in their own eyes, but beautiful and precious before God. And so, the locks of the beloved being described, she begins to describe his eyes, saying:

*His eyes are as doves upon the streams of waters, which are washed with milk, and sit beside the fullest floods.* The eyes of the beloved are the contemplatives, who contemplate supernal and spiritual things with the eyes of the heart. Who are as doves, because they live simply, and have a simple eye, that is, intention, so that their whole body is lightsome, that is, whatever they do or intend, they do for God's sake. They are doves also at the windows of their eyes, because they are borne toward earthly things neither by rapacity nor by love, having a dull sight in these, but in spiritual and salutary things [0510C] a sharp and clear one. This is the right eye, which, lest it scandalize us and lead us into error by harmful [sic: *nox a*] subtlety, cleaves continually to the documents of Scripture. And this is what is declared, that they are upon the streams of waters. Doves are wont to sit upon the streams of waters, that they may see the shadow of the hawk in the waters, and so, casting themselves upon the water, escape his talons. So the good see the shadows of the demons in Scripture, while they find there their every kind of deception, by which they are wont to deceive not only through open evils, but even through hidden ones. In which they see also that they mock now through the appearance of good, now through a false understanding, things which by the simple and by those less exercised in spiritual matters are believed sound and perfect. And so against every error or impurity [0510D] that the enemy might suggest, or that some erring and seduced man might affirm, the divine words must be looked into, and the things which are suggested or heard must be compared with these, that they may be received or confuted. For these are chaste words, silver tried by fire, proved from earthly things, and purged by the sevenfold Spirit. For whatever is consonant and agrees with the sacred Scripture which the Holy Spirit founded must be believed sound and whole, and whatever disagrees with it must be reckoned harmful. For that cannot be sound which in any way differs from the Holy Spirit. Streams too are wont to render the eyes of those who look into them clearer. So Scripture, looked into, makes the eyes of the heart more perspicacious, because the declaration of the words of God enlightens, and gives understanding [0511A] to little ones: for he who elucidates the words of God is enlightened, and is disposed to the understanding of the words of God: for the mystical senses, while they are searched out, instruct the mind, and open the sense to understanding. These doves too are washed with milk, that is, by the sweetness of their mother, grace, they are alike cleansed from sins and kept. For through spiritual consolation delight grows bitter and cheap, and earthly and carnal cleanness [cj: *munditia*; read *immunditia*, "uncleanness"] too; and through this one is pricked, so that he repents of what he has committed, and commits no others. Again, these washed doves sit beside the fullest floods. These fullest floods are the perfect joys of that life, beside which they sit, because they remove themselves from earthly delight or consolation, and study to conform themselves to the knowledge and contemplation of the supernal things, and to make them [0511B] near. For they lie down between the borders, that they may see that rest, because it is good, and the land, because it is best. They choose the utmost things of this world, because they take the supports of life for sustenance, not for pleasure, so that, dwelling thus in its borders, they may in some measure touch the borders of that life also, and lay hold of something of those joys. They lie down in the borders, because although they are here in body, there they dwell in mind, as the Apostle says: *Our conversation is in heaven [n: (Philip. III)]*. But because they use this world as though they used it not, and desire the future rest, and contemplate it in mind, they are declared to sit beside these floods. Which rest they would by no means see through contemplation, if they had not stillness of mind. Whence this sitting beside the floods [0511C] signifies their stillness, for to sit belongs to one who is still. And so, set upon the streams of waters and washed with milk, they sit beside the fullest floods, because, enlightened through Scripture, and cleansed from sins and vicious affections by the operation and sweetness of grace, they have attained stillness of mind and purity of heart. Through which two they draw a sweet draught from that living fountain, and thirst for the fountain itself, saying: *my soul hath thirsted after God, the living fountain; when shall I come, and appear before the face of God? [n: (Psal. XLI.)]* [var: Vulg. *fortem*, "the strong living God"] But those who are still imperfect and weak are not washed with this milk; because, although they have received the sweetness of consolation from God, yet not so full a cleansing alike from sins and from vices. Whence they sit [cj: *resident*; read *non resident*, "they do not sit"] beside these floods, [0511D] because they do not yet have this stillness of mind. Their sweet affection or joy is not perfect or pure, because it proceeds from a heart less cleansed. For there works together in them either natural lightness, or some prosperity, or a hidden temptation of sin. Such men through sweet affection are sometimes rolled down to the accustomed joy of carnality. For the fire of carnal concupiscence is still vehement in them through temptation, whence, cheerfulness being received, it is easily kindled, as though wood were applied. When their affection is directed toward God, it sometimes comes about that, from the occasion of places, the state, having been made lighter, is changed into a carnal one, so that, having begun with the spirit, they consummate in the flesh. Whence some in such a state suffer a motion of the flesh, or even a greater frailty. In these the sweet affection [0512A] is turned into sin. Whence, when they feel a motion of the flesh, they ought at once to draw themselves back from this sweetness, and turn to some good. For such men bitter things are more expedient than sweet, hard than soft: namely the bitterness and grief of penance, and the labor of the fight against the pleasures of the flesh, or of their own will. For harmful delight is better cured by its contrary; and sweet things, as they delight sensuality, so also make its strength. And so through hard things and labors false delight is extinguished, and stillness of mind is acquired, that there may be a sitting beside these floods, and that the supernal things may in some measure be contemplated.
