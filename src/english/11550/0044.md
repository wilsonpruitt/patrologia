## CHAPTER XXXVIII. *On the members of the beloved.*

*His cheeks are as garden-beds of spices planted by the perfumers* [0512B] [n: (Cant. V)]. The cheeks are the martyrs, who, pouring out their blood for the faith of Christ, blush as cheeks in the face of the Bridegroom. Deservedly beautiful, because they adorned the whole body of the Church, whose members they were. A great fire of love burned in them, which so inflamed them unto torments. Great was the sweetness within, which sweetened the most bitter death. This is charity, the love, namely, of God, the love of the highest good, strong as death, nay stronger than death. This had mortified in the martyrs the desires of the body, and therefore they prevailed to expose the body itself to death. Such men lived spiritually, not carnally. Now they themselves lived no longer, but Christ lived in them. Whence they could die for Christ, who lived in them, and whose spirit they had. These are the cheeks of the beloved, which blushed in the face of the militant [0512C] Church through the penalty of martyrdom, and in the triumphant Church blush with a special comeliness through the garden-bed. Which are as garden-beds of spices, and filled with virtues, which virtues after the manner of spices have alike a sweet refreshment and an odor. For the mind which they fill they refresh delightfully, and they send forth to others the odor of good repute and of example. These garden-beds are planted by the perfumers. The perfumers are the preachers, spiritual physicians, healing the languors of souls by their instruction. These have compounded a precious pigment for the faithful. a sweet draught, namely spiritual sentences with which they have steeped us. By these perfumers are these garden-beds planted, which virtues have been described by them, and written down in books, concerning which it is added:

[0512D] *His lips are lilies dropping the first myrrh.* The lips of the beloved are the preachers, because they speak his words, and announce his will. Which lips are as lilies, because they have the whiteness of innocence, and the odor of good fame. Their doctrine is beautiful, and is fragrant, because a clean life adorns and commends it. For the example of good work strengthens their preaching, and they easily persuade who accomplish in work as they teach in word. Because therefore they do what they teach, they are white and fragrant. These are the skilled physicians, first healing themselves, and taught from the remedy of their own disease to know and to heal the diseases of others. But they drop the first myrrh, when first of all they mortify themselves, and so [0513A] preach mortification to others, beginning to do, and so to teach. The first myrrh is also the penalty of martyrdom, which they drop upon the faithful, when they preach that the body is rather to be exposed to death than that one should depart from God, or commit a mortal sin. The death of Christ too is the first myrrh, which they specially preach. For he himself is the head of martyrdom, from whom martyrdom chiefly began, and through whom the faithful were strengthened to endure martyrdom. The first myrrh is the best; whence it signifies the death of Christ, which was best, because it was the redemption of the whole world. This these lips drop, that is, the preachers, and they set it before the faithful to be imitated, and always to be held in memory. This, thought upon, mitigates the goads of the flesh, extinguishes the temptations of the enemy, consoles in desperation, blots out [0513B] sins, mortifies vices, strengthens the mind, confers grace. The death of Christ, his griefs and labors, are our merits, not his own. For all the things which he did or endured, he merited not for himself, but for us. Coming also in death to the tribunal of Christ, we ought to place our confidence chiefly in his merits, and, for the evils committed or the goods omitted, to set these against the accusing enemy. And so the death of Christ is the first myrrh, the best myrrh, because it mortifies all our evils, so powerful that there is nothing so unto death, and unto eternal death, which it does not loose and quicken. There follows:

*His hands are turned on the lathe, golden, full of hyacinths.* The hands of the beloved are the works of the good, which are turned on the lathe, because they are straight and perfect. As a lathe-turning [0513C] is rolled straight and smooth, and suffers in itself nothing uneven, no obstacle; so the good work nothing reprehensible, doing nothing in which a neighbor may be offended, or scandalized, or weakened. And so the working of the good is straight and polished, because they seek heavenly things alone, not turning aside to the right or the left, but walking by the royal way. For they rule themselves, and direct themselves to that which is salutary and expedient, not to that which human cupidity seeks. They exercise themselves more where the advancement is greater, and less there where the profit is less. For many are exceedingly anxious about the least things and neglect the greater, performing works of the kind that are good, but not doing them in charity. They fortify indeed the castle of their soul with many works, [0513D] but they leave one opening for the enemy by which he may enter. Of which castle this will be the firm fortification, that a man always have the intention of not committing a mortal sin, and, if he shall have committed one, of repenting at once. And likewise the solicitude of knowing mortal sins, and of being secretly contrite for those which he is not able to know. The working of the good, therefore, is illuminated by divine wisdom. Whence these hands are called golden, because they shine through divine wisdom, and are not darkened through sin or through ignorance. They shine also before men to the glory of the heavenly Father, because they are not darkened by sins or by indiscretion, and all the things which they do are done for God's sake, and with a simple intention. Whence it is added that they are full of hyacinths, because they are done by hope and desire of heavenly things alone: for the hyacinth [0514A] is a stone of an airy and green color. These hands are full of hyacinths, because all the working of the good, every intention, seeks the supernal reward, and God's good pleasure. For this cause it grows bright after the likeness of the sapphire, and is fresh in good, that it may please God, and may enjoy God. They are full of hyacinths, therefore, so that no place lies open in them for vain praise; they do not seek it, they do not ever receive it when offered, but always look to this stone, to the heavenly beauty, to that precious pearl, and for its sake reckon all things cheap and despise them. This stone too is changed with the air; so are the good, because they are changed according to the time. For now they exercise rigor, now clemency; now they amend excesses, now they dissemble them. And these things they do not through negligence, but through discretion. For all things [0514B] have their time, and those who do not observe the opportunity of the time deprive themselves of the fruit of profit. For sometimes an evil is better tolerated than reproved. And so according to the time and the manners of men they change themselves, nor by this mutability do they become unstable, but firm and stable. Because, while they are changed that they may better bring their causes through to effect, in the things which they intend to do they always prosper, and suffer no mutability of impediment. For they change the manner, but not the mind, and the same intention remains, although not the same action. There follows:

*His belly is of ivory, set with sapphires.* The belly of the beloved is the good married, keeping themselves chastely and using this world as though they used it not. Now the elephant is a chaste animal and of a cold nature. Whence it also [0514C] signifies those who are joined, or know one another carnally, not in order to satisfy lust, but either for the sake of begetting offspring, or of avoiding incontinence. For the intention of offspring has merit; incontinence to be avoided, pardon: for matrimony excuses it; the cause of lust, damnation, because it is a mortal sin. And so married persons who serve their own pleasure are not truly the beloved's, but the possession of the devil; nor has Christ a dwelling in these, but the devil has power: as the angel taught Tobias, saying that the demon has power over those who so undertake marriage that they are at leisure for their own lust, like the horse and the mule [n: (Tob. VI)]. But the belly of the Bridegroom is set with sapphires. The sapphire is a stone having the appearance of a serene [0514D] sky. Whence it signifies the good married, who are steeped in heavenly disciplines, meditate on the supernal things and have a taste for them. These are set with sapphires, because amid worldly acts they do heavenly works also. For although they are divided, so that they think on the things that are of the world, how they may please their spouse, they nonetheless think on heavenly things, and how they may please their Maker. This is the setting of the sapphire, that walking in the flesh they war not according to the flesh, but their conversation is in heaven
