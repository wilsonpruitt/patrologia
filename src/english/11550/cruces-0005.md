## 1. The Song lemmata — every named word checked against the received text

The four Song lemmata in this range were read word by word against the Sixto-Clementine
Vulgate before anything was written. **Three of the four agree with the received text
exactly; the fourth diverges by one pronoun.** Recorded in full so that the absence of a
`[var:]` on them is evidence rather than silence.

| chunk | col | printed lemma | Vulg. | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0005 | 0416B | *Invenerunt me vigiles qui custodiunt civitatem* | Cant. 3:3, identical | agrees — no marker |
| 0006 | 0419A | *Paululum cum pertransissem eos, inveni quem diligit anima mea* | Cant. 3:4a, identical | agrees — no marker |
| 0007 | 0421A–B | *Tenui **illum**, nec dimittam, donec introducam illum in domum matris meae, et in cubiculum genitricis meae* | Cant. 3:4b *tenui **eum**, nec dimittam…* | **one-word divergence — see §1a** |
| 0009 | 0426A | *Adjuro vos, filiae Hierusalem, per capreas, cervosque camporum, ne suscitetis, neque evigilare faciatis dilectam, donec ipsa velit* | Cant. 3:5, identical in wording | agrees — no marker; see §1b on *Hierusalem* |

### 1a. col 0421A–B — *Tenui illum* for Vulg. *tenui eum*. NO `[var:]`, deliberately.

Migne prints *Tenui illum, nec dimittam* where the Clementine has *tenui eum, nec dimittam*.
The divergence is real and is recorded here, but **no marker fired**, because Pattern 14's own
threshold is a divergence "in a way that changes the sense," and *illum* for *eum* changes
nothing: both are the accusative masculine pronoun with the same referent, and *illum* is in
any case the pronoun the very next clause of the received text itself uses (*donec introducam
**illum** in domum*). Richard's own re-quotations at 0421C and 0422A both print *illum*, so it
is his consistent form, not a one-off slip. Firing here would put a maroquin note on a page
where a reader has nothing to learn, which is the marker-spam failure Pattern 16 warns of in
its own domain.

⚠ **This is the entry to overturn if the work's editorial line is that pronoun-level lemma
divergence should be visible.** The judgment is mine and it is the only sub-threshold call in
the range; the evidence is stated above so a later reader can reverse it in one edit.

### 1b. col 0426A — *Hierusalem* for *Jerusalem*: a spelling, and NOT a defect.

Per Pattern 9 and the brief. The English reads "Jerusalem"; the Latin twin keeps *Hierusalem*
verbatim, so nothing is concealed. Same treatment at 0427B/0427C (*atriis Hierusalem*) and
0428B (*Filias ergo Hierusalem*). Likewise *genitricis* for *genetricis* at 0421B, and
*coelorum* / *coelestibus* throughout: attested medieval orthography, no italics, no marker.

## 2. `[var:]` — Migne's scripture diverges from the received text (2)

### 2a. 0007 @0422B — John 14:23, *mandata mea servabit* for Vulg. *sermonem meum servabit*

> *Si quis diligit me,* inquit, *mandata mea servabit, et Pater meus diliget eum…*

Vulg. Jn 14:23 reads *Si quis diligit me, sermonem meum servabit*. Migne's *mandata mea* is
the wording of Jn 14:21 (*Qui habet mandata mea et servat ea*) drawn into 14:23 — a conflation,
whether Richard's, his copy-text's, or the compositor's, and Pattern 14 forbids guessing which.

**This is the range's most consequential divergence, and it is consequential precisely because
it is invisible.** Richard's whole gloss hangs on the commandments: the sentence introducing
the quotation is *quae mandata illius servat*, and the sentence closing the paragraph is *qui
mandata custodierit* (0423A). A translator who supplied the familiar "will keep my word" would
have produced a smooth page on which the proof-text no longer proves what Richard adduces it
for — 7914 @1079C's shape exactly. Rendered as printed, with
`[var: Vulg. *sermonem meum servabit*, "will keep my word"]` beside it.

Pattern 5 note: *inquit* sits **outside** the italics between two italic runs in the Latin; the
English reproduces that division exactly (`*If any one loves me,* he says, *he will keep…*`).
No guillemets anywhere in this range, and none were introduced.

### 2b. 0007 @0423A — Job 14:15, *Voca me* for Vulg. *Vocabis me, et ego respondebo tibi*

> *Voca me et respondebo tibi,* ait B. Job

Migne turns the future indicative into an imperative and drops *ego*. The sense changes: the
received text is God's promise ("Thou shalt call me, and I will answer thee"), Migne's is the
speaker's petition ("Call me and I will answer thee"). Richard's gloss reads the printed form
as a **sequence of two agents** — *Vocat Deus per gratiam visitantem, et respondebit homo per
mandatorum impletionem* — which the imperative supports and the Vulgate future does not
naturally, so the printed reading is what the exposition needs. Rendered as printed with the
received reading beside it.

### 2c. 0008 @0425A — Isa. 42:15, *germen* for Vulg. *gramen*

> *Desertos faciam,* ait Dominus, *montes et colles, et omne germen eorum exsiccabo*

Vulg. Isa. 42:15: *Desertos faciam montes et colles, et omne gramen eorum exsiccabo*.
*germen* ("shoot, bud") for *gramen* ("grass") — marked
`[var: Vulg. (Isa. 42:15) *gramen*, "grass"]`. Rendered "all their shoot," which is what is
printed; the gloss that follows (*per montes, majores… per colles, minores*) hangs on the
mountains and hills, not on the herbage, so nothing in the argument is disturbed either way —
but the word is a different word and the reader is entitled to know.

⚠ **The note on that verse is misattributed and is reproduced misattributed.** Migne prints
`[n: (Psal. XLII)]`; the verse is **Isaiah** 42:15, not Psalm 42, whose text has no such
clause. The `[n:]` content is Migne's citation and passes through verbatim under the sacred-
marker rule; the correction is recorded here and nowhere else in the text. Same class, 0005
@0416C: Migne cites `[n: (Job I)]` for *in terra pedicam abscondit, et decipulam super semitam
ponit*, which is **Job 18:10**. Both left verbatim. (No `citation-corrections.json` entry
proposed from this range — neither has a following *Ibid.* depending on it, but the runbook's
false-positive 2 says corrections are upstream of anaphora resolution and this range is not the
place to decide it.)

## 3. `[sic:]` — carried type (6)

In every case below the marked run is verbatim in the Latin twin (checked mechanically), and in
every case the **force the run carried is supplied in plain words beside the marker**, per 7a⁗.

### 3a. 0006 @0420D — *diliculum* for *diluculum*

> *quod ad diliculum, et non ad plenam diem venerit*

The same word is printed correctly twice in the immediately preceding lines (*fit diluculum quo
surgit*, 0420C; *et diluculum compunctionis hominem sibi demonstrat*, 0420D), which is what
makes *diliculum* this plate's own defect rather than an orthographic convention — an i/u
interchange is not a medieval spelling class, so Pattern 9 does not exempt it. Rendered
**"that he has come to daybreak [sic: *diliculum*], and not to the full day"**: the noun is the
whole object of *ad*, so quarantining it bare would have left the clause with no destination
and the antithesis with only one term (7a⁗ q1, in its "the hole is the size of a lexeme" form).
The English therefore says *daybreak* and the marker sits beside it.

### 3b. 0006 @0420D — *exignum* for *exiguum*

> *ita per eamdem intelligit exignum esse quod invenit*

An n/u misreading; *exignum* is not a Latin word. Rendered **"understands that what he has
found is but little [sic: *exignum*]"** — the predicate adjective is the whole point of the
sentence (and is picked up nine lines later by *paululum se memorat invenisse dilectum*), so
again the sense stands outside the quarantine.

### 3c. 0007 @0423B — *id* for *in*

> *sicut etiam virtus id vitium vertitur, si nimie, vel extra tempus exerceatur*

*id* is a real word but has **no grammatical slot** here: *vertitur* + accusative *vitium*
requires the preposition, and "virtue is turned that vice" construes as nothing. Pattern 12's
first failure case, so the type is carried; and because what fell out is a **preposition**,
7a⁗-b applies — the English reads **"is turned into [sic: *id*] vice"**, with the preposition's
force in plain words. `[cj:]` was considered and declined: Pattern 18's 2026-08-15 widening is
scoped to negations and correlatives and was explicitly not read wider.

### 3d. 0007 @0423B — *ac* for *ad*

> *nec cessandum, sed per hanc progrediendum, donec ac perfectionem veniatur*

Identical shape: *ac* is a real word with no slot (accusative *perfectionem* is left stranded),
and the missing element is again a preposition. Rendered **"until one comes to [sic: *ac*]
perfection."** Note that Richard prints *ad perfectionem* correctly three lines later
(*ad perfectionem non pertingit*, 0423C), which is the internal evidence for the conjecture.

### 3e. 0009 @0427B — *vineunt* for *vincunt*

> *Cervi quoque spineta transeunt, et venenum vineunt.*

Not a word. **This is the range's clearest 7a⁗ q1 site**: the marked run is the clause's only
predicate, so a bare marker would have left "and the harts … venom." Rendered **"and overcome
[sic: *vineunt*] venom"**, and the conjecture is secured by the next sentence, which prints the
verb correctly and in the same sense (*venenum suggestionum inimici malitiamque mundi
**vincentes***).

### 3f. 0009 @0427D — *Die* for *Dic* (Luke 10:40)

> *Die illi, ait Martha, ut me adjuvet, [n: (Luc. X)]*

Vulg. Lk 10:40: *dic ergo illi ut me adiuvet*. *Die* is a real form (ablative of *dies*) with no
slot — the clause has no other verb, and "on the day to her, says Martha, that she help me"
construes as nothing. Treated as carried type rather than as a Pattern 14 divergence
**because the defect is a compositor's c→e, not a variant reading**: the shape of the error, not
how it reads, is what separates Patterns 12 and 14 here. Rendered **"Tell [sic: *Die*] her, says
Martha, that she help me"** — the imperative supplied in plain words beside the marker (7a⁗ q1),
the marker wrapping only *Die* and not *illi*, since *illi* is not part of the defect.

Migne's comma before the `[n:]` and his semicolon after it are both kept (Pattern 8).

## 4. `[cj:]` — the printed word is real and the faithful English misleads (3)

### 4a. 0006 @0420D — *in perfectione* for *imperfectione*

> *Sic homo, percepta gratia et per hanc cognita in perfectione sua, vultum pallio operit*

*in perfectione sua* is impeccable Latin and renders in place — "known, through it, in his
perfection" — **and that is exactly the problem.** The clause it stands in says the opposite:
the man covers his face *quia quanta ignorantia tegatur agnoscit*, the paragraph's thesis is
*intelligit exignum esse quod invenit*, and the chapter ends *non perfecte, nec ad votum*. The
faithful English asserts a knowledge of his own perfection in a passage entirely about his
imperfection. Glossed additively:
`in his perfection [cj: *in perfectione*; read *imperfectione*, "his imperfection"]`.

⚑ Note this is also a **word-division** defect (7a⁗-b), of the *fused/split* kind that no
mechanical test sees — here in the split direction, where the split happens to produce two real
words forming a valid phrase. That combination is why `[sic:]` is wrong for it (it renders in
place) and why `[cj:]` is right (the rendering asserts something false).

### 4b. 0007 @0421D — *vivendus* for *videndus*

> *Puritati enim cordis vivendus Deus promittitur, sicut scriptum est: Beati mundo corde,
> quoniam ipsi Deum videbunt*

*vivendus* is a real gerundive. Rendered
`God is promised as to be lived [cj: *vivendus*; read *videndus*, "to be seen"]` — and the
evidence sits in the same sentence: the proof-text Richard adduces is *ipsi Deum **videbunt***,
and the two clauses before it are *Deum contempletur* and *Israel videns Deum dicitur*. Nothing
in the passage is about living; the whole chain is about seeing.

### 4c. 0007 @0423B — *delictum* for *dilectum*

> *Sponsa ergo affectuose, pariter et desideranter delictum quaesivit, inventum vero ut ad se
> introduceret non minus sollicita fuit.*

*delictum* ("offence, sin") is a real word and renders in place, producing "the bride sought
the offence affectionately and longingly" — a sentence Richard is not merely not arguing but
arguing against, and whose own second clause (*inventum vero ut ad se introduceret*) has a
masculine antecedent that *delictum* cannot supply. Glossed
`the offence [cj: *delictum*; read *dilectum*, "the beloved"]`. The single-letter
*dilectum*/*delictum* pair is a standing hazard in this work (*dilectus* is the fixed
vocabulary's own head-word), so it is worth a look in every range.

## 5. Would not construe / rendered as closely as the printed words allow (no marker)

Each of these is a real word or phrase that renders in place, does **not** assert something the
author argues against, and therefore takes no marker under Pattern 18's own bar. They are here
because Pattern 7 requires the log.

- **0007 @0421B — *effectum* for *affectum*.** *Cupit ergo effectum suum purgari, voluntatem
  mortificari* — the parallel members are *voluntatem*, *spiritum suum*, and (at 0421B, nine
  words earlier) *carnaliter affici*, so *affectum* is near-certain, and the fixed vocabulary
  makes *affectus* = affection. Rendered **"that her effect be purged"** as printed. Declined
  for `[cj:]` because "her effect" is opaque rather than false — it makes no claim Richard
  denies. ⚠ The nearest call in the range; if Pattern 18 is read one notch wider it belongs
  there.
- **0008 @0424B — *expungit mente* for (probably) *compungit mentem*.** *quia nunc expungit
  mente desiderio divini amoris, nunc spe et gaudio…* — every other member of the long series
  is a bare instrumental ablative and the sentence has no object at all, which is what suggests
  *mentem*; and *compunctio* is this work's own fixed term, used three times in the neighbouring
  columns. Rendered **"now he pricks with the mind by the desire of divine love"** — the printed
  case kept, the verb rendered at its nearest English sense. No marker: the ablative is
  grammatical, and English can carry it.
- **0007 @0422B — *minus perfectiorem*.** *interdum plus afficit minus diligentem et minus
  perfectiorem* — a double comparative. Rendered literally, **"and is less more perfect."**
  Comparative reversal is one of 7a's five named mechanisms, so smoothing this to "less
  perfect" would have been the silent repair; the awkwardness is Migne's.
- **0007 @0423C — *cognito periculo suo hanc recipit*.** *Revelat enim mala sua homini, et
  roborat eum ut corrigere possit, cognito periculo suo hanc recipit, qui per hanc se corrigere,
  et ad interiora progredi negligit.* As printed the sentence contradicts itself: the man who
  neglects to correct himself is said to receive grace. *in vacuum hanc recipit* is the obvious
  conjecture (and the phrase *in vacuum hanc recipiat* is printed verbatim in the very next
  sentence). Rendered as printed — **"his own peril having been known, he receives it, who
  neglects to correct himself through it"** — and declined for `[cj:]` because the defect is an
  omission of a phrase, not a single printed word the gloss could open on.
- **0007 @0422C — *quid quod***. *Unde etiam non diligere arguebantur, quid quod delectaret
  magis quam quod expediret amplectebantur.* Read as the classical idiom *quid quod…* ("what of
  it, that…"), which is real Latin and needs no marker, and rendered so. Migne closes the
  sentence with a **period**, not a question mark; the period is kept (Pattern 8), which is why
  the English reads as a statement rather than a question.
- **0009 @0428C — the *aestuet* clause.** *non vult exterius implicari, nec curarum saecularium
  aestuet humanae conversationis et mutabilitatis vento contingat eam ab eo exiguo quod accepit
  exsiccari.* Two subjunctives (*aestuet*, *contingat*) with no connective between them; the
  underlying text was very likely *nec curarum saecularium aestu et… vento contingat*, i.e. an
  ablative *aestu* + *et*, which the compositor has fused into a verb. **Nothing was supplied**:
  the single printed *nec* is distributed over both verbs — "nor let him seethe with worldly
  cares, nor let it befall her… to be dried up" — which is the closest English the printed words
  allow without inventing a *lest* the plate does not have. ⚠ Note *contingat **eam*** is
  feminine while the subject of the paragraph is masculine *imperfectus*; the English keeps
  "her," because reassigning the pronoun to match the subject would be exactly 7a's fifth
  mechanism (reference mis-bound).
- **0005 @0416D — *linita*.** *qui utrumque postem suum et superliminare domus linita non habent
  de agni Dei sanguine* — neuter plural participle against *postem*/*superliminare*. Pattern 9:
  Latin agreement has no English exponent, nothing to emend either way. Rendered "anointed."
  **Flagged so a later sweep does not churn it.**

## 6. Where a gloss hangs on the printed word (Pattern 14 / §Song case 2)

### 6a. 0005 @0417B — *pacatae* vs *pacificatae*: the words are MENTIONED, so they stay Latin

> *Unde dicit beatus Job quod bestiae terrae (daemones videlicet) pacificatae fiunt bonis.
> Non dicit pacatae, quia pacem non habent, sed pacificatae, quia pacem eis faciunt dum eos
> impugnando affligunt.*

Richard's argument **is** the choice of word, so the two words in the second sentence are
*mentioned*, not used, and they stay in italic Latin — the treatment the dialogue register
already prescribes for a word discussed as a word. In the first sentence the phrase is *used*,
and there it is Englished as **"are made peace-making toward the good"**, chosen so that the
etymology the gloss depends on (*pacem… faciunt*) is visible in English too. Rendering both as
"peaceable" would have made Richard's *Non dicit… sed…* say nothing — the *lectulus* failure in
another dress.

Divergence recorded, no marker: Vulg. Job 5:23 reads *bestiae terrae pacificae erunt tibi*.
Migne's *pacificatae fiunt bonis* is woven into an indirect statement governed by *dicit
beatus Job*, i.e. it is not quoted **as** scripture, so Pattern 14 does not fire — but the
divergence is precisely the one Richard's gloss exploits, and *pacificatae* (the past
participle, "made peace-making") is not the Vulgate's adjective *pacificae* at all.

### 6b. 0007 @0421D — *supplantator* / *videns Deum*

*Jacob enim supplantator dicitur… quia Israel videns Deum dicitur*, with *ut amplius vitia
**supplantet*** in between. The etymologies survive translation intact here, so the words are
Englished in their printed italics — **"Jacob is interpreted *supplanter*"** / **"Israel is
interpreted *seeing God*"** — and the hook the gloss hangs on (*supplanter* → *supplant*) is
carried in English. No Latin needed to be kept, and none was.

## 7. Non-material divergences recorded for completeness (no markers)

Scripture Migne weaves into Richard's own sentences rather than quoting as a lemma. None
changes an argument; all are logged so that a later reader knows they were seen and weighed.

- 0005 @0416B — Heb. 1:14, *qui haereditatem **capiunt** salutis* for Vulg. *capient*.
- 0005 @0417B — Isa. 10:7, *ad **interemptionem** gentium non paucarum* for Vulg.
  *ad internecionem*. (The *non paucarum* is the Vulgate's own; carried, per negation fidelity.)
- 0006 @0420D — 3 Reg. 19:12, *sibilum aurae **levis*** for Vulg. *sibilus aurae tenuis*.
- 0007 @0423A — Matt. 7:21, *Non omnis qui dicit: Domine Domine* — Migne drops the Vulgate's
  *mihi*. Printed in roman, not as a lemma.
- 0009 @0428D — Eccle. 10:1, *muscae morientes… perdunt **suave unguentum** gratiae* for Vulg.
  *perdunt suavitatem unguenti*. Migne has recast the noun phrase and Richard has inserted his
  own gloss (*id est cogitationes mundanae*) into the middle of it, so it is his sentence, not a
  quotation; rendered as printed.
- 0009 @0428A — Luke 10:41, *Martha sollicita dici possit circa **pauca*** against the Vulgate's
  *sollicita es et turbaris erga plurima*. **This is deliberate authorial inversion, not a
  divergence**: Richard's whole point is that beside Mary's *circa multa* Martha is the one busy
  about few things. No marker, no crux beyond this line.

## 8. Fixed-vocabulary compliance, and the one tension

Followed throughout: *sponsa* = the bride · *sponsus* = the Bridegroom (capitalized) ·
*dilectus* = the beloved · *meditatio* = meditation · *contemplatio* = contemplation ·
*compunctio* = compunction · *mens* = mind · *anima* = soul · *affectus* = affection /
*affectuose* = affectionately · *quies* = stillness / *vacare* = to be at leisure.
*lectulus* / *lectus* do not occur anywhere in 0005–0009, so the diminutive is untested in this
range.

⚠ **One tension, followed against my own judgment and logged rather than decided.** At 0007
@0421C Migne prints *perhibet se non **quieturam**, nec cessaturam a precum instantia* — the
verb of *quies*, but here meaning to stop praying, not to be inwardly still. The fixed entry
("stillness, of the mind's rest") would give "she will not be still," which in this clause reads
as its near-opposite: the sentence is about her refusing to leave off importunity, and the
stillness she is seeking is the very thing she gets by not being still. Rendered **"she declares
that she will not rest, nor cease from insistence in prayers."** The noun *quies* is
"stillness" everywhere it occurs in the range (0424A ×2, 0425B, 0425D twice, 0426A, 0428A); only
this one verbal form departs, and the departure is flagged here rather than propagated.

Related, and not a departure: *requies* / *requiescat* (0426A, chapter head VIII) is a different
word and is rendered "rest" without tension.

3. **The two misattributed `[n:]` citations** (§2c: `(Psal. XLII)` for Isaiah 42:15 at 0425A;
