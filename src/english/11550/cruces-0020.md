## 2. ⛔ THE WORK'S KNOWN SPLIT-TYPE SITE — 0020, col. 0451D/0452A: *hos a tendit*

**Printed:** *Dum itaque anima devota **hos a tendit** et horum sanctitatem meditatur, et ad
eorum imitationem se erigere nititur…*

**Done:** the whole broken run is carried untranslated in italics inside a Pattern 12 marker —
`While therefore the devout soul attends to these [sic: *hos a tendit*] and meditates upon their
holiness` — and, per **7a⁗ question 1**, the verb the marker swallowed is supplied in plain
English **outside** the quarantine ("attends to these"). Without that, the first clause of a
three-clause *dum* period would have had no predicate at all, exactly the 1917A `[sic: *eccidit*]`
shape.

**Why, and the corroboration is inside this same chunk.** *a tendit* is *attendit* divided across a
space (Pattern 10, split direction; `translation-style.md` 7a⁗-b). Three sentences later Migne sets
the same verb correctly, of the same subject-matter:

> *Qui enim bene agentes **attendit**, et eorum virtutibus … delectatur* (col. 0452A–B)

and twice more in the same column — *quia semper bonos **attendunt*** and *quales **attendit***
(0452B–C). The reading is therefore not a conjecture at all; the plate itself prints *attendit*
three times within twenty lines of the defect.

**Scope of the quarantine.** The marker takes `hos a tendit` entire, including the well-formed
`hos`, per Pattern 10's *Ad dam* rule and per the launch brief's explicit instruction. This is the
7a⁗-b hazard in its pure form: a split run leaves a hole the size of a lexeme, and here the lexeme
was the clause's only finite verb. 9637's *in offenso* for *inoffenso* is the cautionary case in the
other direction — there, marking only part of the run would have swallowed a negating prefix. Here
nothing negative or interrogative is inside the marker, so 7a⁗ questions 2 and 3 pass; only question
1 needed answering, and it is answered in plain words beside the marker.

## 4. `[var:]` — non-Song scripture where Migne's wording is materially divergent

### 4a. 0022, col. 0460D — 2 Cor 12:10

**Printed:** *Paulus … dicit: **Quando infirmor tunc fortior sum et potens** [n: (II Cor. XVIII)].*
**Vulgate:** *cum enim infirmor, tunc potens sum.*
**Done:** rendered as printed — "When I am weak, then am I stronger and powerful" — with
`[var: Vulg. *cum infirmor, tunc potens sum*, "when I am weak, then am I powerful"]` placed **after**
the `[n:]` marker so the note's position in the marker sequence is untouched.
**Why material:** Migne's text carries a comparative (*fortior*) that the Vulgate does not have at
all, and doubles the predicate (*fortior sum et potens*). Richard adduces the verse precisely to
prove that a man *merits more* in the labour of temptation — the argument is comparative, and the
comparative in the quotation is what it rests on. Conforming this to *tunc potens sum* would have
removed the one word the proof-text was quoted for: 7a″'s mechanism exactly.

⚠ The note reads `(II Cor. XVIII)`. 2 Corinthians has thirteen chapters; the locus is 12:10. `[n:]`
contents are Migne's and pass through verbatim (see §7 below).

### 4b. 0023, col. 0462D — Eccli. 28:6

**Printed:** *Sicut enim in Ecclesiastico legitur: **In mandatis Dei tabitudo et mors**
[n: (Eccli. XXVIII)]*
**Vulgate:** *tabitudo enim et mors **imminent** in mandatis ejus.*
**Done:** rendered as printed — "In the commandments of God is wasting and death" — with
`[var: Vulg. *tabitudo enim et mors imminent in mandatis ejus*, "for wasting and death threaten in
his commandments"]`.
**Why material:** the received text makes wasting and death *threaten* (a warning against
implacability); Migne's, with *imminent* gone and the phrase re-ordered, makes them **contents** of
the commandments — and that is what Richard's gloss requires, since he reads *tabitudo* as
compassion and *mors* as mortification of the vices, i.e. as two commanded goods. The divergence and
the exposition stand or fall together, which is why the reading is rendered and not repaired.

## 5. `[cj:]` — the printed word is real Latin and the faithful English asserts something false

### 5a. 0021, col. 0455C — *Eliae* for *Eliu*

**Printed:** *sicut in Job legitur: En venter meus quasi mustum. Quod etiam ipsi **Eliae** contigit,
qui verba protulit per tumorem sui spiritus…*
**Done:** `Which happened also to Elias himself [cj: *Eliae*; read *Eliu*, "Elihu"]`.
**Why:** *Eliae* is a perfectly good form (Elias, gen./dat.), so Pattern 12 has nothing to wrap and
Pattern 7 renders it — and the page then attributes Job 32:19 to **Elijah**. The corroboration is
two lines later in the same sentence-group, in Richard's own voice: *quo se incitari **Eliu** apud
Job putavit*. He also uses *Elias* correctly of the real Elijah twice in this chunk (0456C, and
0454C in 0020), so the two names are distinguished everywhere else in the range. Additive gloss per
Pattern 18; nothing removed.

### 5b. 0022, col. 0461B — *se* for *si*

**Printed:** *quia, **se** recte offeras, et recte non dividas, peccasti, ait auctoritas*
(the *auctoritas* is Gen. 4:7 in its Old-Latin/LXX form, *si recte offeras…*).
**Done:** `because, itself [cj: *se*; read *si*, "if"] thou offer rightly, and divide not rightly,
thou hast sinned, says the authority`.
**Why `[cj:]` and not `[sic:]`:** the run is a real word, so Pattern 12's wrap has no purchase; but
what went wrong is **the conjunction that governs the mood of the whole protasis** — 7a⁗ question 3
— and a bare `[sic:]` would have parked the conditional inside the marker and let the two
subjunctives read as a bald assertion ("thou offerest rightly and dividest not rightly"). Pattern 18
is scoped to exactly this class ("incoherence is not a defence where the word that went wrong is a
negation or a correlative"), and its 11064 @1142D case is the mirror image (*si* for *sic*). The
negation *recte non dividas* is carried in the English unaltered.

### 5c. 0023, col. 0461D — *vitae* for *vittae*

**Printed:** *Labia ergo sponsae, quia verba comprimunt ne dissolute fluant, **vitae** comparantur.
Quia vero per dilectionem Dei tinguntur, coccino assimilantur.*
**Done:** `are compared to life [cj: *vitae*; read *vittae*, "a fillet"]`.
**Why:** the chapter's entire figure is the *vitta coccinea* of the lemma, and this very sentence is
the summary restatement of it, paired with *coccino assimilantur* in the next clause. The same
sentence in its first form, eight lines earlier, prints the word correctly: *Labia sponsae **vittae**
comparantur quia sicut **vitta** constringit crines*. "Compared to life" is fluent, false, and
exactly the Pattern 18 shape (a single-letter slip yielding a *confident wrong reading* rather than a
visible hole — the 11064 @1141B *carons*/*carens* hazard).

### 5d. 0023, col. 0463B — *convivium* for *convicium*

**Printed:** *nec invidet, nec **convivium** respondet, nec conviciati aliquo modo nocet*
**Done:** `nor does he answer a banquet [cj: *convivium*; read *convicium*, "a reproach"]`.
**Why:** "answer a banquet" is grammatical English and plainly not what Richard wrote; the same
clause ends *nec **conviciati** … nocet*, and the sentence before speaks of *stultitiam
**conviciantis***. The root is printed correctly twice within fifteen words of the defect.

## 6. `[sic:]` — carried type

### 6a. 0021, col. 0457C — *possis*

**Printed:** *maxime si aliquid ibi annexum sit, quod delectare humanitatem **possis***.
**Done:** `especially if something be annexed there which might be able to delight [sic: *possis*]
his humanity`.
**Why:** *possis* is second person singular stranded in a relative clause whose subject is *aliquid*
— the 11632 *ama*/*sternis* class (Pattern 12's test 1: a real word with **no grammatical slot**,
which therefore cannot be carried by any English). Per 7a⁗ question 1 the modal force is supplied in
plain words beside the marker so the clause keeps a predicate.

### 6b. 0021, col. 0457C — *benegnitas*

**Printed:** *cordis levitas, naturalis **benegnitas**, prosperitas, vel orta aliqua causa laetitiae*
**Done:** `natural [sic: *benegnitas*], prosperity, or some arisen cause of gladness`.
**Why:** metathesis of *benignitas*; not a word, so Pattern 7 has nothing to render and the run is
carried untranslated. 7a⁗ checks all pass — the marked run is one item in a list of subjects, and the
clause keeps its verbs and its (absent) negation. The bare adjective "natural" left standing beside
the marker is the intended cost. Note that *benignitatem* is printed correctly at 0460A in 0022,
which is the corroboration.

### 6c. 0024, col. 0465C — *efficians*

**Printed:** *quia erat hostia sufficiens pro omnibus, sed non **efficians***
**Done:** `because he was a victim sufficient for all, but not [sic: *efficians*]`.
**Why:** *efficians* is not a Latin form (for *efficiens*, in the scholastic *sufficiens*/*efficiens*
pair — Christ's sacrifice sufficient for all, efficient not for all). Carried untranslated. ⚠ **7a⁗
question 2 was the live risk here and it is answered:** the negation `non` sits **outside** the
marker and is rendered ("but **not**"), because the whole doctrinal point of the sentence is the
denial. Had the marker taken *non efficians* entire, the English would have said Christ's sacrifice
was efficient for all, which the next sentence explicitly denies (*Non enim omnes salvos fecit*).

## 8. Passages that would not construe, or construe only awkwardly — rendered as printed

Pattern 7 governs all of these: the English is as close as the printed words allow, no marker,
conjecture here only.

### 8a. 0020, col. 0452D — the missing full stop
*…quosdam per uberiorem dulcedinem sapientiae reficiens **uberiora ergo sunt pascua** in hoc monte.*
Migne prints no stop between *reficiens* and *uberiora*, so the participial clause runs straight into
a new independent sentence. **Pattern 8: never supply a mark the plate lacks.** The English carries
the run-on: "…refreshing some through the more abundant sweetness of wisdom more abundant therefore
are the pastures on this mountain." Conjecture: a full stop after *reficiens*.

### 8b. 0020, col. 0454A–B — the stranded future perfect
*quandoque illo congrediente nobiscum, et nunc praevalente, nunc superato proficit oratio, et manus
in orando **remissius fuerint sublevatae**, ita ut experiatur anima has vicissitudines…*
*fuerint sublevatae* has no protasis to attach to; the sentence needs a *si* (or *quandoque … cum*)
before *manus*. Rendered literally ("and the hands in praying shall have been lifted up more
slackly") and left. **Deliberately not given a `[cj:]`:** what is missing is a whole word Migne does
not print anywhere in the sentence, so there is no printed word for the gloss to open on — Pattern
18's verbatim-in-the-twin guard cannot be satisfied (contrast 7561 @0648, where the gloss could open
on the printed *solum*).

### 8c. 0021, col. 0456C — *spiritum commotioni, et igni non inesse Dominum*
*spiritum* is accusative where the parallel datives *commotioni*/*igni* require *spiritui*. **English
nouns carry no case, so there is no exponent for the defect** (Pattern 9's gender-mismatch logic
applies unchanged): rendered "says that the Lord is not present in spirit, and in commotion, and in
fire". The negation *non inesse* is carried. Marked here so a later sweep does not churn it.

### 8d. 0021, col. 0458B — *sapientiam in horum vacuitate scribunt*
"…and write wisdom in the emptiness of these." *scribunt* is a real word in a real slot, and the
clause construes; it simply says something odd for a passage about the shorn sheep grasping and
discerning. Conjecture: *sciscunt* or *scrutantur*. Rendered as printed, no marker — the English
does not assert anything *false*, only something obscure, which is Pattern 18's declined class
("merely uncertain stays in the crux").

### 8e. 0022, col. 0460B — *Virtutes enim, cum omnes, sint pares*
The comma after *omnes* breaks *cum omnes sint pares* in the middle. Both marks are preserved
(Pattern 8) and the English mirrors the break: "For the virtues, since all, are equal, cannot all be
exercised equally." Reads oddly; the plate reads oddly.

### 8f. 0022, col. 0461A — *Si vero imo, quia frequentius in venialibus labitur…*
*Si vero imo* has no apodosis and *imo* has no construction. Rendered "But if rather, because both
the weak and the strong slip more frequently into venial sins…". The whole sentence is the least
construable in the range. No marker: *imo* is a real word occupying a real (if unintelligible) slot,
and the conjecture would have to supply a clause, not a word.

### 8g. 0022, col. 0459C–D and 0460A — two anacolutha, both carried
*unde contingit ut **iracundus**, etiamsi bono zelo moveatur, amaritudo naturalis se misceat* —
nominative *iracundus* stranded before a new subject *amaritudo*. Rendered as printed.
*Si quis **levis** fuerit aut remissus plusquam debeat, **ille** rigorem districte, **iste**
clementiam exsequetur dissolute* — *ille*/*iste* need two antecedents and *levis aut remissus* gives
only near-synonyms, so *ille rigorem* answers to nothing. Conjecture: *rigidus* (or *severus*) for
*levis*, cf. *tristis, vel rigidus aliquis* in the next sentence. Declined for a `[cj:]` because
"light" is not a false assertion, only a mismatched one.

### 8h. 0022, col. 0461B — a supplied *and*
*quae et in se, et in aliis fructificant fetus spirituales generant* — asyndeton with no mark
printed. Rendered "bear fruit **[and]** beget spiritual offspring", the supplied word bracketed per
the corpus's existing convention (~95 instances of bracketed supplied English). No punctuation was
invented (Pattern 8).

### 8i. 0024, col. 0464C — *noxium ruborem et salubrem convertens*
Rendered literally, "converting the hurtful redness and the healthful one". The sense the sentence
needs is *in salubrem* ("into a healthful one"), which is what the surrounding argument states twice
(*ruborem rubore commutat*; *rubens per poenitentiam quae rubuit per culpam*). A one-word
preposition is missing rather than mis-set, so as at §8b there is no printed word to hang a `[cj:]`
on.

### 8j. 0024, col. 0464C — lowercase *prudenter* after a full stop
Migne prints *…oblivioni tradat. **prudenter** itaque sponsa…*. The English capitalizes
("Prudently therefore the bride…"): sentence-initial capitalization is English orthography, not a
textual reading, and the Latin twin preserves the plate. Logged, not marked.

### 8k. 0024, col. 0465C — *antequam sustinet novit*
Indicative *sustinet* where the temporal clause wants *sustineret*. Construes as printed and is
rendered so ("before he undergoes it"). Pattern 9 family; no marker.

### 8l. 0024, col. 0464D — *latent virtutes, sed dum pulsatur adversis, detegitur*
Plural *virtutes latent* followed by singular *detegitur*; the subject slides to *anima*. English
"they lie hidden … it is uncovered" carries the slide rather than smoothing it to "they are
uncovered", which would have been a silent repair with no exponent left on the page.

### 9a. Etymological play kept in Latin — 0023, col. 0461C

*Dilectio haec **Dei lectio**, vel **Dei electio** dici potest.* No English trio shares a root with
"love", so the words stand in italic Latin with a gloss apiece: "This *dilectio* may be called *Dei
lectio,* a reading of God, or *Dei electio,* a choosing of God." This is `translation-style.md`
§ *Song of Songs* case 2 (the *stylus*/*epistylium* and *murenulae* precedents) — an English word
sharing no root would make the gloss explain nothing. The following sentences then work the play out
(*Legit enim Deum … Eligit etiam Deum*), and those are translated normally.

### 9b. *caprae* / *capreae* — one English, deliberately

Migne's lemma has *caprarum*; his exposition then shifts to *capreae* at 0452B and keeps it
(0452B ×2, 0452C, and again at 0461B in 0022, *capreae istae*). *caprea* and *capra* are used
interchangeably in medieval Latin, **and Richard nowhere argues from the difference** — so this is
Pattern 9 (attested spelling variation, no defect) combined with Song §case 3 (printed Latin
effectively identical, no gloss depends on it): rendered **"goats" throughout**, which also keeps the
lemma hook the exposition repeatedly reaches back for ("These goats, namely holy meditations, come up
from mount Galaad"). Inventing an English distinction ("goats" vs "roes") would have made Richard
mark a difference he does not mark. Logged because it is a judgement against the visible variation
in the twin.

### 9c. *dispice* rendered "despise" — 0023, col. 0463A

Migne prints *et **dispice** ignorantiam proximi* (Vulg. *despice*). Rendered "despise", **not** as
the other verb *dispicere* ("discern"), and no `[var:]` fired. Two reasons: *dispicio* for
*despicio* is ordinary medieval e/i orthography (Pattern 9, "attested medieval spellings are correct
Latin"), and Richard's own next sentence adjudicates it on the page — *despicit quoque ignorantiam
proximi, **id est contemnit***. Where the author glosses the word himself, our marker would be
telling the reader something he has already been told (the 8715 declining class in Pattern 18).

### 9d. Non-material scripture divergences left unmarked

- **0023 @0462B, Prov. 10:19** — Migne *Qui moderatur labia prudentissimus est*; Vulg. *qui autem
  moderatur labia **sua** prudentissimus est*. Dropped possessive, no sense change.
- **0024 @0465A, Job 33:21** — Migne *ossa quae tecta fuerant **nudantur*** (present); Vulg.
  *nudabuntur* (future). Not italicized in Migne — it is woven into Richard's own sentence

- **0024 @0465D, Isa. 53:5** — Migne *quodque **contritus** est propter scelera nostra*; Vulg.
  *attritus est*. Again indirect (*quodque…*), unitalicized, and *contritus*/*attritus* is not a
  sense change ("crushed"/"bruised"). Rendered "crushed".
- **0022 @0459A, Heb. 4:12** and **0024 @0466D, Job 16:21** (*Cum verbosi sint amici ad Deum stillat
  oculus ejus*) are adapted into Richard's own syntax with no italics and no `[n:]` in the Heb. case.
