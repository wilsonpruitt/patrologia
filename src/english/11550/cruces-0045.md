## 0. Vocabulary choices recorded up front (the *descriptio sponsi* catalogue closes here)

These are the concrete nouns the gloss hangs on in 0045–0046, and the closing chapter's
already-ratified Latin-in-italic terms.

1. **0515A, *crura* = "legs."** Not "shanks" or "thighs." The whole gloss is that these are the
   men *qui circumeunt infirmos visitantes* — who **go about** visiting the sick, whose door lies
   open to the traveler, who *pergunt* to others and *rectos gressus pedibus suis faciunt*. The
   English must be the limb one walks on, and the *columnae marmoreae* / *rectus* / *erectus* /
   *infractus* chain ("upright," "erect," "unbroken") has to stay visibly a chain about standing
   straight. "Legs" carries both; "thighs" carries neither.
2. **0518B–0520B, *guttur* = "throat."** The lemma word of CAPUT XL and it is glossed thirteen
   times in one chapter (*guttur sponsi est internus sapor ejus* … *suavissimum guttur ejus
   praedicare possunt* … *guttur ejus per quod divinitatis verba processerunt*). Richard needs the
   one organ that both **tastes** and **speaks**, and "throat" is the only English noun that does.
   "Voice," "palate," and "mouth" each break one half of the double gloss.
3. **0518B ff., *sapor* = "savor," *gustus* = "taste," *suavitas*/*dulcedo* = "sweetness,"
   *suavis*/*dulcis* = "sweet."** ⚠ **Merge item:** *suavitas* and *dulcedo* collapse onto one
   English word here, and they stand side by side at 0519B (*illius suavitatis gustus, dulcedinis
   illius praelibatio* → "the taste of that sweetness, the foretaste of that sweetness").
   Preserving the pair would need an invented distinction, so I let them collapse — this follows
   the work as already shipped (25 "sweet" / 29 "sweetness" in chunks 0000–0029), and the lemma
   hook *suavissimum* = "most sweet" is what actually has to survive. **Also for the merge:
   chunks 0000–0029 print both "savor" (9×) and "savour" (5×); I used American "savor"
   throughout. One spelling needs picking at reconciliation.**
4. **0516A–0517A, *species* = "beauty."** Kept against "appearance," because the gloss is a
   chain — *species ejus ut Libani* → *bene species dicuntur* → *speciosum forma prae filiis
   hominum* → *speciem deformitatis speciosam* → *singulariter species Christi prae omnibus
   speciosa*. "Appearance" makes *speciosa* unrelated to *species*; "beauty"/"beautiful" keeps the
   hook. ⚠ **The seam:** at 0521A the same word is the technical *per speciem* against *per fidem*
   (2 Cor 5:7), where "beauty" is simply wrong, and I rendered **"by sight"** there. Two senses of
   one word two columns apart; both are in the Latin, neither is drift. Logged so a merge sweep
   does not flatten them.
5. **0516B, *candidatio* left in italic Latin** (*nec tantum est candidus, sed etiam candidatio*)
   — ratified item 3, and it matches 0016/0443B, which already keeps it. The adjacent
   *candidus* is translated ("white"), which is what makes the untranslated noun legible as the
   word Richard is coining on.
6. **0522C, *ferculum* left in italic Latin** (*veri Salomonis singulare ferculum*) — ratified
   item 3, no re-opening. **0522D, *reclinatorium* = "reclining-place"** — same ruling.
7. **0521A, the *quies* split, one site each way in a single paragraph.** *quiesce post laborem*
   → **"rest** after labor" (the inheritance the Bridegroom is calling her into: eschatological).
   *In sinistra quievisti* → "In the left hand thou **hast been still**" (the present life's
   contemplative quiet, set against the *dextera* still to come). The ratified split holds and
   the two senses are eight words apart. Logged as the site the brief asks for.
8. **Pattern 17.** CAPUT XL addresses one soul (*comprehendas … praedicabis … judicabis*) → *thou*
   throughout; *o filiae Hierusalem* (0520D) and *Hujus pulchritudinem cogitate* are plural → no
   archaism. CAPUT XLI is the Bridegroom to one soul → *thou*; CAPUT XLII's *tu quisquis es in
   jubilo*, *tecum sit Deus*, *vere surrexisti* are all singular → *thou/thee*.

## 1. `[var: …]` — Vulgate divergences

### 1a. ⛔ 0522B — **the work's last lemma, and it diverges.** MARKED.

> Printed: ***Quae est ista quae ascendit, et transiens deserta? [n: (Cant. III)]***

Three forms of one question are now in evidence, and this work prints two of them:

| witness | reading | sense |
|---|---|---|
| **Migne here (0522B), cited as Cant. III** | *quae ascendit, **et transiens deserta*** | **and crossing the deserts** — an active passage *through*, plural |
| Vulg. Cant. 3:6 — and **this work at chunk 0010** | *quae ascendit **per desertum** sicut virgula fumi* | through the desert, singular |
| Vulg. Cant. 8:5 | *quae ascendit **de deserto**, deliciis affluens* | **out of** the desert |

**What I did:** rendered the printed participle — "and crossing the deserts" — and marked
`[var: Vulg. Cant. 8:5 *de deserto*, "out of the desert"; Cant. 3:6, which the note cites, *per
desertum*, "through the desert"]`.

**Why it fires** (sense, not spelling, per ratified item 1): *de deserto* makes the desert the
place she is **leaving**; the printed *transiens deserta* makes it the place she is **passing
through**, and turns a prepositional phrase into a second finite-force participle governing a
plural accusative. The note's own citation (Cant. III) matches neither reading exactly, so the
divergence cannot be resolved by supposing Migne quoted the other verse.

**⚠ And the reason it is printed this way is almost certainly the verse form, which the brief
could not have known: CAPUT XLII is not prose — it is a rhymed Marian rhythmus**, and *deserta*
is a **rhyme word**: *deserta · operta · referta* (…*et transiens deserta? / Quo tendit aut quid
portendit floribus operta? / Papae, furem non offendit opibus referta*). The rhyme scheme runs the
whole chapter (*Holoferni · spernis · sternis*; *Hebraea · Judaea · ea*; *Deus · Judaeus ·
Sadducaeus*). So the divergence is metrically motivated, and the participle is load-bearing on the
poem, not on an exposition. **Per Pattern 14 I state the two readings and do not speculate in the
marker** — the reasoning lives here.

**⚠ Note for whoever collates further: Richard does not gloss this lemma at all.** CAPUT XLII
never expounds *transiens deserta*; it simply proceeds into the litany. So the divergence turns on
the sense of the lemma itself and on nothing downstream — which is why it is marked once, at the
lemma, and nowhere else.

## 2. `[sic: …]` — carried type

Both confirmed the way ratified item 6 says to confirm one: grep the full PL TEI (5,276 files) and
let the corpus be its own control.

1. **0519D · *comprenhendere*** for *comprehendere* (*ut clare videre non possint, et spiritualia
   comprenhendere*). **CONFIRMED and marked.** `comprenhend-` occurs across the whole PL TEI in
   **one file — this one, this site.** Intrusive *n*. **7a⁗ check:** the marked run is the
   clause's only infinitive, so the English supplies the sense beside the marker — "and comprehend
   [sic: *comprenhendere*] spiritual things" — not a bare hole.
2. **0520C · *desirabilis*** for *desiderabilis* (*qui etiam desirabilis est in membris suis*).
   **Marked, and here is the full evidence, which is weaker than case 1 and should be seen:**
   `desirabil-` occurs **6 times in the whole PL TEI** (5× *desirabilis*, 1× *desirabilem*) across
   six files, this one among them — so it is **not unique to this plate**, and Pattern 9's
   "reserve carry-through for forms unique to this plate" cuts against marking it. I marked it
   anyway on two grounds: (a) *desirabilis* is not an attested medieval orthography, it is
   haplography of *-de-*, so Pattern 9's actual test (is the form in the lexica?) fails; (b) the
   same sentence prints *desiderabilis* correctly four times within thirty words and *desirabilis*
   once, which is a compositor slipping, not a scribe's convention. **7a⁗ check:** the run is the
   clause's predicate adjective, so the sense stands in plain words beside the marker — "who is
   also desirable [sic: *desirabilis*] in his members." **If a later reviewer prefers Pattern 9
   here, the fix is to drop one marker and change nothing else; the English already reads
   correctly with it removed.**

## 3. Declined `[cj: …]` — census candidates, recorded not glossed

The bar (ratified item 7) glosses only where the wrong word is a **negation or a correlative**.
None of these is. All rendered literally per Pattern 7; all named here so the census has them.

1. **0515A · *cum Tobia pergunt ad indigenas*** — "they go with Tobias to the **natives**."
   Almost certainly *indigentes*, "the needy": the whole sentence is about dividing one's means
   *unicuique … prout possunt*, and Tobit's alms are to the poor, not to the indigenous. **This is
   the range's largest single sense-shift from one letter-run, and it is still declined** — not a
   negation, not a correlative. Rendered "to the natives."
2. **0522A · *qui hic experte cognovit*** — "who here knew **by experience**." The sentence is
   built on 1 Cor. 13:12 (*ut cognoscat perfecte, et sicuti cognitus est*), where the Vulgate's
   contrast to perfect knowing is ***ex parte***, "in part" — so a fused *ex parte* → *experte* is
   the obvious reading, and fused word-division is a documented shape (Pattern 10, *interra*,
   *vovistvirum*). **Declined, and it does not even reach Pattern 10:** the corpus control shows
   *experte* is a **real word** in the PL, the ablative of *expers* (*a ferina legumque experte
   vita*, 6843; *nationes experte sunt sevitiam*, 9481), so it is not the non-word class, and
   medieval *experte* as an adverb of *expertus* is defensible in a chapter that elsewhere writes
   *experientia docente* (0520B). Rendered as the adverb.
3. **0517C · *et si tantum quantum Christus*** — see §4.1; a possible dropped negation, declined,
   and the reason it is declined rather than glossed is that the missing word is not *printed
   wrong*, it is **absent**, which `[cj:]` cannot address.
4. **0518D · *quia adhuc ex faece carnalitatis accessit*** — "because it has thus far **drawn
   near** out of the dregs of carnality." *accessit* where the argument wants a verb of *not yet
   having departed* (*recessit*). Declined; rendered literally.
5. **0522D · *a toto refulgens aurea*** — "**golden, shining from the whole**." Possibly *auro*
   for *a toto*, or a genitive lost. See §4.4. Declined.

## 4. Passages that will not construe — rendered as literally as the printed words allow

1. **0517C · *Exaltata est haec beata quasi cedrus, sublimata est sicut Christus, et si tantum
   quantum Christus, Christus minimus fuit in regno coelorum…*** Rendered "…has been made lofty
   like Christ, **and if as much as Christ**: Christ was the least in the kingdom of heaven…" The
   clause has no apodosis and, as printed, concedes what the paragraph goes on to deny —
   the chapter closes *Sublimis igitur Maria, sed **sublimior Christus***, "Mary is lofty, but
   Christ loftier." **A dropped *non* (*et si non tantum quantum Christus*, "though not as much as
   Christ") would make the whole paragraph construe and would make its last sentence follow.**
   Per the negation-fidelity rule I did **not** supply it: the contradiction is the finding.
   ⚠ This is the crux in the range I would most want a second reader on.
2. **0521D · *Cujus passionis gustus amore mihi sapiunt, sed confectum ex illa redemptionis
   humanae aromate suavi me reficit, nec martyrum myrrha, nec confessorum aromata haec gaudia
   percepissent, si non mortis meae myrrha percepisset.*** Rendered literally, including all
   three negatives and the singular *percepisset* against plural *percepissent* — "neither the
   myrrh of the martyrs nor the spices of the confessors would have perceived these joys, if the
   myrrh of my death had not perceived them." *confectum* is taken as a neuter substantive ("that
   which is confected out of it"); *gustus … sapiunt* is plural against singular *passionis
   gustus*. Nothing emended.
3. **0519B · *Hic bonitas Dei tenuis gustus est in futuro satietas.*** Rendered "Here the goodness
   of God is a slender taste, in the future satiety." The nominative *bonitas* where the contrast
   wants a genitive (*bonitatis Dei tenuis gustus*, "a slender taste **of** the goodness of God"),
   and no punctuation between the halves. Rendered as printed; not marked (Pattern 9 — a case
   ending with no English exponent once the phrase is Englished).
4. **0522C–D · *haec est veri Salomonis singulare ferculum et turris eburnea, a toto refulgens
   aurea tota delectabilis, haec est in quo se reclinat rex…*** Rendered "…and the ivory tower,
   golden, shining from the whole, wholly delightful; this is she **in whom** the king reclines
   himself…" Two things printed here and neither emended: *a toto* (see §3.5), and *in quo*
   (masculine/neuter) referring to the feminine *haec* — a **Pattern 9 gender mismatch with no
   English exponent**, since English "whom" carries no gender. No marker, no crux churn.
5. **0522C · *Singularis ferus, rex est Assuerus, Esther magni nominis.*** Rendered as printed,
   asyndeton included. *Singularis ferus* is Ps. 79:14's boar, i.e. the devil, and belongs
   logically with the Aman/Mardochai lines before it rather than with Assuerus; the verse form
   licenses the ellipsis. Nothing supplied.
6. **0523A · *translator non sic ventilat, fides allegat ita quod regnet jam cum Deo!*** Rendered
   "the translator does not so winnow it, faith so alleges, that she now reigns with God!"
   *translator* in a chapter *de assumptione* is presumably the one who *translates* her (carries
   her over — cf. *arca foederis … magno translata jubilo* three lines earlier), and *ventilat*
   "winnows/fans" resists any reading I can defend. **The negation is carried as printed.** Not
   emended, not marked.
7. **0523A–0524A · *Ave, Maria, gratia plena; tecum sit Deus. Corruptionis nostrae quam somniat
   Judaeus. Tabescat in fallacia seductor Sadducaeus: nam vere surrexisti.*** *Corruptionis
   nostrae* is a **hanging genitive governed by nothing printed**; rendered as the fragment it is
   ("Of our corruption, which the Jew dreams"). The rhyme (*Deus · Judaeus · Sadducaeus*) is
   intact across it, so whatever the genitive depended on stood in the same line, not in a lost
   one.
   ⛔ **I am NOT reporting the 0523A → 0524A band gap as a defect** — that is known false positive
   #1 (band marks are positional, not four per column), and the **only** admissible signal, text
   broken across an anchor, is absent: the `[0524A]` anchor falls between *Sadducaeus:* and *nam
   vere surrexisti*, which construes cleanly across it. The syntactic break sits mid-paragraph,
   two sentences earlier. Recorded here as an observation only, with no `[ed:]` and no tei-patch.
8. **0518B–C · *consolatio spiritualis, quae suaviter gustantes expertos afficiunt.*** Plural
   *afficiunt* on a compound subject (fine), but *gustantes expertos* is two accusative plural
   participles in asyndeton. Rendered "which sweetly affect those who taste and have experience
   of them." Nothing emended.
9. **0518C · *Si enim bonitatem Dei cogitando comprehendas, et ad saporem ejus pertingens, …
   praedicabis.*** Anacoluthon in the Latin (a subjunctive protasis, then a participle, then a
   future indicative apodosis). Carried through as printed rather than regularized.
10. **0522B · *nam favus mellis mihi erant dulces in moribus et operibus.*** Singular *favus* with
    plural *erant*. Rendered "for the honeycomb of honey were to me those sweet in manners and in
    works." Pattern 9 territory; no marker.

## 5. Interjection kept in Latin

**0522B · *Papae*** (*Papae, furem non offendit opibus referta*). Kept as ***Papae!*** in italic
Latin. It is the Greek-derived exclamation of astonishment; every English equivalent I could
reach for ("Strange!", "Wonder of wonders!", "Ah!") is a guess about the poem's tone rather than a
rendering of a word, and inventing tone inside a rhymed sequence is the kind of silent editorial
hand Pattern 7 exists to forbid. **Flagged for Wilson as a genuine open call** — if the house
prefers interjections Englished, this is a one-word change and the only site in the range.

## 6. Structural findings for the resume note

1. ⚠ **The work has 42 chapters, not 41.** Chunk 0047 carries **two** `## ` heads, `CAPUT XLI.
   *Sponsus sponsam invitat ad haereditatem suscipiendam.*` at the top and `CAPUT XLII. *De
   assumptione Mariae, et ejus laudibus.*` at col. 0522B. Both reproduced 1:1 in place, both
   translated (label + italic summary) per the ratified head rule; both print a closing period, so
   both take "CHAPTER XLI." / "CHAPTER XLII." Confirmed against the frontmatter `heads[]`, which
   also lists two.
2. ✅ **The last column anchor of the work is `[0524A]`, matching the manifest's `colLast` for
   11550 (0405A–0524A).** Verified against `src/latin/11550/manifest.json`.
3. ✅ **The work closes cleanly, and it closes twice over.** CAPUT XL ends with a full doxology
   (*…ad quam nos perducere dignetur Jesus Christus sponsus Ecclesiae, qui vivit et regnat in
   saecula saeculorum. Amen.*) at 0520D — which reads like the end of the commentary proper. CAPUT
   XLI then expounds Cant. 5:1 in the Bridegroom's own voice, and **CAPUT XLII is a rhymed Marian
   rhythmus on the Assumption** ending in a prayer (*…ut tecum valeamus perpetue gaudere, Amen.*)
   at 0524A. **No mid-sentence break, no colophon, no printed *Explicit*.** The two Amens are
   worth a line in the resume note: the last two chapters read like an appended Marian coda to a
   commentary that had already signed off, and the byline caution recorded for 11545 (prologue
   Richard's, body Walter's) suggests somebody should eventually ask whose coda it is. **That is
   an attribution question, not a translation one — I did not act on it.**
4. **Register note on CAPUT XLII, and it is the one thing a reader of this chapter must know:**
   it is **verse, not prose** — rhymed, accentual, in Migne printed as a continuous paragraph. I
   translated it as prose (house register; no rhyme attempted, no line breaks introduced, the
   paragraph reproduced 1:1) but the rhyme is what explains the chapter's ellipses, its asyndeton,
   and both of its scripture divergences (§1a, §1c). If the front end ever sets verse as verse,
   this chapter is the corpus's candidate.
