## TREATISE ONE.



## PROLOGUE.

[0211B]

Being asked by friends to write something toward the explanation of the tabernacle, I was afraid, I confess, to satisfy their request, lest in that very thing I should seem to have derogated from the authority of the Fathers. Yet he himself who published the common exposition gives me the boldness to attempt it, when in the midst of expounding he adds. These things, he says, upon a most difficult matter we have taken care to set out briefly, according as we seemed to ourselves to have understood them, ready to learn truer things in these matters if anyone should be willing to teach us. In all of which, nevertheless, the sense of the allegory stands wholly clear. See how confidently he puts forward the sense of the allegory, who nevertheless had prefaced the exposition of the letter under an ambiguity. He therefore who so confidently gives the testimony of his own certainty to the allegorical exposition, and denies it to the historical, plainly intimates that in that [0211C] exposition of his he did not satisfy even himself. Why do you reproach me, or force me to believe him in a thing wherein he does not himself believe himself? But, that I may confess the truth, those who, it is now agreed, stand present at the contemplation of the highest truth — these I fear rather to offend than to render favorable, if I should hesitate to bring forth into common use a truth I have come to know, as though for the sake of reverence toward them. Let us consider how many things, how great, how deep, they handled with all diligence; how many things they expounded historically, how many allegorically, how many tropologically, with wonderful reasoning; and then it will cease to be a wonder if in some one place they left an exposition less than sufficient. It is therefore no great thing, nor any wonder, if in some one point we can add something that may avail toward greater evidence or plainer understanding. [0211D] These things have been said on account of those who accept nothing except what they have received from the most ancient fathers. But is he who unceasingly brings forth new fruits for the refreshment of the outward man never to be believed to impart no further [0212B] advances of knowledge, for the renewing of the senses of the inward man? Where then, I ask, is that word, "many shall pass over, and knowledge shall abound"? Let no one, therefore, I beseech, be indignant with me if I gather from the Lord's harvest the ears that remain, and those that could escape the hands of the reapers, and gladly dispense them [sic: *eroganero*] to the hungry and to those who ask.

## CHAPTER ONE. *On the making of the ark.*

When the Lord was about to give Moses commandment concerning the construction of the tabernacle, first of all he instructed him about the making of the ark, so that by that very thing he might intimate that a certain prerogative of dignity set it far before all the rest that were to be made in the tabernacle. But the things that are said of its material or its measure or the manner of making it are plain enough [0212C] and need no exposition. He commands four circles to be made at the four corners; but how this is to be understood he shows next: *Let two,* he says, *be on the one side, and two on the other [n: (Exod. XXV)]*. They were therefore not altogether at the corners, but beside the corners: two on the one side, and two beside the corners on the opposite side. It can be doubted what one ought to think about the propitiatory, in that the author describes it separately. But I understand the propitiatory to be nothing other than the lid of the ark itself, namely a certain plate made of the purest gold out of one piece: whence also it had with the ark, both in length and in breadth, one and the same measure throughout. But, that no ambiguity upon this may remain to you, hear what Scripture [0212D] itself sets forth of the ark, and what of the propitiatory. For of the ark it is written: *In which thou shalt put the testimony that I will give thee [n: (ibid.)]*, and far afterward of the propitiatory: *Before,* he says, *the propitiatory wherewith the testimony is covered, where I will speak to thee [n: (Exod. XXX)]* The testimony [0213A] that is placed in the ark, how, I ask, is it covered by the propitiatory, unless because its lid is not one thing and the propitiatory another! But the excellence of its dignity is intimated in this very thing, that it is described separately. From the propitiatory itself the answers of the Godhead, according to the measure of the divine propitiation, were now given, now not given. Rightly therefore did it receive the name of propitiatory, in which the Lord was accustomed to exhibit or to display the prerogative of his propitiation. But it is to be understood that that golden crown which ran round the top of the ark, where also it was fastened, enclosed that whole propitiatory within itself, and, raising itself higher, stood above the thickness of it.

## CHAPTER II. *On the fashioning of the two cherubim.*

[0213B]Of the cherubim he says that they look upon one another. In body indeed they were turned toward the outer tabernacle; but with faces turned they looked toward that part where the ark stood in the midst, beneath their wings. Those two cherubim that were in the temple of Solomon had without doubt been made after the likeness of these. What therefore we read of those, we equally understand of these: that the one cherub touched with his wing the one wall, and the other with his wing touched the other wall [n: (II Paral. III)]; but with their other wings they touched one another in the midst. Just as therefore those of the temple, so neither could these stand upon the ark and the propitiatory, because the ark had only two cubits and a half of length. But in those cherubim the extension of the joined wings held ten cubits, in these five cubits. What therefore is said of these, [0213C] that they were above the ark of the testimony, ought not to be so understood as though they stood upon the ark itself, but that they rose above the ark itself with their whole body.

## CHAPTER III. *On the forming of the table.*

But that I may say briefly what I think concerning the form of the table: it seems to me to be like an ark without a bottom; yet so that the upper surface be understood to be wholly flat and immovably fastened to the parts beneath. It had, moreover, a certain golden projection running round the top of the table, which the author himself, on account of the likeness, calls a lip; and to this there adhered a crown four fingers in height. And it was adorned with various carvings; and, as the variety of the carving required, [0213D] pierced with frequent cuttings: whence also it is called *interrasilis*. Upon this there was also another set above it, like a certain little rod, round and flat, or as it were interwoven of several little rods, and joined to that first for firmness and for beauty; nay, so wholly made one with it that both seemed to be one. These things suffice concerning the table: for the rest are of themselves well enough known.

## CHAPTER IV. *On the fashioning of the candlestick.*

Now we must look at the candlestick, and chiefly at that in it about which there can be doubt. Its cups are described after the manner of a nut. Now a cup seems to have the half of a sphere. It has therefore a roundness hollow and empty and cut off above. But a nut, widening itself from below, is stretched out into a belly; [0214A] and from above, sharpening itself little by little, is gathered to a single point at the summit. A cup, therefore, has a spherical roundness, but cut off, and an empty concavity; a nut, however, an oblong roundness, and yet not empty nor cut off. Since therefore it had two things in common with a cup (namely an empty concavity together with the cutting off of the roundness), he calls it a cup, not a nut; and because it had only one thing in common with a nut (namely the oblong roundness), he does not name it a nut, yet declares that it has the likeness of a nut. Both the shaft, therefore, and all the six branches had an ornament of this kind: first a roundness oblong like a nut's, but cut off; then a spherical roundness, which he calls a little sphere; and after these two joined to it, it had [0214B] a flower after the manner of a lily. And so the shaft first ran down to the first cup as though failing into it; and again it emerged, as it were, out of the middle of the flower, with two branches on either side. In the same manner it is to be understood in the second place and in the third. But at the top it had a like ornament, except for the [sic: *ad junctione*] of the branches. And you have, from the likeness of the shaft, what is to be understood of the branches.

But it is to be noted that a little sphere is said to be under two branches in three places of the shaft. For the roots of the branches had the lily-flower not so much under them as about them, but the little sphere they had wholly under them. So much for the candlestick; now we must look at the tabernacle.
