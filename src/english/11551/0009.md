## SECOND TREATISE. On the temple of Solomon, according to the letter. (cont.)

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ There follows: *Its brim was like the brim of a cup, and the leaf of a lily turned outward.* ⟦s2⟧ From this description it is understood that its brim was bent back toward the outer parts; whence also that measure is understood to have been taken beneath the brim which is described above in these words: *And a little cord, of [sic: tringinta] cubits, girded it round about.* ⟦s3⟧ For that measure too, of ten cubits, is not taken beneath the brim, but where it is read there, from brim to brim: *It held two thousand baths, and three thousand metretae.* ⟦s4⟧ Concerning these measures it seems better to forbear [0237B] than to say anything, since under the same names different measures are understood by different writers. ⟦s5⟧ In what follows certain vessels are described, in which the holocausts were washed, which this book calls lavers, and which Paralipomenon names basins. ⟦s6⟧ Concerning these basins we read there in this manner: *He made also ten basins, and set five on the right hand and five on the left, that they might wash in them all the things which they were about to offer in holocaust [n: (II Paral. IV)]*. ⟦s7⟧ In this book he describes the bases of the lavers before the lavers, when he says: *And he made ten bases of bronze, each base of four cubits in length, and of four cubits in breadth, and of three cubits in height.* ⟦s8⟧ Since what is said concerning the measure of the bases is obscure, I say first what seems to me about it; afterwards the letter will be explained. [0237C] But, that what we judge about it may be the better understood, let us treat the several matters as it were limb by limb. ⟦s9⟧ First, then, let there be set two bronze plates of four cubits, pierced through in the greater part of themselves, as much in length as in breadth. ⟦s10⟧ Let the one have a square opening of equal sides, the other a round one, so that the side of the square does not exceed the diameter of the other. ⟦s11⟧ But let the plate with the square opening lie at the bottom. ⟦s12⟧ And above this, about its opening, let four plates be raised, firmly joined both to the first plate and to one another at the four corners of the opening; and let them hold three cubits in height. ⟦s13⟧ And at the four corners of the plate that lies below, beside the joining of those raised, let bronze columns be raised having three cubits in height. [0237D] But the plate with the round opening ought so to lie upon both the raised plates and the columns that it joins all things together above, as the first plate joins all into one below. ⟦s14⟧ And above the opening of the uppermost plate there ought to be placed a certain hollow as it were of a hemisphere, having in every respect the form as it were of a basin turned upside down. ⟦s15⟧ For the summit of this hemisphere fell away into a certain flat roundness, which held a cubit and a half every way, upon which the laver sat. ⟦s16⟧ But about the upper parts of the bases there came forth, on four sides, certain supports, which Scripture calls little shoulders, which received and upheld the bellies of the laver on every side. ⟦s17⟧ What is said of the wheels and of their axle seems plain enough; but now let us return to the exposition [0238A] of the letter. ⟦s18⟧ It is said, then, concerning these bases: *And the work of the bases itself was chased.* ⟦s19⟧ This is said with respect to that part in which the engravings were. ⟦s20⟧ There is one kind of engraving in which the outlines of any body are traced round only on the surface; another in which the whole image of the body is formed limb by limb, and the field which lies between the engravings is hollowed out, and is scraped away in part or utterly. ⟦s21⟧ And so, to distinguish it from the other kind of engraving, the work of the bases has been called chased.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ There follows: *And carvings between the joinings, and between the little crowns and the plaits, and lions, and oxen, and cherubim; and in the joinings likewise above.* ⟦s2⟧ He calls the four sides of the plate set underneath at the bottom joinings, each of which joined into one the two columns on either side and the adjoining [0238B] plate set upright; but the joinings above are called the sides of the uppermost plate, by which they were likewise joined into one from above; and carvings are described as being in each of these. ⟦s3⟧ But in the bases the columnar part along the sides, on those plates which stood raised foursquare, had little crowns carved on it, and likewise plaits, as it were certain withies, artfully interwoven one with another. ⟦s4⟧ Between these plaits, then, where they folded back one into another in the manner of a circle, and likewise between the little crowns and the plaits, there are said to have been carved also lions, and oxen, and cherubim: *And beneath the lions and the oxen, as it were thongs of bronze hanging down, and four wheels for each base, and bronze axles.* ⟦s5⟧ All these things are plain enough. ⟦s6⟧ And what is added concerning the little shoulders is clear from what has been said before: *And on the four [0238C] sides as it were little shoulders of molten work beneath the laver, looking one toward another.* ⟦s7⟧ These things are plain, but what is added concerning the workmanship of the laver seems somewhat obscure: *The mouth also of the laver was within, at the top of the head; and what appeared outside was of one cubit, all round, and it likewise had one cubit and a half.* ⟦s8⟧ Think of the workmanship of the laver itself as the round form of a box having no lid. ⟦s9⟧ Its head, its upper parts; the top of the head, the circuit of the opening. ⟦s10⟧ But the top of the head, that is, the edge of the opening itself, girded and cemented to itself a certain smooth hollow clinging to it, made in the manner of a pipe, which he calls the [sic: *liberos*] of the laver, because through it what was poured in passed through into it. ⟦s11⟧ But the length of this held half a cubit; [0238D] half a cubit indeed descended into the belly, while one cubit appeared outside. ⟦s12⟧ It is said, then, that the mouth of the laver was within, at the top of its head, since a pipe of the kind we have described enclosed within itself the uppermost part of the edge. ⟦s13⟧ These things concerning the workmanship of the laver being interposed, he returns to the description of the bases, when he adds: *But in the corners of the columns were various engravings; and the middle spaces between the columns were square, not round.* ⟦s14⟧ There were therefore engravings not only in the little crowns of the bases, but also in those corners of theirs which lay outside the circle. ⟦s15⟧ He calls intercolumns the plates spoken of above, raised foursquare between the four columns. ⟦s16⟧ But if the plate set underneath had a round opening, [0239A] and a round hollow rose on high about it, assuredly the intercolumns would not have been square but round.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ There follows: *The four wheels also, which were at the four corners of the base, clung together beneath the base.* ⟦s2⟧ For the axles upon which the bases set above rested, passing from one side of the bases and projecting on either hand, and thrust into the wheels, coupled the wheels one to another. ⟦s3⟧ The things which are added in what follows concerning the bases or the lavers seem clear from what has already been said before. ⟦s4⟧ At the end of the description he makes mention again of the columns described above, where he also inserts something which he had not said before: *And he finished all the work of king Solomon in the temple of the Lord: two columns, and the cords of the capitals upon the heads of the columns, [0239B] two of them, and two nets, that they might cover the two cords which were upon the heads of the columns.* ⟦s5⟧ Until now he has made no mention of the cords of the capitals; now he determines that there were two upon the heads of the columns. ⟦s6⟧ He calls them cords of the capitals, since they were in the capitals. ⟦s7⟧ But as to its being said that he made two cords upon the heads of the columns, it can come into doubt whether he made one on each, or rather two on each. ⟦s8⟧ But this very thing is determined by what is added concerning the nets: *And two nets, that they might cover the two cords which were upon the heads of the columns.* ⟦s9⟧ We have, from what has been set down and expounded above, that there were two nets on each capital. ⟦s10⟧ What therefore he says — two nets — is to be referred [0239C] to each capital. ⟦s11⟧ But the nets of each capital could not cover two cords, if there had not been two on each. ⟦s12⟧ What further he made, he adds when he says: *And four hundred pomegranates in the two nets.* ⟦s13⟧ It is to be known for certain that in this place four hundred is to be read, not forty, as many codices have it, corrupted by the fault of the scribes. ⟦s14⟧ This is easily proved from what is read above, where it is said: *But there were two hundred orders of pomegranates round about the second capital.* ⟦s15⟧ He for whom this attestation does not suffice, let him consult the Hebrew truth by means of the Hebrews, and he will find that what we say is true. ⟦s16⟧ But it is further added concerning these pomegranates, how they were ordered in each of the nets: *Two rows of pomegranates in each [0239D] of the nets, to cover the cords of the capitals, which were upon the heads of the columns.* ⟦s17⟧ There accords with this reading what is read in Paralipomenon, where it is written concerning the pomegranates or the nets: *Four hundred pomegranates also; and two nets, so that two orders of pomegranates apiece were joined to each of the nets, which should protect the *epistylia* and the heads of the columns.*

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Note that the one says rows, the other calls them orders. ⟦s2⟧ According to what we have read above and expounded, there were two hundred orders of pomegranates in the two nets, namely a hundred in the one net and a hundred in the other. ⟦s3⟧ There were therefore a hundred orders in each according to that reading; two in each according to this reading. ⟦s4⟧ Without doubt there were a hundred [0240A] according to one consideration, and two according to another consideration. ⟦s5⟧ If you attend according to one dimension, you will find there two hundreds; if you consider according to the other, you will find there a hundred pairs. ⟦s6⟧ What he had said above concerning the nets, the same he repeats concerning the pomegranates. ⟦s7⟧ For of both he says that they covered the cords; whence it is understood how far the nets descended, which had the pomegranates hanging from their extremities over against the cords. ⟦s8⟧ From what has gone before, as we have already shown, it is given to understand that on one capital there were two nets, and only two cords. ⟦s9⟧ One order of pomegranates of the one net, therefore, could cover one cord, and likewise one order of the other net could cover the other. ⟦s10⟧ What then is it that is said, that [0240B] in each of the nets there were two rows or orders of pomegranates to cover the cords of the capitals? ⟦s11⟧ But in this it is noted that the comeliness of the work, or the reason of its signification, did not allow the first order to have a cord over against it; and therefore in each of the nets a second order was added, that it might be set before it in that place where reason demanded that a cord be placed. ⟦s12⟧ The first order of pomegranates in each of the nets, then, marked off the nets, as we said above, into sevens of rows; but the second, set before the cords, veiled them from the eyes of those who looked on. ⟦s13⟧ These things concerning the more obscure matters I have set out as I was able. ⟦s14⟧ If anyone should charge me not so much with falsity as with prolixity, let him know that I had rather seem too prolix to the smatterers than too obscure [0240C] to the simpler sort. ⟦s15⟧ But what has been said by me perhaps too prolixly, anyone more skilled has it in his power to recall to a compendium.
