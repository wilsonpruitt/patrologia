## Work-wide conventions (reconciled at merge)

- **The column's four members keep their Latin, in italic: *stylus*, *epistylium*
  (pl. *epistylia*).** Richard argues the vocabulary etymologically at 0233A —
  *epistylium dici non solet nisi quod stylo inhaeret* — so an English pair like
  "epistyle"/"shaft", which share no root, destroys the very thing being argued.
  The two batches disagreed here (first half Latin, tail anglicized) and the tail's
  22 instances were converted to the first half's scheme at merge. Same principle as
  the *Theotocon* precedent in 7561.
- ***capitellum* → "capital"** in both halves, deliberately: it is a standard English
  architectural term and carries no etymological load in Richard's argument.
- **"shaft" is reserved for the LAMPSTAND's shaft** (chunk 0000, with its branches,
  cups and lily) — a different object. It is never the column member.
- Other fixed terms: cortina / sagum / velum / operimentum; *annulus* vs *circulus*
  (load-bearing at 0222B–D); net / row / order kept distinct (Richard's argument at
  0240A turns on *versus* vs *ordines* not collapsing); laver / basin / sea;
  propitiatorium, oraculum.

## Terminology fixed for this batch

Richard's tropological scheme rests on a technical vocabulary he uses with care, and several terms have no obvious English default. The renderings below are held constant across chunks 0000–0007 and should be held constant in the rest of the work.

- **cortina → "curtain"; sagum → "hair-cloth"; velum → "veil"; operimentum → "covering".** Four distinct words for four distinct textiles, all of which English wants to call "curtain." *Velum* is used both for the assembled sheet of ten curtains ("velum cortinarum") and for the sanctuary veil; "veil" carries both without collapsing into *cortina*. *Saga cilicina* is rendered "hair-cloths of goats' hair" — redundant in English, but *cilicina* is a separate printed word and Richard argues from its roughness (0217A).
- **annulus → "ring"; circulus → "circle".** The distinction is load-bearing at 0222B–D, where Richard's whole argument is that the *annuli* of the grate and the *circuli* of the altar must be the same objects. Rendering both "ring" would answer his question for him. The same convention is kept for the golden *circuli* of the curtains at 0215A.
- **craticula → "grate"; arula → "hearth"; retiaculum → "network"; stipes → "post"; vectis → "bar"; basis → "base".**
- **propitiatorium → "propitiatory."** Not "mercy-seat": Richard derives the name from *propitiatio* in the same sentence (0213A, "Merito ergo propitiatorii nomen accepit … propitiationis suae praerogativam"), and the etymology has to survive.
- **oraculum → "oracle."** Likewise: 0227A is an extended note on the word's three senses.
- **tabulatum → "boarding"; tabula → "board"; mansio → "storey"; coenaculum → "upper room"; deambulacrum → "walkway"; laquear → "ceiling."**
- **stylus, epistylium** are kept as italic Latin (0232D–0233B). Richard's four-member analysis of *columna* turns on the etymology *epi* + *stylus* ("Nam epistylium idem sonat quod super. Inde epistylium, quod ponatur supra stylum"), which "shaft"/"architrave" destroys. *Basis* and *capitellum* have unproblematic English ("base", "capital") and are translated. Note the consequence: *hastile* of the candlestick (0214A–B) is "shaft," and *stylus* of the column is not.
- **interrasilis** (0213D), **coclea** and **vitis** (0225B), **cameron** (0231C) are kept as italic Latin — in each case Richard is naming the word, not using it.
- **holocaustum → "holocaust"** throughout, including the chapter head *Descriptio altaris holocausti*.

Note: these are editorial choices, not defects. Italic Latin here means "term deliberately left in Latin" (register section of `translation-style.md`), never `[sic: …]`.

## Chunk 0000

**0212B — `eroganero` for *erogavero*.** Printed: "et esurientibus atque petentibus libenter eroganero." No such Latin verb form exists; the future perfect of *erogare* is *erogavero*, and it is required by the parallel *collegero* in the same clause. Non-word type under Pattern 7: carried into the English untranslated, in italics, marked `[sic: *eroganero*]`. Compositor's *n* for *v*.

**0214B — `excepta ad junctione calamorum` for *excepta adjunctione*.** Printed: "Similem vero ornatum excepta ad junctione calamorum habebat in summo." *excepta* governs an ablative, and *ad* + ablative *junctione* does not construe; the reading required is *adjunctione*, "except for the attachment of the branches" — which is exactly Richard's point, that the topmost ornament is the same as the others minus the branch-junctions. Split type under Pattern 10, and the WHOLE run is carried (including *ad*, which is a real word), marked `[sic: *ad junctione*]`.

**0212B — `nunquam credendus est nullos … impertiri?` — double negative, and a question mark on a statement.** Printed: "Sed qui assidue producit novos fructus … nunquam credendus est nullos de caetero scientiae profectus impertiri, ad innovandos sensus hominis interioris?" The argument requires *nunquid* ("is he to be believed to impart NO further advances?"); *nunquam* + *nullos* makes the sentence assert the opposite of the prologue's whole thesis, that God still gives new understanding. *nunquam* is a real word and can be rendered, so under Pattern 12 it takes no marker: rendered literally, "is he … never to be believed to impart no further advances." The `?` is preserved per Pattern 8. Near-certain *nunquid*.

**0211B–D — Bede's exposition quoted with no quotation marks on the plate.** Printed: "dum inter exponendum adjungit. Haec, inquit, de re difficillima … si quis nos docere voluerit. In quibus tamen omnibus patet allegoriae sensus perlucidus." Migne prints neither guillemets nor italics; the quotation is marked only by *inquit*. Per Pattern 6 no `« »` are supplied (they would falsely claim the plate prints them) and none are added at all here, since the *inquit* already marks it and the quotation's end is not typographically determined. The full stop after *adjungit* — which breaks the sentence immediately before the quotation it introduces — is preserved per Pattern 8.

**0211D → 0212B — the column band A is skipped.** The anchors run …[0211D] … [0212B]…, with no [0212A]. Reproduced as printed; column 0212A is occupied on the plate by matter our TEI does not carry at this point. Not an omission of ours.

**0213A — `nisi quia non aliud est ejus operculum, et aliud propitiatorium!`** A question closed with an exclamation mark. Preserved per Pattern 8.

**0212D — the lemma *Coram, inquit, propitiatorio … ubi loquar tibi* has no terminal stop.** Printed: "*… ubi loquar tibi [n: (Exod. XXX)]* Testimonium quod in arca ponitur…". **0213B — `Sicut itaque illi de templo, sic nec isti … stare poterant.`** The *sicut* clause has no verb; the sense is "just as those of the temple [could not], so neither could these." Rendered with the ellipsis carried as English syntax allows, nothing invented.

**0213B — measurement pair: joined wings of ten cubits (Solomon's cherubim) against five (the tabernacle's), over an ark of `bicubitum et semissem`.** Reproduced as printed, including the plural verb *tenebant* with the singular subject *extensio*. ## Chunk 0001

**Pattern 7**: a printed reading that is construable is rendered literally even

## Terminology fixed across chunks 0008–0014

Richard's architectural vocabulary is technical and he distinguishes terms that
English blurs; the renderings below are held constant through all seven chunks.

- *capitellum* → **capital** · *epistylium* → **epistyle** · *stylus* →
  **shaft** · *basis* → **base**. Richard's whole exposition at 0232D–0233A
  turns on *columna* being used now of the shaft alone, now of all four members,
  so *columna* is always **column** and never silently narrowed.
- *retiaculum* → **net** · *versus* → **row** · *ordo* → **order**. The
  distinction is load-bearing: 0240A opens *Nota quod unus dicit versus, alius
  ordines vocat*, and the whole hundred-vs-two reconciliation depends on the two
  words not collapsing. *ordo* is also **order** in the tables' column headings
  (0249), where Migne uses the same word for a table column.
- *funiculus* → **cord** · *malogranatum* → **pomegranate** · *concatenatio* →
  **chaining** · *catenula* → **little chain**.
- *luter* → **laver** · *concha* → **basin** · *mare* → **sea**. Richard notes at
  0237A and 0237B that his two books use different words for one object; the
  English keeps them different too.
- *humerulus* → **little shoulder** (the diminutive is Scripture's, and Richard
  flags it as Scripture's: *quae Scriptura humerulos vocat*).
- *interrasile* → **chased** · *caelatura* → **engraving** · *coronula* →
  **little crown** · *plecta* → **plait** · *intercolumna* → **intercolumn** ·
  *ejectura* → **projection**.
- *tabula* is **plate** in the bronze-work of 0237C–0239A and **table** in the
  chronological tables of 0247–0255. Same Latin word, two objects.
- *conregnare* → **reign together**; *conregnantium* → **those reigning
  together**, in the treatise heads and throughout. (See the *congregantium*
  crux below.)
- **Biblical names keep Migne's Latin forms** (Hieroboam/Jeroboam, Zamri,
  Ambri, Achab, Josaphat, Ochozias, Joathan, Ozias, Osee, Ezechias…). No
  conventional English equivalents are substituted, per the scripture policy;
  where the plate spells one king two ways, both spellings stand and are logged.

## Chunk 0008 (cols 0233C–0237A)

- **col. 0234C** — `Utrumque **capitelium** columnarum fusile erat.` *capitelium*
  is not a word; *capitellum* is printed correctly a dozen times in the same
  paragraph. Carried untranslated: `[sic: capitelium]`. It sits inside an italic
  scripture lemma, so per Pattern 10 it takes no second italic marker.

- **col. 0234D** — `Sed non multum curandum quid, quibus verbis dicatur, dummodo
  sensus auctoris veraciter intelligatur` — the paragraph simply **stops**; the
  plate prints no terminal point. Pattern 8: none supplied in the English either.

- **cols 0234D–0235A** — `alius autem ordo, septem similiter versuum esset supra
  centum malogranata, **iu** uno appensa.` *iu* is not a word (a turned or
  broken *n* in *in*). Carried: `hung [sic: *iu*] one`.

- **col. 0235A** — `Bene ergo dicitur: *Perfecte duos ordines per circuitum
  retiaculorum singulorum.*` The lemma Richard is quoting, printed three lines
  above at 0234D, reads *Et **perfecit** columnas, et duos ordines…*.
  *Perfecte* is a real Latin word (the adverb "perfectly"), so under Pattern 12
  it is rendered as ordinary English — "*Perfectly two orders round about each
  of the nets*" — and not carried. Intended: *Perfecit*.

- **col. 0235A** — `inferior autem medietas erat super summitatem
  **mologranatorum** appensorum.` Non-word for *malogranatorum*, which the same
  sentence spells correctly twice. Carried: `[sic: *mologranatorum*]`.

- **col. 0236A** — `primo supra **stillim** [stylum?] ponebatur epistylium`.
  *stillim* is not a word, and **Migne has already flagged it himself** with his
  own bracketed conjecture `[stylum?]`, which is reproduced verbatim like any
  other printed matter. Per Pattern 12 (the 10517 `( sic )` precedent) **no
  `[sic: …]` of ours is added on top** — ours marks defects Migne passed over,
  not defects he caught. The word is carried in italics.

- **col. 0234B**, II Paral. III lemma — `quasi quaedam retiacula quae capita
  **regerent** super epistylia`. *regerent* ("should rule") where the Vulgate,
  and Richard's own next sentence (`Ecce de retiaculis dicit, quod **tegerent**
  capita`), have *tegerent* ("should cover"). Real word: rendered literally as
  "should rule," so that the English preserves the very contrast Richard's
  gloss silently corrects.

- **col. 0236D** — Richard's own arithmetical crux, and he says so:
  `Et resticula triginta cubitorum cingebat illud per circuitum… omnino
  impossibile videtur.` A cord of thirty cubits round a sea of ten cubits'
  diameter (π ≈ 3). This is the **author's** problem, deliberately raised and
  deferred (*haec quaestio melius in sequentibus solvitur*), not a misprint; the
  figures stand.

- **cols 0234A / 0235C** — the second capital is *quinque cubitorum altitudinis*
  and the epistyle *tres cubitos altitudinis*, while 3 Reg. VI gives the
  capitals *quatuor cubitorum*, which Richard reads as **breadth** not height.
  Recorded because chunk 0010 (0240D) depends on 5 + 3 = 8 to reach the
  thirty-five-cubit column of Paralipomenon.

## Chunk 0009 (cols 0237B–0240C)

- **col. 0237A/B** — `*Et resticula, **tringinta** cubitorum cingebat illud per
  circuitum.*` Non-word for *triginta*, spelled correctly at 0236D and again
  within this chunk. Carried inside the italic lemma without a second italic
  marker: `[sic: tringinta]`.

- **col. 0238B** — `In basibus autem **columnare** per latera, in tabulis illis
  quae per quadrum stabant erectae, coronulae erant insculptae`. *columnare* is
  not a standard noun, and the phrase is very likely *intercolumnare* /
  *intercolumna* — the term Richard defines two sentences later at 0238D
  (*Intercolumna vocat tabulas superius dictas*), of exactly these plates. Since
  a neuter adjectival *columnare* is at least formable, it is **rendered** as
  ordinary English ("the columnar part along the sides") rather than carried;
  flagged here because the sense depends on it.

- **col. 0238C — boundary case, worth a human's eye.** `adhaerentem sibi teretem
  quamdam concavitatem in modum fistulae factam cingebat atque conglutinabat:
  quam **liberos** luteris vocat, quia per **ipsum** sibi infusa transigebat.`
  *liberos* is a real Latin form (accusative plural of *liber*) but agrees with
  nothing: the relative *quam* is feminine singular, and the resumptive *per
  ipsum* is masculine singular, so the printed word matches neither side of its
  own sentence. Rendering it as a real word ("the free-born of the laver") would
  be actively misleading, which is the case Pattern 12 flags as reopening its
  boundary. It is therefore **carried untranslated** — `[sic: *liberos*]` — and
  the conjecture recorded here: *labrum* or *labium*, the rim, which is what the
  whole passage describes and which *per ipsum* would then resume.

- **cols 0238C–D** — `Hujus autem proceritas **semis cubitum** tenebat, dimidius
  vero cubitus in ventrem descendebat, unus autem cubitus exterius apparebat.`
  The total length is half a cubit, yet half a cubit descends inside and a whole
  cubit shows outside: the parts exceed the whole by a cubit. Rendered as
  printed. Intended, on the parts, something like *sesquicubitum* (a cubit and a
  half) — which is also the measure the lemma at 0238C gives (*unum cubitum et
  dimidium*).

- **col. 0239C** — `Sciendum pro certo quod hoc loco legendum sit, quadringenta
  non quadraginta, secundum quod habent codices multi vitio scriptorum
  depravati.` Not a defect: Richard's own emendation of his Bible, kept intact,
  along with his appeal `per Hebraeos, Hebraicam veritatem consulat`.

  circumdabantur` is syntactically ambiguous, and the Vulgate takes *centum*

- **cols 0240D–0241A** — `nonaginta et sex` / `nonaginta sex` pomegranates

  A genuine disagreement in the sources, left standing.
