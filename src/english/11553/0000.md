## PROLOGUE. *A literal explanation of the living creatures and of the wheels.*

[0527A]

To many, the Holy Scriptures grow far sweeter when they are able to perceive in them some fitting understanding according to the letter. And then, as it seems to them, the structure of spiritual understanding is more firmly [0527B] established when it is aptly founded upon the solid ground of the historical sense. For upon the superfluous and the empty, who could found anything solid, or firmly establish it? For since the mystical senses are drawn out and formed from the fitting likeness of those things which are set forth in the letter, how shall it instruct us unto spiritual understanding in just those places where it assails itself, or sounds nothing but what is frivolous? And so men of this sort are often scandalized rather than edified, when they run upon such places of the Scriptures. But the ancient Fathers, on the contrary, gladly received it when they fell upon such places of the Scriptures as could not stand according to the letter. For from these they compelled certain men — men who received Holy Scripture, yet mocked the allegorical senses — [0527C] from these absurd places of the letter, I say, they compelled them to take refuge in spiritual understanding, since they did not dare to deny that the Holy Spirit had written nothing emptily in however great a foolishness of the letter. Hence I judge it to have come about that the ancient Fathers silently passed over the exposition of the letter in certain more obscure places, or handled it somewhat more negligently — men who, had they pressed on more fully, could without doubt have done it far more perfectly than any of the moderns. But neither do I silently pass this by, that certain men, as though out of reverence for the Fathers, are unwilling to attempt what those men left undone, lest they seem to presume something beyond their elders. But having such a veil for their own sloth, they grow numb in idleness, and deride, mock, and blow away the industry of others in the investigation and discovery of truth. But he who dwells in the heavens [0527D] shall laugh at them, and the Lord shall mock them. But let us receive with all eagerness what has been treated by the Fathers, and let us search out with all alacrity what was left undone by them, and let us bring forth for the common good, with all liberality, what has been shrewdly discovered, so that what is written may be fulfilled: *Very many shall pass over, and knowledge shall be manifold [n: (Dan. XII)]*. Behold, that wonderful vision concerning the heavenly living creatures, which the prophet Ezekiel saw, blessed Gregory expounds according to the mystical understanding. But how it may be able to stand according to the letter, he does not say. Of the second vision he says that it cannot stand according to the letter. And this indeed is true, but according to that acceptation which he himself there assigned to it; for if we should wish to examine that same letter of which he says this [0528A] according to another acceptation, perhaps, even according to the historical sense, we shall be able to draw out of it a fitting understanding. Assuredly, the same man says, in the same exposition of Ezekiel, that for the most part in sacred speech something is said obscurely for this reason: that, God wondrously dispensing it, it may be expounded in manifold ways. Let the manifold [0528B] exposition of Holy Scripture, therefore, never be rejected, in so far as it seems to agree with reason, or to serve usefulness. In so far, then, as each has received grace in this matter, let no one hesitate to seek with all diligence those things which the sagacity of the preceding Fathers either passed over of set purpose, or, entangled in the occupation of more necessary matters, could not unfold. And so I gladly say what seems to me concerning that prophetic vision; for perhaps this exposition of ours, such as it is (even if it cannot satisfy another man's skill), may perhaps in some measure help the diligence of others in this very thing, and their zeal for inquiry.

Behold, it is said of the living creatures [n: (Ezech. I)]: *The likeness of a man was in them.* This likeness of a man, so far as pertains to the letter, in part was, [0528C] and in part was not, as the words subjoined declare. In part it was, because they had the face and the hands of a man; and again in part it was not, because they had wings and calves' feet, and several faces, whence also it is added concerning them: *Four faces to one, and four wings to one.* If the heavenly living creatures have four faces, and have them on four sides, then, contrary to the nature of earthly creatures, they are able at one and the same moment to see toward every side. And *their feet were straight feet.* It behooved them to have straight feet, so that they might be able, as is gathered from what follows, to walk toward every side without any turning of the body: *And the sole of their foot was as the sole of a calf's foot.* The said living creatures are described as having the sole of a calf, so that they may be understood to be furnished not by contrivance, as men's are, but by nature. But that [0528D] they might be able in like manner to walk toward every side, it behooved the form of their feet to exceed in some part the likeness of a calf's. But in this, that it is said that *sparks* were in them, or rather from them, as of *glowing bronze,* it is shown that they had fiery faces which scattered burning sparks. *And they had faces and wings on four sides.* Assuredly, if, as was said above, they had four faces; if they had them, as is here said, on four sides, then it must be that on each single side (so far as concerns the letter) they had none but one alone. And they had four wings, so that, turned in whatever direction alike, they might be able to fly in every direction.

It is to be noted that they had four faces, four wings, [0529A] four hands, and feet of the same number; yet he does not seem to express the number of the feet, for the reason that it is neither rare nor unusual to have four feet. And their wings were joined one to another. And so the distance between the living creatures as they walked was as great as the joining of their outstretched wings could allow. They did not turn back when they walked, but each one went before his own face. Since in what follows he is going to say, They went and returned, how is it here said that as they walked they did not turn back? But see whether perhaps "they turned back" is put here in one sense and there in another, if we are not to make the prophet lie. For "they turned back" seems to sound the same as "they turned about." But in what follows it seems to sound the same as "to go back." [0529B] Turning back, therefore, they did not turn back, because, wishing to turn back — that is, to go back — they did not turn back, that is, they were not turned to the rear, because they were not turned about. Hence it is that, speaking long afterward of the wheels, he says: *When they went, they walked toward the four sides, and they did not turn back as they walked.* Assuredly, if they went toward four sides, it was needful for them to go and to go back. How then, as they walked, did they not turn back, save in the manner determined by the words subjoined? For at once it is added: *But to the place whither the first, which was going, inclined to go, the rest also followed, and they were not turned about.* Behold: in place of what he first put, "they turned back," repeating the same sentiment he puts "they were turned about," and he determined what he had first put obscurely. [0529C] But what shall we say amid these things, save that we have recourse to the sentiment of blessed Gregory, that, God dispensing it, a thing is therefore put obscurely so that it may be expounded in manifold ways? And without doubt it is brought about by this obscurity of Scripture that, although perhaps there be one exposition according to the letter, it may be manifold according to the mystical understanding. Let it be said, therefore, of the living creatures, that they did not turn back when they walked. But why they could go and go back without any turning of the body is shown from what is subjoined: *But each one went before his own face;* as if he said openly: It was therefore not needful for them to turn the body when they wished to go back, because they had a face toward that side also We, when we wish to go back to what is behind us, must needs use a turning of the body, we who [0529D] have only one single face. But those creatures turned back, and yet were not turned to the rear, but went before their own face — that one, namely, which they had toward the side to which they wished to go. There follows:

*The likeness of their countenance was the face of a man, and the face of a lion on the right of the four of them. And the face of an ox on the left of the four of them. And the face of an eagle above the four of them.* Behold how in his description he proceeds by certain degrees. First he said that they had four faces; afterward he said that they had them for four sides. He now says lastly what sort of side, I say, they had. If the said living creatures had, after our fashion, a right side and a left and a hinder part, it would be easy for the prophet to express the several sides, and according to them to distinguish the faces [0530A] one by one. But this he could not do, because in them every side was a front side. He did, therefore, as he could: he first puts one face, and according to it distinguishes the rest. He says, then: *The likeness of their countenance was the face of a man.* Who does not know that in us the countenance is wont to be the chief and more principal part? And so it is said that the countenance — that is, the first and more principal face — in them was the face of a man. And on the right of that human face of the four of them was the face of a lion. And on the left of the same was the face of an ox.

It is to be noted that of the eagle's face he does not say on which side it too was, because from what precedes this very thing was manifest. He said only that of which, had it not been said, there could have been doubt: namely, that that [0530B] face was assuredly higher than the rest. *And the face,* he says, *of an eagle of the four of them was above.* That is, higher than the rest. And birds indeed are wont, as we all know, to have longer necks than other animals. *And their faces and their wings were stretched out above.* His saying, "and their faces," is a defective sentence, as though he said: and their faces were in this manner, and their wings stretched out above those faces. For he sets "and their faces" before it, so that he may the more fittingly bring in what he wished to say of the wings: *The two wings of each joined.* This same thing he had said above in other words, but he repeats it in order to add: *And two covered their bodies.* It is not so to be understood as if they always covered their bodies with the same two wings, [0530C] and flew with two others only, since they did both with both, but by turns. When they had determined to fly before the human face, they did it with those two wings which were on its right and on its left; and with those same ones they flew in the contrary direction, that is, before the eagle's face, but with two wings they covered their bodies. But when they wished to fly before the lion's or the ox's face, they did the contrary: *And each one walked before its own face.* He seems to repeat the sentiment set down above, but I believe he shows something else here. Above he taught that they did not turn the body for this reason, that on the side to which they wished to go they had a face which they might follow, and they walked before it. Here he seems to me to wish to say this, that they went toward as many sides as the sides [0530D] on which they had faces. For it is the same as if he said: Each one of them walked before any face of its own whatever. What follows agrees with this sentiment:

*Where the impulse of the spirit was, thither they went, nor did they turn back as they walked.* Truly they went before each one of their faces, because wherever the impulse of the spirit was — whether for going, or for going back, or for turning aside toward any side whatever — they so went thither that they were not turned about, and between going and going back they used no turning of the body. *And the likeness of the living creatures, their appearance was as of burning coals of fire, and as the appearance of lamps.* Behold what we have already said above, that they had fiery faces, which poured forth heat after the [0531A] manner of burning coals, and light after the manner of lamps.
