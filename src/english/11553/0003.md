## CHAPTER III *In what manner, and by how many circumstances, this mystical description is commended.*

[0537B]

It is indeed to be noted first of all who he was, by whom, and in what manner, whence and whither he was led who saw these things and wrote them and left them to us to be read: *The hand of the Lord,* he says, *came upon me, and brought me thither. In the visions of God he brought me into the land of Israel [n: (Ezech. XL)]*. He who saw these things was assuredly a prophet of the Lord. Yet by whom was he led, save by the hand of the Lord; or in what manner, I ask, save as it is written, in the visions of God? And he was brought into the land of promise — there is no doubt before, that it was from the land of Babylon, from a land to a land with the spaces of so many lands lying between. Whom, I ask, would it not move; whom by right ought it [0537C] not to move, that such a man was led by such a one, in such a manner, and so far, that he might see and hear what he should afterward write and leave to those coming after to be read? Who but a senseless man would hear negligently, or dare to neglect, what Holy Scripture commends with so many circumstances? But not even the very time in which the prophet saw these things did he wish to pass over in silence. For perhaps he foresaw that this very thing pertained to the commendation of the vision itself: *In the twenty-fifth year of our transmigration, at the beginning of the year, on the tenth [0538B] of the month, in the fourteenth year after the city was struck down, on this very day the hand of the Lord came upon me, and brought me thither in the visions of God.* Many seasons of the Jewish captivity had already flowed by; long since had the children of Israel labored under great affliction; and it was as merciful as it was necessary that they should receive, amid so many hard and long-lasting evils, some great consolation, and should feel the proofs of the divine mercy toward them, lest amid so many calamities they should utterly fail, and should despair from the very foundation of the divine help. But who would doubt that it is a great thing, nay a thing above man, that a prophet of the Lord, led by the hand of the Lord, sees in the visions of God? *He brought me,* he says, *into the land of Israel, and set me down upon an exceeding high mountain, upon which was as it were [0538C] the building of a city inclining toward the south, and he brought me in thither [n: (Ezech. XLIII)]*. It is not to be passed over negligently that the building shown is said to have been set upon an exceeding high mountain. For we shut off from ourselves the understanding of many things if we consider these matters negligently, as we easily notice from what follows. In this building, assuredly, the inner court held the innermost place; but the temple, without doubt, held the highest. Hence it is that in the last part of this description it is read, where it is said: *This is the law of the house: on the summit [0539A] of the mountain are all its borders; round about it is the Holy of Holies [n: (Ezech. XLIII)]*. Here, after his custom, he calls the temple "the house." The mountain, then, rose little by little to higher ground as far as that place in which the temple was built, and it sloped toward that place from every side, and there spread itself out into a certain flat and even plain, which received the structure of the temple with an equal eminence on every side. For if on any one side the temple stood out higher, without doubt not all its border would hold the summit of the mountain. Let us consider first, if it please, this building as though it were set upon a level, so that the form of the mountain and the situation of the buildings may afterward be more rightly understood. As is gathered from the foregoing, the middle wall, by which the outer court is girded, is proved to enclose from every side to the opposite side [0539B] five hundred cubits.

That we may begin, then, from the east: of these five hundred cubits the outer court possessed two hundred; in the third hundred was the inner court; in the fourth hundred the temple was situated; and the fifth hundred, and beyond that, it left behind its back. Yet let this be said according to the consideration of the plain surface only, for to this measure the swelling of the mountain added not a little, concerning which it will be needful to consider more diligently in its own place. From this consideration, nevertheless, it is easy to notice at what place the mountain swelled higher, or what sort of form it had, or on which side it was more sloping. And this building is rightly said to have the likeness of a city, in that it was girded on every side with walls, made fast with gates, [0539C] and crowded with many buildings. *And behold a man whose appearance was as the appearance of brass, and a line of flax in his hand, and a measuring reed in his hand. And he stood in the gate.* This man, who is described as having the appearance of brass, seems to bear the image not of an earthly but of a heavenly thing. Heavenly things, therefore, we ought to hope for from the heavenly one, and sublime things from the sublime; and let us handle with a studious mind the things which he says or does. But as for this heavenly man's carrying in his hand a line and a measuring reed to measure out the building — would that it might avail at least for this among disdainful hearers, that no one dare to reprove us when he has seen us subtly seeking out and investigating the situation, the quality, and the measure of the several buildings, and with such great diligence assigning them, and proving each single thing as we can, [0539D] either by the authority of Scripture or by the attestation of reason. But lest perhaps what has been said thus far be less than enough for the commendation of these things, let us hear still what the same man says, and what the prophet of the Lord enjoins concerning them. *And the same man spoke,* he says, *to me: Son of man, see with thine eyes, and hear with thine ears, and set thy heart upon all things that I shall show thee; for thou hast been brought hither that they may be shown thee; announce all things that thou seest to the house of Israel.* He is bidden to perceive by sight the situation and the measure of the buildings, and by hearing the rite and the manner of the ceremonies; and to set his heart to each single thing, to understand subtly, to retain by memory, and not to keep greedily to himself alone the talents of so great knowledge — but neither to this man nor to that: nay rather, to announce all things to the house of Israel. [0540A] Whom, I beseech you, could so great an attestation of the heavenly oracle not render attentive or astounded, and inflame with desire toward the understanding of these things? But let us now return to this, that we have heard the same man standing in a gate. To which of the three aforesaid walls this gate may seem to belong, we can notice from what is added in what follows.

*And he came to the gate,* he says, *which looked toward the eastern way, and went up by its steps.* Without doubt the gate to which he came is one, and the gate from which he came, in which he had also first stood, is another. But it is clearer than light that the gate to which he was coming is that of the outer court, not of the inner court. For the things that follow indicate this so openly that they need no proof of ours. But if he came from some gate of the inner court to this [0540B] eastern gate, which he measures in the first place, he would assuredly have to descend by its steps, not to ascend. But it is said: *To him he went up by its steps.* But lest we should be able to take this of some other gate of the outer court, manifest reason seems to go against it. For first, in the outer court, he takes the measure of the eastern gate, and afterward passes on in order to the measuring of the others. Why, I ask, would he not take first the measures of that gate in which he had first stood, if he had wished this to be understood of some other gate of the outer court. It is plain, therefore, that this gate in which he first stood was of the outermost wall, of which wall it is said: *Behold a wall on the outside round about the house on every side [n: (Ezech. XL)]*. But since concerning this wall and its measure, with the testimony of Scripture adjoined, I believe I have sufficiently [0540C] spoken above, I here avoid repeating the same things: *And in the man's hand a measuring reed of six cubits and a palm [n: (ibid.)]*. In this place he defines the measure of the reed, which above, when he was speaking of it, he by no means determined. Why, I ask, is a palm added to six cubits in this reed, save because in the act of measuring it is grasped and held by that part? As often, then, as we hear the prophet in measuring reckon up the measure of the reed, we ought to understand nothing else — although some judge otherwise — than six cubits. Hence it is that the little chamber is described as first having the measure of a reed, and afterward it is written of the same by way of recapitulation. And the little chambers were of six cubits on this side and on that. This very thing can assuredly be proved [0540D] in many ways, which a sufficiently diligent reader will find for himself: *And he measured,* he says, *the breadth of the building with one reed, the height also with one reed.* If what is here said of a certain building, I know not which, is referred to the whole, the truth and authority of the whole description is emptied out. Is it not, I beseech you, far more right and far more fitting to refer it to a part, than by referring it to the whole to empty out the whole? Does it not seem more agreeable and more consonant with reason to believe that Scripture in this place describes the measures of a certain building which was beside or above the gate in which it records that the man was standing, and had not yet withdrawn? But could Scripture, because it once named the whole a building, not thereafter designate a part by this word, or ought it not to have done so? [0541A] Did it not likewise name the whole "the house," and yet here it did not fear in what follows to refer this name to a part, when it says: And the interior of the house was in the sides of the house? What if we find this very name of "building" set down where it can in no wise be referred to the whole? And there were chambers before the building, and a way before their face, after the likeness of the chambers which were on the northern way.

But even this alone ought to make us credited in this matter: that if we wish here to refer this name of "building" to the whole, we are convicted, as has been said, of annulling the credit of all the things that are here said. Yet I think there is no one so shameless, none so impudent can be found, that he would not be ashamed in this matter to contradict us in a reason so open, [0541B] and would not blush to reprove us in this opinion, when he has clearly seen that all the rest can stand according to the letter after our exposition — if in a truth so perspicuous he has not mocked at sound understanding.

But so much for these things: now let us return to the exposition of the letter: *And he measured the breadth of the building with one reed, the height also with one reed.* If he had said: He measured the breadth of a certain building in that place in which he first stood — if, I say, he had said this, he would have taken away all ambiguity. Yet not because he did not say this did he take away the truth, but after his custom he clouded it over. But one thing he compels to be understood from what follows, [0541C] the other he hints from what precedes: for since it is recorded that the man who was measuring these things had not yet withdrawn from the gate in which he had first stood, it is also manifestly hinted that this building was joined to that place. But what shall we say that this is, that, though he speaks of breadth and of height, he is altogether silent about length — since naturally the length always comes before the breadth or the height?

But mark how artfully, in that he said the breadth, he did not keep silent about the length. For where the length and the breadth are equal toward every side, it may rightly be called breadth.
