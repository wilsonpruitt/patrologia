## CHAPTER VI. *On the remaining things concerning the portico, for the completion of the whole description.*

[0547A]

This being done, it is agreed beyond doubt that between the two extremities of the outer little chambers there are thirty-four cubits, and that eight cubits still remain over on either side. For it is agreed that thirty-four and twice eight fill up fifty. And so, this being completed, if over against the side margins of the little chambers — a low wall being first drawn underneath, then columns erected as we see in cloisters — we raise as it were so many walls as far as the middle porch, we without doubt divide one porch into seven. See how the position of the little chambers divides one porch into seven; and yet it does not leave them all of the same size, but some greater, [0547B] others smaller. This division therefore being made, we can find here what we sought above: namely porches, some of eight cubits in breadth, others of five cubits, yet both of twenty-five cubits in length. For those two which were between the three little chambers had five cubits in breadth, but twenty-five in length. But those two which had been beyond the little chambers on this side and on that held eight cubits in breadth, and twenty-five in length. But also those three in whose ends the three little chambers were situated spread themselves in breadth within the walls by six cubits. But whatever we have now said of one porch on one side of the way, all this we ought to think of the other on [0547C] the other side of the way. For it had just as many little chambers situated in the same way, and was divided throughout into just as many and equal parts. And so the two porches on this side and on that are subdivided into fourteen; and when the middle one is joined to them, fifteen subdivisions of one portico are counted at each several gate. After these things which we have now said concerning the division of the porticoes, it remains here still to ask what we ought to think concerning those thresholds which Scripture mentions. For Scripture plainly designates three thresholds, and declares each one of them to be the threshold of the gate. But one gate could not — just as neither ought it — have more than one threshold; and even if we are willing to assign one of these to the gate of the wall and another to the gate of the porch, it still remains to ask concerning the third. Let us therefore first ask [0547D] which of these is said to be properly the gate's, and, as for that which is not properly the gate's, why it is asserted to be the gate's. But none of those fourteen subdivisions which we showed above had more than eight cubits in breadth; none of them, therefore, could take in a threshold of ten cubits' breadth — subdivisions whose entrance was only from their front, beside the middle porch. To no one, therefore, is it doubtful that that threshold is properly called the gate's which is read to have had ten cubits of breadth. This at any rate Scripture hints plainly enough when, after the description of the threshold of ten cubits, it at once added concerning the length of the gate: *And he measured the breadth of the threshold of the gate, ten cubits; and the length of the gate, thirteen cubits.* For he who had indicated the breadth of the gate from the breadth of the threshold — it remained for him to describe its [0548A] length alone, which he had not yet expressed. But since one gate is raised on one side of the porch over against another on the other side, in such wise that it seems one gate, and quite often several are called one, what reason, I ask, will there be why the one should be believed greater and the other smaller? It is certainly agreed that the two other thresholds had only six cubits of breadth; and if we wish to assign one of them to the other gate, we are convicted of maintaining that it is smaller than the other. But if Scripture meant this, or wished us to think it, why, I ask, did it not at once add concerning the length after the description of the threshold, as it did concerning the other? Why is the breadth of each gate expressed from the description of the thresholds, and — the length of the one alone being carefully expressed — is the length of the other passed over altogether in silence? But by keeping silence concerning [0548B] the one, it hints plainly enough by what it so carefully expressed concerning the other: that what it said of the one it wished to be understood wholly of the other. And so each gate — or, if this sounds better, one gate on both sides — had a threshold of ten cubits, and was of ten cubits in breadth and held thirteen cubits in length. It now remains to ask concerning the other thresholds: whose are they, and, as for those which are not properly the gate's, why are they said to be its? But according as he often names the whole portico "gate," what wonder is it if he calls that its own which was within it? Thus assuredly, if we desire to understand the writer's meaning, we must beyond doubt rather mark and know the modes of his speech than dispute about the propriety of expressions. And so let those thresholds which are described as being of six [0548C] cubits be called the gate's, because they were within the gate; yet they are believed to be properly of certain of those porches which we assigned in the last subdivision. But it is manifest that they cannot be said to be of those which were of six cubits, if these lay open with their whole front; but if not, then they could by no means at all take in a threshold of six cubits. But neither could they be of those which lay between the little chambers, because these held only five cubits in breadth, and beyond doubt could not at all take in thresholds of six cubits. It remains, therefore, that those two aforesaid thresholds belonged to those porches which we proved above to have eight cubits in their breadth. For from those things which are said above, those two porches which are placed beyond the little chambers on this side and on that are proved to hold sixteen [0548D] cubits. And if anyone should wish to divide these into two unequal parts, and to assign less to the one and more to the other, so far that one of them might even take in a threshold of ten cubits, and should in this manner attempt to void that earlier assertion of ours — then assuredly neither of those porches will be of eight cubits, nor will there any longer be able to be found one to which that saying of Scripture can rightly be fitted: *And he measured the porch of the gate, eight cubits.* If therefore one of these is of eight cubits, assuredly the other also will be of eight cubits. For whatever is taken away from the number sixteen for the one is beyond doubt left to the other. But since we have now spoken sufficiently, as I judge, concerning the description of the portico, and have pursued in order those things which seemed to be said in this place concerning the little chambers or the thresholds, I am free [0549A] in this place, for the greater clearness of the things said and of the things to be said, to gather into one figure the form of all those which above were brought forth part by part, so that the mind of the beholder may easily be able, from the contemplation of it, to gather the place, [0550A] position and number, the quality, quantity and proportion of them all. [0549]

[0549B]But since it is difficult, or even impossible, to represent the length and the breadth and the height of buildings on a flat surface under one and the same figure, I judge it sufficient to have shaped the position and place of all these things which we have said, and, proportional lines being drawn, to have laid as it were a kind of foundation of them all. For when we look upon lines adjusted by a proportional commensuration, and understand by them nothing else than certain laid or marked-out foundations of walls, it is easy to understand besides that from this drawing out of lines walls rise on high, and that a roof rests upon those same walls. If anyone should perhaps accuse me of prolixity in this our exposition, let him see, I beg, how rightly he does so. But, that I may confess the truth, I had rather be wearisome to the more subtle than obscure to the slower, especially in so obscure a matter, where nearly all the things that are said [0550B] need proof rather than exposition. But let us now return to the exposition of the letter, which for the greater part now — at least in those things which are said concerning the porticoes — we trust to be plain.
