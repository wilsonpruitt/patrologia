## CHAPTER VII. *Exposition of the letter upon the description of the eastern gate.*

*And he measured,* he says, *the threshold of the gate by one reed, the breadth* (that is, one threshold), *by one reed in breadth.* As we showed above, although this threshold was not the gate's own, yet he says that it is the gate's for this reason, that it was within its portico, which also he was often accustomed to designate under the name of the gate. But how plainly, as I judge, Scripture hints that that man who enters the gate measures this threshold in the first place, and before the little chambers, [0551A] in that it is found in the first of the aforesaid VII porches. But since it was first said, He measured the threshold by one reed, the breadth, what is it that, by expounding, or as it were by correcting what he had as it were improperly said, he at once subjoined: that is, one threshold in breadth? But to one considering more diligently, what else does this exposition or correction of the sentence seem to be, save a certain declaration of the prophetic understanding, lest it should escape us what the prophet had wished to say? As if he were openly saying that he had indeed improperly called the threshold the threshold of the gate, but ought to have said "a certain threshold" or "one threshold." That therefore this threshold can in some manner be called the gate's, he advises by the first expression; that it is not properly called the gate's, he teaches by the appended [0551B] exposition. In that therefore "one" is said, it warns both that there are other thresholds there, and that this is not properly called the gate's. But in that he sets down "breadth" twice, he wished by the driving of it home to commend it more attentively, lest anyone should wish to refer what is said of this threshold, and afterward of the threshold of ten cubits, to the same threshold, when he had found "breadth" said in both places. If however "length," not "breadth," were set down, perhaps it would seem more properly said. In fine, where we have "breadth," the lxx, Jerome being witness, put "length." But often "breadth" is set down so that it seems to signify not the kind of quantity, but simply quantity. There follows:

*And the little chamber, one reed in length, and one reed [0551C] in breadth, and between the little chambers five cubits,* in which words *he measured* is to be repeated. Mark now with how fitting an order he proceeds, and how little by little and part by part he scatters the seeds of knowledge. First he speaks of the threshold which was in the first porch; afterward of the measure of the little chamber which was in the second; then indeed he adds concerning the measure of the space which was between the little chambers, in which he now shows that there are several of them; and last of all he comes to the last porch, which was situated beyond all the little chambers, whose threshold first, and afterward the measure of [sic: *ispius*] porch, he describes, when he says: and the threshold of the gate beside the porch of the gate within, one reed. And he measured the porch of the gate, eight cubits, and its front, two cubits. But that this threshold likewise is called the gate's, is here taken in the same [0551D] way as above. But he plainly determines where this threshold was: namely beside that porch which was within, and lay in the middle between the two porches lying about it. From this passage, as I judge, it is plainly shown that the fronts of the smallest porches were joined to the middle porch.

It must indeed be noted that, after he had named this threshold the threshold of the gate, just as above, he at once showed by the appended determination that it is not properly called the threshold of the gate. For if this threshold had been properly called the gate's threshold, beyond doubt it would not have been needful to subjoin by a determination where it was. But now where it was is determined in that which is said, *beside the porch of the gate within.* [0552A] And so that threshold whose measure he was taking was beside that porch of the gate which was within, and held the middle place in the triple portico. After the measure of the threshold he joins on the measure of the porch itself, when he says: *And he measured the porch of the gate, eight cubits, and its front, two cubits.* This measure at any rate could not suit the middle porch. For the middle porch was nothing else than a passage, or way, which lay open from gate to gate through the middle of the portico. And so its length, according to the breadth of the whole portico, had to be of fifty cubits. But its breadth could not be less than the breadth of the gates, which beyond doubt, as will afterward be clear, was of X cubits. It is clear therefore that the aforesaid measure was of that [0552B] porch whose threshold he had first described. But when the measure of the porch is taken, what is it, I ask, that it is not determined of which it is — of length, namely, or of breadth? But he is often wont to leave without determination what he foresees that a prudent reader can of himself understand from other circumstances. For after it has been established that the two collateral porches of the triple portico on this side and on that have [sic: *quinquagina*] cubits of length and twenty-five cubits of breadth, it will be able to be plainly weighed that the smallest porches were of twenty-five cubits in length. For it behooves us to understand the smallest porches as nothing else than certain crosswise sections of the largest porches. And so the breadth of the last porch [0552C] is described in that which is said: And he measured the porch of the gate, eight cubits. But this must be noted, that almost whatever has been disputed up to this point concerning measure seems to determine the breadth of the smallest porches. But where we have, *and its front, two cubits,* Jerome again sets down another reading: *and in the front of the same,* or *in the margins, two cubits.* From which words it is plainly gathered what those two cubits designate in this place — the two which seemed to remain over in the margins to a doorway of six cubits, one on this side and the other on that. For if it is agreed that the porch has eight cubits of breadth, it is necessary that for its doorway of six cubits' breadth two cubits remain over in the front. But in the two cubits of the front, according to our reading, he perhaps gives us better to understand that [0552D] space which was above the gate itself up to the planking of the overhanging upper chamber; and according to this, what is said, "its," ought to be referred not to the porch, but to the gate: for how this sentence avails for completing the height of the whole will appear in what follows. But what follows, The porch of the gate was within, we ought to refer to the whole portico. And so this portico was within, because it was situated within the wall to which it adjoined. There follows:

*Moreover the little chambers of the gate toward the eastern way, three on this side and three on that.* One measure of the three, and one measure of the fronts on either side. See how little by little he advances. He determines the number of the little chambers, concerning which it was not yet established; and to the measure of the one little chamber which is described above, the measure of the three is subjoined. [0553A] But because the number of the little chambers is now established, it is equally established also concerning the number of the porches; but it is not yet established concerning the measure of them all. For the position of the little chambers (according as appeared above) divides one side of the portico into VII sections, which Scripture is wont to call porches, the last of which it has already named a porch above. For the three little chambers had shut in between them two spaces of five cubits, and had left two others shut off beyond them, and the three themselves held three; all which run up into the number seven. After these things the threshold of the gate itself is described, when it is said: *And he measured the breadth of the threshold of the gate,* X *cubits, and the length of the gate,* XIII *cubits.* Behold, this threshold, like the foregoing ones, is called the threshold of the gate, and yet here, as in the foregoing, nothing of determination [0553B] is added; and from that very fact it is hinted that it is truly and properly called the gate's. But that it is truly and properly called the gate's will be able to be easily proved when the measure of all the porches has been established. But the measure of them all is indeed easily proved from that which is at once subjoined: And the margin before the little chambers of one cubit, and one cubit the boundary on both sides. And the little chambers were of six cubits on this side and on that. In that he says that there was a margin of one cubit before the little chambers, and one cubit the boundary on both sides, we ought to take it under the same sense: namely, that the margin of one cubit was not only in the front of the little chambers, but also on either side. But we understand this margin about the little chamber as nothing else than a certain projection of the wall, such as is wont to be made either for sitting on, [0553C] or for the strengthening of the wall. For often a wall is founded broader in its lower parts, and when it has been raised a little it is drawn in again. If therefore the little chambers were of six cubits, and had still one cubit on both sides, as also in front, in the margin up to the boundary, then from boundary to boundary of the little chambers there were eight cubits. And so three little chambers took up three times eight, that is XXIV, cubits; and between the first and the second little chamber there were V cubits, and likewise between the second and the third another V, which, added to the foregoing, make XXXIV. But the last porch also is read to be of eight cubits, which number, if it is added to the former, the sum of the aggregation extends up to XLII. And so the eight which still remains over out of fifty remains for the first [0553D] porch. For the whole portico from the gate to the front of the porch, as is afterward read, has fifty cubits. None of porches of this kind, therefore, could take in a threshold of X cubits. It is clear therefore that it was properly the gate's. But it is hinted also from this, as was said above, that after the measure of the threshold, in which the breadth of the gate also is contained, there is at once subjoined concerning its length: *And he measured,* he says, *the breadth of the threshold of the gate,* X *cubits, and the length of the gate,* XIII *cubits.*

It must be noted how, after the measurement of the little chambers, of the porches, and of their thresholds, he describes the measure of this gate; from which it is given to be understood that this which he measures was the inner gate. See therefore how skilfully the measure of the first gate, concerning which nothing [0554A] is said, is taught from the description of this gate. For reason does not allow us to believe that the outer gate is smaller than the inner, nor does the authority of Scripture suffer it to be broader. For from the sides of the gates, and from the sides of the middle porch — where, namely, the fronts of the smallest porches adjoined the middle porch — up to the twin walls of the whole portico on this side and on that, there were XXV cubits. For those porches which on this side and on that adjoined the sides of the gates and the middle porch, as is more fully grasped from the description of the other porticoes, had only XXV cubits in breadth. If therefore, twice XXV cubits being taken on this side and on that, we add X cubits (namely the breadth of the gates), we without doubt fill up the measure of the fronts of the whole portico, namely LX cubits. If [0554B] therefore the fronts on this side and on that had only LX cubits, the gates will not be able to have more than X cubits at all. Above we showed how orderly he proceeded while he described one side part by part until he came in order to the inner gate. One side being described, he briefly subjoins that the measure of the little chambers is one and the same, both on this side and on that, that he might teach compendiously that whatever is said of one side ought to be understood of the other. Hence it is that in the descriptions of the other porticoes, while he studies brevity, he carefully describes one of the collateral porches, of fifty cubits in length and of twenty-five cubits in breadth, and, passing over the other in silence, leaves the same things to be understood of it.
