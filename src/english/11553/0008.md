## CHAPTER VIII. *On those things which pertain to the description of the whole.*

[0554C]

After the description of the parts, three things are subjoined under one continuation, in which the measure of the whole is briefly comprehended and completed. The first pertains to the height, the second embraces the length of the whole portico, the third the breadth of the whole. To the height pertains that which is said: *And he measured from the roof of the little chamber unto the roof thereof, a breadth of twenty-five cubits, the gate, door over against door.* To the length pertains what follows: *And he made fronts of sixty cubits.* To the breadth, what is set down last: *And before the face of the gate which [0554D] reached even unto the face of the porch of the inner gate, fifty cubits.* But how from the first clause the height of the whole may be observed will appear more fully from the last parts of this description. And we believe that throughout them all there were upper chambers or lofts, as we find almost everywhere in the other porticoes of houses, or in other buildings. And so we understand the roof of the little chamber as nothing else than the floor of the loft, so that the same planking was both the roof of the little chamber and the floor of the upper chamber; and perhaps, according to the custom of the land of the Palestinians, we ought to understand that the roof of the gate was flat, not raised. And so the upper chamber above was of a height of twenty-five cubits, according to the height from roof to roof. But note that he set down "breadth" where he ought to have set down "height"; [0555A] for in his usage he is often wont to misuse the name of breadth: for since there are three kinds of dimension — length, breadth, and height — breadth is often so set down that it seems to determine only the quantity of the space, and not any kind of dimension. And so in this place he set down "breadth" improperly, just as above where he described the thresholds. But in that it is said, door over against door, the expression is defective; but "having," or something of that kind, can be supplied. And so he measured the gate having in the little chambers themselves door over against door, because the doors of the three little chambers on one side were set opposite to the doors of the three others. But in that "fronts" is set down in what is subsequently added — *And he made fronts of sixty cubits* — it is referred to the twin face of the portico: the one by which it looked toward [0555B] the inner parts, the other by which it looked toward the outer. But of its inner front he understands what is subsequently added: *And at the front, the court of the gate on every side round about.* And so this pavement went about three sides of the portico, as the way lying on both sides evidently shows, about which the position of the chambers was ordered.

It must certainly be noted that the measure of the fronts of LX cubits is taken from within the walls, as is easily gathered from other places — as from that place where a porch of XXV cubits in length and of V cubits in breadth is described, as will be shown there. We ought also to think concerning the measure of the breadth, namely, that it was taken within the walls. But in that [0555C] which was said, and he made fronts of LX cubits, the length of the whole portico was determined; but now in that which is subjoined its breadth is determined: and before the face of the gate which reached unto the face of the porch of the inner gate, fifty cubits. By "the face of the gate" he calls the inner surface of the wall, namely that which he had toward himself who was within and was taking the measure. He measured therefore from the inner side, not from the outer, because if he were taking the measure from the outer he would find so much more than L cubits as the wall held in thickness. But by "the face of the porch of the inner gate" he means that part of the porch which it had toward the temple, and which clung to the inner gate. But the gate reached unto the face of the porch in this, that it extended its portico as far as [0555D] that place. But in that it is said "unto the face of the porch," it seems to answer to two things: namely, how far the gate reached, and how far the space of fifty cubits extended itself. He made therefore, or measured, before the face of the gate which reached unto the face of the porch of the inner gate, fifty cubits. There follows:

*And slanting windows in the little chambers, and in their fronts, which were within the gate on every side round about, he made from the upper parts.* In these words it is repeated: he made, therefore, slanting windows, that is, broader within and narrower without. He made them in the little chambers and in their fronts, that is, chiefly in the fronts — which windows were within the gate, that is, within the portico, because the little chambers in which those same windows were stood within the [0556A] portico itself. For concerning the windows which were of the porches themselves and not of the little chambers, he took care to add consequently: *And likewise,* he says, *there were windows also in the porches round about within,* where also is added: *and before the fronts a painting of palm trees,* which needs no exposition.

It must be noted that he says in the plural "in the porches," and "within"; but I think that from this place it easily becomes known that he sometimes comprehends the whole portico under the appellation of the gate. For although a gate of ten cubits might easily comprehend one little chamber, yet it could by no means comprehend several, so that it should have had to be said of it: And slanting windows in the little chambers which were within the gate. There follows:

*He led me out to the outer court.* It is said toward the inner parts indeed, [0556B] and yet he is led out who goes forth from the portico in which he was before. But he is led out to the outer court, because there was another also, the inner, *and behold, chambers, and a pavement laid with stone in the court on every side round about. Thirty chambers round about the pavement.* Concerning these chambers we wish for the present to say nothing else, save that fifteen were on this side and fifteen were on that. But this pavement had XV chambers on the north, and fifteen on the south. The portico of the outer court on the east, the portico of the inner court on the west; whence is that which is afterward added: *And the pavement at the front of the gates, according to the length of the gates, was lower,* and lower for this reason, that the gates, or the portico [0556C] itself, were raised up above it with great loftiness, and it lay down below in the depth between them. From this place likewise it is clear in which direction the length of the porticoes ought to be taken. For if the pavement was at the front of the gates according to their length, it is agreed that the length of the porticoes was that which extended itself beside the pavement. Next he determines the measure of the pavement, which he had not yet expressed: *And he measured the breadth from the face of the lower gate unto the front of the inner court on the outside, a hundred cubits toward the east and toward the north.* He calls the gate of the outer court the lower gate; for the gate of the inner court was in a more eminent place. For the building had been set upon an exceedingly high mountain, as [0556D] Scripture mentions in the very beginning of the description. The gates, therefore, which were outer were themselves also lower; and those which were inner, the same were also upper. And so by "the face of the lower gate" he wishes the front of the outer porch to be understood; by "the front of the inner court on the outside" we ought to understand the outer face of the upper portico. For the inner portico turned its face toward the outer parts, and the outer none the less turned its own toward the inner parts

It must certainly be noted from this place that he attributes the place in which the inner porticoes were now to the inner court, now to the outer. Whence also in what follows he will say of the same portico or porch: *And its porch, that is, of the outer court. But on the east was the lower gate, and on the west the inner gate.* How [0557A] therefore did he measure from the face of the lower gate unto the front of the inner court a hundred cubits toward the east, since this is from east into west, not from west toward the east? Wherefore a distinction is to be made there: and he measured the breadth from the face of the lower gate unto the front of the inner on the outside. And there another sentence is to be begun, and from the foregoing "he measured" indeed is to be repeated, thus: *And he measured a hundred cubits toward the east and toward the north,* that is, a hundred cubits from the west toward the east, and likewise a hundred cubits from the south toward the north; or what he says, toward the east and toward the north, we can understand thus: Toward the east, that is, toward the eastern region, he measured a hundred cubits between gate and gate. And toward the north, that [0557B] is, the northern region, he likewise measured a hundred cubits between gate and gate, so that this last was said by anticipation. Now sufficiently, as I judge, has it been said thus far concerning the description of one portico; and what follows concerning the rest can easily be understood, almost throughout, from the aforesaid without exposition. But let him who shall have read these things know that almost all which we have discussed concerning the porticoes can be proved from the authority of Scripture, and that many things could also have been proved by us with fuller reasoning, which we willingly pass over while we avoid wearisomeness.
