## CHAPTER IX. *On the northern and southern portico of the outer court.*

[0557C]After the description, therefore, of the eastern gate, the measure of the northern gate is described, in these words: *The gate also which looked toward the way of the north of the outer court he measured, both in length and in breadth; and its little chambers, three on this side and three on that; and its front and its porch, according to the measure of the former gate: fifty cubits its length, and its breadth twenty-five cubits.* Here he takes "gate" properly, which he measured, like the former, of thirteen cubits in length and of ten cubits in breadth; and its little chambers, three on this side and three on that, all of six cubits; and its front, of sixty cubits; and its porch according to the measure of the former gate, [0557D] fifty cubits its length, and its breadth twenty-five cubits. Such a porch he nowhere there described openly, and yet such a one, as is proved from this place, was there — and not one only, but two, because one was on the one side and the other on the other side, besides that one which lay in the middle; nor for that reason was there any the less there one great porch such as he describes there, because the parts do not take away the whole, but constitute it. Hence it is that, the parts being first described, afterward there is subjoined concerning the whole, when it is said: *But its windows, and the porch, and the carvings, according to the measure of the gate which looked toward the east.* Behold, here "porch" is repeated; but it is first set down with reference to the parts, and afterward is referred to the whole. In that therefore he first says "porch," not "porches," he puts number [0558A] for number, just as in what follows he sets down "little chamber," not "little chambers." We can, however, repeat from the foregoing "on this side and on that," and refer it to the porch. After these things something is added here which is not read concerning the former gate, which nevertheless is rightly understood of that one also, according to whose measure this one is described: *And of seven steps,* he says, *was its ascent.* Common to both is what follows, *and a porch before it.* All the outer gates, indeed, had their porticoes before them. For the inner ones, as we have already said, had their porticoes behind them. There follows:

*And the gate of the inner court over against the gate of the north.* For this inner gate of the north had its front over against the outer northern gate, and its right side over against the eastern gate described before. [0558B] Or, in that he says that the gate of the inner court is over against the gate of the east and of the north, we can take it of different gates, so that we understand that the gate of the inner court is over against the gate of the north, just as the gate of the inner court was over against the gate of the east; and then what he says of the gate of the east is a repetition of that sentence which he set down above concerning the same. But although he is unwilling here to describe its measure, yet he wished here to touch briefly upon it, that he might more fittingly subjoin the measure of the pavement which lay between it and the outer gate: *And he measured,* he says, *from gate to gate a hundred cubits.*

It must be noted that he describes only the length of a hundred cubits. For how great the breadth of the pavement was, or what is to be thought concerning it, will appear better [0558C] from what follows. The northern gate being described, there follows afterward concerning the southern gate: *And he led me out to the southern way, and behold a gate which looked toward the south; and he measured its front and its porch according to the foregoing measures, and its windows and its porches round about, like the other windows: fifty cubits the length, and the breadth twenty-five cubits.*

It must be noted that it is said "according to the foregoing measures." For if all are of the same measure, whatever is said less fully in one seems to be supplied from the addition of another's description. That "porch" which is set down first ought to be referred to the whole; but to the parts, that "porches" which is repeated — which is clearly plain from the measure of their description. For it is subjoined to the second, [0558D] and not to the first, that which is said, fifty cubits the length and the breadth twenty-five cubits.

It must be noted in this last sentence how to the two things premised two are subjoined: and its windows, and its porches round about. Behold two; there follow two others: Like the other windows, fifty cubits the length, and the breadth XXV cubits. To that which is first premised, "its windows," there answers first "like the other windows"; to that which is premised in the second place, "and its porches," there answers in the second place, "fifty cubits the length, and the breadth twenty-five cubits." He seems to have described no such porch [0559A] at the first gate, nor more than one at the second. Yet from the description of this one it is shown that there, as here also, there were several. But the reasoning of the first description does not suffer that there were more than two such at each of them, on account of that which is read there concerning the measure of the length or of the breadth: of the length, and he made fronts of forty cubits; of the breadth, and before the face of the gate unto the face of the porch of the inner gate, fifty cubits. But if it is agreed that there were two porches of such size at all of them, it remains that at each there is over a third also, of fifty cubits in length and of ten cubits in breadth, so that the whole together ought rightly to be called a triple portico, and may be able to fill up the aforesaid measure. There follows:

[0559B] *And by seven steps was the ascent to it:* which indeed is read also at the second gate · *And a porch,* he says, *before its doors,* not behind the doors, as in the inner gates. There follows:

*And there were carved palm trees, one on this side and another on that, on its front.* The palm trees of the fronts are now called carved, now [sic: *depietae*]. But perhaps in both places both are to be understood, that is, that they are carved and overlaid with colors. At the former gates also he had spoken of the carving of palm trees. But that one was on this side and another on that, he had not determined. But as above, after the description of the outer gate, he makes mention of the inner gate, that he may more fittingly bring in the measure of the pavement lying between: *And the gate,* he says, *of the inner court [0559C] in the southern way; and he measured from gate to gate in the southern way a hundred cubits.* It must be noted that he understands the portico by the gate, and that he describes only the length of the pavement: for its length will appear better from another place.
