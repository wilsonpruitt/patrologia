## CHAPTER X. *On the three porticoes of the inner court.*

After the outer gates, behold, he describes the inner gates. And after the last one which he described, he passes to the one nearest to it, when he says: *And he brought me into the inner court to the southern gate, and he measured the gate according to the foregoing measures, its little chamber, [0559D] and its front, and its porch, by the same measures, and its windows, and its porch round about, fifty cubits in length, and in breadth twenty-five cubits.* What is read above concerning the outer gates is repeated concerning the inner gates: and he measured, he says, the gate according to the foregoing measures. But in that he says "little chamber," not "little chambers," he puts number for number. But that which is subjoined, and its front, and its porch by the same measures, can be referred to the whole, and then in what follows "having," or something of this kind, must be understood: And he measured its windows, and its porch round about, [sic: *habenta*] L *cubits in length, and in breadth* XXV *cubits.* "Its windows," that is, the porch's, he calls the windows of the walls [0560A] surrounding the whole on the outside. But "the windows of its porches," that is, of the porch, he calls the windows of the walls cutting through the smallest porches. Nor ought this to be passed over, that he calls the parts of the whole "the porches of the porch." But if "of the porch," not "of the porches," is read (for either reading is found), it is referred to the same sense, and this is said of the porch of the porch of one side in such wise that it may be understood of both. It must be known, however, that by Jerome not "of the porch," but "of the porches" is set down. There follows:

*And the porch round about, the length of twenty-five cubits, and the breadth of five cubits.* See how in every description still something new is added which he had not said in the foregoing. A porch of five cubits in breadth he had nowhere above [0560B] described, and yet if it was at this gate, then it was at all the others, because it is clearly agreed that there was one measure of them all. But where, I ask, was the porch of this one, save between the little chambers? For Scripture describes a space of five cubits between the little chambers. And not one only, but between three little chambers two on the one side, and two on the other side. As therefore it is read in the first description concerning a porch of eight cubits, so what is read in this description concerning a porch of five cubits is to be understood of the several ones throughout. By this porch of twenty-five cubits in length it will easily be able to be proved that that measure of the fronts of forty cubits was taken within the walls. There follows that in which the inner porticoes [0560C] differ from the foregoing. *And its porch,* he says, *toward the outer court.* For the foregoing looked inward, and this one outward. *And its palm trees,* he says, *upon the front.* Where "it had" is understood; but this too was common to it with the rest. There follows:

*And there were eight steps by which it was ascended through it.* In this again the inner ones differ from the foregoing, which had only seven. After the southern gate he passes to the eastern: *And he brought me into the inner court by the eastern way, and he measured the gate according to the foregoing measures, its little chamber, and its front, and its porches, as above, and its windows, and the porches round about, the length of fifty cubits, and the breadth of twenty-five cubits.* In this description alone "porches" is set down twice in the plural, [0560D] and rightly indeed, because the description of the smallest porches also is completed out of the preceding description. And so he seems in this description to make mention of a twofold division in that he wished to set down "porches" twice. For one portico is first divided into three porches. Then two of these, the greater, are subdivided into fourteen others. And so after the measuring of the smallest, he who measures seems to proceed to the measuring of the greater. Let the first therefore be referred to the smallest, the second to the greatest, whose measure is there subjoined in that which is annexed, the length of fifty cubits, and the breadth of twenty-five cubits. There follows: *and its porch, that is, of the outer court,* that of it, [0561A] the porch, he says, is of the outer court, as if he were openly saying: that very porch which is its own, that is, of the inner court, is in some respect rightly called the outer court's — whose it ought by no means at all to have been called, if after the likeness of the outer porticoes it had lain within its own gate; but now it is rightly called that one's to which it adheres. Then there is subjoined that which is read in the preceding: *And palm trees carved upon its front on this side and on that, and its ascent by eight steps.* Then the last description of the gates follows, when it is said:

*And he brought me to the gate which looked toward the north, and he measured according to the foregoing measures its little chamber, and its front, and its porch, and its windows round about, the length of fifty cubits, and the breadth of twenty-five [0561B] cubits. Its porch looked into the outer court, and a carving of palm trees upon its front on this side and on that, and its ascent by eight steps.* Here, because nothing new is added, exposition indeed seems superfluous. There follows: *And through the several chambers a door in the fronts of the gates. There they washed the holocaust.* That he says the chambers, or the doors of the chambers, to be in the fronts of the gates, we ought to understand of the inner porticoes. For since the doors were in the fronts of the chambers, and all looked toward the pavements which lay before the inner porticoes, it is now rightly said that the doors of the chambers were in the fronts of the gates. The position therefore of the chambers is determined in that which is said: And through [0561C] the several chambers a door in the fronts of the gates. Let therefore the line by which the fronts of the gates are bounded be drawn out, and from it as far as the outer wall let the chambers be ranged. For whatever shall have been behind that line will not be in the fronts of the gates. In the first description of all he had spoken of the chambers, but throughout all the others he makes no mention of them. Why therefore is this which is here said of the chambers annexed to this last description, unless that through it the same might be given to be understood of them all. Likewise also what he annexes concerning the eight tables, he wished, as I judge, to be understood of all three inner gates. All three inner gates had eight tables along the sides of their porches, although Scripture does not seem openly to determine this. But see how shrewdly Scripture [0561D] in certain places wished to pass over certain things in silence, which reason indeed, more diligently sought out, does not suffer to lie hidden. Certainly of the chambers it is said: There they washed the holocaust. But of the tables also it is written: *And upon the tables the flesh of the oblation.* If anyone therefore thinks that the eastern gate alone has chambers, or that only at the northern gate there were tables, let him see, I beseech him, whether reason suffers that the eastern gate not have a place where the things which are washed there ought to be laid up? Or that the northern gate not have a place where the things which are laid up there may be washed? Are the things which are sacrificed at the northern gate washed at the eastern, and the things which are washed at the eastern sacrificed at the northern? Is neither of these done at the southern gate, because in its description [0562A] no mention was made of the chambers? I know indeed that the Fathers pass negligently over certain places of the Scriptures which they could easily penetrate, while they desired to have found, and rejoiced in, things which could not stand according to the letter, that by this at least men might be convinced that allegory ought to be received, which in those times still very few would scarcely have been willing to receive. Let it therefore scandalize no one if we say anything other, or otherwise, than he shall have found in the glosses. Let no one disdain that we wish to gather the ears of grain that remain; let no one wonder that some could have slipped from them, or rather that they of their own accord left them to the poor — they to whom this was divinely enjoined, to whom it was divinely given to have filled the granaries of so many volumes out of the harvest of the Scriptures. But you wish to honor and [0562B] defend the authority of the ancients; yet never do we more truly honor the lovers of truth than by seeking, finding, teaching, defending, loving the truth. Attend therefore not to whether I say something new, but something true. I little fear to say something different, so long as I say nothing perverse. But behold, I return again to the exposition of the letter, and attend rather to what it itself wishes to say than to what others will say of me.
