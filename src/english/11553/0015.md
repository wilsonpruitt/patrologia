## CHAPTER XV *How, or from what, it is gathered what the steepness of the mountain is.*

[0573D]But since we have completed those things which meanwhile seemed to be said concerning the site of the buildings, the place demands that we now speak, at least in part, of those things which we promised were to be added for the completion of the foregoing; and so we shall return to the exposition of the letter. First, therefore, let us consider how many cubits the inner court has from east to west according to the consideration of the plane — which, so far as the surface goes, was on every part, without any ambiguity, of a hundred cubits; but that: *And he measured the court, a hundred cubits in length and a hundred cubits in breadth, foursquare.* But, as was shown above, from the north toward the south also this court enclosed a hundred cubits according to the plane. But two hundred cubits on this side and two hundred [0574A] on that the two porticoes with the court interposed held, and in this manner from the one extremity to the other there were five hundred cubits. But on account of the same likeness and quantity of the porticoes we observe that the eastern porticoes also, with the court interposed, occupy equally two hundred cubits according to the plane, and extend as far as the middle hundred. All the inner porticoes, therefore, adhered according to one likeness to that middle hundred, and over against its middle; and because they held a measure of sixty cubits within the walls, from this measure there remained over, on either side, twenty cubits of that middle hundred.

But we taught above that the chambers of the sanctuary adhere immediately to two porticoes of the inner court, that is, to the northern [0574B] and to the southern, and bound the western side of the inner court. Whence it is agreed that the inner court, from east to west, according to the reckoning of the plane, occupies not more than eighty cubits. But if the eastern way ascends uniformly from the outer gate as far as the site of the temple, assuredly the pavement which lay between the two porticoes, of a hundred cubits, will obtain not more than eighty cubits according to the consideration of the plane, although so far as the surface goes it had a hundred without ambiguity.

Hence, therefore, it is given to understand that the walls of the porticoes, just as those of the three aforesaid houses, are of five cubits in breadth. For [0574C] it is agreed that each one has only fifty cubits within the walls. This was the cause why above we judged five cubits to be assigned to the wall, and assigned the other five to the way, out of those ten which we found to be beyond the separate building.

In this place indeed we wish to give that warning which everywhere we ought to hold for certain, and as it were for a rule: that whether any building whatsoever be situated on the level or on a slope, it contributes nothing to its measure, nor takes anything away. For whatever Scripture teaches concerning the measure of the buildings, it behooves us to take according to the consideration of the plane also. Since, therefore, the northern porticoes, or the southern also, were in all things like to the eastern [0574D] porticoes in measure and in quality, it behooves us to estimate the like concerning the pavements interposed, and that they occupied no more than eighty cubits according to the plane. This, however, was between these and the eastern and the pavement, that [sic: *iliud*], according to one dimension, had eighty cubits, as has been said, but according to the other dimension possessed a hundred cubits, even according to the plane. But the measure of these, according to one dimension, was of eighty cubits, and according to the other of eighty-five cubits, according to the consideration of the plane. For these two pavements seem to begin from the east from one and the same line as the inner court also, yet by five cubits they seem to extend beyond its boundary on the west — which from the foregoing [0575A] it is most evident to understand. Yet after the likeness of the inner court they too can have on that part a hundred cubits so far as the surface goes. The northern and the southern pavement, therefore, are raised from a twin side, but the eastern indeed and the inner court from one side only. But if it is asked concerning the inner court how much it was raised on the one part, this is easily and without scruple discovered by one skilled in the discipline of geometry. Without doubt the court, which had eighty cubits at the base and a hundred at the surface, was higher by sixty cubits in its more eminent part. We can deliver most certain rules of this matter, but we fear to be burdensome to the simpler sort. We shall meanwhile give some proof of this matter, by which anyone, however simple, may [0575B] grasp a trial of our assertion.

[0575C]Let a line of any magnitude be drawn out, divided into five equal sections, and marked at the several sections with several letters. And let the lying line be A B C D E F. Above this, at the fourth section, that is, upon E, let another line be erected, in such wise that it lean more to neither part, but make on this side and on that right angles, and equal to one another, and contain three measures, each of them severally equal to the aforesaid sections. And let the line erected upon E be E G H K. Let yet a third line be drawn out from A to K, obliquely raised. This [0575D] therefore being done, taking a compass, if you find the first and the third line, that is, A F and A K, equal, you will hold by your own experiment the credit of our assertion. Let it be supposed, then, that each section is of twenty cubits. A F, therefore, that is, the first line, will be of a hundred cubits; and A K, equal, namely, to it, that is, the third line, will be of just as many cubits. But the erected line, that is, E K, will have sixty cubits. The court, therefore, as has been said, which had eighty cubits at the base and a hundred cubits at the surface, was without doubt higher by sixty cubits in its more eminent part; the form of which matter you have manifestly in the triangle A K E. But this triangle's base, that is, the lying line, and of it which the hypotenuse, that is, the inclined line, if you draw them out to whatever length, [0576A] and apply to them the cathetus, that is, the line erected orthogonally, of whatsoever magnitude you make the triangles, you will see its three lines always answer to one another in the same proportion. For always the hypotenuse will be to the base *sexquiquarta*, and the base to the cathetus *sexquitertia*. From the outer side of the eastern gate, therefore, unto the front of the porch of the temple the height of the mountain grew by two hundred and ten cubits, if the steepness of the same mountain rises uniformly in that direction. Since therefore any portico whatsoever held sixty cubits from the outer side unto the inner side, the earth on the inner side, according to the aforesaid tenor of the steepness, was more eminent by forty-five cubits. But the seven or eight steps of ascent, of which we read above, [0576B] we believe to occupy the breadth of the wall alone, which was of five cubits, or of seven feet and a half, so that there is a step to each foot, and the eighth step can be numbered, or not numbered, with the other seven.

Hence it is that concerning the outer southern portico we read: *And by seven steps was the ascent to it;* and likewise concerning the inner southern: *And there were eight steps by which the ascent was through it.* Were those on which the ascent was made to it outside it, and those on which the ascent was made through it inside it, so that the breadth of fifty cubits, which was within the walls, should have only eight steps? who would think so? But the ascent was made to it [0576C] and through it by those very steps which the breadth of the wall held. On them the ascent was through it, because the wall was of it; and on them the ascent was to it, because through them there was access to its inner parts.

Now, therefore, from the aforesaid we can conjecture how great the height of one portico is. For the inner gate, as we read, had thirteen cubits of height; to which if we add those two cubits which the gate, as was said above, had in front as far as the upper chamber, the sum rises to the fifteenth number. But from the roof of the little chamber, that is, from the floor of the upper chamber, unto the roof of the gate there were, as is read, twenty-five cubits. Let these twenty-five be joined to the foregoing, [0576D] and we have forty. To all these we add yet five cubits above the roof of the gate itself, as it were for making battlements, and thus we fill up forty-five cubits of height on the inner part. But it was, as was said above, lower by forty-five cubits on the outer side; and according to this its whole height on the outer part seems to rise to ninety cubits. It seems indeed to have two upper chambers on the outer part, but one on the inner part; and of the third and innermost little chamber it is said to be:

*And he measured the gate from the roof of the little chamber unto its roof.* [0577] [0579]
