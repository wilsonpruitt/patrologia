## CHAPTER XVI. *A description of the temple and of its porch.*

*And he brought me into the porch of the temple, and he measured the porch, five cubits on this side and five cubits on that.* In that he says "on this side and on that," we ought to understand it not concerning the gate of the temple, but of the porch. But the breadth of the gate, as is read afterward, was of ten cubits. Whence it is agreed that the whole length of the porch, as is afterward [0580] determined, was of twenty cubits. There follows:

*And the breadth of the gate, of three cubits on this side and of three cubits on that.* If we consult the usage of speech, we are accustomed to accommodate the name of "gate" to a threefold sense. For by the name of gate we sometimes designate the door itself, which is opened and shut. Sometimes the opening itself, or the approach, through which passage is made. Sometimes the compass itself by which the opening is girt, [0581A] that is to say, that part of the wall which embraces the door, and is discerned to be unlike the rest of the wall in material or in form for the sake of ornament, and to overtop it. For we understand one thing and another when we hear or read "he carried off the gates of the city," or "he stood in the gate of the city," or "he adorned the gates of the city with varied marble and varied carvings." By an equivocation of this kind the sense is confounded in this place also, unless it be subtly discerned. But it is agreed from what follows that the breadth of the gate was of ten cubits, and that the sides of the gate (that is, either leaf) hold five cubits of breadth. That therefore which is read in this place, "he measured the breadth of the gate, of three cubits on this side and of three cubits on that," it behooves us to understand according to the third acceptation. He measured, therefore, the breadth of the gate, of three cubits, [0581B] that is, the wall itself, adorned with varied carvings up to that much of space. Which sense Scripture itself also seems to determine in that it says "on this side and on that." "But the length of the porch, of twenty cubits, and the breadth, of eleven cubits." The length of the porch extended along the breadth of the temple, and therefore one measure of both is found; "and by eight steps was the ascent to it," that is, to the gate; "and there were pillars at the fronts, one on this side and one on that." These pillars we believe to have been placed on this side and on that, over against the aforesaid three cubits of the gate, where he measured the breadth of three cubits on this side and of three cubits on that. *And he brought me into the temple, and he measured the fronts, six cubits of breadth on this side, and [0581C] six cubits of height on that, the breadth of the tabernacle [n: (Ezech. XLI)]*. [var: Vulg. reads "of breadth" in the second member also, not "of height"] Let those see how they understand this place concerning the breadth of the tabernacle, who dispute concerning the construction of the tabernacle. For to determine that in this place I do not think to be of the present business. But that he is said to have measured the fronts, of six cubits of breadth on this side and of six cubits of height on that, we can understand in this manner: First he measured how much of breadth there was in the side of the gate as far as the wooden wall which carried the sides which are afterward described, on the inner part of the gate. Secondly he measures not what breadth there was on the other side — for this he weighs from the measure already taken — but what height there was from the upper parts of the gate as far as the first upper chamber; but if the unfolding of the leaves [0581D] had ten cubits, and the breadth of the temple within the walls was of twenty cubits only, it is consequent that we understand those projections on this side and on that, against which the leaves were beaten back when the gate was shut, to be of one cubit in breadth, and the opening itself between those projections to have eight cubits. Otherwise we cannot find six cubits on this side and on that. There follows: *And the breadth of the gate, of ten cubits; and the sides of the gate, of five cubits on this side and of five cubits on that.* Behold what we said above concerning the breadth of the gate, or of the leaves. After these things the length and breadth of the temple itself is determined, when it is said: *And he measured the length, of forty cubits, and the breadth, of twenty cubits.* The outer house being described, he passes to the inner house, and describes in [0582A] these words that which is called the holy of holies: *And having entered within, he measured in the front two cubits, and the gate, of six cubits.* Just as in the former description he first of all measured the breadth of the gate, of three cubits on this side and of three cubits on that, so here first of all he measured two cubits in the front, that is to say, that part of the wall which seemed to pertain to the ornament of the gate.

But it must be noted that the compass of ornament of this kind, as well of this gate as of the former, seems to gird them on three parts, that is, from above and from either side. But at the former gate the measure is taken on either side, and what is to be thought concerning the upper parts of the gate is silently intimated. But here he who measures seems to take the measure in the upper parts of the gate, [0582B] and to show by intimating what ought to be thought of either side; this seems to be that which there he is said to measure "on this side and on that," but here "in the front." There follows:

*And the gate, of six cubits, and the breadth of the gate, of seven cubits.* If the projections on this side and on that about the opening, against which the leaves were beaten back, be understood to be not of one cubit, as above, but of half a one, what is said will be manifest. In that therefore he says the gate of six cubits, [is] to understand the opening itself between the projections. But that which he adds, "and the breadth of the gate, of seven cubits," it behooves us to refer to the breadth of the leaves. For if the opening between those projections was of six cubits, and had a projection of half a cubit on this side and on that, it is manifest that the coupling of the leaves [0582C] held seven cubits of breadth. Above he said what of breadth there was in the side of the gate, but how much of breadth the opening had he did not openly say, which nevertheless he left to be understood along with it. But here, conversely, he determined the measure of the opening, and gave to be understood along with it what of breadth it had on either side. For if the opening itself extends to six cubits only, without doubt on either side seven cubits of breadth are found. But that the same breadth was in the upper parts of the gate and in the side, he showed above. But how different it was in this place, he does not at all say. What else therefore does he seem to intimate by keeping silence, save that we ought to estimate like things of like? It is given therefore to be understood that there are seven cubits [0582D] of breadth in the upper parts of this gate, as also on either side.

It must indeed be noted how he says some things openly and intimates others by keeping silence. And how he alternates this very manner of speaking, and displays, so to say, the skill of his art. For what in the first place he expresses openly, this in what follows, when he has come to a like place, he silently intimates; and conversely, what in the foregoing he had intimated by keeping silence, this in what follows he manifestly determines. This is what we marvel at in carvings, where the intricacies of knots are woven. For what in one coupling of the interlacings is laid over that it may appear, in that which follows is laid under that it may disappear. There follows:

*And he measured the length, of twenty cubits, and the breadth, of twenty cubits, before the face of the temple.* [0583A]But how the length and breadth of the inner house is described in these words, is shown by the determination that follows, when it is said: *And he said to me: This is the holy of holies.*

But it must be noted that this measure of length or of breadth, as well of the outer as of the inner house, is equal to that measure which we read concerning the temple of Solomon. But double to that which we find concerning the tabernacle. But since Scripture in this place is altogether silent concerning the height, it can be doubted concerning the outer house to which it ought to answer: whether to the temple of Solomon according to equality, or to the tabernacle of the covenant according to the said proportion. For it will have twenty cubits if it be double to the measure of the tabernacle, but thirty if it be equal to the measure [0583B] of the aforesaid temple. But if we say that there were thirty cubits of height as far as the first upper chamber, it behooves us to concede that the gate, of ten cubits of breadth, has a height too tall and very incongruous to its breadth, that is, of twenty-four cubits. For from the aforesaid we have that there were only six cubits of height above the gate. But if it be said that the height as far as the first upper chamber was of twenty cubits, consequently the gate as well of the outer as of the inner house will have a height apt and congruous to its own breadth. For the outer will be of fourteen, the inner of thirteen cubits. Behold how from the height of six cubits which he had above taken above the gate, [0583C] the height as well of either gate as of the upper chamber is gathered, which he had not openly said. But how much of thickness the wall of the temple had, he determines when he subjoins: *And he measured the wall of the house, of six cubits.* There follows: *And the breadth of the side, of four cubits, on every part round about the house.* By these sides which he now describes I understand nothing else than certain walks arranged along the wall, within and without, on the two sides of the temple; and they were, from the lower parts as far as the upper, one after another immediately joined to one another. Whence is that which follows:

*But the sides, side to side, twice thirty-three.* And perhaps they are for this reason called sides, because they were on the sides of the temple only, and not on the fronts. But since [0583D] there was a height of twenty cubits only beneath the first upper chamber of the temple, it could not hold more than three sides. For as is read in what follows, from the foundation of the one unto the foundation of the other there was a space of six cubits only. There were therefore three within, from the bottom as far as the upper chamber, and thirty without, from the bottom as far as the top. But the inner parts of the temple in their upper portions did not have sides of this kind, as the things that follow make manifest. Well therefore is it said "twice thirty-three," in that there were thirty-three on the one side and thirty-three on the other. But he adds yet more concerning the description of these sides, when he says: *And there were projections which entered through the wall of the house, on the sides round about, that they might hold, and might not touch the wall of the temple.* [0584A] Beams, therefore, projected from the walls and supported the sides; but for this reason the plankings which were founded upon them did not everywhere reach as far as the wall, namely, that they might admit sight, and that the gaze of those looking on might run freely from the lowest parts as far as the upper, and, comprehending the whole under one view, might be able to judge of the whole.
