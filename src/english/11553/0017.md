## CHAPTER XVI. *A description of the temple and of its porch.* (cont.)

*And there was a pavement rising up in a round by a winding stair, and it carried into the upper chamber of the temple by a circuit. Therefore the temple was preferable in the upper parts, and thus from the lower parts the ascent was made to the upper into the middle.* [var: Vulg. *latius*, "wider"] A way of this kind, ascending by steps and bending itself into a circuit, we see daily in the ascents of towers, or of lofty buildings of this kind. But in that he says, "therefore the temple was wider [0584B] in the upper parts," he manifestly determines that the sides described did not everywhere reach as far as the topmost parts of the upper chambers, or were altogether not in the upper parts of the temple; and therefore the amplitude of the temple was wider there, where it had no obstacles of this kind between the walls. But that which is said, "and thus from the lower parts the ascent was made to the upper into the middle," seems to show that this temple had two upper chambers, and to divide it into three dwellings. But in that which is called "the middle," we understand the first upper chamber, in that that first upper chamber was the middle of the three dwellings. Above he had described the breadth of the sides in that he said: *And the breadth of the side, of four cubits, on every part round about the house.* But now he describes their height [0584C] in that which he here subjoins: *And I saw in the house the height, the sides founded to the measure of a reed, by a space of six cubits.* He does not say "of six cubits and a palm," although he has premised the measure of the reed. Whence from this place also we can note what we have already said above concerning the measure of the reed: "and I saw," he says. But where did he see them? In the house. But in what manner he saw the height, [so also] the sides founded round about; therefore they were in the house, and according to the height of the house they were founded one above another. There follows:

*And the breadth by the wall of the side without, of five cubits.* It seems to me that the same place is designated in these words which is afterward called the place of prayer. It was, therefore, of five cubits in breadth, as it were [0584D] a certain appendage made alongside the wall of the first and lowest side, that there might assemble there for the sake of prayer those who either could not or dared not enter the temple. He measured, therefore, a breadth of five cubits adhering to the first side from end to end along its whole wall on the outside. There follows: *And the inner house in the sides of the house.* This is said for this reason, lest the sides should be thought to be lacking about the inner house; "and between the chambers a breadth of twenty cubits round about the house on every side." Concerning these chambers he has as yet said nothing, but, certain things concerning the place of prayer being interposed, he straightway subjoins their description. *And the door,* he says, *of the side for prayer: one door toward the way of [sic: Aquilenis], and one door toward the southern way, and the breadth of the place for prayer [0585A] of five cubits round about.* Behold, he repeats concerning the place of prayer, that he may add. For above he had neither called that place the place of prayer, nor [said] that it was round about, that is, that it encompassed the temple on three sides. Let us now gather into one the things we have heard concerning the measure of the temple, and let us first see concerning its breadth. In the first place the temple itself, within the walls, nay rather within the sides, held twenty cubits of breadth. But the inner sides on this side and on that it had of four cubits, and the walls of six cubits of breadth. The sum of these rises to forty. But the outer sides had likewise on both sides four cubits of breadth, and the place of prayer had five on either side. All which if you gather into one, without doubt you find fifty-eight. There remains [0586A] therefore on both sides a way of one cubit between the temple and the chambers, which we read above to be over against the twenty cubits of the inner court. But this way could be beneath the projection of the roof which covered the place of prayer, and then the front of the temple filled up sixty cubits. But the length of the temple is read to have been of a hundred cubits, as is expressed in what follows and is gathered from what precedes. For the temple itself had forty cubits of length within the walls, but the holy of holies twenty, and the porch eleven of the breadth of the temple. But the four walls held twenty-four cubits, and the place of prayer five cubits. All these, if you reduce them into one, you will fill up the sum of the hundred. [0585]
