## CHAPTER XVII. *A description of the buildings which lay next to the temple.*

[0587A]

But now it seems that we must speak of those chambers concerning which it is said:

*And between the chambers a breadth of twenty cubits round about the house on every side [n: (Ezech. XLI)]*.

Now we taught far above concerning these chambers, which lay about the temple, how they were distant from one another by twenty cubits. But because the separate building, as was shown above, extended itself a hundred cubits between the northern and the southern house, and a space of twenty cubits still lay between on either side, it is plain that between the northern and the southern house there were a hundred and forty [0587B] cubits according to the level; whence it is agreed that between the temple and each house there were, this one thence, forty cubits: for it was shown that the remaining sixty are taken up by the breadth of the temple.

But how will a space of forty cubits be able to contain that court, of which it was said above, of fifty cubits in breadth? But plainly what the place did not have according to the level accrued to it from the swelling of the mountain. If therefore we understand that court to have forty cubits according to the level and fifty upon the surface, we shall see that the slope of the mountain in this place and in the aforesaid courts answers to itself in the same proportion. For a hundred stands to eighty in the same proportion as fifty [0587C] to forty.

After the description of the temple there is joined the description of the separate building: and the building which was separate and turned toward the way looking to the sea, of seventy cubits in breadth. But the wall of the building was of five cubits in breadth round about, and its length ninety cubits. All these things, as I judge, are sufficiently plain from what has gone before, and therefore they do not need another exposition.

But by "the sea" he here means that which far afterward he calls the great sea, which was on the west: for there was another on the east, which he calls the eastern.

But after he had said that this building was turned toward the sea, he at once brings in something concerning its breadth, that by that very thing he might hint according to which dimension it was turned toward the [0587D] sea.

But it must be noted that, because the temple, the separate building, the adjoining court, and the building both northern and southern were of one length, he records the length of them all under a single continuation:

*And he measured,* he says, *of the house* (that is, of the temple) *the length, a hundred cubits; and that which was the separate building, and its walls, of a hundred cubits in length.*

*The breadth before the face of the house, and of that which was separate toward the East, of a hundred cubits.*

*And he measured the length of the building over against the face of that which was separate at the back, the ethecae on either side, of a hundred cubits.*

[0588A]It must indeed be noted that that breadth of a hundred cubits, which he measures before the face of the house, that is of the temple, can be called both breadth and length, but in different respects. For if you consider that whole court in which the temple was situated between the northern and the southern house, from the south to the north it will be of a hundred and forty cubits in length, and of a hundred cubits in breadth toward the East, just as he here says. But if you attend not to the whole, but to any one part on whichever side of the temple, it will be, as is read in what follows, of a hundred cubits in length and of fifty cubits in breadth.

But under the following sentence he determines the site of the buildings. For the building described above was separate at the back of the temple, and over against its own face [0588B] it had, on this side and on that, on either side of the temple, a certain building.

*And he measured,* he says, *the length of the building over against the face of that which was separate at the back, the ethecae on either side, of a hundred cubits.*

You see surely that on either side there was a building, and that each had ethecae, and that by that very thing whereby he measured the ethecae he also took the measures of the houses.

We understand these ethecae to be certain fore-lofts [sic: *Ante solaria*], which, as Jerome testifies in these words, at Rome they call lofts projecting from the walls of the upper chambers, which some of the Greeks call *exedrae*.

What follows seems to be a recapitulation with an addition:

[0588C] *And the inner temple, and the porches of the court, the thresholds and the oblique windows, the ethecae round about on three sides over against the threshold of each, and paved with wood round about in a circuit.*

But it is plain why he said the temple was inner, because it held the middle place among the aforesaid buildings. He names them porches of the court, because they lay next to the court.

But above he had marked out ethecae on either side, while here he says they are on three sides; whence it is agreed that the separate building also had ethecae. From which fact we prove that there is the same likeness in the three, as much in quality as in quantity. But the ethecae, as is said, were over against the threshold of each one, because they projected outwardly from the upper chambers themselves. [0588D] For lest they should be thought higher or lower than the upper chambers themselves, the positions of the fore-lofts are expressed by the thresholds of the lofts.

By "the paving of woods round about" he means the planking with which the lower parts of the ethecae were floored, which, as has been said, seemed to gird the three sides of the temple: *But the earth,* he says, *was up to the windows.*


Whence it can be proved how great the slope of the mountain was, since the walls of the buildings which were on the temple's side had the earth up to the windows.

But in that he says cherubim and palm trees up to the inner house — this having been the holy of holies — he seems to wish to determine that the inner sanctuary did not have carvings of this kind. They were therefore, as is said, [0589A] up to the inner house, that is, in the outer temple, and outside, that is, in the porch, which by the same consideration seems able to be called "outside" by which the inner house is called "within." They were, I say, in the temple and in the porch, throughout every wall round about, not only within but also outside, wrought to measure, both the cherubim and the palm trees.

There follows:

*And a palm tree between cherub and cherub, and the cherub had two faces.*

*The face of a man beside the palm tree on this side, and the face of a lion beside the palm tree on the other side, set forth throughout all the house round about.*

*From the ground up to the upper parts of the gate cherubim and [0589B] palm trees were carved on the wall of the temple.*

*A four-square threshold, and the face of the sanctuary, sight over against sight of the wooden altar.*

All these things, as I judge, are plain of themselves, and do not need our exposition.

But the last sentence expresses the site of the inner altar, in that it was placed in the middle of the temple over against the gate of the sanctuary. This was the altar of incense. For that one which he touched on briefly above, and of which he treats more fully in what follows, was the altar of holocaust.

But by the words that follow he seems to express not only the manner of quantity, but also the fashioning [0589C] of the work

And so he says:

*Three cubits its height, and its length two cubits.*

These things are referred to quantity; the following to quality:

*And its corners, and its length, and its walls, of wood.*

Perhaps he was silent concerning the breadth for this reason, that the length and the breadth were the same, and that in whichever direction it could be called length, just as in whichever it could be called breadth. But it seems to have four timbers set upright at the four corners according to the height, to which four others were coupled both above and below, [0589D] laid out according to the length and the breadth, to which boards were fastened and joined together into a single frame. He calls them corners, therefore, for this reason that [sic: *qnod*] those timbers which were raised at the corners held the corners. But he names as "length" those timbers which measured the length of the altar.

And by "walls" he means the joining of the boards by which their gaping was covered.

There follows:

*And he spoke to me this is the table before the Lord.*

And after these things is added the description of the doors both of the temple and of the sanctuary:

[0590A] *And there were two doors in the temple and in the sanctuary.*

*And in the two doors on either side there were two little doors apiece, which folded one into the other. For there were two doors on either side of the doors.*

I think it is manifest from this place that the same noun is set down in a different acceptation. Otherwise how will it stand, that he says: "There were two doors on either side of the doors"? That which he first calls "doors," then, is to be referred to the fabric which was of wood. That which he afterward calls "of the doors" is to be referred to the entrance which lay open in the wall. But the wall was of six cubits' thickness, and that very thickness of it held two doors, as much in the temple as in the sanctuary. For one was in the wall on the inner side and the other on the [0590B] outer. And of these two doors one hung on the right and the other on the left, after which manner it was in all things also in the temple of Solomon.

This is what is said there: « And two doors of fir wood on either side, and each door was divided in twain [n: (III Reg. VI, 34)], » this is what he had said above: "Sides of five cubits." What there he calls "sides," here he calls "little doors." These sides were coupled with iron fastenings, and when the gates were opened they were folded one upon the other. Just as it is said here, so it was said there, that by holding one another they were opened. And for this reason one door was divided into two and folded together, lest it should exceed the breadth of the wall while it was being opened.

[0590C] *And there were carved on the doors themselves of the temple cherubim, and the sculpture of palm trees, just as they were set forth on the walls also.*

All these things are sufficiently plain of themselves.

But rightly is it subjoined:

*Wherefore there were thicker timbers on the front of the porch outside, over which were the oblique windows.*

For the matter required thicker timbers to be there, where for the sake of adornment there had to be worthier and excellent carvings. And it is given to be understood from these words that the walls were covered with timbers, just as in the temple of Solomon.

There follows:

*And the likeness of palm trees on this side and on that, on the little shoulders [0590D] of the porch, according to the sides of the house and the breadth of the walls.*

These things are plain:

*And he led me out into the outer court by the way leading to the north, and brought me into the chamber which was over against the separate building.*

It is agreed that he treated of the front of the porch of the temple in the last place, from which place he now seems to pass through the northern gate of the inner court into the chamber described. Rightly indeed. For this chamber certainly had its face toward the aforesaid walk of ten cubits in breadth, which began from that place where [sic: *sta*] portico was joined to the portico of the northern house, [0591A] and where, as is gathered from this place, namely on the side of the triple portico, an approach lay open to that chamber:

*And he brought me,* he says, *into the chamber which was over against the separate building, and over against the edifice verging to the north.*

*In the face of the length, a hundred cubits of the door of the north, and of the breadth, fifty cubits.*

*Over against the twenty cubits of the inner court, and over against the pavement laid with stone of the outer court, where there was a portico joined to the triple portico.*

*And before the chambers a walk of ten cubits in breadth, looking toward the inner parts, of a way of one cubit; and their doors toward the north.*

[0591B]We have expounded the description of all these things above so diligently that we do not know whether we ought to add anything (unless perhaps for the exposition of the letter) to what has been said. By "the door of the north" he means that door of the temple of which he had spoken above, and "the door of the side toward prayer. One door toward the north." Not yet indeed, because what he first set down as if in the singular, he afterward marked out in the plural. "And he brought me," he says, "into the chamber," and in what follows, "and before the chambers a walk of ten cubits in breadth, looking toward the inner parts, of a way of one cubit." But that which follows, "And their doors toward the north" — if *earum* is read there, it is to be referred to the porticoes; but if *eorum*, it is to be referred to the chambers. For in either case the sentence will be able to be true.

[0591C]There follows:

*Where the chambers were lower in the upper parts.*
