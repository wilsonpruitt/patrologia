## CHAPTER XVII. *A description of the buildings which lay next to the temple.* (cont.)

Because they supported the porticoes which projected from them, from the lower and from the middle parts of the building. The description of the walk having been premised, it is well added where the chambers were; for they were beside the walk, and clung to it on the north.

This too must be noted, that he says "porticoes" in the plural, which before he called "portico." Indeed, as is easily weighed by these words, both the house and its portico were divided into a threefold storey. For if the building had lower parts and middle parts, it certainly had highest parts also. For a middle is not spoken of save with reference to two extremes. And so the house had, as also [0591D] did its portico, chambers at the bottom, one upper chamber in the middle, and another upper chamber at the top. The portico too had chambers in the lower parts over against the chambers of the house, which chambers supported the porticoes. For there were porticoes in the upper parts of these, one over against the first upper chamber and another over against the second upper chamber, which were supported by the chambers. The lower portico projected from the inner parts of the building, because it was situated above the chambers, which answered to the lower parts of the buildings. The upper portico projected from the middle parts of the building, because it was situated above the first portico, which answered to the middle parts of the building. Let us now consider of what sort that is, that the chambers are said to be lower in the upper parts. Indeed, if we estimate the steepness of the mountain about this place according to [0592A] the former manner, we shall find the earth on one side of the building, on the temple's side, higher by sixty cubits than on the other side. But who would not know that, reason requiring it, the pavements of the buildings must be laid upon some level surface? Lest therefore we should think that the earth had been hollowed out on one side by sixty cubits, Scripture says that the chambers are lower in the upper parts, that we may understand one chamber divided into several, and that each has its own pavements — level indeed in themselves, but some more elevated than others, and, according to the ascent of the mountain, raised one above another after the manner of steps. Where therefore the pavements were more elevated, there the chambers were lower, that is, having less of height; and this same thing we understand of those chambers which were beneath the porticoes. [0592B] The same of that division of the six porticoes which we distinguished into seven portions according to the breadth and the ascent of the mountain. The same we judge concerning the chambers which were about the pavements. And for this reason these divisions were indeed long, but little broad. For the same chambers are read to have been of fifty cubits in length, which nevertheless seem to have not more than nine cubits in breadth. Yet these divisions of the chambers seem not so much fenced off by walls as distinguished by columns — yet so that a wall of no small height (as is wont to be done in cloisters) supported these columns, a wall which would receive the elevations of the pavements and set apart the gradations of the elevations. And so in this the chambers of the outer court differed from [0592C] these chambers, that those had columns, but in these there were none.

*For they were three-storeyed,* he says, *and had not columns, such as were the columns of the courts.*

But well is it added concerning these porticoes, "therefore they projected." Because they were supported by the chambers, therefore they projected; and it is indeed subjoined how, or how much, they projected from the earth.

*They projected,* he says, *from the lower and from the middle parts of the building, from the earth by fifty cubits.*

If this over-projection of the porticoes is marked off from the earth upward by fifty and fifty cubits, the whole height of the house seems to have a hundred and fifty [0592D] cubits. But the lower portico did not everywhere project fifty cubits from the earth, but on that side where the earth was lower. But because according to the aforesaid reckoning the earth on one side of the house, on the temple's side, was higher by sixty cubits, and the first upper chamber of the house, according to the description of the portico, projected on the other side by only fifty cubits, the earth seems to project outwardly by ten cubits on the temple's side above the pavement of the first upper chamber. What therefore we read above, that the earth was up to the windows, we ought to understand of the windows of the first upper chamber, so that one went down to its doors by certain steps, as we see in cellars. But we weigh the ethecae to be over against the higher upper chamber. [0593]

[0593B]But since the chambers which were beneath the porticoes clung, together with those very porticoes, to the house, they had on the inner side one wall common with the house itself; which wall indeed was longer than the chambers of the porticoes by as much as the breadth of the house exceeded their length. By the outer wall therefore, which clung to the outer court, the length of the chambers is expressed, when it is added: *And the outer enclosure along the chambers which were in the way of the outer court, before the chambers, its length fifty cubits.*

It must indeed be noted that he does not say that the portico itself [0594B] has fifty cubits in length, but that its outer enclosure, that is, that wall which it had toward the outer parts, was of fifty cubits in length — lest this measure be thought to have been taken within the walls, as in the porticoes above. It is plain therefore, as we have already said above, that it had one wall common with the house, so that it ought to be counted into the measure of both, so that there may be settled concerning the chambers of this portico that which is read of those chambers which are described after the likeness of these. *According to their length,* he says, *so also their breadth.* But that seems to be a general [0595A] sentence which is brought in beneath, when it is said: "That the length of the chambers of the outer court was fifty cubits." For since it is said, not "of them," but "of the chambers," a reference to the aforesaid does not seem to have been made, but a general sentence uttered, so that one length may be understood of all the chambers of the outer court.
