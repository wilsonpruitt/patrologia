## CHAPTER XVIII. *On the other things which remain, unto the end of the description.*

After the description of the chamber which was over against the twenty cubits of the inner court and over against the pavement of the outer court, and after the description of the portico clinging both to the pavement and to the triple portico, see with what wonderful reasoning he repeats what he said far above. *And the length,* he says, *before the face of the temple [0595B] a hundred cubits.* Did he not express this same thing when he defined both the length and the breadth of the inner court? But the prophet understood that it followed, according to the description of the aforesaid, that the length before the temple could not have a hundred cubits according to the level. But by repeating the same again he admonished by inculcation that it without doubt had in some manner what, according to the consideration of the level, it could in no wise have. There follows:

*And beneath these chambers there was an entrance from the East for those entering into them from the outer court, in the breadth of the enclosure of the court which was over against the eastern way, in the face of the separate building.* What the chambers are that are before these chambers, and what the entrance is beneath these and above those, that is, between both sets of chambers, [0595C] we have already said above, and we avoid repeating the same; but the court which he here records was not only in the face of the separate building, but also in the face of the northern house, and that for the greater part. But because the separate building stood farther off from it, it is said chiefly of that of which it seemed less. The chambers being described which were beneath the porticoes of the northern house in the way of the north — whose likeness those which had been beneath the porticoes of the separate building held in all things — he brought in their description compendiously enough by the adjoining of the likeness, when he says: *And before the building there were chambers, and a way before their face, after the likeness of the chambers which were in the way of the north.* If these [0595D] chambers had in all things the likeness of those which were in the way of the north before the northern house, then they supported the porticoes which projected from them, from the lower and from the middle parts of the building. Likewise in the rest we understand one likeness in both, as we have taught above, and we ought not to repeat the same. One thing which he seemed to have passed over above, namely their breadth, he seems in this place to determine, and he embraces in one utterance the measure both of these and of those, when it is said: *According to their length, so also their breadth.*

The description being completed of all the buildings which were toward the north, he comprehends in a brief sentence those which stood toward the south, when he says: [0596A] *And every entrance of them, and the likenesses, and their doors, according to the doors of the chambers which were in the way looking toward the south wind.* A door at the head of the way, which way was before the separate porch, for those entering by the eastern way. Whatever we have said concerning the chamber which was over against the twenty cubits of the inner court, whatever concerning the edifice verging to the north, or its porticoes, whatever we have taught above concerning the walk or the entrance, the whole of it we ought to understand of those things which were toward the south, according to the manner of their site, so that in these and in those there be entrance according to entrance, and likenesses according to likenesses, and doors according to doors, so that in all things throughout all there be on both sides one likeness. See nevertheless how shrewdly he expressed by name one thing [0596B] concerning which there could most be doubt. For perhaps someone would with difficulty believe that the separate building had two porches, lest by having two it should in this exceed the form of the other edifices, since in all the rest it had held the same likeness with them. He says therefore: *A door at the head of the way, which way was before the separate porch.* If there was a way before the separate porch on the one side, there will without doubt be both a way and a porch on the other side, so that on both sides there be one likeness. But this is plain, that the head of that way looked toward the East, which way began from the outer court beside the portico of the northern building. But manifest are the other things which follow, up to that place where it is said: *And when he had completed the measures of the inner house, he led me out by the way of the gate which [0596C] looked toward the eastern way, and he measured it on every side round about.* By "the inner house" he means the temple, which held the middle place among the other buildings. In that he says "he measured it," it must be understood either as that inner house, or as that way, or as that gate. But how had he completed the measures of the inner house, if it still had to be measured? But that he measures in this place the wall dividing between the sanctuary and the place of the common people is most manifestly determined. And so he was measuring the outer gate of the eastern way on every side in this, that he was taking the measures of that wall on this side and on that. And he measured toward the east wind with the measuring reed five hundred reeds by the measuring reed [0596D] round about. And he measured toward the north wind five hundred reeds by the measuring reed round about. And toward the south wind he measured five hundred reeds by the measuring reed round about, and toward the west wind he measured five hundred reeds by the measuring reed. By the four winds he measured it. But what that is, as has already been said, whose measure he took, he shows by a subjoined determination, when he says: *Its wall,* he says, *on every side round about, a length of five hundred cubits and a breadth of five hundred cubits, dividing between the sanctuary and the place of the common people.* It perhaps troubles someone that the same wall is said to have five hundred reeds and five hundred cubits, since the reed is read above to have been of six [0597A] cubits. But nothing hinders our understanding a diversity of reeds, since the manifest sentence of the prophet compels us to this very thing. But if this place of Scripture seems to suffice less for its own testimony as to the diversity of reeds, let us see what is said in what follows, where after a few things the measure of the altar is described: *But these,* he says, *are the measures of the altar in the truest cubit, which had a cubit and a palm.* What is this truest cubit, in which there was a cubit and a palm? Either this is said of the instrument, that is, of the reed, by which measuring is done, or of the measure which is taken by the instrument. For we call a "pole" both that with which we measure and the measure which we take. So "palm," so "cubit," so "sextarius" is said both of the measure by which measuring is done and of the measure [0597B] which is measured. If therefore in this place we take the cubit not as the instrument but as the thing measured, how will it be the truest cubit where there was a cubit and a palm, that is, more than a cubit? But if by "cubit" we understand the instrument, that is, the reed, we call it truest in this, that it had a cubit wherewith it might measure, and a palm whereby it might be held while measuring. And it could be (which I myself also believe) that in the reed of six cubits, on that part where the palm was over, one cubit together with that same palm was set apart and determined by some sign, wherewith indeed that had to be measured which remained below the measure of the full reed. As therefore a part of bread is called bread, and a part of a house is called a house, so a part of a reed could be called a reed. But eighty and [0597C] three reeds with a third of the greater fill up five hundred of the lesser. Perhaps therefore, that he might avoid enmities — nay rather, without doubt for the sake of a certain mystery, that he might express a full and mystical number — he set down in this place the lesser reeds, and what had to be taught mystically he expressed by a mystical number.
