## Sermon on the Mission of the Holy Spirit

[1017D] *The Spirit of the Lord hath filled the whole world; and that which containeth all things hath knowledge of the voice [n: (Sap. I)]*. Behold, brethren, what manner of Paraclete we receive from the Lord's promise; behold what manner of comforter we have. Perhaps you desire to know more plainly how great he is, or of what sort. He is everywhere, he can do all things, and altogether, since nothing can lie hidden. Such assuredly he had to be, who could meet all men everywhere — he who had to lead back so many exiles, to gather men so scattered, to strengthen so many that were sorrowful, to restore so many that despaired unto pardon. Such likewise he had to be, who could fill whatever he willed — he who had to heal so many that were sick, [1018D] to protect so many that were oppressed, and to guard them amid so many perils, and to fortify them against so many temptations. Such, finally, as would be ignorant of nothing — he who had none the less to convert so many unbelievers, to teach so many that were incredulous, to instruct so many that were untaught, to school so many that were unknowing. Such a comforter, protector, and teacher as was needful, we have indeed received: a comforter everywhere present, a protector almighty, a teacher knowing all things. And assuredly a comforter everywhere present was required by men consigned to so many miseries; a protector almighty, by men corrupted by so many vices; a teacher knowing all things, by men led astray by so many errors. O sweet comforter! [1019A] O strong protector! O effectual teacher, who is everywhere, who can do all things, who knows all things; and what else do those words set down above seem to propose, if they be weighed worthily, than that our Paraclete, promised and sent, is everywhere, can do all things, and knows all things! The Spirit of the Lord hath filled the whole world, and that which containeth all things hath knowledge of the voice. If this Spirit of the Lord fills the whole world, how much more must he fill the kingdom of the heavens? For who could doubt that he is in the heavens? Scripture therefore made its assertion for that part concerning which perhaps someone could have doubted. We have therefore an argument from the lesser, that this Spirit ought to be believed everywhere present. For if he is everywhere on earth, how much more, as has been said, everywhere in the heavens? He will therefore be in every place. [1019B] But do you wish to know this also, that this Spirit can do all things, of whom it is already established that he is everywhere? But how does he not avail for all things who contains all things? Surely, if anything could subsist without him, this Spirit would in no way be said to contain all things. If therefore nothing can subsist without him, surely everything that is has its being from him. Of him therefore from whom is the being of all things, it is certainly established that he can do all things. It is therefore an argument from the whole that he can do all things, that without him nothing subsists, nothing can subsist. You have heard of his presence, you have heard of his power; do you wish still to know what ought to be thought of his knowledge? Attend therefore to what that is which is asserted of the same in that which is said: "And that which containeth all things hath knowledge of the voice." It is the same as if it were said, [1019C] "of all voices" — save that the former expression is taken more fittingly than the latter for this reason, that the former is aptly accommodated not only to particulars, as the latter also is, but even to generals, which the latter cannot be. This spirit therefore has knowledge not only of voice generally, but also of voice of whatever kind specially, and even of any voice whatsoever singly. But since he has knowledge of voices, has he also of things? So, beyond doubt. For knowledge of things always accompanies a full knowledge of voices, but knowledge of voices does not always accompany knowledge of things. Because we often come to know many things which we think of, whose words nevertheless we do not know or have forgotten. But who would have full acquaintance with this voice [1019D] which is "ox," if he is wholly ignorant of the thing itself? But I think that the angels would have full knowledge of things even if there were no voices. Knowledge of things can therefore naturally exist even where there is no knowledge of voices. Certainly Adam, who imposed names upon things before there were words, had acquaintance with things. Otherwise how would he impose fitting words upon those of which he had no acquaintance? For every thing that Adam called of the living soul [n: (Gen. II)], that is its name. It seems therefore to be more to have knowledge of voices than of things, since knowledge of things, as has been said, always accompanies knowledge of voices.

It is indeed to be noted that it does not say knowledge of speech, [1020A] but of voice. Now every speech is a voice, but it cannot be converted: for not every voice is also a speech. Now certain voices signify by nature, such as the lowing of oxen, the bleating of flocks; but a speech is a voice significant by convention. Every voice therefore signifies something either naturally or accidentally. But I judge there to be nothing which cannot be expressed by speech or signified by voice; since therefore the signification of voice extends more widely than that of speech, it is rightly said of this Spirit that he has knowledge of the voice. But, as has already been said above, knowledge of voices always carries with it knowledge of things. It is therefore an argument that this Spirit knows all things, that he is asserted to have knowledge of the voice. If he knows that which is greater, then much more that [1020B] which is less. But it has been shown that knowledge of voices is more than knowledge of things. It is therefore an argument from the greater, that all things must be known by this Spirit. The first argument therefore is taken from the lesser, the third, as has been said, is taken from the greater, but the middle argumentation has its argument from the whole. Let it be said therefore of this Spirit, let it be said: *The Spirit of the Lord hath filled the whole world, and that which containeth all things hath knowledge of the voice [n: (Gen. II)]*. Let it be said that he fills the whole world, that he may be believed to be everywhere; let it be said that he contains all things, that it may be weighed that he can do all things; let it be said that he has knowledge of the voice, that it may be understood therein that he knows all things.
