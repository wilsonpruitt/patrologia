## DEDICATORY EPISTLE.

*To the most reverend and most ample prelate, the lord WILLIAM JUXON, bishop of London and Lord High Treasurer of England, PATRICK YOUNG. Greeting.*

That oblivion has full often, *most Ample and [sic: Reverendissim] Prelate,* given occasion for a more renowned fame, and that many things have fallen in order that they might afterwards rise again more adorned, is a thing to which assurance is given and abundant witness borne not only by deserted ruined walls and by rubble long neglected, which, laid low by age, put on a new face and a more august form: but by the monuments of genius also, which are day by day corrected, are published in more emended form, and by the sedulous diligence of the studious are drawn forth, first out of mould and dust, into the light and into the day. Among these is this devout and elegant writing of thy predecessor, which until now has perhaps lain buried in oblivion, that in this learned age it might emerge from the darkness with a greater splendour, and might look again, under the auspices of thy most celebrated name, upon the light which it had first drunk in within thy walls. His aged infirmities, or rather those of the age in which he lived, which occur here and there in phrasing or in matter: if, out of your singular piety, *Your most Ample Lordship* be willing to cover them with but the hem of your own garment, and to deem him worthy of your patronage together with his *Acoluthus* (for to go forth into public alone, without a companion, was neither seemly, nor did the condition of so great a man allow it), and kindly to interpret this our small labour in publishing the two, you will make a step toward greater things, which may hereafter be dedicated to thy name, and will bind me, most devoted to *Thy Amplitude,* by a closer bond of observance for the time to come. Given the day before the Nones of November. In the year MDCXXXVIII.

## GILBERT'S PREFACE.

[1147A]To his lord and most dear friend Robert, by the grace of God bishop of Hereford, brother Gilbert, humble minister of the Church of London: to be exercised in those things which are God's, and in the same to be consummated with a most blessed end.

Lest the authority of thy command, my lord, should seem to me empty or cheap, I have straightway sent thee the book which thou didst ask for: the book, that is, which with the Lord's help I lately put forth upon the Song of Songs, and which I entrusted to no man before thee, either to be copied or to be looked into. For it was not written by me for praise, nor for the sake of a public commendation, but that I might hold in memory the things which, the Lord teaching, I had learned: and that I might show that these things are not, after the manner of the Jews, to be cheaply despised, but to be read humbly with the inmost understanding, and to be understood in the same spirit in which they [1148A] were spoken. And when it had been read through by thee with the utmost attention from beginning to end, thou didst receive it with much alacrity of mind, and, as I hear, didst commend with much praise that I had applied my zeal to this study: and, that nothing might be wanting to its perfection, thou didst ask that a Prologue be fitted to it by me, so that the completion of the work might be worthy, and to it a rendering of thanks be made; for it is written, « Love is strong as death [n: (Cap. VIII, 6)]. » For as there is nothing mortal that can break the bond of death, so there is nothing possible that the urgency of love cannot obtain. This it was that emptied the Lord, and bound him with human bonds; this it was that, after the cross and the sepulchre, led him down to the lowest seats, that he might snatch thence those who had believed that he would come, [1149A] and were diligently awaiting him: this it is that reforms into the liberty of grace those whom, at the dictate of justice, it had made sons of wrath, mercy tempering judgment. This changes my purpose, so that I set a prologue to a work which I undertook to expound without a prologue. For the author of the work was Solomon, who imitated the ancient writers, who do not make their readers attentive by prefixing a prologue, but by showing them the matter of which they are about to treat arouse their attention, and turn their zeal toward the recognition of the things that are said. Whence that saying of Moses: « In the beginning God created heaven and earth: but the earth was without form, » [var: Vulg. *inanis et vacua*, "void and empty"] etc. [n: (Gen. I, 1)]. And that of Exodus: « These are the names of the sons of Israel, who entered into Egypt with Jacob [n: (Exod. I, 1)]. » So, [1149B] running through the whole Pentateuch, thou wilt find the books of Judges and of Kings, and the others besides, written without a prologue; and if prologues have been added to them, thou wilt recognize that they were added long afterwards, after the coming of the Saviour. Imitating these, Solomon begins straightway from the very matter of which he is about to treat, knowing that the matter itself which is handled has in itself either a manifest usefulness, by which it may allure, or an evident reason, by which one may despise the things coveted, or theological considerations, by which one may cast these [1150A] from the mind, and cleave to him alone who is the founder and creator of them. Three books therefore he made, which he does not adorn or enlarge with prologues: one, of « Parables »; another, « Ecclesiastes »; this third, « Sirasirim ». In the « Parables » he allures men to the possessing of those things by which the present life is furnished, that a man's own necessity may be relieved, that in the things wherein there is need his neighbour may be succoured, and that to the indigent there be given as necessity requires. But in « Ecclesiastes » he warns that the mind be not entangled [sic: implieetur] in these things, that love be not bent toward the things which are transitory: but that it judge them as vain, and decide upon and desire the things that are higher than these. Whence, adding « Sirasirim » next in order (which is called the « Song of songs »), in which, flying past all things, the soul stands beside the divine majesty, [1150B] embracing him as a bridegroom, and presenting to him out of her affection all that love can be. The soul, therefore, or the Church, kindled with the fire of love, not seeking these visible things but sighing after the invisible with the whole affection of the heart and the whole intention of the mind, he thus brings in: as one seeking God, yet not yet apprehending him, and, that she may hold him as apprehended, saying these things.
