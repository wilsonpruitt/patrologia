## CHAPTER ONE.

[1149C]

1. *Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth.*

Who is this, so renowned? of form and grace so elegant? that, boldly seeking the kiss of the highest King, she says, « Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth? » Is this soul acting in a dream, feigning for herself what she wills, snatching herself away into some hope without substance, forming empty visions within herself, and even now hoping to apprehend what she could not obtain by laborious endeavours? For whither has this spirit ascended, so unmindful of gold and so forgetful of silver, accounting the precious stone as nothing, so spurning all that the world possesses and imparts to its own to be possessed, that, thrusting these away with the hand of holy perfection, she says: Stand apart, you; my love, withdrawn from you, [1149D] has been carried over into the things above, seeking him alone who alone truly is, and who furnishes to all things alike the cause and the effect of their being; sighing after whom, and impatient of holy desire, this soul communes with herself, and speaks, saying, « Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth: » for a soul so inspired speaks not vain things, but the highest. Long since has she drunk up the streams of Solomon's wisdom, and heard him disputing in « Ecclesiastes »: for once indeed, dainty and tender, when she had not yet come from outward forms to inward things, when she had not yet ascended from the creature to the Creator, [1150C] she clung to the world with all her embraces, and bound herself with all her affections to the very things that are seen: but having entered the school of spiritual doctrine, she was accounted worthy to hear no common master, but that Solomon supreme in wisdom, appointing for holy Church a certain introductory lesson, by which he might call away every elect soul from the world, and might join her, once called away and drawn apart, to him who is above the world, by the indissoluble bond of charity. For that supreme Solomon, seeing in the spirit that almost every soul was as it were digging in the earth, entangling herself in secular business, and no longer lifting her love upward but bending it lower; in order that he might raise up the bowed and wretched one, came down from the height of his own contemplation to the depths of human converse, calling back [1150D] the wanderer, instructing and reforming her with saving precepts, and so at length, by certain steps as it were of ascent, leading her through to the heights of holy contemplation and of love. First of all, therefore, he promises great things to the tender age, saying, « The parables of Solomon: to know wisdom and discipline, and to understand the words of prudence, and to receive the instruction of doctrine [n: (Prov. I, 1 seq.)]. » He suggests winning things, adding, « A wise man hearing shall be wiser, and he that understandeth shall possess governments. » Thus he does not at once preach to her contempt of the world and of the things that are in the world, [1151A] nor rehearse the hard and lofty matters of a straiter life; « for first that which is animal, then that which is spiritual [n: (I Cor. XV, 46)]. » Whence the best of masters, lest by urging weighty things at once he should blunt at the very beginnings the soul which he had taken up to be instructed, permits her, as though for the passing of her years of infancy, to use freely the world and the things that are of the world, and to take delight in them: yet appointing her a law, that she flow not away in sinning, nor turn to the use of malice and of lust what she has received by the great gift of a great author. He sets forth, therefore, to her who now listens attentively, the Parables; and with as many Parables as he instructs her, with so many laws does he inform and bind her to living well and honourably: which Parables indeed, if a prudent reader shall look into them more attentively with the eye of the mind, [1151B] he will understand that within them there is assigned most fully, for an honourable converse, a form of life for the possessors of this world and for those who pass their days in the common life. For because « without faith it is impossible to please God [n: (Heb. XI, 6)], » in the very opening of the treatise faith and the worship of the highest Godhead are inculcated, when it is said, « Hear, my son, the discipline of thy father, and forsake not the law of thy mother [n: (Prov. I, 8)]. » For the discipline of the highest Father, which the Church has most fully learned from him, is this: « To believe that he is, and that he is a rewarder to them that love him [n: (Heb. XI, 6)], » [var: Vulg. *inquirentibus se*, "to them that seek him"] and that in the end of the times he sent his Son in the flesh, and through him fulfilled his promises. But of the law of our mother the Church, the first is that natural law, engraved by the finger of God on the hearts of men; the second, that written upon tablets [1151C] of stone; the third, the law of the Gospel, ministered in the Holy Spirit and in grace. To forsake not the law of our mother, therefore, is this: not to withdraw from the unity of the Church, and from the worship of the highest Godhead, which she received inwardly by the inspiration of grace. But the remaining commandments of the Decalogue shine forth in distinct places throughout the whole volume of the Parables; and by these, and by very many other precepts besides, there is set forth as it were a certain secular holiness, and the life of secular men is ordered by a fit governance of reason; so that a man may so use the world as not to turn aside from God, and so possess the things permitted and made subject to him as unceasingly to abstain from the things forbidden. In the first instruction, since satisfaction has been made to human desires, and it has been permitted to man to use temporal things soberly, according to what was said to the first man [1151D] by the divine voice, « Increase and multiply, and fill the earth, and subdue it, and rule over the fishes of the sea, and the fowls of the air, and all things that move upon the earth [n: (Gen. I, 28)]; » there is now place for a second instruction, that concerning the things which man has long possessed he may now hear what judgment is to be made: whether he is to cleave to them with love, or whether love is to be raised from them to something higher and far better: whence Solomon, running through with the pen of truth each several thing that is under the sun, says in the second volume, « Vanity of vanities, and all is vanity [n: (Eccle. I, 2)]; » and mightily showing that this vanity is present in all transitory things, that the mind is by no means to be wrapped up in vain things, nor love to be entangled in vain things, [1152A] he sets apart from vanity the soul which he had taken up to be instructed, and applies her separately to truth, when at the end of that same work he adds, « Youth and pleasure are vain [n: (cap. XI, 10)]. » And a little after, « Remember thy Lord thy Creator in the days of thy youth, before the time of affliction come, and the years of which thou shalt say, they please me not [n: (Cap. XII, 1)]. » And a little after, in the very close of the discourse, « Fear God, and keep his commandments: this is every man [n: (ibid., 13)]. » By which compendious close of words, what else does he instil into the minds of men but the fear of that highest Godhead, and the reverence of the worship that is owed. For this is the way, this is the access to life, if the mind, seized with holy fear, always turn aside from unlawful things, and the mind, kindled within with the fire of love, [1152B] be unceasingly girded for good things: for man has been set midway in the order of the rational creature, having the good Angel above him, the evil one below, and, when this life has slipped away, he is to be brought to an equality with the one or the other according to the merits of his life. He therefore, as he is midway between these, so has something in common with each: love, namely, with him who is above, fear with him who is below: for the evil angels fear and tremble, but the good love perfectly; and as the evil angel does not rise up to love, so the good angels do not come down to fear, according to that saying, « Perfect charity casteth out fear [n: (I Joan. IV, 18)]. » Since, therefore, love alone wholly carries the good angel into itself, while fear alone presses the evil one downward, [1152C] there is not, apart from man, in the order of the whole creation, any in whom fear runs together with love, in whom these two meet joined to one another. To man, therefore, it is said, « Fear God, and keep his commandments: this is every man: » as if it were said, All this, that is, the whole of this, namely, « to fear, » and by loving « to keep the commandments, » belongs to man; that by fearing he may turn aside wrath, and by loving may attain to grace. Or if it be said, « This is every man, » that is, through fear and love a man is made perfect. Or, « This is every man, » that is, unto this every man is created, this does not differ from the same understanding, since this is the manner and the cause of his creation: that he should so escape wrath, and merit grace.
