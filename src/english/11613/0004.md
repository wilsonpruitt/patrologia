## CHAPTER ONE. (cont.)

For hence the prophet says, « Mercy and truth have met each other [n: (Psal. LXXXIV, 11)], » which is the same as if it were said, mercy and justice began to come each against the other; for they that meet each other come from opposite sides, and tend as it were in contrary directions; so mercy and truth, straightway after the sin, began to tend in different ways: truth was exacting man for punishment, while mercy [1160B] was disposing something better for his repair. And this was a wonderful meeting, justice dragging man to the lower regions, and the other desiring to bring man back to the things he had lost. But as justice prevailed, man was given over to punishments, tasting miseries and hardships and death in the world, corruption in the sepulchre, and after all things, darkness in hell. And when satisfaction had now been made to justice in great part, the fault having been long punished, after many courses of years, place at length was given on high to mercy, which was ever knocking and pressing hard, in the hidden and secret places of that holy and highest Trinity itself; where it was disposed as wonderfully as devoutly, that in him in whom the Father had created all things, in him he should restore the things that were lost. This counsel [1160C] is that before [cj: *antequam*; read *antiquum*, "ancient"], of which the prophet says, « Let thy ancient counsel, O Lord, be made true. » This is that which, « hidden from the ages [n: (Col. I, 26)], » Paul calls a « sacrament. » The Son therefore obeyed the Father, and so much the more wonderfully and the more sacredly, in that it was not a younger obeying an elder, not a lesser a greater, but one almighty, great, himself great, immeasurable, almighty, equal in all things, who obeyed; as it is said in the prophet, « In the head of the book it is written of me, that I should do thy will [n: (Psal. XXXIX, 9)]. » For great is the book of the knowledge and wisdom of God, in which, though thou shouldst read for ever, no end of it ever comes: its inner pages he alone who wrote it has looked upon. In this both men and angels advance without end; and that treasure of God, however many [1160D] be for ever drawing from it, can never be drained: from end to end his wisdom alone and highest reaches. In this all men are written, and the days are formed, and the merits of each are comprehended, and the blessedness due to every one is contained expressly.

In the head of this book, as in its chief part, is written this sacrament of the Incarnation, in that among all the things which God has wonderfully wrought, in creating each thing, in conserving what he has created, and in disposing by a wonderful law the things he conserves, nothing is so astonishing or so wonderful as that, by his own self-emptying, at the command of commanding mercy, he seems to have been visited with injury to himself. But « the works of the Lord are great, sought out according to all his [1161A] wills [n: (Psal. CX, 2)], and his judgments are true, justified in themselves [n: (Psal. XVIII, 10)]. » God therefore, ascending the cross, and spreading out his innocent hands to the nails, as he consecrated the place of death unto life, so those who were held in death he called back to life. For as his blood dripped from the cross the rocks were cleft, that a bodily entrance might lie open by which that most sacred price, received by angelic hands, might be borne down to the lower world; that, the fruit of the tree of life being shared, they might run back into the liberty of life who were held in hell under the faith and at once the expectation of this grace. What is there, then, of which justice may now complain? In paradise a tree was set, « good to eat of, fair to the eyes, and delightful to behold; and the woman took of the fruit thereof, and did eat; and she gave to her [1161B] husband, who did eat [n: (Gen. III, 6)]: » and disobedience being thus consummated, the way to death was prepared: in the Church too the tree of the cross was set up, whose aspect was horrible and whose taste most bitter; its fruit was that temporal death by which the guilty were punished, who for divers crimes were condemned to the cross: to this tree that eternal wisdom stretched out its hands, and tasted this fruit, with no serpent suggesting it, nor woman persuading. Once indeed the serpent had done this: that the woman, coveting what she ought not, should be delighted, as the flesh is; that the man, as it were the rational sense, should acquiesce in the same; and so it had prepared for itself a way by which it might glide into the hearts of the immortal, and draw all things to itself: but [1161C] at the cross all that is his is made void; the serpent suggests nothing to Christ; here the flesh is not delighted; the rational sense itself, in the spirit, draws the flesh after it, to endure the hard things of the cross and to taste the bitter things of death. Virtue, therefore, resides in the cross; and, since he ascended it who is the virtue and wisdom of God, both virtue and wisdom are placed in it. This opens the way of virtue, which, although it be narrow, yet leads to life. The tree of paradise, then, put forth a fruit by which both the suggestion might be received, and concupiscence be aroused by certain enticements as it were: the cross, now set in the bosom of the Church, assuredly presents a fruit which, if thou shalt taste it in the mind, thou dost not succumb, neither with the enemy suggesting within, nor with the flesh enticing thee; but the enemy is conquered in his first encounter, and the flesh is at once called back to serve the spirit, [1161D] and the whole man is brought back into the love of consummated virtue. This, then, is the place in which mercy, which is true peace, kisses justice; for here satisfaction is made to both at once, since both man is redeemed, mercy so working, and to justice far more is paid than was owed; and, according to the Apostle, « The handwriting that was written against us is mightily nailed to the cross of Christ, and blotted out [n: (Col. II, 14)]. » For a handwriting is so drawn up that, after it has been cut and divided into parts, in each part of it the full record of the transaction is retained. Such is our condition with the malignant enemy: for when we sin, [1162A] the fault is with us, gravely engraved by the very pen of the perverse deed, and it is straightway taken up by the memory of the ancient enemy, and firmly retained as impressed there; so that, if there be none to redeem, none to save, there may always be at hand for him something to lay to our charge: which handwriting indeed has been blotted out by the death of Christ, through which both the fault is remitted to us, and his accusation against us is no longer feared.

But what is this, since man was held to an eternal punishment, but the Son of God bore only a temporal death for forty hours? how was satisfaction made, in his death, to the divine justice, which exacted eternal punishments from man? But [sic: *proculdio*] satisfaction was made. For let there be weighed in the balance the very death of the Son of God, momentary if you please, and the eternal death of all men, [1162B] however much it be owed; to whom is it doubtful that the death of the only-begotten, though temporal, outweighs the death, though eternal, of whatsoever mortals? For deservedly it is a greater thing, and by nature more wonderful, that God should die, who is liable to no sin nor to death, than that a multitude however great of mortals and sinners should die. For hence blessed Job says, « Would that my sins, whereby I have deserved wrath, and the calamity that I suffer, were weighed in a balance *(Job VI, 2)*. » That Job, which is « sorrowing, » designates the Lord Jesus, is now doubtful to none: he, pleading our cause before the Father, as set upon the cross, says, « Would that my sins were weighed; » naming as his own, indeed, our sins, whereby we have deserved wrath, in the unity [1162C] and sacrament of the body and the head, and saying: Would that thou wouldst consider, Father, the sins whereby man has deserved wrath, and the calamity, that is, the punishment which I suffer upon the cross, made man unto obedience to thee. For if thou consider both, as truly thou dost consider, my punishment, like the sand of the sea, will appear the heavier, that is, my punishment together with obedience will far outweigh the sins of men; and it will be enough for thee, for the expiation of his fault and for the discharge of the punishment owed by him. Holy Church, therefore, understanding by the spirit with which she was enlightened that this reconciliation was to be given through the hand of the mediator, the Lord Jesus, amid the soliloquies with which the spirit cherished her and kindled her holy love, within the delays of a hope long continued, repeated this Song of love in the hearts of the elect [1162D] with frequent sighs, so as to say, « Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth. » Understanding, indeed, in him who kisses, the Father, by whom the Son was sent for this grace; and designating, in the mouth itself, which after the usual manner is put forth for a kiss, the Son who was sent, who is the mouth and the wisdom of the Father; and expressing, in the kiss itself, which is the pleasing sweetness of those who kiss, the Holy Spirit, who is the love of both: she professed, as most truly as most devoutly, the faith of the holy and highest Trinity, and the incarnation of the Word that was to come, and, through the sacraments of the incarnate Word, the full reconciliation of man with God. For what is more devout than this holy supplication? What can be purer [1163A] than this prayer? In the very beginnings we are at once taught how prayer is to be made, and in what measure supplication is to be offered to the Most High: for « without faith it is impossible to please God [n: (Heb. XI, 6)]; » and the prayer that is without faith he cannot hear. Whence James says, « But let every one ask in faith, nothing wavering [n: (Jac. I, 6)]: » for it is faith that first opens the entrance for prayer into the inner sanctuary of the exalted majesty. Whence she who desires to be heard begins from the Sacraments of faith, so as to say, « Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth; » as if she said: In that I desire to be heard, I stand forward to ask in this faith; in the very substance of the deity I confess unity, in the same I confess and adore the Trinity of persons. It is a confession of unity, that which was said, « Let him kiss; » but of the Trinity, that one who kisses, and a mouth, and a kiss [1163B] are named. The one who kisses is the Father himself: the mouth itself which is put forth for kissing is the Son: and the kiss which is completed by the mouth of him who kisses is the Holy Spirit. Which truth indeed neither dissipates the very substance of the Trinity, nor tears asunder that of the Unity. He indeed who kisses is neither his own mouth by which he kisses, nor the kiss itself which is ministered by his mouth. So too the mouth itself is neither he who kisses, nor the kiss itself which is given. The kiss also itself is neither he who kisses, nor the mouth itself from which it is. Yet far be it that, when we see a kiss given by someone, we should therefore understand three substances: for the substance of him who kisses is one; and both the mouth itself and the kiss are not indeed in another, but only in that very substance of him who kisses. So [1163C] the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit, since they are most truly three Persons, of whom none is the other: this truth of the Trinity takes nothing away from the unity, or from the simplicity of the substance of God; even as that simplicity itself neither adds anything to, nor lessens, the Trinity of the Persons. But because it is not enough to believe divine things concerning God, unless his humble things also be most truly believed, she uses a most exquisite word, saying, « A kiss; » designating most fully by this both the Word that was to be incarnate, and the sacrament of human reconciliation that was to come. And since these things have been thus said, let us, the Lord helping, renew both our spirit and our pen for the treatise following.

*Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth.*
