## CHAPTER ONE. (cont.)

Let us see how these things cohere with those that have been said. [1172C] The Bridegroom marks at once her who asks the kiss and burns with this zeal of spirit: and because hope that is deferred afflicts the soul, lest it afflict her and, by drawing out her hope at length, defer it, he answers her thus as she desires: what thou askest shall be done, « For thy breasts are better than wine. » Which is as if the Bridegroom should say within himself: Thou indeed, my beloved, thou who art joined to me in a heavenly wedlock, not uselessly, nor as one barren, but as one who art fit to bear sons and to nourish them that they may be brought to perfection, from this, that thou hast breasts made fruitful by the heavenly dew. These things are joined thus, lest the reading of the Holy Spirit should seem less coherent with itself, and as it were unstitched. Henceforth let us see what the things that have been said signify. Yet first [1172D] it must be asked: why does the praise of the Bride begin from the breasts? Why does the Bridegroom himself keep silence about the praise of the other things which stand out as worthy of praise? For why is the face adorned with tresses not praised, why the cheeks, why the lips, why the eyes, which he afterwards calls dove-like? and why is the whole praise of the Bride contained first of all about her breasts? This indeed is so because, whereas in the Bride, as has been said, many things please the Bridegroom, there is nothing in which he takes more delight than that she herself should become a mother, should nourish peoples, and, made fruitful by the heavenly dew, should minister with breasts full of the Holy Spirit holy teachings, as it were the nourishment of milk, to sons beyond number. This, then, is that in which the Bride pleases him more, in which she is more at the Bridegroom's heart, whence she is the more praised, and is extolled by him as with [sic: *praecontis*]. Shall we turn aside to the breasts, [1173A] and consider what the breasts of the Church are? The breasts (*ubera*), then, are so called from plenty (*ubertas*), because into them there flows a certain great store of milk, which we suck, and by which we are fed, and by which we are sustained while we pass through the years of infancy. As these are fixed in the breast, so to the mind of the Church, who is the Bride, two Testaments are set forth, that she may meditate upon them day and night, and gather in them an abundance of milk with which she may feed peoples beyond number, may nourish them, and lead them through even to perfection. For some milk is sweet, some sour; yet each stands good and full of savour. What is sweeter than to believe one God, and to love him with the whole heart, the whole soul, the whole mind, not to harm one's neighbour, to abstain from murder, adultery, and theft, and false witness, and from all those things [1173B] which are forbidden by the Law? But to those who sin willingly and maliciously the Law itself sets forth sour milk, adding this: « Let a man render soul for soul, eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot, burning for burning, wound for wound, bruise for bruise [n: (Exod. XXI, 23, 24, 25)]. » Which milk indeed seems sour to him who suffers, but to a good King, and to a Prince established according to the fear of God, it is a soothing milk and most sweet to the taste; because, « when the pestilent man is scourged, the fool will be the wiser [n: (Prov. XIX, 25)]. » And this is the will of the Lord, that one man in the people restrain many, and so punish those who sin maliciously that the Church of God may rejoice in much peace, and may shrink from having any of her sons made sorrowful by the sour taste of the milk. [1173C] But the substance of this milk of which we treat is that which is resolved into butter, and into curd, into cheese, and whey: these declare the degrees of the virtues, by which one must ascend from the least to the perfect. For the whey (*serum*), which is gathered late (*sero*) from the milk, is itself the fear of the Lord, of which it is said, « The beginning of wisdom is the fear of the Lord [n: (Psal. CX, 10)]. » From this there is an ascent into repentance of heart, and confession of mouth, and satisfaction of work: so that thus, through the suffrages of prayers, through many fastings, through alms widely distributed, and through all the things by which sins are purged, one may come to the firmness of the cheese, as it were to a certain consolidating of the virtues: and thus, a certain curd of peace having been made toward all, an ascent may be made to the broad realms of charity, which is designated by the butter. [1173D] The page of the New Testament also overflows abundantly with the sweetness of this milk, which hints to us that the floodgates of heaven are opened, and shows that what was held sweeter among the delights of God has been sent forth to us from heaven unto salvation. It declares that the Word made flesh, that is, God made man, has mercifully come to the aid of man in himself as man, setting forth his sayings and deeds, which Christ wondrously performed in this life; promising also his kingdom, and the life which will be without end, to all the good. It gladdens every man coming into this world with the sweetness of this milk. To the rest, however, it gives sour milk to drink, saying, « Woe to you, Scribes and Pharisees, because you shut the kingdom of heaven before men [n: (Matth. XXIII, 13)]. » [1174A] In which place, threatening « Woe » seven times, he hints at that milk, sour indeed to the setting of the teeth on edge, which he is to give at the end to all the reprobate, saying, « Go, ye cursed, into the everlasting fire, which is prepared for the devil and his angels [var: Vulg. *Discedite a me*, "Depart from me"; and *paratus* for *praeparatus*] [n: (Matth. XV, 41)]. »

These things, therefore, concerning the breasts of the Bride having been tasted beforehand by way of a little preface, this also must be added: that as the breasts are so fixed in the human body that one holds the right side, the other the left, so all Scripture divinely inspired is so divided by its warnings and words that certain things are appointed to the left hand of the Church, certain things to her right. For the Church has a left hand, and she has a right also. Her left hand is this life, her right the life which is to come, which is also eternal. And so all the Scriptures which either [1174B] the first or the New Testament contains, which have been so published as to order this present life, teaching how a man ought to use this world and the things which are of the world, so that he may run through this life without offence to God, turning aside from the vices which it forbids and pursuing the virtues which it commands, all these are appointed to the left breast of the Church. But to the right breast are assigned those which persuade us to despise the world and the things which are of the world, to shrink from its delights, to love God alone, to long for him, to be dissolved hence and to be with Christ. Whence that saying, « Behold the elect of God: they tame the flesh, they strengthen the spirit, they command demons, they flash with miracles, they preach the heavenly fatherland by their manners and their life, and hasten to reach it even through torments; they can be slain, they cannot be bent: » [1174C] and things of this kind rightly belong to the right breast. Enriched, therefore, with these breasts, the Bride feeds peoples, and leads those nourished in the first virtues to the highest and perfect things; and rightly does the Bridegroom applaud her, saying, « Thy breasts are better than wine. » But the wine, in comparison with which the Bride's breasts are called better, what does it signify but the sciences beyond number in which the human mind entangles and wraps itself? so much so that it neglects the things which are God's, and scorns to seek out and search into those things which are God's. Who could number these? who could comprehend them under a number? The arts indeed are seven, which are for this reason called « liberal », because free men spent their studies upon them; learning first what are the elements of speech, [1174D] and expressing these both by their characters and by their names; joining these to one another, fitting syllables together, and forming words out of syllables, and speeches out of words: its own labour entangles this art, stretching the mind out into the infinite, and, unless it be greeted first upon the threshold, racking it grievously with its torments. But let logic be left to its own sweats; the more it advances with itself, the more it turns away from God those who give their labour to it. But the adorned labour of orators serves falsehood as much as it serves those things which are true; they reckon that excellently said, whether it be true or false, which is uttered ornately and (to speak after their manner) aptly. In so far as this yields obedience to falsehood, it is far from him who is simple truth, offering the holy knowledge of the Gospel and without paint. Why should I enumerate the numbers [1175A] of arithmetic, the consonances or dissonances of music, the figures of geometry? in which « there is labour, and affliction of spirit [n: (Eccle. I, 17)]: » since Wisdom says, « God created man upright, and he himself hath mingled himself with questions beyond number [n: (Eccle. VII, 30)]. » Furthermore astronomy, dividing the heaven by colures and parallels, giving names to the planets and to the stars, and assigning the motions of each in the circles which are given them, noting now their stations, now their progressions, now their retrogradations, and at length measuring out by its own rules the equalizations of them all in one philosophical year, which contains not less than fifteen thousand years. This, observing the elevations, the depressions, [1175B] and the middle stations of the sun in one hundred and eighty-two circles, and of the other planets in the circles appointed to them, distinguishes floods, tides, plenty and want, and the middle seasons, and presumes in a manner to foretell the things which God before the ages disposed to come in a fixed order: this inebriates minds with much wine, and while it studiously considers the works of God, it wanders away from God, and either is utterly ignorant of his humility, or loves it less.
