## CHAPTER ONE. (cont.)

There are also the arts which are called « mechanical », which by the wise men of this world are indeed reckoned necessary for use, but not so far worthy; such as agriculture, architecture, the working of wool and weaving, the shoemaker's craft too, and the smith's and the founder's, and others beyond number, which necessity, or pleasantness, or at any rate pleasure has found out. This is the wine with which the world is given drink, or rather is made drunk; so that while it seeks what necessity, or what [1175C] pleasantness, or what pleasure, or what curiosity suggests, it passes over the things which are God's: and whether God was made flesh, whether he suffered, whether he rose again and ascended and promised eternal things to mortals, it is either utterly ignorant, or perhaps despises what it knows. But what does the Bridegroom say to the Bride concerning these things? « Thy breasts are better than wine; » declaring that it is far better to be nourished simply by the milk of the Church than to be proudly and uselessly made drunk with the things which have been named. Hence Isaias says, « Why do you spend your silver not upon bread, and your labour not unto fullness? » [n: (Isa. LV, 2.)] For to spend silver not upon bread is to consume eloquence and labour upon those things which do not give the knowledge and the vision of God: of which David says, « I shall be satisfied [1175D] when thy glory shall appear [n: (Psal. XVI, 15)]. » But why they ought to be reckoned better, he adds the cause, saying, Because they are « fragrant with the best ointments. » That ointments well compounded by the art of physicians should smell well is not to be doubted. Of these some are piercing, some soothing, some preserving. For some pierce, that they may heal; some soothe the things which are painful; some preserve, that they may remain untouched by rottenness. This is the effect of the spiritual virtues. For penitence grieves that one has committed that which it laments: after the wailing, and the confession, and the works of satisfaction, hope now soothes that which was painful: hence charity preserves unto eternal life the things which have been made solid. The « breasts » therefore of the Church « are better, » that is, those simple [1176A] teachings which are drawn out from the breasts of the Church are mightier and more effectual unto the soul's salvation than the « wine, » that is, than all the figments and inventions which secular wisdom has found out, that with them it may give drink to and inebriate those following it and giving their labour to it: and therefore « they are fragrant with the best ointments, » that is, fragrant in that manner in which those ointments are fragrant which are the best.

*Thy name is oil poured out.*

This is the Bride's friendly and honourable answer to the Bridegroom. The Bridegroom lately gratified her with his commendation, saying: « Thy breasts are better than wine; fragrant with the best ointments. » And she, as though giving him thanks, answered him with this commendation, saying, « Thy name is oil poured out, » as if she should say: if my breasts are to be praised, [1176B] this has come to them from thy proclaiming; for what is brought forth from my breasts is from thine, whose name is oil poured out. By which answer she doubtless hints that the faith which is in the name of Christ transcends every faith: and that, as oil, with whatever liquids it be mingled, without doubt transcends them all and floats above them, so the faith which is signed and founded in the name of Christ, and proclaimed by the teachings of the apostles, remains above and prevails over all the inventions [sic: *invensionibus*] against it which have already been named, or which perhaps may yet be found: whence it is also said, « Thy name is as oil, » that is, the faith which is in thy name is thus as oil. And that she may express this the more, there is added, « Poured out. » For this oil was not poured out during the whole time in which the Word was not [1176C] made flesh; in which, sin prevailing and mercy not obtaining, the whole race of men was made over to the darkness of hell and held under the dominion of the evil one: but after satisfaction had been made to wrath, and the time of fullness and of grace had come, that fruitful olive tree, namely God the Father, from whom is the origin of every good, sent forth the dove bearing as it were the branch of the olive, and appointed his Son to be made flesh through this branch; and the oil pressed out from this branch he poured forth as widely through the world as this incarnation was made known most widely to the whole world in many and various ways. This profited men beyond number by its anointing; « for virtue went out from him, and healed all [n: (Luc. VI, 19)]. » This, by enlightening them, turned men beyond number from the darkness of blindness to faith. [1176D] This restored sight to the blind, hearing to the deaf, speech to the dumb, put fever to flight, and restored the paralysed limbs, and raised the dead, restoring to them the breath of life. This made solid the waves of the sea beneath his steps. This multiplied twelve loaves to satisfy nine thousand men.

Why should I linger in the telling of these things? Let us hear John, saying, « There are also many other things which Jesus did; which if they were written every one, I think the world itself could not hold the books that should be written [n: (Joan. XXI, 25)], » that is, could not give credence to the multitude of the signs which were to be committed to books. For the pouring out of this oil enlightens the world. It gladdens the face of the Church, it fits and prepares her food; so that without the mingling of this, the spirit can scarcely prevail to be satisfied with its own food. [1177A] For as Paul says, « Without faith it is impossible to please God [n: (Heb. XI, 6)]. » For if without charity I should offer all the things which are of the world, and even myself, so that I burn, I am nothing [n: (I Cor. 13)]. For if, offering that without charity, I am nothing, then doing it without faith I am found to be utterly nothing. In Mark, the widow offering two mites is said to have offered more than the others who were offering great things [n: (Marc. XII, 44)]; here by the two mites understanding the love of God and of one's neighbour, in which the Church, when she offers, offers more than those who, giving their own without these, offer much and waste it. The faith of Christ, therefore, without which neither charity nor any good whatever can be had, is that which seasons, fits, and prepares the food of the soul, so that things offered in faith may please; and however small they be, they are nevertheless great, [1177B] and obtain from God a kingdom truly great, namely of the heavens. Rightly therefore has it been said that the « name » of Christ, that is, his faith, in which we must be saved, is « oil poured out, » that is, communicated to the world and approved by virtues; wherefore there follows:

*Therefore the maidens have loved thee.*

For hence was the love of the maidens stirred up, hence arose the beginning of their loving. For they saw the signs, they saw also the wonders which obeyed him at a nod; they saw a man set in weakness, holding all the things that are man's save sin and ignorance, and doing those things which are above man; and they were moved to believe him above man, [1177C] whom they saw sustaining fasts of forty days and nights, then changing water into wine, then transfiguring himself on the mountain, with his face shining in the manner of the Sun, showing also in his garments the whiteness of snow, holding converse with the dead, and the Father bearing witness out of the cloud, « This is my beloved Son, who is pleasing to me in all things; hear him [var: Vulg. *Hic est Filius meus electus, ipsum audite* (Luc. IX, 35); the wording *dilectus* and *qui mihi per omnia complacet* answers rather to Matt. XVII, 5, *in quo mihi bene complacui*] [n: (Luc. IX, 35)]; » and so at the last, having undergone a manifest death, triumphing in immortality over that death itself, and so returning to the heavens whence he had come; what could they do but believe him God, venerate and adore him, and love him above all things? Him therefore « the maidens have loved, » that is, the souls lately converted to the faith: and such a soul I judge Peter's to have been at the first, when he said, [1177D] « Depart from me, because I am a sinful man, O Lord [n: (Luc. V, 8)]. » Peter had fished the whole night, and had taken absolutely nothing; at his word he let down the net, and took a copious multitude. Peter had been a little youth, and gladly heard the Lord; but strengthened by this deed he grew up into a youth, afterwards into a most steadfast young man, and so came forth into a perfect man. And of Paul the persecutor what shall I relate? who at one moment a persecutor, at the next was made a little youth, the Lord enlightening him and saying, « Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me? I am Jesus of Nazareth, whom thou persecutest [n: (Act. IX, 45)]. » At this voice he was straightway truly converted, [1178A] saying, « Lord, what wilt thou have me to do? » [n: (ibid., 6)] If [cj: *Si*; read *Sic*, "So"] made a little youth, he heard from Ananias the words by which he grew up, by which he was made a great man, and the wall and defender and propagator of the whole Church; so an unnumbered multitude of believers, coming to him at the first and hearing from him the word of life, drawn at first by a little, then enticed more by the word, from which they understood the signs and wonders which they will be able neither to resist nor to gainsay, protested, by word and by work, that this name alone is to be adored, and to be loved before all things which are under heaven: whence there is added, « Thy name is oil poured out; therefore the maidens have loved thee. » There follows:
