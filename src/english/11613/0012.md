## CHAPTER ONE. (cont.)

But because, as Solomon says, « The just man is first the accuser of himself, taking the beginning from herself, she says, « I am black. » Which testimony indeed, despicable as concerning herself, is attended to more than what is most worthy of commendation. For what comeliness has the Bride, if she appear to the Bridegroom black, if dark, if horrible in the look? if she whom love flees should present herself to embraces; and she whom love follows, whom it joyfully receives, should plainly be found lacking these things. It must therefore be attended to, that this blackness [1184C] has in itself more of form, more of comeliness, more of honor and of seemliness, than all that which allures the sight, which snatches to itself and distracts the affections of mortals: for thence there is subjoined, « I am black, but beautiful. » This blackness spiritual affections work. For the Holy Spirit brings in this blackness: he pours the love of God into hearts, gives contempt of the world, gives to spurn the things that fall away, and to love the things which are eternal: to these things he rouses the affections, labors for the acquiring of them, prays in them, fasts in them, in them assiduously macerates the body, and prepares the world to be contemptible to itself, and itself to be contemptible to the world. Our Lord Jesus Christ declares that this blackness shall be present to him, saying, That « I must go to Jerusalem, and suffer many things from the elders, and [1184D] the Scribes, and the chief of the Priests, and be slain [n: (Matth. XVI, 21)]. » Which blackness of his the text of the Gospels announces, setting out the things which he sustained, which he willed to suffer for the redeeming of man; and which at the last, by wonderfully rising again, and by so ascending into the heavens, and by sending the Holy Spirit, he changed into a wondrous form of comeliness. This blackness laid hold of Peter also, his apostle, when Herod, delivering him over to four quaternions of soldiers, kept him taken, and, bound with two chains, held him fast in the prison; who, when in the morning he was to be brought forth to the sword, that very night being taught by an angel, passed through all the guards, and, the angel leading, escaping also the iron gate which was opened to him of its own accord, « Coming to himself, said: [1185A] Now I know truly that the Lord has sent forth his angel, and delivered me, etc. [n: (Act. XII, 11.)] » Paul also, asserting that he has part in these things, says: « In very many labors, in prisons more abundantly, in stripes above measure, in deaths often [n: (II Cor. XI, 23)]. » This blackness therefore we have assigned to Christ, who is the head of the whole Church, and to his chief members, Peter and Paul, for this reason, that from it it may be established that all who godly will to live in Christ must of necessity sustain bodily persecution, such as these, or such as all the martyrs far and wide through the world have unconquerably suffered for Christ; or at the least a spiritual one, such as the apostle bewails, saying, « Unhappy man that I am! Who shall deliver me from the body of this death? » [n: (Rom. VII, 24.)] For blackness [1185B] enfolds all men, whether such as is from the persecution of the body, or such as is from the assault of the mind that struggles against it.

This Bride therefore says to the daughters of Jerusalem, whom she has taken up to be instructed: ye daughters of Jerusalem, attend, because « I am black, » prompt indeed for every peril, for the taking up of every kind of death, prepared for the undergoing of every temptation; so that « neither death, nor life, nor Angels, nor Principalities, nor Powers [n: (Rom. VIII, 38, 39)] » [var: Vulg. *neque virtutes*, "nor virtues"], nor the rest which follow, which the apostle enumerates, « can separate her from the charity of God, which is in Christ Jesus. » Thou also, who desirest to be called a daughter of Jerusalem, by looking upon me, by setting me before thee as an example, stand fast, drawing near to the service of God, lest thou be [1185C] easily moved; because out of this blackness an unspeakable form of comeliness shall come to thee: for so it is written, « I am black, but beautiful. » Which form indeed is first from this, that man was created to the image and likeness of God; « to the image, » from reason; « to the likeness, » from the imitation of virtue. For if a man have reason, and cease to imitate virtue, he is indeed an image; but the likeness in which he might be able to please is wanting: of such men it is written, « But God, seeing that the malice of men was great upon the earth, and that all the thought of their heart was bent upon evil at all times, it repented him [n: (Gen. VI, 5, 6)], » and he blotted them out, not recognizing the image deformed, and the likeness not admitted into comeliness. [1185D] But in so great a multitude of men as was blotted out by the waters, how many faces there were which after the manner of man were called most fair, out of the fitting composition of God! how many lofty heads of hair! how deep the breasts! how open for work the shoulders! how many bodies framed for good uses, had they willed it! how many legs! how many hands! how many arms! in which, if nothing were sought save that which is outwardly fair, they would surely not have been enfolded in the waters, nor found in the lowest places. But all these, wrapped about with the blackness of sin, were plunged into a black house, namely, hell, allotted to the prince of blackness of this kind, therein experiencing what the difference is between those blackened by sin, and [1186A] those who for Christ are bound fast by sufferings, and by the things which accompany sufferings. These gnash in the darkness; the glory of the others is in the light: whence also it is here said, « I am black, but beautiful. » For the Church of God is black, whether by the suffering of the body, or by the maceration of the flesh, and the wearing down of the spirit, because it follows Christ, until it may reach through to him. There follows:

*As the tents of Cedar, as the skins of Solomon.*

Two things having been set before, two also are subjoined. For to that which was said, « I am black, » there is subjoined that which was said, « as the tents of Cedar: » but to that which was set down, « but beautiful, » there is placed beneath that which was said, « as the skins of Solomon. » [1186B] Which being so set down, let us so understand them. For Cedar is understood to have been the son of Ismael [n: (Gen. XXV, 13)], who, after he had grown up, disdaining to dwell among his brethren, chose for himself a solitude to be inhabited, raising for himself tents which should take upon them all the inclemency of the air, and should avoid no excess either of heat or of cold. In them he sustained the burning Crab, in them he did not fear the Goat stiff with cold, in them he sustained the assaults of whatsoever men came against him, and, breaking the arms of them that warred, he withstood them both by the strength of his body and by the solidity of his tents. The Church therefore, or any perfected soul, says that she is black as the tents of Cedar, because, as these could be beaten back by no onsets, so neither could these women be turned away from the love of God and from the worship of his justice [1186C] by any threats, by any terrors of adversaries, or by any scourges: according to that word of blessed Paul, « I know that no creature at all shall separate me from the charity of God, which is in Christ Jesus [n: (Rom. VIII, 39)]. » There follows that which has now been said, « as the skins of Solomon. » Which skin indeed is to be understood not of sheep, not of goats, not of any animals at all; but the very skin of Solomon; of which it is also said elsewhere, « Thou hast clothed me with skin and flesh, thou hast put me together with bones and sinews [n: (Job X, 11)]. »

This therefore is the skin which covers his face and his neck, fencing his throat, his breast and his arms, covering also the vital parts, and shutting in and closing within itself the rest of the body, so far as [1186D] it goes. But who does not know that Solomon was a most wealthy king, to whom the Lord gave a wise and understanding heart, so far that none before him was like to him, and who obtained all the kingdoms from the river of the land of the Philistines even to the border of Egypt, they offering gifts to him, and serving him all the days of his life. What could be lacking to him, which did not obey him in food and in the adornment of clothing? For the highest comeliness and the highest beauty was present to him; whom. whosoever is well in his wits understands to lack nothing whereby the life of men may be adorned. To this skin therefore holy Church, or any perfected soul, rightly compares herself, [1187A] saying, « I am beautiful as the skin of Solomon; » because as among those human things nothing is understood to be fairer than this skin, nothing more worthy; so among those things which are toward God nothing is found more adorned or more exact than holy Church, than the perfect soul. But if any man raise his contemplation higher, by this Solomon may be understood that great Solomon, our Lord Jesus Christ, who in his own blood made peace over all things, who made both one, confederating the heavenly things and the earthly, that in the heavens there may be glory to God, and on earth peace to men of good will. To this skin the Church conforms herself, and every faithful soul, hoping with a sure hope that she may be with him in blessed immortality, being about to be immortal, and to be endowed with eternal blessedness in incorruption.
