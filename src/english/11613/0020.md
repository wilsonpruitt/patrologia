## 10. (cont.)

Which is as much as if it were said: with the Lord Jesus suffering I have suffered together, and with him conquering I too gladly rejoice. For the world resounds far and wide with his victory, proclaims it, and by proclaiming it echoes it back, as appears in the vineyards of Engaddi. Engaddi is a plain beside Jericho, in which stand trees planted after the manner of a vineyard; from which, when they are wounded, there flows down balsam, useful indeed and precious: [1206C] which plain indeed signifies the breadth of the Church, which is known to spread itself now into the four parts of the world. This is named « Engaddi, » which is, « the fountain of the kid; » and as it signifies the Church, so the fountain which was in it signifies baptism, which is the fountain given to the Church, in which, while kids are cleansed, out of kids lambs are made. The power of Christ appears in these things, where from the wounded tree flows balsam, just as from the wounded Christ flowed the oil of blessing, when he said, « Father, pardon them, for they know not what they do [n: (Luc. XXIII, 34)]. » [var: Vulg. *dimitte illis: non enim sciunt*, "forgive them: for they know not"] Which balsam indeed is mingled with oil, that a most holy chrism may be made. With this chrism indeed the fountain of Engaddi is consecrated, which is called the fountain of baptism; with this the kids are anointed, thus passing over into the people of God; with this they are again confirmed, [1206D] lest Satan tempt them, and they be turned away from the truth; with this churches and altars are sanctified to God; with this the chalice is anointed, wherein God, delivering himself wholly to man, is made propitious; with this again kings are consecrated, bishops anointed, priests ordained, offering to Christ his own gifts on behalf of the people, and rendering to him what is his. This therefore is what the Bride says, « A cluster of Cyprus is my Beloved to me, » which appears in « the vineyards of Engaddi, » that is, in the churches spread abroad through the whole globe of lands, which all proclaim his glory, and announce his victory with great praise. There follows:

14. *Behold thou art beautiful, my friend, behold thou art beautiful. Thine eyes are those of doves.*

In which place this is to be observed, that the Bridegroom has already magnified his Bride with a threefold commendation. saying, [1207A] « I have likened thee to my company of horsemen: » and that other, « Thy cheeks as a turtledove's: » adding also a third, « Thy neck as jewels. » To whom the Bride also answers with a threefold praise, saying, « While the king was at his repose: » and that other, « A bundle of myrrh is my Beloved to me: » and that which follows, « A cluster of Cyprus is my Beloved to me, in the vineyards of Engaddi. » Whence, because there are now discourses of peace mingled between them, the Bridegroom, applauding the integrity of the Bride, whom he knows that he himself has loved, and now loves, and will in time to come always love, and knows that all this increases her beauty, commending her confidently, says, « Behold thou art beautiful, my friend; behold thou art beautiful. » But hearing this beauty commended, let us not attend to that beauty by which little women are wont to allure their [1207B] lovers, by which they are wont to lead those who have put their trust in them from the camp of chastity to the camp of uncleanness: for we have learned that God detests this, through the prophet saying, « The day of the Lord of hosts upon everyone that is proud and lofty, and upon everything that is beautiful to the sight [n: (Isa. II, 12, 16)] » : and pursuing it further, he says, « The Lord will take away in that day the ornament of shoes, the little moons, and the chains, and the necklaces, and the bracelets, and the bonnets [n: (Cap. III, 18, 19)], » and the rest that follow; all which without doubt are things that minister to this corruptible beauty, and to that which leads to corruption, and that turn the mind away from what is honourable and pleasing to God. But that beauty is to be attended to which true humility makes in the mind, the fear of God, holy [1207C] penance, humble confession, satisfaction for sins, peace toward all, and true concord, temperate chastity, and that which is above all, holy charity toward God. Of this the prophet makes mention, saying, « All the glory of the King's daughter is from within [n: (Psal. XLIV, 14)], » that is, from the beauty which is within is all the glory of the faithful soul, which is the daughter of God; whose glory is in « golden borders, » that is, in the consummation of the virtues. For the border is the finishing of the garment, which is well recorded as « clothed round about with variety, » that is, adorned with the varied diversity of all the virtues.

This indeed is to be attended to, in this is God well pleased, of which Scripture makes mention, saying, « I will recount [1207D] to thee all my years in the bitterness of my soul [n: (Isa. XXXVIII, 15)]. » For this omits no time, but that it calls to mind both the time itself and the sins committed in it: this bewails what was committed in infancy, what in boyhood, what in adolescence: this most bitterly accuses the excesses of youth; this powerfully convicts the enervating of manly strength, and grievously blushes that it has failed in something in the sluggishness of old age. Which blushing indeed confounds the demons, puts hell to flight, opens heaven, wins over the angels, renders God propitious; and when, falling down, it says, « Father, I am not worthy to be called thy son: make me as one of thy hired servants [n: (Luc. XV, 21)], » it earns the first robe, and the ring, and it glories that the shoes [1208A] of glory are set upon its feet. So, so does he name her beautiful, saying, « Behold thou art beautiful, » and he repeats that she is beautiful, adding, « Behold thou art beautiful. » Which repetition without doubt denotes that there is to be another for the soul itself or for the Bride, which he thus makes plain to the understanding. For the soul or the Bride, however perfect, here indeed rejoicing in beauty, feels a threefold misfortune; one from the flesh, which the Apostle laments, saying, « Unhappy man that I am, who shall deliver me from the body of this death? » [n: (Rom. VII, 24.)] another from a neighbour; a third from any unclean spirit. This is made manifest in Job, whom the flesh solicits, whom his wife counsels not well, whom his friends, at the prompting of the malignant spirit, compel to go astray. But the prophet counsels him, saying, « His truth shall compass [1208B] thee with a shield: thou shalt not be afraid of the terror of the night, of the arrow that flieth in the day, of the business that walketh about in the dark, of invasion, and of the noonday devil [n: (Psal. XC, 5, 6)]. » Where indeed some temptation is shown to be manifest, some hidden, some grievous, some light. For « the arrow » « that flieth in the day, » is manifest and light; « the noonday devil, » manifest and grievous; « the business that walketh about in the dark, » hidden and grievous; but « invasion » is understood to be hidden and light. But all these temptations will not be present in that life which is to come. For the glorified flesh will not be able to be tempted, nor will there be in it anything that resists the spirit. No spirit therein shall then be hurt by a neighbour; because then charity will be complete, so that each may love his neighbour as himself. But from [1208C] the malignant spirit what vexation shall there be able to be? who will neither be present, nor will there be able to remain to him any license at all of tempting the good, even for their testing. From then on his dwelling will be in hell, where he will be tormented by that very fire with which burn the wretched whom God has cast away, who by consenting to temptations have been sold under sin. There follows:

*Thine eyes are those of doves.*

The beauty of the Bride having been shown, he now subjoins what the cause of this beauty is: for this is its cause, that « her eyes are those of doves, » that is, that her whole regard is dove-like. For the dove, walking in her pasture, gathers only clean grains, feeds on these, nourishes herself with them: so also [1208D] this soul or Bride of God, while, disdaining no one for her own feeding, but hearing many, she considers each single thing, and what is said purely, holily, and truly, these things she keeps in mind, the rest she spurns and shudders at. The dove too is without gall, she neither hurts another bird with her beak, nor tears it with her claw, denoting the simplicity of this Bride, who is not angered unreasonably, nor anyone by word, nor by deed, but however greatly offended, keeps herself within the camp of her own innocence. She has groaning too instead of song, because, desiring to be present at the presence of her Lord, while this is denied her, bewailing that all things are wanting to her, she groans withal, and having this groaning instead of song, in groaning she says, « My soul [sic: *animi*] [1209A] hath thirsted for God the living fountain; when shall I come and appear before the face of God [n: (Psal. XLI, 3)]. » [var: Vulg. *ad Deum fortem vivum*, "unto the strong living God"] She flies in flocks, because in all that she flies, in that she desires to be aloft, she chooses by no means to be separated from the Lord's flock, but to be taken up with them, to be glorified and made blessed with them. This dove-like eye would indeed have been necessary to Eve, when « she saw the tree that it was good to eat of, and beautiful to the eyes, and delightful to behold, and she took of the fruit thereof and did eat, and gave to her husband, and the eyes of them both were opened [n: (Gen. III, 6, 7)]. » Then they knew themselves stripped of obedience, ensnared in disobedience, and girt about with all manner of evils and liable to them. From her there proceeded in Cain an eye by no means dove-like, and thence it wandered wickedly through the world, and was opened to the concupiscence [1209B] of women. So that now there are few who say, « I was an eye to the blind, and a foot to the lame. I was the father of the poor: and the cause which I knew not, I searched out most diligently [n: (Job XXIX, 15, 16)]. » There follows:
