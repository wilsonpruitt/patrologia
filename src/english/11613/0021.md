## 10. (cont.)

Thus the lover answers the lover with an equal commendation: being called beautiful, she declares that he too is beautiful, saying, « Behold thou art beautiful, my beloved. » Hearing which, let us not attend to the beauty which is in creatures corporeal or incorporeal. For a certain beauty is present in these corporeal things, arising from the fitting disposition of their parts, when part is fitly joined to part, and so one comely form is produced from their fitting conjunction. [1209C] The greatest indeed among creeping things is the dragon, among swimming things the whale, among flying things the griffin, the elephant among four-footed beasts, the giant among men: in all of which, if the disposition of the parts itself be attended to, and the fitting conjunction of part to part, a form beyond doubt most beautiful, and the highest wisdom of the Creator, is found in them all. So there are some middling, and some are understood to be least; which, unless they have contracted sin, or the divine power has reserved them to itself to be healed, are known to display in themselves a wondrous beauty. If therefore thou attend to that great Adam, from whom the whole race of men is begotten, if thou attend to him, who and of what sort he was created, who he was before sin, who he was when the Lord [1209D] brought to him all the living things of the earth, and taught him what he should call them; it will be clear that he shone with a certain wondrous beauty: and Abraham, knowing that Adam would afterward obtain mercy through the incarnation of Christ and his death, passed through all places, and chose for his own burial that place alone in which Adam was buried, that double cave which he bought from Ephron the Hethite [n: (Gen. XXIII, 19)]; where with their wives rest Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and the great Adam. But is the beloved of this one called beautiful with this beauty? Far from it; for there follows, « And comely. » Whence it is given to be understood that he is beautiful with that beauty to which nothing is fitting, nothing is joined, or can be joined, save what is [1210A] comely. This beauty was not Adam's, in whom, if for a time sin was not present, yet it could be present, whence also he sinned.

But if thou attend to the heavenly bodies, the sun, the moon, and the stars, these indeed are beautiful, which with their own light illumine the darkness of the world, so that the things which lack light of their own, being illumined by these, may shine again: but of them it is written, « They shall perish, but thou remainest: and these shall grow old as a garment [n: (Psal. CI, 27)]. » [var: Vulg. *omnes sicut vestimentum*, "all of them as a garment"] But looking upon the angels, and the orders even up to the Cherubim and Seraphim, and stretching thy gaze above them, the holy Trinity alone will meet thee, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, whose beauty is that which neither eye has seen [n: (I Cor. II, 9)], nor ear heard, nor has it ascended into the heart of man, which God will make manifest [1210B] to those who love him. [var: Vulg. *quae praeparavit Deus*, "which God hath prepared"] Of which it was said to Moses, « Man shall not see me and live [n: (Exod. XXXIII, 20)]. » For here it is not granted to the living to see what is promised to be seen by the man already glorified and taken up into the true life, the Lord saying, « This is life eternal, that they may know thee the true God, and Jesus Christ whom thou hast sent [n: (Joan. XVII, 3)]. » [var: Vulg. *solum Deum verum*, "the only true God"] This is the form, this the fairness, this the beauty, into which the angels also desire to look [n: (I Pet. I, 12)]. This is that beauty of which David says, « I shall be satisfied when thy glory shall appear [n: (Psal. XVI, 15)]: » which appearing to us and showing itself visibly, there is nothing that we should seek further, there is nothing wherewith we should desire to be further refreshed; with this beauty therefore it was said, « Behold thou art beautiful, my beloved: » [1210C] to this beauty also, because there is nothing that cleaves to it, nothing that can be mingled with it, save what is honourable, and what is believed worthy of all glory, there is rightly joined, « And comely; » so that he who seeks to stand by this beauty may always hold himself upon the summit of virtue and in comeliness. There follows:

*Our little bed is flowery.*

Behold, she who praises comeliness in the Bridegroom seems, pierced through by love of him, to call him to the little bed, that she may fully enjoy his presence, that she may be gladdened by delight in him. To which she seems to entice him both by the pleasantness of the bed and by the delightfulness of the place, saying, « Our little bed is flowery. The beams of our houses are of cedar, our panelled ceilings of cypress. » For it is pleasant [1210D] to rest on a flowery bed, and safe indeed to lie down beneath beams of cedar and panelled ceilings of cypress: and these things indeed are true, but let us attend to them more deeply, if we take care to understand them spiritually. For this little bed is not indeed a place of softness, not of obscene buffoonery, but the little bed to be attended to is that of delightful rest in the Lord, and of perfect contemplation in him. For contemplation is when a man first considers himself, who he is, and whence he has come, and to what he has come, and searches out, and is not ignorant, that God created for him a rational soul, and sent this into his body, and that he, being so born, received the grace of baptism, and so grew up in faith; and being taught by faith, he does not doubt, the truth of faith teaching it, that the Son of God came into flesh, underwent death [1211A] in it, and rose on the third day for the redeeming of man, and thus, that man might ascend to life, ascended into heaven, and thus drew all things to himself. Attending to these things, he knows not whither to turn himself; he despises all that he has done hitherto; he bewails with tears the sins which infancy, or youth, or another age committed; he casts himself down; he purposes to transform himself into some other man; and thus he himself makes ready a bed for God with the hand of devout and diligent contemplation. Which « little bed » nevertheless is still understood to be « flowery. » For these are the beginnings of contemplation, which give hope of good advancement and of ripe fruit, which will then be present, when the world shall now be forsaken and wholly trodden under the foot of the mind, when now all things [1211B] that pass away shall be despised, and those alone loved which always abide and stand unchanged. Knowing therefore, this soul or Bride, that this flower of good imitation has come forth by the gift of God, and that he himself has sent these things before as the first beginnings of his coming, she calls him to the flowery little bed, that he may rest in it, and bring this flower through to the ripeness of holy perfection. She asserts also that this little bed is common to the Bridegroom and the Bride, while she herself receives this contemplation within her, and knows that it comes forth from the gift of God and from his presence. There follows:

16. *The beams of our houses are of cedar, our panelled ceilings of cypress.*

This is a persuasion drawn from safety; for as it is pleasant to rest on a flowery bed, so also it is safe and secure to rest beneath a roof [1211C] that is covered with beams of cedar. For the cedar and the cypress are of an incorruptible nature, they know not how to be corrupted, they preserve alike their greenness and their scent: by their scent too they put serpents to flight and kill them, and thus provide safety for those resting beneath them. Thus therefore is this house roofed, into which the Bridegroom is invited by the Bride. No rain comes down here, from which the overshadowings of cedar and cypress should protect it. For these cedars are indeed the angels, to whom the keeping of this house is committed: for of them it was said, « Scandalize not these little ones: for I say to you, that their angels always see the face of my Father, who is in heaven » [n: (Matth. XVIII, 10)]. [var: Vulg. *Videte ne contemnatis unum ex his pusillis*, "See that you despise not one of these little ones"] But « the panelled ceilings » [1211D] which furnish the beams with defence and strength, which preserve their own greenness, and preserve a most excellent scent, by which serpents are killed, these are the holy archangels, furnishing strength to the angels, ever green with that greenness indeed in which they were created, and with that very greenness with which they were endowed when they withstood him who said, « I will set my seat toward the north, and I will be like the Most High [n: (Isa. XIV, 14)]. » And the scent of their virtues kills the malignant spirits, lest they disturb the hearts of the good, lest they rise from good beginnings to greater things. But it must be observed that when the Bride says, « The beams of our houses are of cedar, the panelled ceilings of cypress, » she uses that manner of speaking by which the whole is understood through a part: for in « the bed, » to which the better part is ascribed, the foundation of the house itself and the walls are understood: but in « the beams » [1212A] and « the panelled ceilings, » which are the chief part of the roof, all that belongs to the roof is ascribed.
