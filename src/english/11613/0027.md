## CHAPTER II. (cont.)

Since therefore it is certain that the Lord made his leaps into Peter, the first of the apostles, in these ways and in very many others, it remains that in those same ways we understand it of all the rest, who, being sent into the world, both preached him and converted the nations to him, as is most well known by the wondrous showing forth of their works. For into the last of the apostles, namely Paul, he gave a leap, when, a light being sent forth from heaven, he thus called him, cast down upon the earth and seeing nothing, « Saul, Saul, [1227C] why persecutest thou me? it is hard for thee to kick against the goad [n: (Act. IX, 4-15)]: » who, being sent to Ananias, received his sight, and was baptized, and, the Lord leaping into him, openly confessed before all that he was his disciple. How many leaps God made into him, how wondrously he raised him up, how choice he made him by a wondrous doctrine and conspicuous to all, his epistles teach; and those which are called « The Acts of the Apostles » plainly declare it. And so the Bride says of him, « Behold, this one cometh, leaping upon the mountains: » she joins also what follows, « Skipping over the hills. Why this is so, let us see. For what we have said of the mountains, the same must be said of the hills. For some hills are good; whence it is said, « Bless the Lord, ye mountains and hills [1227D] [n: (Dan. III, 75)]; » and that of Isaias, « The Lord shall be exalted above the hills [n: (Isa. II, 2)]. » [var: Vulg. *et elevabitur super colles*, of the mountain, not of the Lord] But some are evil; whence in this place it is said that the Lord leaps upon the mountains, but skips over the hills: for where he leaps, there he is; but with those over whom he skips he makes no stay, but forsakes and abandons them. Who then are these? they who make themselves great by inventing heresies; in these they exalt themselves, they desire to be made equal to the height of the mountains, they turn peoples after them and draw them; they make themselves great when they are not, boasting that they have God, when they are far from him. Among these is Arius, among these Eutyches, among these all the heresiarchs whom the holy Church marks out, shuns, and condemns: over these indeed the Lord skips. [1228A] He skips over the hypocrites also, who in words confess that they have known the Lord, but in deeds deny him. So indeed he skips over all whom neither faith fortifies, nor the holiness of life makes honourable. The cause of this thing is this; because,

9. *The beloved is like to a roe, and to a young hart.* [var: Vulg. *dilectus meus*, "my beloved"]

To these animals the coming Bridegroom is rightly likened, both because God beholds all things clearly, and because he tarries on the lofty height of the virtues, is fed among them, measures out his leaps among them, and discerns also in whom the ancient serpent lies coiled at rest, or uncoiled draws out his folds; and these indeed he turns aside from, even as there is no fellowship of Christ with Belial. There follows:

[1228B] *Behold he standeth behind our wall.*

This indeed is promised of the beloved, because he is like to a roe. For he is keen of sight, as one who sees the Father, and, living the Father, beholds all things in the Father; not only the acts of works, but also the affections of thoughts. Whence he now admonishes all, that we all fortify ourselves for his gaze, by thinking what things are good, what are useful, what may seem worthy of the sight of God; and that we fulfil these same things by a consequent act. For he says, « Behold he standeth behind our wall. » Him also she said to be like to a young hart, that is, a son of the ancient fathers, having from them a body taken from a virgin, yet liable to no sin nor weighed down by the mass of sin; inasmuch as [1228C] the force of his Father's nature has grown up in him, so that hidden things are not concealed from him, that serpents flee him, that poisons do not harm him. To him therefore, to whom our hidden things must be laid open, to whom the serpent is wholly displeasing, who spurns everything that is infected with his poison, let us fit ourselves, that he may see what things are worthy of God, and may behold nothing in us infected with the serpent's poison, whereby it might please him to turn aside from us: « For behold he standeth behind our wall, looking down through the windows. » [var: Vulg. *respiciens*, "looking in"] Who is said indeed to stand, that is, by the most excellent disposition of his state to hold himself unmovably for the beholding of all things which he looks upon; to whom neither do the good things which we do add anything, nor do the evils perpetrated by us diminish anything of his glory; but all things subject themselves to the eternal retribution which they have deserved. Who indeed [1228D] « standeth behind the wall; » for the wall is that outward part of our condition (wherewith we are clothed, wherewith we are covered, whereby we are subjected to numberless temptation), namely our body, which, as a wall is built of stones and mortar, so is fitted to us by a fit disposition of its parts; which assuredly, because she sees that her beloved is about to take it up, or perhaps has already taken it up, she names indeed « our, » that she may show him a partaker of our condition. But here the Beloved « standeth behind the wall, » that he may freely behold it, as though set before him, and freely mark through the windows and the lattices what is done within. For the windows of our body are the five bodily senses [1229A], namely sight, hearing, taste, smell and touch. Some indeed use these unto good things, and make themselves good; others, drawing them by most abandoned habits toward evil things, consequently make themselves most wicked: of whom Jeremias says, « Death is come up through our windows [n: (Jer. IX, 21)]: » which indeed is then, when the eyes of the sinner see a woman to lust after her; for he has committed adultery with her in his heart: and thus death has entered in to the soul through the windows of the eyes. But also when a man receives vain hearing, and especially the false sciences of perverse teachings, then death enters in to the soul through the windows of the ears. And so of the rest, of smell, of taste, of speech and of touch: when any soul abuses these, and applies itself to evil, death now enters through these windows [1229B], and gains the primacy. But if the soul, beholding the adornment of the world, and from the beauty of creatures understanding God the founder of all, wonders at his works, and praises the creator of the works, unto this soul life enters through the windows of the eyes. But also when it inclines its hearing to the word of God, and is delighted with the reasonings of his knowledge and of his wisdom, to this soul the light of wisdom enters through the windows of the ears; of whom Isaias says, « Who are these that fly as clouds, and as doves to their windows? » [n: (Isai. LX 8.)] For seeing in spirit the loftiness of the apostles, and wondering at their swift course through the world, he says, « Who are those, » etc.: but wondering at their simplicity and innocence, he subjoins, saying, « And as [1229C] doves to their windows, » that is, simple and innocent, and in all their sight, and hearing, taste, smell and touch imitating that dove which appeared above the Lord on the day whereon he was baptized

So then, standing behind the wall, this beloved contemplates through the windows by which gate each man seeks to enter, as the Lord says: « For wide is the gate, and broad the way that leadeth to death, and many there are who go in thereat: but narrow is the gate, and strait the way that leadeth to life, and few there are that find it [n: (Matth. VII, 13, 14)]. » [var: Vulg. *ad perditionem*, "to destruction"] These therefore he looks down upon, as labouring below; he looks in upon them, that he may help them in their beginnings, and that those labouring in their own strait, [1229D] grace being now freely given, he may lead further on, and, beginning the narrow way, may bring them, set at length in the breadth of charity, to blessed glory. But those entering the wide gate, and holding the broad way which leadeth to destruction, he exhorts, admonishes, and calls back, first by the natural law, then by Scripture, then by the patriarchs, by the prophets, hence by himself, then by the apostles, and by all the doctors, that they may return, that they may forsake the way begun; lest, glorying in the breadth of the way, and at length entering through the broad gate, they enter into darkness, and the dreadful places of perdition, which are even now truly destined for those running by this way. These things the beloved contemplates through the windows, contemplating [1230A] namely the manifestly good and the manifestly evil; and not content with these alone, he looks forth through the lattices; for he says: « Looking down through the windows, looking forth through the lattices [n: (I Tim. I, 15)]. » For lattices are narrow openings, not lying open after the manner of a window, but drawn out narrowly lengthwise by the masons under the vault; through which the beholder sees whom he wills, nor yet can he be seen by them, because neither does he will it.

Who then are they whom he thus beholds? they who either do evil things not evilly, or perform good things not well. They do evil things not evilly, as Saul, and they that are like him. For these dragged the men of this sect bound to the synagogues and to the tribunals: which indeed was evil, but was not done evilly. For in this, deeming that they were rendering [1230B] obedience to the creator, serving the law, and repressing transgression, they burned with a zeal indeed good, yet not according to knowledge, and believed that they were worthily avenging an injury brought upon the supreme Godhead. Whence Paul himself says: « I persecuted the Church of God: but yet I obtained mercy, because I did it ignorantly in unbelief [n: (I Tim. I, 13)]. » For he was ignorant that God the Father had a Son, and that he had sent him into the world for a remedy: he did not know that this was Christ Jesus. He knew that the circumcision of Abraham and the law of Moses had been given by the Lord, but he did not know that this had lasted in its integrity until the time of John the Baptist, and that then the things promised in it had expired: and therefore these things were revealed to him, and the things done [1230C] in ignorance were forgiven. Him therefore the Lord looked upon through the lattices, seeing him and pitying him, when indeed he hoped nothing of mercy, but breathed wholly « threatenings and slaughter » unto evil. There are also those who perform good things not well, who assuredly, having faith and exercising the works of faith, pray much in the streets, distribute their goods to the poor and the needy; but while they do these things, not having the charity which is the virtue of works, and not seeking to attain God by these works, but, because the whole world is subject to God, desiring to be honoured and to be set over honours, they lose all that they do, and that which has been distributed without charity, that they forfeit. For Paul says, « If I shall distribute all my goods to the poor, and myself also [1230D] so that I burn, but have not charity, I am nothing [n: (I Cor. XIII, 3)]. » [var: Vulg. *nihil mihi prodest*, "it profiteth me nothing"] The same also says, « The word of God is living, and effectual, and more piercing than any two-edged sword: and reaching unto the division of the soul and the spirit, of the joints also and the marrow, and a discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart; nor is there any creature invisible in his sight *(Hebr IV, 12)*. » Whence whatsoever they do, they are before God, they do not escape his eyes; nor is he slow to conceive in mind not only what they do, but what they intend thereby: seeing the manifestly good and evil through the window; but those who hide themselves, beholding through the window; [d: but those who hide themselves, beholding] through the lattices.
