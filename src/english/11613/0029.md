## 10. (cont.)

That is, The voice of the preacher has been heard on every side throughout the world in the land now ours. For anyone is preached [cj: *Praedicatur*; read *Praedicator*, "a preacher"], not seeking out the prudence which is of the earth, nor the little fables which are utterly empty, announcing by his song the departure of winter and the coming of spring; he moans here, and, loving chastity, he dwells on high, [1234D] he flees the converse of men, remaining in body in the world, in mind in heaven: whence the Baptist says, « Offspring of vipers, who hath shewed you to flee from the wrath to come? Bring forth therefore fruits worthy of penance: and begin not to say, We have Abraham for our father [n: (Luc. III, 7, 8)]. » But Peter says: « Do penance, and be every one of you baptized in the name of Jesus Christ unto the remission of sins; and you shall receive the gift of the Holy Ghost [n: (Act. II, 38)]. » Paul also says: « Not in rioting and drunkenness, not in chambering and impurities, not in contention and envy. But put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ [n: (Rom. XIII, 13, 14)]. » But these [1235A] voices of the turtle, who could number them? He alone knows them, God, who forms the voice and the hearts. There follows:

13. *The fig tree hath put forth her green figs.*

The fig is a most well-known tree, which abounds in leaves, and fills with fruit him who tends and possesses it: it produces its fruits in divers ways. For when spring is begun, and now a little advanced, it produces its first fruits in small measure, which are called green figs, and are bitter, and by reason of their bitterness are spat out by the taste, and are cast aside among bitter and worthless things: but the same tree, with some little advance of time, bears fruit, giving a most delightful fruit, and most pleasant to the taste by its inborn sweetness. This is the fruit for the sake of which it is tended, for the sake of which it is dug about, and for the cultivating of which labour is manifoldly bestowed. But when its first fruit is born abundantly, [1235B] because then almost all the bitterness that is in the fig is sent forth, there is good hope of that which follows, that the more bitter this one is, the sweeter that which follows may be sent forth. Of this fig therefore it is said, « The fig tree hath put forth her green figs. » For this is the fig tree of which it is said in the Gospel: A certain man had a fig tree planted in his vineyard, to which the lord coming for three years, and finding no fruit on it, said to the dresser: « Cut it down: » of which the dresser said: « Lord, let it alone this year, until I dig about it, and dung it [n: (Luc. XIII, 7, 8)]. » This indeed is said to be the Church, to which the Lord coming for three years (in one year, namely, that time which was before the law; in the second, namely, that which was under the law; in the third [1235C] indeed, that which began under grace) found no fruit; whence, being angered, he uttered these words, « Cut it down. » For although from the very beginning of creation the Church has indeed always existed, yet through all this time it existed in so few that its fewness, compared with the copious multitude, would be held as nothing, and reckoned for nothing. For in all this time, if thou find any just men, and compare them with the rest of the multitude, the comparison will be as if thou shouldst compare some small thing with an infinite multitude; whence our Lord and Saviour says: « The foxes have holes, and the birds of the air nests: but the Son of man hath not where to lay his head [n: (Matth. VIII, 20)]. » But this tree, now dug about, fortified by the admonitions of the holy apostles, taught by the holy examples of the martyrs, [1235D] the bitterness of martyrdom being now despised, now brings forth sweet fruits throughout the world: now it fills with the sweetness of its figs those whom it bewailed in the bitterness of its passion. Whence it is subjoined

*The vines in flower have yielded their sweet smell.*

But of the vineyards of the Lord there is one, and there are many: for the Church is one, and many. One indeed is the congregation of all the faithful who from the beginning loved God, and who love him still, and who shall love him: this indeed is one, abiding in the unity of faith, and of hope, and of charity: which indeed shall then appear, when the Lord shall judge the world, they who shall receive the kingdom from the Lord, the Lord saying: « Come, [1236A] ye blessed of my Father, possess the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world [n: (Matth. XXV, 34)], » who then shall already be free from the « snare of the hunters, and from the sharp word [n: (Psal. XC, 3)]. » For the snare of the hunters is the temptation of malignant spirits, which they stretch out for wretched mortals, that they may ensnare them in sins, and lead the ensnared to death: but the sharp word (than which nothing in the world can be sharper) is that which they shall hear, « Depart from me, ye cursed, into everlasting fire, which was prepared for the devil and his angels [n: (Matth. XXV, 41)]. » Which indeed the good also shall hear, but, with the hand of the Lord protecting them, they shall not fear, because he who freed them from the snare of the hunters, he also freed them from the sharp word, so that they may not dread it either, [1236B] but may rest secure in the glory already received. This indeed is what shall be there hereafter, which here is divided into many parts: for here there is a certain Church of Jerusalem, a certain Church of Antioch, a certain Church of Rome, and others innumerable throughout the world. For every congregation of faithful men, in the places which the Lord has chosen for himself, has there established a solemn house of prayer, in which they may pray, in which they may lament, in which they may render thanks for benefit: and these indeed are the vines which in flower have yielded their sweet smell. For the good that is done here is wholly in flower, sending forth from itself a good report; but it shall bring forth fruit when for the good rendered in time it shall receive an everlasting reward. [1236C] There follows:

*Arise, my friend, my comely one, and come, 14. my dove in the clefts of the rock, in the hollows of the wall.* [var: Vulg. *in caverna maceriae*, "in the hollow of the wall," singular]

The Beloved has now called forth the Bride, that she might come to him: he has invited her also by these seven things set forth, saying, « The winter is past; gone is the rain, and departed; the flowers have appeared in our land; the time of pruning is come; the voice of the turtle has been heard in our land; the fig tree hath put forth her green figs; the vines in flower have yielded their sweet smell. » By all which the opportuneness of the time was shown, that she should come. And still persevering in the same calling of her to himself, he repeats, saying, « Arise, my friend, my bride, and come, my dove. » He adds also, « In the clefts [1236D] of the rock, in the hollows of the wall. » Which words indeed add the memory of the Lord's passion. For who is ignorant that the apostle said of Christ, « And the rock was Christ [n: (I Cor. X, 4)]. » But that there are clefts in this rock, who would not know, who doubts not that Christ was fastened to the cross with nails? That there is also a hollow in this wall, who would not know, who doubts not that what he reads is true? For it is written, « One of the soldiers with a spear opened his side, and immediately there came out blood and water [n: (Joan. XIX, 34)]. » Which side assuredly is rightly named a wall: for a wall is a joining of stones without mortar; and this side indeed was joined without the mortar of human seed, [1237A] and was composed and formed by the hand of the Holy Spirit. Let the Beloved therefore come to her bridegroom who calls her forth, safe in the protection of the passion, fortified with the fortification of faith; let her come out of the world to him who overcame the world; let her come from the place of grief and of suffering to the place where there is no grief or suffering, but perennial peace, and continual exultation. There follows:

*Shew me thy face.*

The Bride obeys her Beloved; she knows no delay when she is called. For thus it was said to her, « Arise, make haste, come to me; » and she, being thus called, arises straightway, comes hastening, and stands by with no delay intervening. But she comes with her head covered, lest the sun burn her face, lest her whiteness be made base by the whiteness of the sun. But to the Beloved standing by and covering herself, the beloved says, « Shew [1237B] me thy face. » For it is just that the Bride be presented to her Beloved with unveiled face. For if Rebecca, at the well whose name is « of the living » and « of the seeing [n: (Gen. XXIV, 65)], » on seeing Isaac came down from the camel and covered herself with a cloak (which indeed was done thus because she was not yet a bride, and he had not yet received her, but brought her, destined for himself, into his mother's house, and took her to wife, and loved her so greatly that he tempered the grief which had befallen him from his mother's death): for this one, long since beloved of her Beloved, long since betrothed to the Bridegroom, and now known by spiritual love and by divine soliloquies, there is no need that she should appear veiled before the Bridegroom, but with unveiled face let her contemplate and love him. « For the word of God is living and effectual, and more piercing than any two-edged [1237C] sword, reaching unto the division of the soul and the spirit, of the joints also and the marrow, and a discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart; and there is no creature invisible in his sight, but all things are naked and open to his eyes [n: (Hebr. IV, 12, 13)]. » What need therefore has he of a covering, to whom neither flowers, nor leaves, nor trees, nor garment, nor any covering are a hindrance, but that he may look upon and consider a thing from the end of its beginning even to the end of its issue; and rightly declare to himself whatever belongs to thought, and to speech, and to ancient habit and to work? Whence in the psalm David himself says three times: « O God, convert us [n: (Psal. LXXIX, 4)]: » and again: « O God of hosts, convert [1237D] us [n: (ibid., 15)]: » and a third time he subjoins: « O Lord God of hosts, convert us [n: (ibid., 20)]. » For it is needful for a man that he be converted from those things wherein he has offended in thought; that he be converted from those wherein he has offended in word; and from those wherein he has offended in deed and in habit; whence after this threefold invocation he subjoins, « And shew thy face, and we shall be saved [n: (ibid.)]; » for we shall be able to see the face of God securely, when God shall have shaken these offences from us, and he himself, being worthily invoked, shall have as it were let them go from his own memory, mercy bringing this to pass. So also God desires to see us with unveiled face, that nothing in us may appear in thought unworthy of him, nothing at all in word that may offend, nothing in act and deed that may displease. Whence he says, « Shew me thy face, » which thou hast made [1238A] worthy to appear to me, now after long pursuits of the virtues, after long desires of my kiss, that I may see whether thou hast done anything that is worthy of my kiss, that is worthy to be rewarded with the prize of my kiss. There follows:

*Let thy voice sound in mine ears.*
