## 10. (cont.)

Where the face is now pleasing, where nothing intervenes that might be reprehended, licence is given of advancing further; that as the sight is delighted by the face of her Beloved, so the hearing may be delighted by the sound of the voice of the same: whence it is said, « Let thy voice sound in mine ears. » Ask what thou wilt; for thou wilt ask nothing, except what is fair and just, and what is worthy to be heard. Wherein it is to be noted that our voices sound in the ear of the Lord in divers ways. For sometimes we sound with a sound audible to God [1238B] alone, when we speak to him with the devotion of the heart only; as it is written of Anna, that « she spoke in her heart only, and her lips only were moved, and her voice was not heard at all [n: (I Reg. I, 13)]: » for her whole devotion was toward God; whence she deserved to be heard and to give thanks, and stood forth worthy to bring forth to the world the great prophet Samuel. God gives heed also to voices uttered with audible sound, if yet the intention of the heart be present, and the devotion which renders these audible; that the hearing may not be soothed only after the manner of birds, but rather after the manner of men, that the groanings of the inner heart may be heard. Hence in the Church the psalms resound, hence the hymns, hence the Alleluia resounds its sweet melody, hence other voices divers and innumerable, which human [1238C] invention puts forth unto the praise of God; which, assuredly, if due devotion accompany them, there is no doubt that they sound in the ears of God and obtain what is asked. Hence also the prophet says: « Sing joyfully to God, all the earth, sing ye a psalm to his name, give glory to his praise [n: (Psal. LXV, 1)]. » And at the end of the work, he concludes with the last psalm: « Praise ye the Lord in his holy places: praise ye him in the firmament of his power: praise ye him in his mighty acts: praise ye him according to the multitude of his greatness: praise ye him with the sound of trumpet: praise ye him with psaltery and harp: praise ye him with timbrel and choir: praise ye him with strings and organs: praise ye him on well-sounding cymbals: praise ye him on cymbals of joy [n: (Psal. 150)]. » Where, admonishing unto praise ten times over, he invites [1238D] to praise the nine orders of the heavenly ones, and man joined to them and made equal to them through grace, saying, « Let every spirit praise the Lord. » There follows:

*For thy voice is sweet, and thy face comely.*

« Thy voice is sweet, » which confesses and prays to me alone, magnifies and glorifies, judges that supplication is to be made to me for the things she has committed, and, after pardon granted, longs that grace and glory be given to her. « And thy face comely, » a face without spot, a face without wrinkle, a face that knows no repulse, and most worthy to be received and numbered among friends.

15. *Catch us the little foxes that destroy the vineyards: for our vineyard hath flourished.*

This Bride knew no delay: called forth by her Beloved, [1239A] who urged that she should come to him, she straightway arose, straightway stood before his majesty; and because she came covered, lest she should be overwhelmed by the glory, she deserved to hear what was said, « Shew me thy face, » which is, With face unveiled, contemplate me; « And let thy voice sound in mine ears, » that is, Ask what thou wilt, thou of whom I know that she will ask nothing but what is just. Received by the Bridegroom, she deserves to understand and to see what was promised, namely, that « The winter is past, the rain has gone away and departed; the flowers have appeared in the land; the time of pruning is come: the voice of the turtle has been heard in the land: the fig tree hath put forth her green figs: the vines in flower have yielded their sweet smell. » For these things the Bride gives thanks to the Bridegroom, knowing that all this is his work; and she herself receives praise and grace from the Bridegroom, because [1239B] his grace in her was not void, but she herself laboured excellently together with the grace given her. But now it is time that he instruct her concerning heavenly things, that he teach her concerning those things which are above her, that, called forth from common action into contemplation, she may understand the things she was contemplating, lest, when she has possessed them, she should stand forth wholly untaught. And because this is to be done by him, and he must in a manner be at leisure for the honour of his Beloved, he admonishes his companions that meanwhile they guard the vineyards which have now flowered, and « in flower have yielded their sweet smell, » lest the foxes destroy them, and the labours bestowed on them be brought to nothing: and he exhorts thus, « Catch us the little foxes. » But he speaks to his companions, that is, to the ministering spirits; of whom Paul says, « Are they not all ministering [1239C] spirits, sent to minister for them who shall receive the inheritance of salvation? » [n: (Hebr. I, 14.)] These are the angels, to whom the life of men is committed to be guarded; whom Jacob saw as he slept upon the stone. He saw a ladder standing upon the earth, and the top thereof touching heaven; the angels also of God ascending and descending by it; and the Lord leaning upon the ladder [n: (Gen. XXVIII, 12, 13)]; to whom here God speaks, commanding that they catch the foxes, who desire to harm this flowering vineyard, and to destroy it, and to bring its fruits to nothing: but these are the adverse powers, made by God at the same time and reprobate, hardened unto the harm of the Church; in this they stand, in this they abide, in this they reckon their successes, if it be able in anything to hinder the Church, [1239D] her that tends toward God, and desires him, and by the flower of good report now sends forth from herself a holy odour, or in some measure to disturb her. Which is plain in Job, is plain in Joseph, which was declared in Tobias, whom all these, as they drew them downward through adversities, were hindered by the good angels from accomplishing what they had begun; but, seeing that all these things wrought together unto good for them, they might perceive that their own power was nothing, save in so far as they were permitted by God. To the good angels therefore that is commanded by the Lord which was said, « Catch us the little foxes, » that is, Those whom you behold wandering like foxes, and disturbing all men by guile, [1240A] catch these, hinder these, and bring it to pass that they can do nothing except what is pleasing to my will. But the Lord makes mention of these foxes in the Gospel, saying: « The foxes have holes, and the birds of the air nests: but the Son of man hath not where to lay his head [n: (Matth. VIII, 20)]. » For to him who said to him, « Master, I will follow thee whithersoever thou shalt go, » the Saviour says, « The foxes have holes; » as if he should say, I receive thee not; I understand thee not to be predestined to me, but given to the foxes, that the foxes may tear thee, and the birds of the air, who are the malignant spirits, may rend thee. The same also he says of Herod threatening him: « Go, and tell that fox; behold, I cast out devils, and do cures to day and to morrow, and the third day I am consummated [n: (Luc. XIII, 32)], as if he should say, Tell this to him who has been given over to the fox for food, [1240B] He shall not slay me; but I, to day, and in the time that is to be until my death, perfect health in all who are weighed down by disease; and then on the third day I shall be consummated, that is, then I shall die, and all things shall be consummated which the enemy man is able to do against me. Of these foxes also David makes mention, saying to the Lord: « But they have sought my soul in vain [n: (Psal. LXII, 10)]. » For they who slew the Lord sought to destroy his soul, but in vain, because he lives: behold the Lord is King for ever. « They shall go into the lower parts of the earth [n: (Psal. XXVIII, 10)]; » for by the love of an earthly kingdom they shall be swallowed up, and blinded, so that they shall seem to have entered into the lower parts of the earth. For grieving to be made subject to the Romans, they were crushed by the edge [1240C] of the sword, and the greatest part of them was given to the foxes, that is, they were delivered to the malignant spirits, that these might feed upon them, and that whatsoever is done might be pleasing to their will. It is read also that Samson took three hundred foxes from the Philistines, and bound their tails together, and set the fire of lamps between the tails, and so sent them into the labours of the Philistines, the standing corn, and the vineyards, and the oliveyards, and consumed all things [n: (Jud. 15)]. Which indeed is, as often as some Catholic doctor of the Church gathers together at once all the heresies of the heretics, and places in the midst of them the lamp of heavenly fire, that is, the fire of eternal malediction; and so, sending it into their labours, consumes what is theirs, and brings it to nothing. Wherein indeed it is to be noted [1240D] that these foxes are bidden to be caught while they are little; for they are little in the very first beginnings of temptations; and they become great when, as the temptation grows, they dash down the minds of men, and lead them into acts wickedly persuaded. For thus is chastity dashed down. First a woman is seen; and by frequent seeing she is coveted; after the coveting, there is a shaking by converse; after the seeing, and the coveting, and the converse, as the evil titillation grows, the act evilly desired is fulfilled, and chastity long assailed is cast down. So also covetousness is fed and nourished by sight, while a man measures not himself, « what he owes, what he does, » what it behoves to be justly done; but stretches himself, even unjustly, to the doing of those things which [1241A] are beyond him. So, possessing one thing, he seeks a second unit, that he may be multiplied tenfold: then, seeking a third, he does this, he seeks this, that he may be multiplied a hundredfold: to which, unless a thousandfold be added, he reckons that nothing has been done, since he is not ignorant that something is wanting to him. So this boundless disgrace of malice strives with the multiplication of number, never setting a measure to itself, so long as it has found some larger number into which it may grow. Whence the Saviour says: « That a rich man shall hardly enter into the kingdom of heaven. » And again: « It is easier for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle, than for a rich man to enter into the kingdom of heaven [n: (Matth. XIX, 23, 24)]. » The camel is the greatest rope that is had in ships, by which, when the tempest grows, ships [1241B] are compelled to stand fast with anchors cast; for this to pass through the eye of a needle is easier, that is, all things being despised, to depart through the hard suffering of death, than, this being avoided, to enter into the kingdom of heaven: which indeed, if it is difficult for man, is easy and possible for God. For we have known many rich men to have left all things for his sake, and to have entered to him through the eye of a needle; which is of the power of God alone, and of his ineffable mercy, who snatches them from the mouth and the lowest jaws of Satan, and leads them through to the heavenly kingdoms. So are the other virtues softened, so do they perish, so does vice possess what has been seized by the craft of the malignant one. Whence it is rightly said, « Catch us the little foxes, » which is, Resist these foxes while they are [1241C] little, shake their first motions, lest they be able to grow, dash them against me, who am the most firm rock. « For our vineyard hath flourished, » that is, it has sent forth from itself the hope of good working, and if it be defended by you, it has fulfilled this your hope with the fruit of good work. There follows:

16. *My Beloved to me, and I to him.*
