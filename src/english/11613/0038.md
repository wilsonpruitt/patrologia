## 4. (cont.)

The Church, which is rightly called the bride of this highest King, purchased by his blood, consists in a division of two parts; one which is already perfect, the other not yet perfect. That one is perfect which has long since entered [1260D] upon the ways of life, which long ago went in the counsel of the good, and stood in the way of the same, and established for itself a seat in the chair of prudence. This one long ago left its darkness behind in Egypt, escaped the persecution of Pharao, and saw him, as he pursued, extinguished in the midst of the waves, because it knew that his power was made void by the waters of baptism. This one, following Moses in the desert, beheld the great works of God, and deserved to hear the words of his law brought forth from his mouth, which indeed it held so long, until it heard the Lord saying to Moses and to the people: « I will raise you up a prophet of the midst of your brethren, like to thee; and I will put my words in his mouth, [1261A] and he shall speak to them all that I shall command him. But he that will not hear his words, which he shall speak in my name, I will be the avenger [n: (Deut. XXVIII, 18, 19)]. » In this I would rightly count together all the fathers of the Old Testament, the patriarchs and the prophets; in the New Testament the apostles, the martyrs, and the confessors: these indeed are all joined and united to God, so that they are one spirit with him, that is, of one spirit with him; for they will that which he wills, and will not that which he wills not: so that there is no discord in them, whom he has himself taken with him into glory. To these I would rightly number the elect of God, living in the flesh, yet knowing nothing of the flesh, so far as is lawful, and long since « fortified » for God by the Spirit of God, to whom « to live is Christ, and to die is gain » ; saying continually to God: [1261B] Draw us after thee, that in the odour of thy ointments we may come to thee; this one indeed is perfect. But that one is imperfect which has lately received the faith, but has not yet fulfilled the works of God: it has indeed known the Lord, that he is good, but, not yet having attained the works of his goodness, it says with the Prophet, « For what have I in heaven? and besides thee what have I desired upon earth? » [n: (Psal. LXXII, 25.)] It has not yet left house, nor father, nor mother; not yet has this poor one followed the poor Christ. Others, having left father and mother, kinsfolk and children, houses and fields, do indeed follow Christ, yet do not deserve to attain him fully: the flesh presses hard, which stirs up and invites the one abiding in the flesh [1261C] to the allurements of the flesh and to its innumerable works: the adversary too presses hard, who assists this persuasion of the flesh, promising long times for penitence, and that, after the desires have been fulfilled, God, if he has been offended, being a merciful and appeasable Judge, can easily be recalled to pardon by tears. Whence also it laments, saying: « Oh! who shall deliver me from the body of this death? » [n: (Rom. VII, 24.)] And so it runs back over the times that are past, considering infancy, boyhood, and adolescence, judging youth and manhood and old age, condemning each single thing wherein it offended God, cleansing and purging itself also by penitence of heart, and confession of mouth, and satisfaction of work, by abstinence and discipline, by prayer and alms, by vigils and teaching, calling down the heavenly mercy upon itself; its sins [1261D] being forgiven, the more deeply it sinned, the more abundantly it now burns, saying, « Thou hast wounded my heart, my sister, my bride; » as if it should say: O thou worthily espoused to God by works, who art justly called the bride of God, yet thou art my sister, since to us God is one Father by creation, and the Church one mother by generation; with the Bridegroom thou canst do much: shew for a sister what thou canst; the wound of love which I have borne in imitating thee, I beseech thee to enlarge, to spread charity wide in me, and by thy holy intercession to bring me through to that perfection wherein thou rejoicest. There follows:

*In one of thine eyes.*

The eyes of the Church are two, one of action, the other [1262A] of contemplation; one wherewith it looks upon its neighbour, the other wherewith it sees God; one wherewith it looks upon the needs of its neighbour, the other wherewith it searches out God and the heavenly secrets. In that eye therefore which is toward its neighbour, which is directed to his needs, it does not ask that regard be had to itself; but in that wherewith it sees God, wherewith eternity contemplates [cj: *aeternitas*; read *aeternitatem*, "wherewith eternity is contemplated"], wherewith it understands the secrets which lie hidden from a man living in the flesh, what is the breadth, what the length, what the height, and what the depth. For because God is charity, and the length of charity is the same as that which is God's; and as God is from eternity, so from eternity charity has existed, and as God is unto eternity, so unto eternity charity shall abide toward his creatures: this indeed is the length of God. But because there is no greater love than that [1262B] a man lay down his soul for his friends: his breadth stood forth in this, that he laid down his soul for his friends, that those whom he had had as enemies he might convert into the unity of faith, of charity, and of peace. But the height of God is in this, that everything which is, is from God; corporeal things indeed, God knitting together the parts of which they consist; spiritual things, God conferring upon them the life whereby they live, whereby they have understanding, whereby by a wonderful opening of the mind they understand the existence of all things: yet these are finite, beyond which thou wouldst understand nothing save God. And this is the height of God; from which he came down into the Virgin, from which he came down upon the cross, that thus at least, being himself man, he might restore by the power of his death all who should confess and believe him to be man. [1262C] But the depth is that of hell, into which he descended, that he might snatch thence those in whom he might recognize the seed of his mother: as was said by the Lord in the chief and first prophecy: « I will put enmities between thee and the woman, and thy seed and her seed; » understanding all to be her seed who should believe that he was to be born, or was born, of her seed, who, being without part in sin, should snatch the world from sin. There follows:

*And in one hair of thy neck.*

That is, In the unity of the hairs of thy neck; which hairs are as one hair. For the hairs are those which come down from the head upon the body, adorning the face and covering the neck; which are all so bound and joined in unity that they seem to be one hair. These are the several [1262D] persons of the saints, who, since they are all from God, strain toward him in such great charity and contemplation that they all seem as one; according to that saying, « And the multitude of believers had but one heart and one soul [n: (Act. IV, 32)]: » this part therefore, desiring to have part in this multitude, says, « Thou hast wounded my heart; » not indeed with the wound of death, which is declared to be deadly, but with that wound whereby life is conceived unto God, is nourished, and is perfected. Desiring therefore to be more wounded with this wound, and desiring to be more enlightened with this light wherewith God is seen, it says twice, « Thou hast wounded my heart, my sister, my bride, thou hast wounded my heart, » desiring that a third wound [1263A] be laid upon it, wherein it may not hang in hope, but may rejoice in the apprehension of that thing which it desires. There follows:

10. *How beautiful are thy breasts, my sister, my bride! thy breasts are more beautiful than wine, and the odour of thy ointments above all spices.*

This is a worthy commendation of a sister by a sister; for it says: O thou who hast deserved to be worthily called the bride of God, who yet hast not denied that thou art a sister to me, « how beautiful are thy breasts! » that is, how beautiful is the teaching of thy breasts for the consolation of the weak, and the sustaining of little ones? And it makes these breasts manifest by a repetition of praise, saying, « Thy breasts are more beautiful than wine, and the odour of thy ointments above all spices. » Concerning which, [1263B] because we have shewn that which the Lord gave, we forbear for the time to repeat these things. There follows:

11. *A dropping honeycomb, thy lips, O bride.*

The sister still perseveres in the praise of the bride, saying: « A dropping honeycomb, thy lips, O bride » of Christ. Nothing is sweeter than a honeycomb; and of the divine words it is said: « How sweet are thy words to my palate! more than honey to my mouth [n: (Psal. CXVIII, 103)]. » For a honeycomb is honey in wax, that is, the spiritual sense of the words in the letter, dropping from the wax, which is as the letter, the spiritual understanding, allegorical, or moral, or anagogical. Or because all the divine words preach the incarnation of our Lord Jesus Christ, in which divers natures are joined, as honey is hidden in the wax which lies open, so the divinity in the flesh. [1263C] Therefore the lips of this bride are compared to a dropping honeycomb, which are proved not to keep silence concerning the testimonies of the divine incarnation.

*Honey and milk are under thy tongue.*

For they who preach his incarnation have honey and milk under the tongue, proclaiming God in the sweetness of honey, and testifying to the innocent man in the milk which is gathered from the earth. For honey is from heaven, because the virgin bee receives it from the heavenly dew, and gathers what she has conceived into the wax; so also the virgin mother conceived from heaven the conceived Son of God, clothed him conceived with flesh, refreshed him clothed with the nourishment of milk, and thus presented life to the world out of herself. Deservedly therefore are they said to have these things under the tongue, who, [1263D] meditating on them in the heart and testifying to them with the tongue, hold them by memory, and in preaching announce them.

*And the odour of thy garments is as the odour of frankincense.*

Garments cover the body, defend it from cold, and if they are more elegant, afford adornment, and confer grace upon the possessor. This the virtues confer upon souls, without which they are naked, and exposed to the colds which come from the north wind, where he has set his seat who has taken care to chill all things: but from this cold those souls are safe which have now left the world, which, following Christ more closely, have deserved to hear: « Come to me, you that labour and are burdened, and I will refresh you [n: (Matth. XI, 28.)]: » whom [1264A] indeed he well refreshes, while he commands them to spurn the world, and to seek God, and to cleave to these things with the whole mind. The soul indeed clothed with right faith, clothed with humility and penitence, clothed with confession and with satisfaction of every kind, when it now hopes well of pardon, and clothes itself with charity of every kind, and, exercising itself in this solicitude as much as it can, now uses garments in which it is pleasing to God, whose odour is best, and the fragrance sweet, « as the odour of frankincense. » Frankincense indeed, set upon the fire, gives forth from itself a most sweet odour: so the virtues, mingled with the fire of charity, bestow here a good example upon the peoples, and give pleasing honour to the Lord, and a most sweet odour.
