## 12.

*A garden enclosed, my sister, my bride, a garden enclosed, a fountain sealed up.*

With beautiful praise she has extolled her sister; she has praised her eyes and the unity of her hairs; she has commended the comeliness of her breasts, and the odour of her ointments, her lips and her tongue, together with the odour of her garments; now she designates the place where such things are laid up: they are laid up indeed in a garden enclosed; and not merely enclosed, but enclosed again. Which garden, twice enclosed, is understood to be she herself, to whom it is thus said: « A garden enclosed, my sister, my bride. » For a garden is called a pleasant place, where flowers give forth their scent, where little beds of divers kinds grow green, where pigments and incenses answer to the balsam, where the shade of any fruit-bearing trees affords a sweet seat and dwelling to one who wishes to rest. A garden of this kind [1264C] is the Church, which has now been received by God, or is being prepared to be received in the near future, which does not delay to present to God the virtue of good works, the appearance also of holiness (which is designated by the balsam or by the other spices), and the shade wherein the Lord may shew himself, in which he may rest and take delight, and to present her very self. This indeed is a garden enclosed. By what enclosing? By the bar of faith indeed; for true faith shuts her in and fortifies her on every side, so that in no part of her does the enemy find a door or an opening whereby he may come to her, by tempting or by in any degree assaulting her: who is again called « a garden enclosed; » but by what enclosing? she shews it, saying, « A fountain sealed up; » as if she should say: Thou art shut in by faith, and not by faith only, but also [1264D] by baptism; for the Church has received baptism, which is the fountain sealed up, in the name of the Father, of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost. And wheresoever the enemy finds this worthily kept, he flees, he vanishes, and, as wax before the face of the fire, so indeed he melts away. There follows:

13. *Thy sendings forth are a paradise of pomegranates, with the fruits of apples.*

« The sendings forth » are good words and works, whereby the Church begets and nourishes martyrs, and sends them over to heaven; others, living holily, she sets forth as an example to their neighbours, and thus sends forth the fruits of pomegranates. The pomegranate shuts up under a red rind a multitude of grains, and its fruit sweetly refreshes: [1265A] so they who pour out their blood for Christ gather to themselves a multitude of virtues: others who, living in peace, work good things, instruct their neighbours and refresh them; and both send forth the odour of the virtues, as it were a paradise of sweetness: for « Paradise » in Greek is in Latin called « a Garden, » or « an irrigation. » But there is a paradise in us, when the soul is animated and quickened by the virtues, and by the infusion of the Holy Ghost, bearing fruit in the nurseries of the virtues, in which God has chiefly set the tree of life, that is, the root of piety; for that is the substance of our life, if we render to God the worship that is due; let the just man therefore abide in paradise, that against the heats of the body and the burning of the flesh he may be cooled by the shade of the virtues, and be guarded by their helps; which is, « with the fruits of [1265B] apples. » For pomegranates are they who depart hence crowned with their own blood: but those fruits which are called only apples are they who, although they are not called martyrs, yet follow after God with honesty of life and with the confession of the true faith. There follows:

*Cyprus with spikenard. 14. Spikenard and saffron, sweet cane and cinnamon, with all the trees of Libanus, myrrh and aloes, with all the chief ointments.*

This is drawn back to that which was said, « Thy sendings forth are a paradise of pomegranates: » not pomegranates only are sent forth by thee; nay rather « cyprus with spikenard, » and the rest that follow, are thy sendings forth. The cyprus is an aromatic tree, with a white seed [1265C] and of good odour, which is boiled in oil, and thence is made an ointment which is called cyprus, wherewith princes also are anointed, that they may lift themselves above others in lofty virtues; and but if with the spikenard [cj: *sin*; read *sit*, "and let it be with the spikenard"], which is lowly, and a herb fragrant and warming, and signifying charity: intimating this, that a prince, however much he excel others in virtue, should always cleave to them in the charity which is designated by the spikenard. « Spikenard and saffron. » Saffron is of a golden colour, designating the divine wisdom, which it is needful that a prince observe, having that before his eyes: « The greater thou art, humble thyself in all things *(Sirac III, 20)*; » and that other: « He that humbleth himself shall be exalted, and he that exalteth himself shall be humbled [n: (Luc. XIV, 31)]. » -- « Sweet cane and cinnamon. » The sweet cane indeed, which is also called cassia, is an aromatic tree, [1265D] but small, of a sturdy and purple bark, which avails for the curing of the troubles of the bowels, and which, by reason of its shortness, is reckoned by some among the odoriferous herbs: it signifies the humble in spirit, who, mindful of the Lord's passion, are prepared to suffer for the Lord. But cinnamon is a short tree, yet odoriferous and sweet, of an ashen colour, availing for the use of medicine, surpassing the sweet cane twofold; signifying those who reckon themselves weak, whose humility has praise and sweetness with God. And well is the cinnamon of ashen colour set after the purple sweet cane, because through the remembrance of the Lord's passion there arises in us a despising of our own virtue. [1266A] « With all the trees of Libanus. » For as the sweet cane and the cinnamon shew the humble thoughts of the saints, so also the trees of Libanus, which are tall and sturdy, shew the lofty actions and prayers of the saints. « Myrrh and aloes with all the trees of Libanus. » Myrrh and aloes are the continence of the flesh, and chastity, which mortify the rottenness of luxury. But myrrh renders the body incorruptible: so bitterness and patience fortify and defend souls against carnal concupiscences. For the kinds of spices are the honour and the advancement of the divers virtues which are in the saints. Out of these kinds are made the first and chief ointments, which heal bodies; well therefore do they designate the virtues of souls, which being gathered together and compounded, a good odour goes forth, and health [1266B] is restored. There follows:

15. *The fountain of gardens, the well of living waters which flow with force from Libanus.*

The praise of a sister by a sister is thus repeated, « The fountain of gardens, the well of living waters. » For she it is in whom is the fountain of all the gardens, that is, the Holy Ghost, who, being God, waters the gardens, and brings them forth into buds, and makes them to have flowers, and fruits, and seeds, whereby they please, and for the sake of which they are cultivated. For from him is Scripture, which, begetting the water of wisdom, refreshes, and does not cease to flow; which is well called « of the gardens, » because it belongs especially to those in whom the virtues arise; which is a fountain as far as the surface, a well in the deep. For Scripture in certain [1266C] places is open, and is easily taken in by the understanding: but in others it is obscure, and unless it be expounded it is not understood. Yet the waters which glide down are living, that is, they assuredly lead to life the men for whom they were given, if they be understood: whence it is said, « The stream of the river maketh the city of God joyful: the most High hath sanctified his own tabernacle. « Which flow with force from Libanus [n: (Psal. XLV, 5)]: » which flow out from the heavenly Libanus, that is, from the whiteness of spiritual and heavenly virtues; « with force, » that is, they run down forcibly. For with great force they run down upon Moses, who described the first beginnings of the world, and spoke with God at a time when no man had withstood; with great force upon the prophets, who foretold that God would be man, when he [1266D] was not yet man; with great force upon David, who so ran through all the mysteries that nothing in them is left untouched; with great force upon the apostles, who, renewing the world afresh, preach life to us, and foretell life eternal. There follows:

16. *Arise, O north wind, and come, O south wind, blow through my garden, and let the aromatical spices thereof flow.*

Thus far the Bridegroom has heard the praise of his Bride, distinguished by six clauses, which he now confirms that he approves, saying, « Arise, O north wind. » For God commands every heavenly power, both that which was created in the heavens and was made reprobate by him through the elation of its mind, and those which stood with him, [1267A] and are joined to him by the firm root of charity. And so the Lord, about to come into his garden, that nothing sad may meet him, and that he may find nothing whereby he may be offended, powerfully commands the north wind to arise, to take itself far from that place, to go to foreign nations, to chill them, and to render them like unto itself. But he commands the south wind to come to his garden, and with its soft breath to blow through this garden, so that its aromatical spices may flow, and that a holy habitation may be made and may please God. He says therefore, « Arise, O north wind, » which is, Depart; I have no fellowship with thee, no participation of light with darkness. Let that Spirit of mine come, who may soften the necks of my own, who may turn the rock into water; who may appoint Samuel, an innocent youth, judge of the whole people; who may make David, the boy harper, a king, singing psalms [1267B] in the spirit of prophecy; who may fit Daniel, a youth, to be the judge of elders, the deliverer of the innocent, and Amos also, a countryman distinguishing the mulberries of brambles, to be among the prophets by that same spirit of prophecy; let him come who (that I may come back to modern times) may appoint Peter the fisherman and Paul the persecutor doctors of the whole Church, who willed to have Matthew the publican for an evangelist, and Mary Magdalen for a herald of grace and of the resurrection; who (that I may make mention of her from whom all these things were begun) consecrated Mary, a virgin pure, holy, and untouched, into θεοτόκον, that is, the Mother of God. Therefore, when he blows through my garden, « Let its aromatical spices flow, » that is, The spices which had been bound fast by the blowing of the north wind, that is, the virtues and gifts of God, shall flow, [1267C] shall drop down, and shall be made manifest to all, which hitherto lay hid. There follows:

*Let my beloved come into his garden, and eat the fruits of his apples.* [var: Vulg. has the singular, "the fruit of his apples"]

The word being heard whereby all things were made, which it is not lawful to gainsay, straightway the north wind departs, the holy Spirit is present, and at his coming its aromatical spices flow, and send forth a most sweet odour; and this Bride, hoping that it will please God, says: « Let my beloved come into his garden. » There is nothing in the garden that may hinder, nothing that can now displease by sight, by smell, or by taste; let him come therefore into his garden, « that he may eat the fruit of his apples, » that is, that he may « eat » the apples which are his fruits, that is, incorporate them into himself, and pass them over into his own body. This therefore [1267D] is the general prayer of the whole Church, that the Bridegroom may come by a certain spiritual grace into his garden, that is, into the Church herself, and may visit her trees and her herbs and her spice-bearing beds, and pluck of them, and taste apples of divers kinds, which are indeed as many as there are divers observances, constitutions, and divers customs in the Church of God: for this diversity of apples denotes the virtues of the divers Churches throughout the world, differing indeed from one another in something, yet without doubt running together into the service of the Bridegroom. For apples are whatsoever fruits are held to be of a tender rind, whereon the Bride desires her Bridegroom to feed, that is, to be delighted with every kind of virtue whereby the Churches stand and minister. [1268A] As this is the general prayer of the Church, so it can be the particular prayer of every faithful soul, that he may come into her, who is discerned to be his garden, and refresh himself with the variety of those virtues which are in her.
