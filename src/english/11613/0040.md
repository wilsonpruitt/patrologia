## CHAPTER V.

1. *I am come into my garden, my sister, my bride; I have reaped my myrrh with my spices.*

The Bridegroom answers those who are held back by desire, and testifies that he has already done the things which she was asking, saying: « I am come into my garden. » And because a sister, addressing a sister, has long used this name, « Sister, » that he may conform himself to her, he answers her with the same word, saying, « My sister, my bride, » as though joined and allied to me by my own blood. « Sister, » because we have one [1268B] Father, God, and one mother, the Church, which refreshes us with her sacraments. He says therefore, « My sister, my bride, » I long since came into my garden, and I have applied this sign of my coming, « I have reaped my myrrh, that is, With a certain sickle of death I have cut them off in this life, and have led them into the storehouses of eternal blessedness: the martyrs who suffered bitter things for me, or even all the others, who have crucified the flesh with its vices and concupiscences: « With my spices, » that is, with all those who are marked out by the report of good works, and therefore worthy of much veneration and renowned. This myrrh indeed he reaped, when he received Abel the first martyr from the hand of Cain: this also he reaped, when he snatched Noe, and Abraham, and all whom he knew to be the seed of his mother, from the curse. [1268C] There follows:

*I have eaten the honeycomb with my honey; I have drunk my wine with my milk.*

The honeycomb holds honey within wax, and when it is taken, it fills him who takes it with great sweetness: so when the preacher of an obscure word expresses the sweetness which is contained in it, he pleases God indeed, and in so far as God is delighted in him, he is taken up and incorporated. « I have drunk my wine with my milk. » The preacher of an obscure word pleases, when its sweetness has been expressed; so also the preacher of a word which is not obscure pleases by its gladness and by the sweetness mingled with it, and is taken up into his body. There follows:

*Eat, friends, and drink, and be inebriated most dear ones.*

[1268D]The Bridegroom, invited to the garden of delights, has used the delights in it, the honey wherewith he was fed, and the wine which he drank: now therefore, lest he seem to keep his banquet to himself alone, lest he be thought to enjoy these things alone, he performs the work of charity, and inviting his friends, that is, the heavenly virtues, to the same, he says, « Eat friends, » that is, be refreshed with the same delights as I, you whom from of old I have perceived to be friends; and « be inebriated most dear ones, » you whom I held dear, when I created you; and most dear, when I confirmed you in the grace of my charity: « Be inebriated, » that is, forgetful of all anxiety, be made glad with the wine of joyfulness. There follows:

2. *I sleep, and my heart watcheth.*

[1269A]The Lord came, being invited, to his garden; he was fed with honey, and drank wine; which refreshment indeed is an incitement to sleep. Whence also, that he may show that same place to be one of rest, and not of laborious working, the Bridegroom says, « I sleep. » Which sleep indeed was fulfilled in Abraham; to whom, sleeping after the sacrifice, the Lord thus said, « Know beforehand that thy seed shall be a stranger in a land not their own, and they shall bring them under bondage, and afflict them four hundred years. Nevertheless the nation which they shall serve, I will judge: and after this they shall come out with great substance. But thou shalt go to thy fathers in peace, and be buried in a good old age. And in the fourth generation they shall return hither: for as yet the iniquities of the Amorrhites are not complete, [1269B] even to the present time [n: (Gen. XV, 13-16)]. » Behold with how watchful a heart the Lord spoke to the sleeping old man, to whom he plainly foretold things that should come hereafter, after four hundred years [n: (Gen. XXVIII, 12)]. The Lord slept also in Jacob, under whose head a stone was set, and he saw a ladder standing upon the earth, touching heaven with its top, and the angels of God ascending and descending by it. Behold with how watchful a heart the Lord spoke to this man, showing him angels ascending, who were presenting to the Lord, leaning upon the ladder from above, the good, who had served him by virtue and had given him their labour through grace; and others descending, who should snatch out from them by grace such as were wrapped in sins, and raise them up, and, when they had been presented [1269C] to the Lord, should make them one body with the rest. The Bridegroom slept also in Joseph, and watched most fully in the heart, that is, in the soul wherein is the seat of the heart [n: (Gen. XXXVII, 7)]: for he saw himself and his brethren binding sheaves in the field, and his own sheaf standing upright, and his brethren's sheaves standing round about and adoring his sheaf. And likewise he saw the sun, and the moon, and eleven stars adoring him [n: (ibid., 11)]. The understanding of these things the Bridegroom opened to his father and to his brethren; for the brethren said: « Shalt thou be our king? or shall we be subject to thy dominion? » [n: (Ibid., 8.)] In like manner the father also said, « Shall I and thy mother and thy brethren adore thee upon the earth? » [n: (Ibid., 10.)] The Lord sleeps also in certain men, and in others he watches in heart, when for some he preserves the memory of the vision, and to others [1269D] he affords the understanding of it [n: (Gen. XL)]. For the ministers of Pharao saw dreams, and telling them to Joseph, both of them received the understanding of their own dream. For the one was restored to his place [n: (Gen. XLI)]; the other, being hanged, perished. Pharao also slept, and, watchful in heart, he beheld seven fat oxen feeding in the marsh, and seven others, foul and wasted with leanness, devouring them, whose appearance and habit of body was wonderful. He testified also that he had seen thin ears, and stricken with blight, devour the beauty of seven other ears. For whom, when he watched in heart unto understanding, who declared to him the abundance of seven years to come; and the famine of seven [1270A] years. The Bridegroom slept also in Nabuchodonosor, who, when he saw the vision, it was revealed to him neither unto memory nor unto understanding; which indeed was laid open to Daniel out of a watchful heart, when he said: « Thou, O king, sawest, and behold as it were a great statue. The head thereof was of the finest gold, but the breast and the arms of silver [n: (Dan. II, 31, 32)]. » Thus indeed he revealed the dream to him, and consequently showed him its mystery and the understanding of it. Such then is that which is said, « I sleep, » that is, whom I will I make to sleep; but while the outward sense is lulled, the inward mind freely contemplates those things which God provides with a watchful heart. But if thou refer it to the passion, such is that which is said, « I sleep, » that is, enduring on the cross the labour of death, thereafter I rest from [1270B] death. « The heart » however « that is mine, » that is, the wisdom of God, to which my heart is the seat, this same « watcheth, » that is, it watchfully preserves, orders, and disposes all things. There follows:

*The voice of my beloved knocking, open, my sister, friend, my dove, my undefiled.* [var: Vulg. *Aperi mihi, soror mea, amica mea*, "Open to me, my sister, my friend"]

While the Bridegroom sleeps in the garden, after a long delay, lest she herself should seem shamelessly to search into his secrets, this bride betakes herself into her own dwelling, the bridegroom being left in that same place under the guard of his friends. Yet meditating upon him in her heart, seeking him, and preparing herself, that she may be able worthily to meet him when he comes to her, and to make ready things pleasing to him when he comes. But the Bridegroom, roused from sleep after the delay, knowing that she had gone away and was anxious for him, does that [1270C] which belongs to a lover: he follows her, and finding the door shut, he knocks at it, saying: « Open to me, my sister; » as if he should say, Receive me coming to thee; reject me not, who seek a dwelling in thee; but be earnest to open to me the door of thy mouth, the dwelling of thy heart within thee, I who, seeking thee alone, desire to find thee, who art « my sister, my friend, my undefiled, my dove. » There is no need to linger in setting forth these names, since from what has already been said they may easily be understood. Yet there is need that we consider how much he wills to be heard, who willed her to be called to him by so many names of affection. There follows:

*For my head is full of dew, and my locks of the drops of the nights.*

[1270D]The cause why the Bridegroom asks that it be opened to him, he shows by these words, saying: « For my head is full of dew. » For thus he shows that he lay down and fell asleep not under a roof, but in the garden; for the garden, as has been said, has no roof; it receives none under a roof, but to him who rests in it the holy shades of the holy trees afford a remedy. Whence he says, here I, falling asleep by night, bring back my head full of dew, and my locks with the drops of the nights: *For dew cometh by night, by night it stealeth beneath*
*the grasses, and with what continuance that one is stretched out*
*it followeth her, and, sitting upon the topmost crown, a drop*
*it maketh, and thus maketh a certain shadow of rain*.


[1271A]This is the dew whereby the grasses grow green again, whereby the plantings sprout, whereby the fruitfulness of the earth is opened, and gladness is plenteously poured abroad upon all in common. The Bridegroom therefore, willing that his Bride be steeped in this dew, willing that she herself be filled with these drops, which are formed by night, that is, apart from the knowledge of men, asks that it be opened to him, saying: « Open to me; » and adding the cause, he says: « For my head is full of dew. For the head of Christ is God, in whom all the fulness of wisdom and knowledge is known to dwell, whom they that receive Christ are known to have in themselves indeed, and his gifts also; and « with the drops of the nights, » that is, with the heavenly virtues, which were conferred [1271B] upon them by night, that is, when man did not yet exist, nor had any knowledge of them. There follows:

3. *I have put off my garment, how shall I put it on? I have washed my feet, how shall I defile them?*

The Bride, hearing the voice of the Bridegroom knocking and desiring to come in to her, the dew which the Bridegroom desires to bring in, and the drops of the nights, being not yet understood, is affrighted, and, not answering the Bridegroom, speaks to herself, and makes her words to herself, saying, « I have put off my garment, how shall I put it on? » For thinking that she is called back to her former and ancient manner of life, and fearing to be entangled in these worldly affairs for her neighbour's necessity, and complaining, yet not answering the Bridegroom, she says to herself, « I have put off my garment, » that is, I have been stripped of my ancient manner of life, and have been converted to Christ in baptism, and have been knit to him by the [1271C] preaching of good men: in what manner shall I now put that on? in what manner shall I embrace what I know not? whence she says, « I have washed my feet, how shall I defile them? » Her feet indeed are her affections, which she washed by believing, by acquiescing in the exhortations of the saints, by hearing Paul, who says, « Not in rioting and drunkenness, not in chambering and impurities, not in contention and envy. But put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ [n: (Rom. XIII, 13, 14)]. » In what manner shall I defile these? how, by departing from them, shall I bend myself to the manners of the world which I had cast away? It is indeed to be feared to return to the action of the world, which cannot long be carried on without sin. There follows:
