## 5. (cont.)

She has said that he whom she sought was not found, nor did he whom she called answer her. Whence it might rightly be said [1276B] and reckoned that the Bridegroom had left her to her own counsel, and had by right suffered her to be seized by those passing by; which he removes, saying, « The keepers of the city found me. » The city of God is holy Church; he who reigns in her, who disposes all her rights, himself sets keepers over her, who may forbid her both to sin and to enter by the broad way which leads many to death, and may diligently teach her to lay hold of the narrow way of the virtues, which few know, and to enter by the strait gate, until she come to eternal life. These are the keepers of the Church, whom the Lord teaches by his Spirit, whom he fills more abundantly with the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and of fortitude, of knowledge and of piety, and with the spirit of fear; and he bids them pour this abroad throughout the Church, that they may securely lay hold of God, whose [1276C] Spirit this is. But these, to whom this guardianship of souls has been committed, marking this soul, or this multitude of souls, seeking God, and labouring in this very thing, that she may find him, receive her mercifully, receive her devoutly. To these they preach the words of life, in these they mortify whatever sins remain, and in him whom they have received they now kindle them more fully and more keenly with the love of the Godhead. This therefore is what is said, « The keepers of the city found me: they struck me, and wounded me. » For those words strike well which slay sins; and those wound well which make the love of God already conceived to burn more fully, and magnify it. « The keepers of the walls took away my cloak [1276D] from me. » We have already said that the keepers of the Churches are their Prelates. But who are the keepers of her walls, must now be said. For it has been said, Gird us about with thy impregnable wall, O Lord, and protect us always with the arms of thy power. For this city is girt with a wall, and lest it be undermined anywhere, there is need of a guard, lest, if an entrance lie open anywhere to the enemy, he who is within sleep the less securely. This wall of the Church, therefore, is faith, and if it anywhere lie open where it has been broken through, there will now be the less rest for every citizen dwelling therein. But the heavenly citizens, namely, the Angels of God, keep this, bestowing their labour upon this, that Satan pierce it not, that they suffer not the Church to be scandalized or troubled in any [1277A] part of the faith. These therefore are the keepers of the Church, namely, the angels of God, going about her indeed and encompassing her with a rampart, and bestowing their labour that faith be not broken in any part of itself, and that hope be not lessened in anything, and that the charity of God, which is the highest virtue, be not corrupted by growing cold. But these keepers take away the cloak of the Church, establishing faith in her, strengthening hope, rooting charity in her, so that it is now nothing to despise all these earthly things, while she is able to cleave to Christ alone. And this indeed is what she says, « The keepers of the walls took away my cloak. » The cloak indeed is an outward covering, that is, all this earthly substance, which was created for the Church's sake: with this the Church protects herself, fortifies herself. [1277B] But she forsakes and despises the whole of it, when she now sees herself given to God and drawing near to him. She prays therefore, saying, « I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem, » that is, ye heavenly Virtues, who are in nothing partakers of this misery, who owe wholly to the heavenly Jerusalem alone what you are, what you savour, that you protect us; « If you find my Beloved » (which indeed it is certain that you will find), « that you tell him, that I languish with love, » that is, for love of him I rest from all earthly things. There follows:

9. *What manner of one is thy Beloved from the Beloved, O most beautiful of women? What manner of one is thy Beloved from the Beloved, that thou hast so adjured us? 10. My Beloved is white and ruddy, chosen out of thousands.*

[1277C]The daughters of Jerusalem, being adjured to announce to the Beloved the love of her that languishes, lest they err, lest they present to one the love perchance given to another, inquire by what sign they may know her Beloved, lest the message of love be borne by them into uncertainty. They say therefore, « O most beautiful of women! » And they call her most beautiful, because there is nothing of spiritual beauty whereby a soul in this life may please God, which the Church has not in herself, which she does not represent out of herself. She cares not for that beauty of which Solomon says, « Favour is deceitful, and beauty is vain [n: (Prov. XXXI, 30)]: » but that which follows, « The woman that feareth the Lord, she shall be praised [n: (ibid.)]: » this she takes up, this she doubts not to offer to the Lord. Innumerable indeed are the virtues which spring from fear, [1277D] and many which spring from love; and if thou shouldst number them all, thou wilt find these in the Church a noble gift indeed, such as is worthy to be presented to God. Worthily therefore he thus names her, « O most beautiful of women! What manner of one is thy Beloved? » By what sign dost thou make it clear to us, that we may know him whom thou lovest, and may know his Father by whom he was sent, whom thou also lovest. And because this is exceedingly divine, and most capacious of both natures in Christ, she doubles her question, saying again, « What manner of one is thy Beloved from the Beloved, that thou hast so adjured us? My Beloved is white and ruddy. » This is the description of the Beloved, which she comprehends under the number twelve, [1278A] saying, « My Beloved is white and ruddy; » wherein she comprehends the twofold nature in Christ Jesus our Lord: the one, which is divine; the other, which is human. The divine indeed she comprehends, saying, « He is white: » but the human, adding, « And ruddy. » In his divine nature indeed he is white, retaining nothing mingled with himself; nothing black, nor red, nor anything coloured with a middle colour: but he himself is white, nay rather whiteness itself, spreading himself abroad into all his creatures: and though he shine in all, he diminishes nothing of himself, but he himself remains whole and entire in himself. Thus therefore he is white, because he is truly God, is truly light, is truly the simple good, which is diffused through all things that are his, and, remaining in itself, is one simple good. But he, having long [1278B] pitied human nature, after many thousands of years came down into the Virgin, and taking flesh from her, and creating for himself a soul, being made man he came into the world, choosing out of the world certain men for himself, to whom he himself said, « You have not chosen me: but I have chosen you [n: (Joan. XV, 16)], teaching the world first by himself, afterwards by them, and thereafter working mighty works; and since the world could not withstand them, it contrived the cross for his death, and laid his body in the sepulchre, from which, when he willed, he rose again, and thereafter remained on earth forty days to console his disciples, and thereafter, while all that were present beheld, he mightily ascended into heaven. He, being set upon the cross, because he redeemed the world by his blood, whose colour is red, [1278C] is deservedly called ruddy; so that in his memory and praise the Bride says, « My Beloved is white, and ruddy, » and adds also, « Chosen out of thousands. » For to this he alone out of the thousands of all men ought and was able to be chosen, who should wipe away the faults of men, who should not have the faults of men, but should be as an innocent lamb, who should take away the sins of the world, and by his innocence do away the guilt of all that believe in him; of whom David says, « Comely in form above the sons of men: grace is poured abroad in thy lips [n: (Psal. XLIV, 3)]; » of whom Isaias says, « Who is this that cometh from Edom, with dyed garments from Bozra? » [n: (Isa. LXIII, 1.)] There follows:

11. *His head is the finest gold. His locks are as [1278D] the topmost boughs of palm trees, black as a raven.*

The commendation begins from his head, which is compared to gold. For as the finest gold is more precious than all things desirable; so also God is found more precious than all things which can be coveted and desired. « His locks are as the topmost boughs of palm trees. » The topmost boughs of palm trees are the higher parts in the palm; and these bore before them the sign of victory; for the victors carrying them declared that they had obtained, as it were in war, the uppermost part, that is, the victory. His locks therefore, cleaving to the head and affording adornment to the head, are the faithful, obedient to God in all things, keeping his commandments, and fulfilling in deed what they [1279A] preach in word, and thus rejoicing that in the battle of this life they have obtained the victory of the palm. There follows, « Black as a raven, » because these, although they advance in virtue, protest that they are black as ravens. There follows:

12. *His eyes as doves upon brooks of waters, which are washed with milk, and sit beside the fullest streams.*

That is, spiritual men, who are clear-sighted and keen to see his mysteries, are « as *colombae* which are washed with milk. » The milk indeed wherewith these must be washed is the reasonable sense without guile; whence it is said, « As newborn babes, desire the rational milk without guile. » [n: (I Petr. II, 2.)] « And [1279B] they sit beside the fullest streams, » that is, beside the fullest writings of the ancient Fathers, wherein nothing but truth is taught, and simplicity is commended in all things. There follows:

13. *His cheeks are as beds of aromatical spices set by the perfumers.*

The use of the cheeks indeed is manifold; for these receive food, grind it, and pass it into the body. This also is the place of shamefastness, to which the blood comes forth, when a man is ashamed that he has said or done something less justly. By these are formed the words which teach Christ, and show beforehand that we ought to believe in him, and to follow him in heart and in work. But the « beds » which « by the perfumers, » namely, by those who discern the [1279C] powers of the perfumes, « are set, » are the Scriptures of the Prophets and of the doctors, which when we take up, when we pass them into our hearts, when we utter them to eager hearers, what are we made but the cheeks of the body of Christ, taking heed in all things to this, that we say or do nothing at which shamefastness should blush, and that we fulfil these things in faith and in work, whereby the care of the hearers toward Christ may grow, and their understanding. There follows:

*His lips are lilies dropping the first myrrh.*

Which is, These are rightly to be called his lips, who are as lilies dropping the first myrrh. The heavenly lips indeed rightly dropping to God the sweetness of odour, these are the hosts of Angels, which were created with the first hosts [1279D] by the supernal Power, which is God, and, when those sinned and willed to reign with power equal to God's, they *expreverunt* it, and, devoting themselves to God, by whom they were created, in all humility and obedience, they trampled under the feet of their mind pride and disobedience, which were the first sins. This indeed was the first myrrh, because that was the first mortification of sin; and all who in that manner persuade men to mortify sins are rightly called the lips of God, and lips persuading that which is dear to his heart. There follows:
