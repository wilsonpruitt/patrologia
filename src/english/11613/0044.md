## 5. (cont.)

[1280A]That by his hands works ought rightly to be understood, is manifest. For since nothing is understood to be more precious than gold, nothing dearer, rightly are his hands golden, that is, understood to be more precious than every work. For what works could be compared with these? He made the deaf to hear, the dumb to speak, he restored sight to the blind, he cured the paralytic, he cleansed the lepers, he raised the dead, he commanded the sea and the winds, he put the demons to flight, he raised also himself when already dead, he showed himself to his own after death, and thus, a manifest victor, ascended into heaven. Nothing is dearer than these, nothing more precious; and these indeed are rightly called turned as well. Among workings no turning is easier: for he who works by the lathe sits, and whatever unshaped matter there is, he so composes it by the lathe, so unfolds it, that what now [1280B] was rough and unshaped, this, being now smoothed, he renders into a most fitting form: to him a leper was offered, and by one word was cured; a dead man was offered, and, cured by one word, went away alive. Rightly therefore are his hands understood « turned, » that is, curing lightly, which so work all things by a word alone, that they are now perceived to be better than they had been. Whence also it is added, « full of hyacinths. » For the hyacinth is a stone whose colour is that of the sky when it is clear. Therefore, that thou mayest understand all these things to be of heaven, and mayest not doubt of the power of heaven upon earth, there is joined, « full of hyacinths, » that is, full of heavenly good works. With this agrees that which follows:

*His belly is of ivory, set with sapphires.*

The belly is that part of our body which more lightly [1280C] receives injury; whence here it signifies that nature of the Lord, the human, namely, wherein he could be hurt, wherein he could be made weak, wherein he could also be afflicted with the cross, as it was said of him, « Truly he hath borne our infirmities, and carried our sorrows [n: (Isa. LIII, 4)]. » Which belly nevertheless is here called « of ivory, » that the firmness of it, which was taken up out of the passion, might be shown; for ivory is most solid, most white, and, as time comes on, from white becomes ruddy. All which things indeed befit Christ; for his whiteness of chastity stood forth incomparable, to which none among the sons of men could be made equal, the Psalmist bearing witness, who says, « Fair in form above the sons of men [n: (Psal. XLIV, 3)]. » There is in it also a firmness which could not [1280D] be corrupted; whence, when his soul descended to the lower regions, it could not itself be hindered, because there was in it no sin whereby it might be held: his flesh also in the sepulchre after death suffered nothing, because on either side sin was absent, whereby both corruption and a charge against the soul might be added. Whence the Psalmist, « Thou wilt not leave my soul in hell; nor wilt thou give thy holy one to see corruption [n: (Psal. XV, 10)]. » But that this is made ruddy after a long time notes the martyrdom of the passion, since they who imitate him have after a long time delivered up their bodies to punishment, and have now deserved to live with him for ever. Which indeed is set with sapphires, that is, adorned [1281A] with the heavenly martyrdoms of these. There follows:

15. *His legs are pillars of marble, which are founded upon bases of gold.*

This is rightly fitted to the preachers, who run through the world, who lift up the name of Christ, being rightly upright and solid, and in all their preaching confirmed upon bases of gold, that is, upon the sayings of the prophets. As it begins from gold, so it is consummated in gold, that thou, prudent reader, mayest have what thou shouldst truly understand concerning Christ. There follows:

*His fairness is as of Libanus, chosen as the cedars.*

Libanus is a great wood, beginning near the Jordan, and stretching itself on high, in length also as far as the Eastern Ocean: its chief [1281B] trees are the cypress and the cedar, whose whitening leaves furnish the name of the wood, so that it is called Libanus; for Libanus is, « a whitening. » In it the dwellings are fair and delightful, so that if any come into them, he has no care of returning, nor is delighted with dwelling among men. This fairness is attributed to God, who is the whiteness of souls, and the delight of all the virtues, unto whom he that has come has no will of returning to human things, no remembrance in his soul of things past. Wherefore? because he himself is « chosen as the cedars. » For the cedars are trees of Libanus, raised upward in length, wonderfully and nobly tressed with foliage, incorruptible, odour-bearing, not fearing the gnawings of worms. He that has come to this glory, it is enough for him to be honoured [1281C] by this glory itself. There follows:

16. *His throat most sweet, and he wholly desirable: such is my Beloved, and he is my friend, ye daughters of Jerusalem.*

What is designated by this throat, unless the sending forth of the New Testament? For the Old went before, wherein it was said: « A man shall render soul for soul, eye for eye, tooth for tooth [n: (Exod. XXI, 24)], » and the rest which follow. [var: Vulg. *reddet animam pro anima*, without a subject "man"] But in the New what has been said? « If any man shall strike thee on the one cheek, offer him the other cheek: and to him that will contend with thee in judgment, and take away thy coat, let go to him thy cloak also. And whosoever shall force thee one mile, go with him other two. Give to him that asketh of thee [n: (Matth. V, 39, 40, 41)]. » [var: Vulg. *in dexteram maxillam tuam, praebe illi et alteram*, "on thy right cheek"] What is sweeter than this? what [1281D] can be more delightful? But if thou consider the whole New Testament, thou wilt find nothing at all but what is sweet and desirable. These things being said, there follows, « Such is my Beloved, and he is my friend, ye daughters of Jerusalem. » There follows:

17. *Whither is thy Beloved gone, O most beautiful of women? whither has thy Beloved turned aside, and we will seek him with thee?*

The proclamation of this Beloved being heard, wherein his Bride commends him through his several members, the daughters of Jerusalem also account him worthy of commendation, and esteem that it will be worthy that a message of love be presented to him. [1282A] Whence, lest they should run in uncertainty, they inquire of the Bride in this manner, « Whither is thy Beloved gone? » and because « to go away » can be understood as to withdraw far off (which indeed would seem a wonder, since she is this most beautiful of women), therefore, correcting themselves, they redouble it, « Whither has thy Beloved turned aside, » that is, whither has he departed for a time? This is perchance thy business, that he may perchance convert some and join them to thee, and cause thee to be enlarged and to be known there where thou art not yet. And when we have learned this, we will seek him with thee, and will take care to offer him the gift of thy love. There follows:
