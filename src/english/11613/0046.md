## CHAPTER VI. (cont.)

For, the wonders having been run through which came to pass at the birth of Christ, and the miracles being finished which bore witness to Christ preaching, and the signs being seen which appeared in his death and after his death, the Church powerfully arose: in the first fathers, the dawn; in the second, as the moon; but now, refreshed with full light, she appeared as the sun, chosen, radiant with full light, and furnishing light to all by her own brightness; wherein it is said, « Chosen as the sun. » For of all the creatures which were bodily created by God, none has been chosen for this, that it should be the eye of the world, and furnish light and illumination to the world on every side, save the substance of the sun alone, which alone [1286A] goes about us, warms us, and increases and fortifies us with benefits. In this manner also has this Bride been chosen, that she might furnish spiritual light to the world, and, the darkness being taken away wherein the world was wrapped, might bestow and confer upon it the light of faith through the apostles, and the holy martyrs, and all the doctors of the faith. Who, when she shall come together whole after the resurrection, will appear terrible as an ordered line of battle of the camp. For when the Lord sits in that day, and pronounces the sentence of judgment: « Come, ye blessed of my Father; possess the kingdom which has been prepared for you from the origin of the world [n: (Matth. XXV, 34)], » [var: Vulg. *possidete paratum vobis regnum a constitutione mundi*, "from the foundation of the world"] so terrible and admirable will she appear, that she gives no entrance to the enemy that approaches her; but, like a line of battle ordered by the hand of the Lord, keeps her own to herself, and by the power of her Lord drives off all strangers. [1286B] Yet blessed Gregory says upon this, « The dawn announces that the night has perished, nor yet does it display the brightness of the day; but while it drives away the one, it takes up the other, and holds a light mingled with darkness. » Therefore he who in this life follows the truth is the dawn, because he does certain things which are of the light, yet is not without the leavings of darkness. When therefore the law of sin contends with the law of the mind, it is still the dawn, because the light which has shone forth has not yet wholly pressed down the darkness, and because the light of the flesh [cj: *lux carnis*; read *lex carnis*, "the law of the flesh"] strikes the law of the mind, and on the contrary light and shadow strive with one another by turns: it will be day when the shadow of sin shall not remain, when the inward light shall have shone perfectly. Therefore the Church whom the Bridegroom admires is deservedly compared to the dawn, because, forsaking the [1286C] darkness of sin, she breaks forth into the light of eternity, and by the knowledge of faith is changed into the light of justice, as the dawn is turned from darkness into light; faith is light, unbelief is night. Therefore the Church, seeking eternal things, glows with the supernal brightness and with the light of justice, and rises as the dawn, because, having left behind the depravity of the world, she turns herself into the splendour of a new light. There follows:

10. *I went down into the garden of nuts, to see the apples of the valleys, and to look whether the vineyard had flourished, and the pomegranates had budded.*

The Bridegroom, having above addressed the whole Church, now turns to that part of her which is here, which is subject to temptation; which, while the rain descends, the floods come, the winds arise and beat upon her house, [1286D] needs help so much the stronger as it is shaken by the more frequent whirlwind. Coming down therefore to her, he says, « I went down into the garden of nuts, » and so forth. But a garden (as has been said) is a place set apart, planted with health-bearing herbs and wholesome trees, which after labours it is sweet to visit and to be refreshed in spirit. Which place indeed designates the Church that is set here, and how she is set apart Isaias designates, saying, « As the heavens are exalted above the earth, so are my ways exalted above your ways, and my thoughts above your thoughts [n: (Isa. LV, 9)]. » By these ways and thoughts he designates the thoughts and works of the Church, which is as far removed from every intention of the wicked as the heaven is distant from the earth. But this garden is planted [1287A] with trees of nuts; which indeed are diverse. For one nut is the hazel, another the filbert, another the chestnut, another the almond, another the pine-nut, another the Libyan, which is called the nutmeg, another the Gallic, which in the common tongue is wont to be called simply the nut. What do these nuts signify? The hearts, assuredly, of the saints, which indeed within display the sweetness of the kernel, that is, the profit of the spirit; but without they show the profit of the flesh, which is so hard that of it it was said, « We have this treasure hidden in earthen vessels [n: (II Cor. IV, 7)]. » [var: Vulg. *habemus autem thesaurum istum*, without "hidden"] And again of it it was said, « I know that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor any other creature shall be able to separate us from the charity of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord [n: (Rom. VIII, 38)]. » [var: Vulg. *Certus sum enim quia*, "I am sure that"] To [1287B] this garden of nuts therefore he goes down, that he may strengthen their kernels within, and subject the bodies of the same without to suffering, laying up for it the rewards of life, which shall be lacking to none that dies for him. He goes down also that he may see the apples of the valleys; for that garden is not planted with nuts alone, but rather with trees which bear apples. For all fruits which are covered with a soft rind are called apples, and these signify the confessors, who, burning within with the love of God, and confessing him without, receive their reward from God in eternal blessedness, although they are not made equal to the martyrs. To these therefore he goes down, who follow after him in the sublimity of martyrdom, and to these who, abiding in the valley of humility, are likewise not slow to follow after him by death: but the Lord spares them, [1287C] willing that they receive eternal blessedness by their will alone, and not also by a death of their own procuring. He goes down also to the newly planted vineyards, that he may look whether they flourish, whether they give of themselves the hope of fruit, whether they persevere in faith, and, an example of approaching, of believing, and of persevering being set before others, enlarge his name: which same thing he desires concerning the pomegranates, namely, that they bud, and by their budding afford to others examples of bravely undergoing suffering for Christ, and pledge the rewards of that recompense which they look for. There follows:

11. *I knew not: my soul troubled me because of the chariots of Aminadab.*

The Lord in the foregoing words admonished [1287D] his Bride, made known to her what she should seek, and set himself before her as the end of her course; for in vain do we run, if we do not lay hold, if we do not find him whom we seek. But hard things have been set before her, at which the flesh is afraid, at which it trembles; but for these the spirit is strengthened and girded by the gift of God, accounting light all that it suffers, while it glories in the gift of its own recompense. Which indeed the Synagogue hearing, stands, is amazed, and trembles, and, returning at length to herself, says, « I knew not, » that is, I was ignorant of so great grace and of the gifts of the spirit conferred upon thee; and therefore, « my soul troubled me, » because I wandered astray in darkness, « because of the chariots of Aminadab. » This Aminadab is read to have been the great-great-grandson of Juda [n: (I Par. II, 10)], who by the interpretation of his own name signifies Christ; for Aminadab [1288A] is interpreted, « The willing one of my people; » for he himself stood forth as the head of the whole people, at whose birth the Angel said to the shepherds: « Fear not; for behold I bring you good tidings of great joy, that shall be to all the people: for this day is born to you a Saviour, who is Christ the Lord [n: (Luc. II, 10, 11)]. » But he stood forth as the willing one of that people; for willingly was he born, willingly shown forth by angels and by great powers, willingly adorned with miracles, willingly betrayed, willingly given unto death; and since he bore all these things willingly, rightly is he named the willing one both of the people and of him. Whose chariots indeed are the four Gospels, which are drawn through the world, while they are preached to the peoples, and are believed, and are received as worthy of all veneration. Beholding which [1288B] the Synagogue is troubled, despairing that that can be false which is thus received in the heart by all, is magnificently honoured, and is crushed out neither by exquisite torments nor by any kind of death. But Saint Ambrose notes two chariots. For anger, cupidity, pleasure, and fear he makes the wheels of one chariot; but to the other he assigns prudence, temperance, fortitude, and justice: in these wheels there is then the spirit of life, when a good driver guides them, restraining these, admonishing these, recalling these, urging these on. These horses indeed are subject to the yoke of faith, bound fast with the bond of charity, with the bridles of justice, with the reins of sobriety. There follows:

12. *Return, return, O Sulamitess: return, return, that we may behold thee.*

[1288C]The Synagogue confessing her slowness, the Church answers by way of consolation, « Return, » and so forth. It is said to the Sulamitess that she return, because at the end of the world faith will be offered by the Church to the Synagogue, which was held captive under unbelief. And she is admonished four times to return, because the Jews, scattered through the four parts of the world, will be gathered together at the end; whence it is said to her, « Return, return, O Sulamitess, » thou who wast held captive or despised under the prophets, « return, return, » that in that liberty wherein we are we may « behold thee. » There follows:
