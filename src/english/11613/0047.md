## CHAPTER VII.

1. *What shalt thou see in the Sulamitess, but the companies of the camps?*

This is the voice of the Bridegroom. Behold, the Church admonishing the Synagogue, [1288D] the Bridegroom, rejoicing, promises that he will give the effect, saying, « What shalt thou see in the Sulamitess, but the companies of the camps? » For the companies of the camps are they who, set as it were in camps in the array of the virtues, fight against the devil, and, God fortifying them and bringing help by his own power, overcome. For then shall the camps of them that war be seen in the Sulamitess, when she shall do battle for the faith which now she assails, and, when Henoch and Elias preach the same, asserting it to be true and to be that wherein one must be saved, she shall bear witness to it by her own death. There follows:

2. *How beautiful are thy steps in shoes, O daughter of the Prince!*

Behold, the Bridegroom praises the Bride, beginning from her steps: whence also, commending her, he says: « How [1289A] beautiful are thy steps in shoes, O daughter of the Prince! » Her indeed, who in her first fathers stood forth as the « daughter of the Prince, » that is, of God who has princedom over all things, he commends with a twelvefold praise, beginning from her steps, advancing through the middle parts, and consummating her praise with the fruitfulness of her breasts; and so he says, O thou who now in thy conversation art worthy to be called the daughter of the Prince, who also in thy first fathers didst worthily deserve to be called his daughter, « how beautiful » now « are thy steps! » For now thou drawest near to God by those same steps by which the apostles drew near; confessing that the Son of God was sent by the Father into flesh, and that he in that same flesh underwent death for the salvation of all, and after the third day rose again, and thus, the victory being won, ascended into heaven, thou standest for him, thou bearest [1289B] witness to him, thou dost not refuse to die for him. Exceedingly beautiful therefore are thy steps, wherewith thou walkest unto God; and this, « in shoes. » For shoes are made of the hides of dead animals; whence by these are understood the examples of the saints: of the Fathers, namely, of the apostles, and of all the martyrs, because, in confessing Christ, they endured that their hides be drawn off them, and their bodies be withdrawn from their souls. To these therefore thou art made equal, to these thou art made a peer, who, having received a sure faith, dost fight for it, and dost labour to be crowned with the glory of the apostles by contending for it. There follows:

*The joints of thy thighs are as* monilia, *which are wrought by the hand of a craftsman.*

[1289C]The thighs, whether called *femina* or *femora*, wherewith we walk and frame our goings forth, are these two, namely the Old Testament and the New: the joining of these is the concord of the Testaments, whereof he says, « The joining of thy thighs is as *monilia*. » *Monilia* indeed are so called from *monere*, to admonish, in that, when they are hung upon the neck and set upon the breast, they admonish her that wears them not to let a stranger's hand come to her bosom, but to keep her faith undefiled to him to whom she is betrothed. They admonish any man also, that where he sees these hung, he be not drawn thither by love, nor by any chance be carried thither by love, lest perchance, being taken in this, he be held guilty of adultery. Whence the two Testaments are deservedly named *monilia*, which admonish us to fear God, and to venerate him, and to love him with the whole [1289D] heart, the whole soul, and all our strength, and to meditate upon him with a firm mind. They admonish also that we love our neighbours, and contrive not nor do to them anything which ought rightly to displease. In these the two Testaments are, and are in concord, which also so strengthen faith as it truly is; and they confirm it to be even as we believe. These also « are wrought by the hand of a craftsman, » that is, through the Son, who is the hand of the Father, and the highest craftsman of all, they were declared to men and written down. There follows:

3. *Thy navel is a turned bowl, never wanting cups.*

The navel indeed is in the middle of the belly, having a certain [1290A] part of it beneath itself, and a certain part above. But this navel is the infirmity of our mortality, one part whereof is below, and the other above. That part is below whereof it is said: « All flesh is grass, and all the glory thereof as the flower of the field. The grass is dried up, and the flower is fallen [n: (Isa. XL, 6, 7)]. » Whereof David says: « In the morning let him pass away as grass; in the morning let him flourish, and pass away: in the evening let him fall, grow hard, and wither [n: (Psal. LXXXIX, 6)]. » The morning also of our infancy, of our childhood, of our youth passes away; the flower of our young manhood passes away; the evening also of our old age passes away: wherein indeed a man falls down in death, grows hard in the corpse, withers in the dust. But that part is above whereof blessed Paul makes mention, saying: « It is sown in [1290B] corruption, it shall rise in incorruption: it is sown in dishonour, it shall rise in glory: it is sown in weakness, it shall rise in power: it is sown a natural body, it shall rise spiritual [n: (I Cor. XV, 42, 43)]. » This navel therefore is as a turned bowl: for that working is lightest which is done by the lathe; and lightest is the working of God, which shall be wrought in the resurrection of all. For as an angel most lightly lifted up Habacuc, and set him in Babylon; so, carrying food to Daniel, he set him back again in Judea [n: (Dan. XIV, 35)]: so shall it be in the resurrection, when at the voice of the angel, at the sound of the trumpet blowing, the dead shall rise again forthwith incorrupt, who indeed were worthy of corruption. This therefore is that part of our infirmity which shall be lightly wrought by the lathe of the divine working, which is called a turned bowl, which [1290C] neither here wants cups, nor in the time to come shall in any measure want them. Here therefore their glory is said to be about to be great, which shall then also be seen bodily, and shall appear. There follows:

*Thy belly is as a heap of wheat, set about with lilies.*

The belly is that part of the body which receives whatever is put into it; and whatever in it can work together for its substance, that it retains, and therewith cherishes the body, increases and advances it, but drives off and spits out the rest. Whence by this is understood the holy memory of this synagogue, which comprehends all things, and these only which are holy, and which work together unto eternal life, and direct the steps thither, these she loves, these she keeps, and in these she goes forward: the rest, as vain and unprofitable, she spits out and [1290D] sends away. Whence also it is said: « Thy belly is as a heap of wheat. » For in a heap of wheat some things work together unto life, and some are otherwise: but of those which work together unto life it is subjoined, that this heap « is set about with lilies, » that is, is trained by the examples of the Fathers, and is taught by the instructions of the heavenly spirits, who are truly lilies. There follows:

4. *Thy two breasts, as two twin fawns of a roe. 5. Thy neck as a tower of ivory.*

This is understood of the breasts of the converted synagogue, as it was said above also of the breasts of the Church; as also that which follows concerning the neck, « Thy neck as a tower of ivory: » yet here is added, « of ivory, » [1291A] that both the brightness of their preaching may be shown, and the firmness wherewith they truly preach Christ, and confirm this by their own death. There follows:

*Thy eyes as the fishpools in Hesebon, which are in the gate of the daughter of the multitude.*

The neck being shown, we come to the eyes; of which it is here said, that they are « as the fishpools in Hesebon. » But Hesebon is interpreted, « a girdle of mourning. » But the « eyes, » that is, they who contemplate the secret things of faith, and show them to the synagogue now converted, to be seen and to be believed, these are « as the fishpools in Hesebon: » for Hesebon, whether it be a fortress or a city, had at its very entrance fishpools, wherein the bodies of those entering could be washed and cleansed, so that trust might thus be had in those who had gone in, [1291B] that they would contrive no evil which should be against the place, or in any measure hurtful to it. So also these, who prepare and show the ways of life to this converted synagogue, provide beforehand and show the way of life to others who are to be converted to her, and that they enter not feignedly, that they receive not Baptism in vain, nor contrive in guile to set themselves against those in whom Christ now reigns, and now holds his own princedom. This therefore is that which is said, « which are in the gate of the daughter of the multitude, » that is, at the entrance of the fortress or the city, where there is a multitude of nations, to whom the word of God is now preached, and they receive it unto ages of ages. For that Simon Magus did this feignedly, feignedly received Baptism, feignedly came to know the sacraments of faith, with all which he afterward sharply [1291C] assailed Peter, the doctor of the Church appointed by Christ. Whence, lest a like assault disquiet her in her conversion, when it shall be given, it is said to her that she shall have eyes providing beforehand for herself, as at the gates of Hesebon provision was made beforehand for the city. But this multitude is rightly called « a girdle of mourning, » because on every side it is girded with mourning. On this side she mourns that she has sinned against God; on that side, lest she sin, she likewise has tears of fear; on this side she complains that he whom she loves is lacking to her, and that the rewards which she seeks and desires are lacking to her. Girded therefore with this mourning, she is rightly called Hesebon, who, surrounded with these and with other tears which compunction gives, rightly makes her complaint. There follows:

*Thy nose is as the tower of Libanus, which looks toward Damascus.*

[1291D]To the nose are assigned those of the Church who perceive near at hand the fragrance of the virtues in the Church, and who know the stench of the vices in her by discerning it from afar. These stand as a tower in Libanus, which designates the whiteness of the Church, who in the Church of God receive before others the name and office of prelacy, and who look toward Damascus. For Damascus is the mother city of Syria, where Cain is read to have slain Abel; whence many evils were brought upon Israel, where unbelief reigns, and sin rages exceedingly. Against this the Church of God looks earnestly, attentively considering whether there be any whom she may snatch away for herself, and, when he has been snatched from the devil, may be able to turn to Christ. There follows:

5. *Thy head as Carmel: and the hairs of thy head as the purple of the King bound in the channels.*
