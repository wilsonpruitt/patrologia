## CHAPTER VII. (cont.)

[1292A]Carmel is a mountain, going up into which Elias obtained that the rain, shut up for seven years, should be given him abundantly by God, signifying assuredly Christ, whom confessing, and asserting the things which are said of him to be true, we pray to the Father that he send forth upon us the gifts of his graces, whereby he may make us pleasing to his will. But this Carmel is interpreted « a mountain, » or, « the knowledge of circumcision; » which rightly befits Christ, for he himself stood forth as the beginning and the end of circumcision. For he himself enjoined this upon Abraham (who saw his day and was glad) and upon his house, and by the austere cutting of this member forbade the intercourse unlawful to him, and all other dishonourable and superfluous things of every member. He also took this upon himself, and foretold that it was consummated [1292B] in himself: for the shedding of his blood, as well upon the cross as in this circumcision, is a sufficient correction for the people of grace, that they abstain from vices, and correct and amend their depraved motions, not by that austerity of the sacrament, but by the receiving of faith alone and of holy baptism. But the hairs of this head are they of whom it is subjoined: « The hairs of thy head as the purple of the King bound in the channels. » The hairs of the head indeed are those which adorn the head, make the face comely, and, ever cleaving to the head, by the great fairness of themselves render it honourable to whomsoever meets it, and most worthy of all veneration. By these indeed we understand those who, cleaving with the whole heart to Christ the highest King in an indissoluble bond of love, could never be divided from him, neither by princedom, nor by any power that wrought it; [1292C] but through all the torments that could be devised deserved truly to cleave to him, and to be called inseparable hairs. And the hairs of the head indeed are as the purple of the King bound in the channels. The channels indeed are the sendings forth of the graces of God, which in many manners he [sends] into his own whom he chooses, and he himself both makes them worthy of the grace of martyrdom and takes them up. And so these hairs are in these channels, and are indeed joined to them, not knowing how to be sundered from them, but rather, some heavenly liquor being received in these, they become ruddy as the purple of the King bound in the channels, conformed indeed to Christ who suffered upon the cross, and now conformed to him in glory. There follows:

6. *How beautiful art thou, and how comely, dearest, [1292D] in delights!*

After the commendation of those things which have been said, he subjoins the praise of the synagogue, which has now been made the Church, saying, Since the things which have now been said stand thus, therefore thou art exceedingly « beautiful » in the work of virtue, and « comely » in the contemplation of the same; dear to us through thy conversion, dearer through the carrying out of virtue, « dearest » to God at thy arrival; whence he subjoins, « in delights, » that is, in those things which have been said, which God declares to be delights, and in these, as in delights, he takes his pleasure. There follows:

7. *Thy stature is likened to a palm tree, and thy breasts to clusters.*

The commendation of her began from her steps; the joining of her thighs also, her navel, and her belly have been commended; [1293A] her breasts, and her neck, her nose, and her eyes, together with her head and her hairs, have been described. Lest this should seem ill-sounding, and out of tune with her stature as too much drawn together into itself, he adds concerning her stature, saying that it is likened to a palm tree, stretched upward, namely, narrow and rough below, spread out and widened toward heaven with its arms, bearing a most sweet fruit, which, joined to the clusters which are the fruit of the vine, renders the supper rich, and the supper itself most delightful by the fruit of the vine. There follows:

8. *I said: I will go up into the palm tree, and will take hold of the fruits thereof: and thy breasts shall be as the clusters of the vine, and the odour of thy mouth as of apples.*

This indeed the Bridegroom says, that he himself will go up into the palm tree; which indeed we believe to have been fulfilled, [1293B] when he went up upon the wood of the cross. To which indeed this also was granted, that as he himself, by going up upon it, stood forth victor over all things, so it also justly obtained princedom over all the trees of the woods, and deserved rightly to be called a palm; whence also it is sung to it: above all the trees of the woods, thou alone wast worthy to bear the King of the ages and the Lord. « And I will take hold of the fruits thereof. » This was the fruit of this palm: immortality, impassibility, and eternal delight also: these fruits of it Christ received upon the cross. For because he humbled himself, being made obedient upon the cross to the Father unto death, even the death of the cross, the Father exalted him, that in his name every knee should bow, of those in heaven, on earth, and under the earth, [1293C] and every tongue confess that he himself is in the glory of the Father, whereunto none comes save through him. « And thy breasts shall be as the clusters of the vine. » The breasts are those wherewith the infancy of little ones is fed and nourished; and these are the sayings of the prophets, whereby long before those times the passion of Christ was prophesied, and announced by many proclamations of the same. Which breasts indeed are now made the clusters of the vine, now showing the very fruit which they prophesied, and only then, when Christ suffered upon the cross, showing forth the effect which they sounded out. « And the odour of thy mouth as of apples. » For the odour of the cross, before Christ ascended it, was horrible; but after Christ the giver of life ascended, all its odours were made most sweet, and, as the odours of apples, most pleasing; [1293D] whence Paul also says: « We are the good odour of Christ unto God in them that are saved, and in them that perish [n: (II Cor. II, 15)]. » There follows:

9. *Thy throat as the best wine, worthy for my Beloved to drink, and for his lips and his teeth to chew upon. I to my Beloved, and his turning is toward me.*

The Bridegroom has now long commended her as worthy of praise, beginning from the lowest and advancing to the highest; which the Bride, holding it acceptable, answers: « Thy throat as the best wine, worthy for my Beloved to drink, and for his lips and his teeth to chew upon. » And so she shows that the praise which the Bridegroom himself has sounded out concerning her is such as it is worthy that it be drunk by the Bridegroom himself, and, passed over into his body, [1294A] be held in memory; and this is that which is said, « for the lips and the teeth to chew upon. » For in the law [n: (Levit. XI, 3)], an animal which does not chew the cud is held unclean, and that which chews the cud is beyond doubt accounted clean: for he who calls back to memory the good things which he hears or reads, and declares them by chewing them with the teeth, or by uttering them with the lips, is affirmed to be a clean animal. Whence also, affirming that this cleanness is in the Bridegroom, she exhorts him to call this praise back to memory, and to account and adjudge her worthy of this praise. And that this shall indeed come to pass she declares, saying, « I to my beloved, and his turning is toward me; » as if she should say, I am worthy of the memory of my Beloved, and he most worthy to be held in memory by me: for I love the Father, I venerate and adore his [1294B] only-begotten Son, I judge the Holy Spirit equal to these in all things. I understand the works of the Father which he wrought for my redeeming; in these I acknowledge the obedience of the Son, and through his Spirit coming into me I receive him turned toward me, and his gifts. And so she says, I am worthy of my Beloved and dear to him: for he emptied himself for me, making himself a child and a little one; to whom the Church sings that saying of Isaias: « A little one is born to us, and a Son is given to us [n: (Isai. IX, 6)]. » He emptied himself also, being made obedient to the Father unto death; and what death? that which was of the cross, which was accounted most unworthy, whereby heaven was denied to a man, the earth was withdrawn from him, the remedy of water was not afforded, [1294C] and, being dead, he was committed in the air to be torn by the birds:

*By these deservedly dear, by these merits to be held dear.*

« His turning » also is « toward me: » for what kind of death should I shudder at, when I understand that the Son of God suffered such things for me? None indeed. For in Stephen I endured the stone, in James the sword, at Rome in blessed Peter the cross, in his partner blessed Paul the troubles of the sword; the boiling oil in John, in Andrew the bitter things of the cross, I bore; and in the apostles, and in all the martyrs far and wide throughout the world, I presented the torments that could be devised. There follows:

10. *Come, my Beloved, let us go forth into the field.*

The charity being shown wherewith the Church is united to Christ, [1294D] she confidently calls upon him, desiring that he himself also come to her who loves him, and behold her loving him, and look through whether the things which have been said of him are true. And so she says, « Come, my Beloved, » whom I love even unto death, who at thy departing didst promise to the Church, saying: « Behold I am with you even to the consummation of the world. » [n: (Matth. XXVIII, 20.)] « Come, let us go forth into the field. » A plain is a place lying open far and wide, containing woods and places lacking woods, on this side beset with thorns, on that showing places not thorny yet untilled, designating this world, such as it was when Adam was cast over into it after his sin, and the sentence was spoken to him, « Because thou hast eaten of the tree whereof I had commanded thee that thou shouldst not eat, cursed is the earth in thy work: in labours shalt thou eat of it all the [1295A] days of thy life. Thorns and thistles shall it bring forth to thee, and thou shalt eat the herb of the earth [n: (Gen. III, 17)]. » [var: Vulg. *quia audisti vocem uxoris tuae*, "because thou hast hearkened to the voice of thy wife"] But this was called a field, after that, being assailed by the ploughshare and the coulter of the earth, it laid aside this horrible face, answered to husbandry, and gladdened its husbandmen with good fruits. And it is called *ager* from this, that it began to be driven, *agi*, by the ploughshare, and to be cast down by the coulter, and (as is the custom of husbandmen) so to be turned over in the same place, that it may be fit for receiving the seed, and be made fruitful for the propagating of the same. To this field therefore the Bride invites her Bridegroom, saying, « Come, » that is, consent to this, that I may have access to thee. « Let us go forth: » Do thou go forth, the flesh being assumed, out of the unspeakable hidden place of thy majesty, that is, appear manifestly; and I, having gone forth wholly with thee out of my hidden places, out of my uncleannesses, out of unbelief, [1295B] believing thee with the heart, confessing thee, humbly adoring thee, cleaving to thee inseparably, am ready to come with thee into this field, that we may consider whether it be well tilled, whether the seeds of the word are to be entrusted to it, and whether any hope of good is to be looked for from it. There follows:

*Let us abide in the villages.*

Dwellings indeed are made in fields, as was said of the plain of Sennaar, wherein the city of Babylon was built by the sons of Noe [n: (Gen. XI, 2)]; towns are made; villages also are made: in the city the rich dwell, in the towns the powerful; but in the villages is the abode of the lesser and the more humble, which is so much the more pleasing to God as it is more humble, and so much the more acceptable as it is the more capable of virtue and of doctrine. Here she desires that the Bridegroom make his stay, [1295C] and that she abide with the Bridegroom, where there is the consecration of his holy body and blood, where the washing away of sins is made in baptism, where there is the daily confession of offences, and the frequent oblation of the faithful peoples is made to the Lord: hence the frequent bestowal of tithes, hence of other gifts also, and at their going forth the holy acknowledgment of the Lord, and their passing over to eternal blessedness. There follows:

11. *In the morning let us rise up to the vineyards: let us see if the vineyard has flourished, if the flowers bring forth fruits.*
