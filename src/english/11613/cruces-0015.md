### A1. *dilectus* / *Dilectus* — capitalization reproduced, never normalized

Migne's own capitalization is carried through occurrence by occurrence: capitalized
*Dilectus* = the Bridegroom, rendered "Beloved"; lower-case *dilectus* rendered
"beloved". The sites in 0019, in order:

- 1203B *Dilecto suo* → "her Beloved"
- 1203B lemma 12 *dilectus meus mihi* (lower case) → "my beloved to me"
- 1203C *Dilectus meus* → "My Beloved"
- 1203D *dilecti sui* → "her beloved"
- 1204B « *Dilectus meus mihi* » → "My Beloved to me"
- 1204C *Dilectus* → "the Beloved"
- 1205A *Dilectus suus* ×2 → "her Beloved"
- 1205A « *Dilectus meus mihi inter ubera mea commorabitur* » → "My Beloved to me…"
- 1205B lemma 13 *Botrus cypri **Dilectus** meus mihi* → "my Beloved"
- 1205C « *mihi **dilectus** meus commorabitur* » (lower case, and the word order changed
  from the lemma) → "my beloved shall abide with me"
- 1205C « *Botrus cypri **dilectus** meus mihi.* » (lower case, against the capitalized
  lemma eight lines above) → "my beloved"
- 1205D, 1206B *Dilectus suus* / « *Botrus [1206B] cypri **Dilectus** meus mihi.* » →
  "her Beloved" / "my Beloved"

Foliot's own participial *dilecta* (1194B *sic dilecta*, 1195A *Sponsa dilecta*) is the
ordinary word and is rendered "beloved" without promotion.

### A2. Fixed vocabulary used in this range

*sponsa* = Bride · *sponsus* = Bridegroom · **amica = "friend"** (a third word, never
"bride", never "beloved"). "Friend" was chosen over the conventional "my love"
because Foliot's gloss at 1196A is built on the etymological tie: *illam **verbo
amicabili** convenit … dicens, « Amica mea »* — "he accosts her with a word of
friendship … saying, « My friend »" — and *amicus Caesaris* at 1202C ("Caesar's
friend") sits in the same range. ⚠ **Merge-time reconciliation item**: 26 instances of
*amica* run through the whole work; if another agent's range fixed a different English,
one of the two must yield, and this is the reason for the choice here.

Also fixed: *equitatus* = "company of horse" · *monile* = "necklace" ·
*murenulae* = kept in italic Latin (the gloss IS the etymology, *murena* the fish —
the 10804 precedent) · *accubitus* = "repose" · *cubile/cubare* = "couch" / "lie
down" · *tabernaculum* = "tabernacle" throughout (Abraham's at 1193A and the
shepherds' at 1195B are the same word and take the same English) · *nardus* =
"spikenard" · *fasciculus* = "bundle" · *botrus* = "cluster".

**mala/malus** in this range: every instance is *malus, -a, -um*, **evil**, never the
Song's apple — 1194D *aut certe malum* ("or else the evil"), 1196C *in malorum
cordibus* ("in the hearts of the wicked"), 1199A *concupiscentia mala* ("evil
concupiscence"), 1203C *in mala trahenti … pessima suadenti* ("drawing into evils …
urging the worst things"), 1205D *modis aliis pessime excogitatis* ("most wickedly
devised"). Checked one by one from the syntax.

## B0. *Dum* → *Cum* at 1199D–1200A — an INTERNAL divergence, and it takes NO marker

**This is the coordinator's case 2, and the site decides it.** Migne's lemma at 1199D
prints Cant. I, 11 *…**Dum** esset Rex in accubitu suo…*, **which is what the Vulgate
has.** The gloss then re-quotes it four different ways, and the first three change the
word:

| col | printed | rendered |
|---|---|---|
| 1199D | lemma: ***Dum** esset Rex in accubitu suo* | "**While** the King was in his repose" |
| 1200A | « ***Cum** esset Rex in accubitu suo.* » | "**When** the King was in his repose" |
| 1200C | « ***Cum** esset in accubitu suo,* » (*Rex* dropped) | "**When** he was in his repose" |
| 1200C | « *esset **in suo accubitu**,* » (word order inverted) | "was in his repose" |
| 1200C | *dum rex in accubitu suo esset* (unguilleted, lower-case *rex*) | "while the king was in his repose" |

Each is rendered as printed and none is harmonized to any other. **What was withdrawn is
only the `[var:]` marker that stood at 1200A**; a draft of this range had it reading
`[var: the lemma above and Vulg. *Dum*, "While"]`, and `verify-english.mjs` rejected it
for opening on an internal comparison instead of a named external witness.

**Withdrawn rather than re-opened with `Vulg.`, for two reasons, and the second is the
one that matters:**

1. **The comparison is internal.** The Vulgate agrees with one of the two forms — the
   lemma's *Dum* — so what the marker was actually recording is Migne diverging from
   himself between 1199D and 1200A, which is precisely what the apparatus has no
   vocabulary for.
2. **Re-opening it with `Vulg.` would have made it validate while asserting a
   materiality it does not have.** *dum* and *cum* with the imperfect subjunctive are
   near-synonymous here, so the gloss's divergence from the received text fails Pattern
   14's own bar ("differs from the received text **in a way that changes the sense**").
   The marker would have passed the validator and told the reader something untrue about
   the weight of the variation. **A marker that validates is not thereby earned.**

So: rendered as printed at all five sites, logged here, no marker. The four-way
re-quotation is still the most interesting thing on the column and a reader meets it in
the English itself, where the four forms differ visibly.

### B0a. Evidence for the proposed rule change (overlap length, not self-quotation)

The coordinator reports this is the session's third sighting, and that the peer on
0040–0043 found the blocker to be **the length of the overlap with the Latin twin**
rather than self-quotation as such — its @1270B marker survived because Migne's
re-quotation stopped short of the full received phrase.

**This case is evidence for that diagnosis, and it also bounds it.** My content
(`*Dum*`, one word) is a maximal-overlap case: a single word that sits in the Latin twin
in both the lemma and, as *dum*, at 1200C. Under an overlap-length rule it fails for the
same mechanical reason the peer's passed — nothing about self-quotation per se. **But
the rule change would not have rescued this marker on the merits**, because it fails
Pattern 14's sense-change bar independently of any validator grammar. Two distinct gaps
are in play and should not be conflated:

- **The apparatus gap** (real, and the proposed rule addresses it): there is no marker
  for *Migne diverging from Migne*, so an internal divergence has nowhere to live but a
  cruces file no reader opens. That is the same argument that founded Pattern 14 itself.
- **The materiality bar** (unchanged, and it disposes of this site): whatever vocabulary
  exists, a near-synonymous conjunction should not be marked.

If a `[req:]`-style internal-divergence marker is ever added, **1194B's *pulcherrima* →
*pulchra* (§B item 1) is the site in this range that would earn one**, not this one:
there the superlative is lost and the gloss is built on the weakened form. It currently
ships as a `[var:]` because the gloss's *pulchra* also diverges from the Vulgate
externally and materially, which is a genuinely different fact from this site's.

⚠ **The 1194B marker's content was tightened in the same pass** — it read
`[var: Vulg. and the lemma above *pulcherrima*, …]` and now reads
`[var: Vulg. *pulcherrima*, "most beautiful"]`. It validated either way; the change is
for consistency with the ruling above, so that no shipped `[var:]` in this range carries
an internal comparison inside its content. The internal observation is preserved in §B
item 1, which is where it belongs.

## B. Scripture divergences — markers fired

1. **1194B, `[var:]` — the lemma's *pulcherrima* becomes *pulchra* in every
   re-quotation.** Migne's lemma line at 1194A prints Cant. I, 7 *o **pulcherrima**
   inter mulieres* with the Vulgate. **All four re-quotations in the gloss print
   *pulchra*** — 1194B « *Si ignoras te, o pulchra inter mulieres, egredere* », 1194B
   « *O pulchra inter mulieres!* », 1194C « *O pulchra inter mulieres, si ignoras te* »,
   1195D « *Si ignoras te, o pulchra, egredere* ». The English follows: "most beautiful"
   in the lemma, "beautiful one" in all four glosses, with one `[var:]` at the first
   gloss occurrence. **Not harmonized in either direction** — this is the divergence
   this range's brief named, and it is the largest in the range.

2. **1196B, `[var:]` — Isa. XIV, 14 quoted as *Ponam sedem meam ad Aquilonem, et ero
   similis Altissimo*.** The Vulgate at 13–14 has *super astra Dei exaltabo solium
   meum, sedebo in monte testamenti, in lateribus aquilonis … ero similis Altissimo*.
   Migne's line compresses two verses into one and replaces *exaltabo solium meum* /
   *in lateribus aquilonis* with *ponam sedem meam ad Aquilonem*. Rendered as printed;
   marker records the received reading. No speculation offered as to cause.

3. **1197B, `[var:]` — Matt. XXII, 30 printed *sed **sunt** sicut angeli Dei in
   coelo*.** The Vulgate has *sed **erunt***. Present for future, and inside an
   argument about what the Bride is being made like *now* in predestination
   (*jam similem te feci* at 1197D), so the tense is not idle. Rendered "they are",
   marker gives *erunt*.

4. **1197C, `[var:]` — the sword-word is John's, not Matthew's.** Migne prints
   « *Mitte gladium in vaginam. An putas, quod non possum rogare Patrem meum…* » under
   a note reading `(Matth. XXVI, 52, 53.)`. Matthew has *converte gladium tuum in locum
   suum*; *mitte gladium tuum in vaginam* is Joan. XVIII, 11. Migne's own note is
   reproduced verbatim (notes are his); the marker records that the wording printed is
   John's. Also *quod* for Matthew's *quia*, and *modo* dropped before *plus quam* —
   both immaterial, noted here and not marked.

5. **1200B, `[var:]` — Joan. I, 17 printed *per Christum, gratia et veritas **donata**
   est*.** Vulgate: *gratia et veritas per Jesum Christum **facta** est*. Given vs.
   made, and *Jesum* dropped. The whole paragraph is about what the Father *disposed*
   and grace *completed*, so "given" is doing work; rendered as printed, marker gives
   the received reading.

6. **1202D, `[var:]` — Gen. XLIX, 11 printed *Ligans ad vineam **pallium** suum*.**
   The Vulgate has *ligans ad vineam **pullum** suum*, "his colt". ⚠ **This is the
   most consequential divergence in the range**: Migne's line has the cloak tied to the
   vineyard, and *pallium* has just been glossed two lines above, in its right place,
   as *vestem exteriorem* from the second half of the same verse. The printed reading
   therefore makes the verse say *pallium* twice and loses the colt entirely — and
   Foliot's gloss that follows (*ad quem asina alligata est*) glosses only the she-ass,
   never the colt, so the gloss is compatible with the printed text and gives no
   independent evidence of what he read. Rendered as printed ("Tying his cloak to the
   vineyard"), marker gives *pullum suum*.

7. **1203C, `[var:]` — Joan. XIV, 23 printed *et Pater meus **diligit** eum*.**
   Vulgate: *diliget*. Present for future again, in a sentence whose other verbs
   (*veniemus*, *faciemus*) stay future. Rendered "loveth", marker gives *diliget*.

8. **1206A, `[var:]` — Phil. II, 8 printed *Christus factus est **pro nobis**
   obediens usque ad mortem*.** The Vulgate has *humiliavit semetipsum, factus
   obediens usque ad mortem*; *pro nobis* is not in the verse, and *humiliavit
   semetipsum* is gone. Rendered as printed; marker records the received reading.

### Divergences examined and deliberately NOT marked

Each of these differs from the Vulgate without changing the sense, so Pattern 14's
bar is not met. They are recorded because the absence of a marker should be a decision,
not an oversight.

- **1192D « Ubi pascas, et ubi cubes »** — the lemma at 1189A (chunk 0014) and the
  Vulgate have no *et*. A connective added in re-quotation.
- **1193B Gen. XVIII, 1** re-quoted as *apparuit Dominus sedenti in tabernaculo*,
  abridging *in convalle Mambre … in ostio tabernaculi sui in ipso fervore diei* —
  which Foliot had already quoted in full a few lines earlier at 1193A. Abridgment,
  not variation.
- **1193B Gen. XVIII, 4–5** drops *et requiescite sub arbore* and the *et* before
  *ponamque*. Abridgment.
- **1195A** — 1 Cor. II, 9 (*quam nec oculus vidit…*) is woven in without guillemets
  and without a note; Migne marks it as nothing at all, so nothing is supplied.
- **1195B « haedos suos »** — the lemma has *haedos **tuos*** (second person); the
  gloss converts to the third person for the exposition. Foliot's own recasting.
- **1198B Phil. II, 6** omits *se* (*esse aequalem Deo*). Immaterial.
- **1198B Act. IV, 12** is recast into indirect discourse (*Nullum aliud nomen sub
  coelo esse, in quo salvos fieri oporteat homines*) inside guillemets, against the
  Vulgate's *nec enim aliud nomen est sub caelo datum hominibus, in quo oporteat nos
  salvos fieri*. Structural recasting to fit *asseverant et adstruunt*; sense intact,
  *datum hominibus* lost. **The nearest of these to the bar** — it takes no marker only
  because nothing in the gloss turns on the dropped words.
- **1198C Psal. I** opens *Beatus cujus in lege Domini voluntas ejus*, compressing
  *Beatus vir qui non abiit … sed in lege Domini*. The note `(Psal. I, 1, 2, 3)` shows
  Migne knew verse 1 was in play. Compression.
- **1200D Gen. III, 14** *Quia fecisti **haec*** for the Vulgate's *hoc*. Number only.
- **1202C Joan. XIX, 12** *Si dimittis hunc* for *Si hunc dimittis*. Word order.
- **1203D Apoc. III, 20** drops *mihi* after *aperuerit*, and prints *introibo* for
  *intrabo*, *ille* for *ipse*. Immaterial.
- **1206A Phil. II, 9** prints *exaltavit **eum*** for *illum* and *dedit* for
  *donavit*. Immaterial.
- **1205C** — the Song lemma is re-quoted with the word order changed
  (« *Inter ubera mea **mihi dilectus meus** commorabitur* » against the lemma's
  *dilectus meus mihi … commorabitur*) and *dilectus* de-capitalized. Foliot's own
  re-quotation; rendered as printed at both places, not harmonized.

## C. Defect markers fired

1. **1195C `[sic: *eonsilium*]`** — *dat Sponsae elatae **eonsilium***. Type, not a
   word: *consilium* with the c broken to e. Carried untranslated in italics under
   Patterns 7/10/12. **7a⁗ applied:** the marker is a noun object, and quarantining it
   alone would leave "the Bridegroom gives to the Bride who was lifted up ⟨hole⟩", so
   the sense stands beside the marker in plain words — "gives counsel [sic:
   *eonsilium*]". The reading is near-certain: the same sentence goes on *si boni
   pertaesa **consilii***, spelled correctly.

2. **1201A–B `[cj: *si*; read *sic*, "so"]`** — *…ut nullus eam tentandi … serpenti
   locus extiterit: **si** totum quod movere mortalium corda consuevit … statim
   virtutis calcaneo contritum extiterit.* *si* is a real word, so Pattern 12 has
   nothing to wrap; but the conditional it opens has no apodosis anywhere, and the
   clause is plainly the second member of the *ita … sic* correlative begun by *ita
   statim … vallata* in the previous clause. This is the 11064 @1142D case exactly
   (*si* for *sic*, "a correlative weakened into a condition"), which Pattern 18 names.
   The gloss is additive: Migne's *si* keeps its English ("if"), the conjecture stands
   beside it.

## D. Cruces taken with no marker

Ordered by column.

- **1193B — *cum ipsum in trinitate personam **tertiam**, et Deum unum in ipsa
  trinitate intellexit*.** Rendered literally: Abraham "understood him in the trinity
  to be the third person". The argument requires only that Abraham saw three persons
  and one God; *tertiam* makes the Son the third person, which is not the order Foliot
  has just laid out (the three men, then the single *Domine*). Possibly *tertium* with
  *personam* attracted, possibly a plate slip for *terniam*/*trinam*. Rendered as
  printed under Pattern 7; no marker, because *tertiam* is a real word occupying its
  slot and the English asserts what the Latin asserts.
- **1195D — *moderate **responderit***, perfect subjunctive where the narrative wants
  the indicative *respondit*, among a run of indicatives.** English has no clean
  exponent for the mood here ("he may have answered" would assert something the passage
  does not), so the rendering is "answered with moderation". Declined under Pattern
  18's scope note: a mood anomaly that merely fails to construe stays declined.
- **1196A — *sic innuens, sibi jam **in te** gratum esse totum quod erat a gratia
  imminutum*.** A third-person sentence about the Bride slips into the second person
  mid-clause. Rendered as printed ("is now pleasing to him **in thee**"); it is a live
  possibility that Foliot is quoting the Bridegroom's own words without marking them,
  which is why nothing is supplied.
- **1196C — *ut hinc egressio, aut electos probent, aut … praecipitent*.** *egressio*
  is a nominative singular standing against two plural subjunctives whose subject must
  be the demons. Rendered so that the disagreement survives in English ("so that hence
  a going forth, they may either prove the elect, or hurl headlong…") rather than
  smoothed to a singular verb, which would repair it invisibly (Pattern 12's second
  test). No marker: the type is not defective, the syntax is.
- **1196D–1197A — the Pharaoh sentence.** *postmodum vero in filios Abrahae, saevientem
  Pharaonem plagis durissimis exercens **Dominus** … immisso equitatu coelesti,
  **vindictam Domino exercuit***. *Dominus* is nominative with a participle and then
  the finite verb takes *Domino* as its dative, so the subject of *exercuit* has to be
  the *equitatus*. Rendered that way ("the heavenly company of horse being sent in, it
  executed vengeance for the Lord"), which is the only reading the printed cases allow;
  logged because the sentence reads at first as though the Lord were the subject
  throughout.
- **1197B — the note `(IV Reg. XIX, 39)`.** 4 Kings XIX has 37 verses; the Angel and the
  185,000 are at verse 35. Migne's note is reproduced verbatim — notes are his
  citations and are not corrected here — and the mis-citation is recorded for the index.
- **1197C — *Qui quidem numerus octoginta millia continet, octo minus*.** Rendered
  literally: "Which number indeed contains eighty thousand, eight less." Twelve legions
  at 6,000 to the legion is 72,000, which is eighty thousand less **eight thousand** —
  so *octo* is elliptical for *octo millia* rather than wrong. No emendation, no marker;
  the arithmetic is recorded here so a reader is not left thinking 79,992.
- **1198D — *Collum enim a colligando dicitur*.** An etymology the English cannot carry
  (*neck* shares no root with *bind*), so both Latin words stand in italic inside the
  rendering: "For *collum*, the neck, is so called from *colligando*, binding together".
  The Song ruling's case 2 (*stylus*/*epistylium*, *murenulae*/*ferculum*).
- **1199A — *Quam custodiendam suscepi, casta sis…*.** The necklace's speech opens with
  an accusative relative and continues with jussive subjunctives addressed to a
  second person; there is no antecedent and no main verb. Rendered keeping the case
  visible ("Whom I have undertaken to guard, be thou chaste") rather than converting to
  a nominative address, which would tidy away the anacoluthon.
- **1199C — *unde auditorum **conviventia** suscipiuntur*.** *conviventia* (from
  *convivere*) is attested in medieval Latin for fellowship or living-together, so it
  is Pattern 9 material and not a defect; rendered "into the fellowship of their
  hearers". Recorded because *convenientia* and *conniventia* are both a single letter
  away and either would give a smoother sentence.
- **1199D — *quae **totum** legem utramque statuit*.** *totum* against feminine
  *legem utramque*. Latin grammatical gender has no English exponent (Pattern 9), so
  the English reads "the whole of both laws" and nothing is emended.
- **1200B — *ab **Eva** usque in **Adam***.** The order is reversed against the
  history: the delay runs from Adam to the fullness of time, and the same sentence goes
  on *ob Adae offensam*. Rendered as printed ("from Eve unto Adam"). Declined for a
  marker: no word is broken, and the English asserts exactly what the Latin asserts, so
  there is nothing for `[sic:]` to wrap and Pattern 18's bar (a real word that makes
  the sentence say something the author is arguing against) is not clearly met — the
  sentence is odd, not contradictory. **Flagged for the blind read as the site in this
  range most likely to be judged differently.**
- **1200C — *dum ille, quem regem super omnia **judicio**, Filius…*.** *judicio* is a
  real word (ablative of *judicium*) but leaves the relative clause with no verb; the
  obvious reading is *judico*, "whom I judge king over all", which would also match the
  first-person voice of the whole speech. Rendered as closely as the printed words
  allow, leaving the clause verbless ("while he whom king above all things by judgment,
  the Son, …"). Not glossed under Pattern 18 because the defect is a stranded case, not
  a negation or a correlative, and the ruling was explicitly not read wider than that
  class.
- **1201B — *nec ejus sint suggestionibus aut deflecti, aut certe omnino submitti*.**
  *sint* with two passive infinitives will not construe (*sinit*? *sinat*? a lost
  *fas*?). Rendered as closely as the words allow, negation carried: "nor are they to
  be turned aside by his suggestions, or indeed to be wholly made subject."
- **1201D — *Hic enim **Dei Patris**, instar Noe, obdormisse dicitur*.** A genitive
  where the sentence needs a nominative *Deus Pater*. English nouns carry no case, so
  there is no exponent for it (Pattern 9 family); rendered "here God the Father … is
  said to have fallen asleep", logged, not emended.
- **1202A — *in quam pater, vibrato jam gladio, collo **filius** extenso consensit*.**
  The ablative absolute wants *collo **filii** extenso*, "the son's neck being stretched
  out"; Migne prints the nominative *filius*. Rendering it as a genitive would be a
  silent repair, so the English keeps it stranded: "to which the father consented, the
  sword being now brandished, the neck stretched out, the son." The page reads oddly on
  purpose.
- **1205A — the note `(Joan. XIX, 39)` inside the Song quotation.** Migne sets the
  citation for the myrrh-and-aloes of the *preceding* sentence inside the guillemets of
  « *Dilectus meus mihi inter ubera mea commorabitur* », where it documents nothing.
  Reproduced verbatim and in place; recorded so the index does not bind John XIX to
  Canticles I.
- **1205B — an unclosed quotation, and it is Migne's.** *Hic enim « myrrhae fasciculus,
  multa passionis Christi memoria est;* opens a guillemet that is never closed; the
  quotation runs on into what is plainly Foliot's own second-person exhortation
  (*quam si teneas…*). Per Pattern 5's corollary the English mirrors the plate and
  supplies no `»`. This is why the body guillemet counts in 0019 are 24 opens to 23
  closes on both sides.
- **1205D — *…quod faciem hominis exhilarat**,** Ait itaque Sponsa haec…*.** Migne
  prints a comma where the sentence ends, followed by a capital *A*. Preserved exactly
  under Pattern 8 (never delete a mark he prints, never supply one he does not), so the
  English carries the same comma splice.
- **1205C — *Cyprus enim, insula vitibus abundans est*.** Foliot reads *cypri* in the
  lemma as the island of Cyprus, not the henna/cypress shrub, and builds the whole
  gloss on its vines and clusters. The lemma is therefore rendered "A cluster of
  Cyprus" so that the gloss explains it; the conventional English "cypress" would leave
  the island paragraph attached to nothing. Recorded rather than marked — this is a
  translator's reading of *cypri*, not a divergence in Migne's text.
