## 1. The named Song lemmata and their divergences (7a″ deliverable)

### 0025 @1221C — 6. *Laeva ejus sub capite meo, et dextera ejus amplexabitur me.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. II, 6):** *Laeva ejus sub capite meo, et dextera **illius** amplexabitur me.*
- Migne prints *ejus* for the Vulgate's *illius* in the second member. No sense change (both
  are the same possessive in English), **no `[var:]` taken**; recorded here because the whole
  gloss below turns on the two hands and the pronoun is part of the lemma chain.
- **Re-quotations, all held to the lemma rendering:** @1221C « Laeva ejus sub capite meo, et
  dextera ejus amplexabitur me: » (full, verbatim); @1221C the paraphrase *quod laeva ejus sub
  capite jam meo est … quod ejus me dextera amplexabitur*; @1222A *Laevam itaque sub capite
  fore intelligit*; @1222B *recte laeva Dei sub capite Sponsae*; @1222C *laevam Dei sub « ejus, »
  id est, Ecclesiae capite*; @1222D « Et dextera ejus amplexabitur me. » and *Quid enim Dei
  dextera*. Every one renders *laeva* "left hand" and *dextera* "right hand."

### 0025 @1221C — ⚠ *laeva* vs *sinistra*: the antithesis, and what was done
Migne prints **two different Latin words for the left** within four lines, and Foliot's
argument stands on the pairing:
- the Song lemma: *Laeva ejus sub capite meo, et **dextera** ejus…*
- the Proverbs proof-text: *Longitudo vitae in **dextera** ejus, in **sinistra** vero divitiae
  et gloria* [n: (Prov. III, 16)].

**Ruling taken:** *laeva* → **"left hand"** (a hand, matching *dextera* "right hand," which is
what the gloss argues about: *Dominum laevam pariter habere et dexteram*); *sinistra* →
**"on the left"** (the substantivized adjective, no "hand" supplied, because Migne does not
print *manus* and Proverbs is describing what Wisdom holds on each side). *dextera* is
"right hand" in both places, since Migne prints the same word in both. The English therefore
keeps the two Latin words visibly distinct without inventing a distinction of sense, and
without flattening both into "left hand." Flagged for a reader's second opinion: an alternative
would have been *laeva* "left" / *sinistra* "left hand," which reads worse against the gloss.

### 0025 @1221C — Prov. III, 16: *Longitudo **vitae***
- **Vulgate:** *Longitudo **dierum** in dextera ejus, et in sinistra **illius** divitiae et gloria.*
- Migne prints *longitudo vitae* ("length of life") for *longitudo dierum*, and *in sinistra
  vero* for *in sinistra illius*. **`[var: Vulg. *longitudo dierum*, "length of days"]` taken**
  on the substantive divergence; the *vero*/*illius* difference is connective and is recorded
  here only.

### 0025 @1223A — 7. *Adjuro vos, filiae Jerusalem, per capreas, cervosque camporum, ne suscitetis, neque evigilare faciatis dilectam, quoadusque ipsa velit.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. II, 7):** … *ne suscitetis, neque evigilare faciatis dilectam, **donec** ipsa velit.*
- Divergences from the Vulgate: **(a)** *quoadusque* for *donec* — same force, English "until"
  in both, **no `[var:]`**; **(b)** the comma Migne sets after *capreas* in the lemma, which he
  drops in every re-quotation. Punctuation rides as printed (Pattern 8): the lemma reads
  "by the roes, and the harts of the fields," the re-quotations "by the roes and the harts."
- **Negation fidelity (Pattern 7a).** The lemma prints *ne … neque*; the English prints exactly
  two, "that you stir not up, **nor** make the beloved to awake," in the lemma and in every
  re-quotation of it. No third negative was introduced and none dropped.
- **The four printed forms of this verse across the range** (this is the payload of the lemma;
  each is rendered as printed, not conformed to the lemma above it):

  | column | printed | English |
  |---|---|---|
  | 0025 @1223A | *…ne suscitetis **eam**.* | "…that you stir **her** not up." |
  | 0025 @1223B | *Adjuro vos, filiae Jerusalem, ne suscitetis eam* (middle clause dropped) | as printed, clause dropped |
  | 0025 @1224A | *…ne suscitetis,* … *ut dilectam non suscitetis, **quoad** ipsa velit* | *quoad* → "until" |
  | 0026 @1225A | *…quoadusque velit* (***ipsa* dropped**) | "until she will" — no "herself" |
  | 0028 @1232B | *Ne suscitetis, neque evigilare faciatis **Dilectam**, **quousque** ipsa velit* | capital **D**, "the Beloved"; *quousque* → "until" |

  **Declined:** no `[var:]` on any of these. They are Migne's own internal re-quotation
  variants, not divergences from the received text, and English has one word ("until") for
  *quoadusque*, *quoad* and *quousque* — the distinction has no English exponent. The
  capitalization of *Dilectam* at 1232B **is** carried (see §3).

### 0026 @1225A — 8. *Ecce ille venit saliens in montibus, transiliens colles.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. II, 8):** *Vox dilecti mei; ecce **iste** venit saliens in montibus, transiliens colles.*
- Migne's lemma prints ***ille*** where the Vulgate has *iste* — and **prints *iste* himself at
  0027 @1227C**, and drops the demonstrative altogether at 0026 @1226B. Three printed forms of
  the same clause in two columns. **`[var: Vulg. *iste*, "this one"]` taken** on the lemma, and
  the three forms are kept distinguishable in the English:
  - lemma / @1225B *Ecce **ille** venit* → "Behold, **that one** cometh"
  - @1226B *Ecce venit* → "Behold, **he** cometh"
  - 0027 @1227C *Ecce **iste** venit* → "Behold, **this one** cometh"
- The lemma also begins after *Vox dilecti mei*, which Migne glosses separately at 0026 @1225A
  (see §2, *moi*). Not a divergence — lemma segmentation.

### 0027 @1228A — 9. *Similis est dilectus capreae, hinnuloque cervorum.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. II, 9):** *Similis est dilectus **meus** capreae, hinnuloque cervorum.*
- Migne's lemma **omits *meus***. `[var: Vulg. *dilectus meus*, "my beloved"]` taken: a whole
  word of the lemma is gone, and it is the word that makes the clause the Bride's own speech.
  The English lemma accordingly reads "The beloved is like…", not "My beloved."

### 0028 @1232B — 11. *Jam enim hiems transiit.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. II, 11):** *Jam enim hiems transiit; imber abiit, et recessit.* Migne agrees
  word for word, splitting the verse into two lemmata (*Imber abiit et recessit.* follows at
  @1232D). **No divergence, no marker.**
- **Re-quotation at 0029 @1236C inverts it:** « Hiems transiit; **abiit imber**, et recessit ».
  Rendered "The winter is past; **gone is the rain**, and departed," so the inversion is visible.
  Migne's re-quotation also drops *Jam enim*; carried as printed.

### 0028 @1233D — 12. *Flores apparuerunt in terra nostra.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. II, 12):** identical. **No divergence, no marker.** The two following
  sub-lemmata (*Tempus putationis advenit.*, *Vox turturis audita est in terra nostra.*) also
  agree with the Vulgate word for word.
- **flores / mala held distinct** through 0025 and 0028: *flores* "flowers" throughout,
  *mala* in the Song sense "apples" (see §4).

### 0029 @1235A — 13. *Ficus protulit grossos suos.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. II, 13):** identical. **No divergence, no marker.** *grossi* rendered
  "green figs" and held fixed through the gloss (@1235A, @1235B twice, @1236C).
- The companion lemma *Vineae florentes dederunt odorem suum.* (@1235D) likewise agrees.

### 0029 @1236C — 14. *Surge, amica mea, speciosa mea, et veni, 14. columba mea in foraminibus petrae, in cavernis maceriae.*
(The verse number sits inside Migne's italic run; reproduced there.)
- **Vulgate (Cant. II, 13b–14):** *Surge, amica mea, speciosa mea, et veni: columba mea in
  foraminibus petrae, in **caverna** maceriae.*
- Migne prints the **plural *cavernis*** where the Vulgate has the singular *caverna*, and the
  gloss immediately argues for **one** hollow — the spear-wound: *In hac etiam maceriae
  **cavernam** inesse, quis nesciat* (@1236D). `[var: Vulg. *in caverna maceriae*, "in the
  hollow of the wall," singular]` **taken**: the divergence is material precisely because the
  exposition needs the singular.
- **Migne's own re-quotation at @1236C reads « Surge, amica mea, ***sponsa mea***, et veni,
  columba mea »** — *sponsa* where his lemma four lines above prints *speciosa*, and where the
  Vulgate has *speciosa*. Rendered as printed ("my bride" in the re-quotation, "my comely one"
  in the lemma). **No `[var:]`**: this is Migne's internal inconsistency, not a variant reading
  of the received text, and the two English words show it plainly.

## 2. Broken and doubtful type

### 0026 @1225A — *Vox dilecti **moi** haec est*
*moi* is not a Latin word. Carried under Patterns 7/10 and marked `[sic: *moi*]`; the
conjecture is *mei* ("my"), near-certain from the Vulgate's *Vox dilecti mei* and from the
lemma this sentence introduces. Consequence recorded: with the possessive quarantined, the
English reads "the voice of the beloved," not "of **my** beloved." Nothing that *moi* governs
is left un-rendered (7a⁗), because *moi* is itself the whole of what is lost.

### 0027 @1230D — the doubled clause
Migne prints: *manifeste bonos et malos videns per fenestram; **hos vero qui se occultant,
intuens per fenestram**; hos vero qui se occultant, intuens per cancellos.* The clause is set
twice, differing only in its final noun. Both are rendered (Pattern 7), and the second
occurrence of the shared run is marked `[d: …]` per Pattern 11's "wrap the repeated occurrence."
**Note the reservation:** the intrusion is almost certainly the **first** tail (*per fenestram*),
not the second — the sense the paragraph has been building for two columns is that the openly
good and bad are seen through the *window* and the hidden through the *lattices*, and the
window clause has already been used. Pattern 11's rule is written for identical runs and does
not decide a doubling that varies in one word; the marker was placed by the letter of the rule
and the analysis left here rather than in the text.

### 0029 @1234C — *Praedicatur enim quilibet*
*Praedicatur* is a real form ("is preached"), so Pattern 12 has nothing to wrap; but the four
nominative participles that follow (*exquirens … nuntians*) and the finite *gemit … habitat …
fugit* have no subject, and the sentence's own opening clause names the *praedicator*.
Pattern 18 applies: `[cj: *Praedicatur*; read *Praedicator*, "a preacher"]`, additive, with
Migne's word rendered first.

### 0027 @1229A — *odoratu, gustu, **dictu** et tactu*
The five senses have just been listed (*visus, auditus, gustus, odoratus et tactus*), and
*dictu* — speech — stands in this second list where a sense belongs. **`[cj:]` declined**: the
word is real, the syntax is sound, and I have no confident reading to propose (*visu* would
duplicate what the eye-window paragraph has already covered; *auditu* is likewise spent). The
list simply names one thing that is not a sense. Rendered "of speech" and left.

### 0026 @1224B — *ab haec vocatione*
*ab* + accusative. No English exponent for the case error (Pattern 9); rendered "from this
calling," no marker, logged so a later sweep does not churn it.

### 0025 @1220D — *nec contepescat*
Not classical, but a transparent compound of *tepescere*; translated normally ("grow lukewarm")
under Pattern 9 rather than carried. Recorded because the form is rare enough to be mistaken
for broken type.

### 0029 @1234C — *hiemis **abcessum***
Assimilated spelling of *abscessum*; attested medieval orthography, translated normally
(Pattern 9), no marker.

## 3. Punctuation, quotation marks, and structure carried as printed

- **0026 @1224B — the broken parenthesis.** Migne prints *(de qua dictum est, « Generationem
  ejus quis enarrabit?) [n: (Isai. LIII, 8)], »* — the parenthesis closes **inside** the
  quotation and the guillemet closes after the note. Reproduced exactly, in that order.
- **0026 @1227A — an unclosed quotation.** *« Petre, macta, et manduca [n: …]; et diceret,
  « Absit a me…* — the first `«` is never closed. Mirrored (Pattern 5 corollary); the chunk's
  guillemet counts are 27 open / 26 close in both Latin and English.
- **0027 @1227C — a second unclosed quotation.** *« Transiliens colles. Quod quare sic,
  videamus.* Mirrored; 21 open / 20 close in both.
- **0027 @1229C — a sentence with no terminal stop.** *…quae supra Dominum die qua baptizatus
  est apparuit* — the paragraph ends without a mark. The English ends without one (Pattern 8,
  "never supply").
- **0029 @1235D — *Unde subditur*** with no colon or stop before the lemma. Carried.
- **0026 @1227B and 0029 @1236D — rhetorical questions closed with a period.** *Quis in eum non
  dixerit insiluisse Dominum, cum … baptizavit.* and *Quis enim ignoret … « Petra autem erat
  Christus ». * Both keep the period; no `?` supplied. Question-mark counts match the Latin
  twin exactly in all five chunks (4 / 7 / 4 / 2 / 5), as does the single `!` at 0026 @1227A.
- **0028 @1232D — *Lamech « homicida »***: Migne guillemets the single word; kept on the word.
- **Capitalization carried:** *dilectus* lowercase → "the beloved"; *Dilectus / Dilecta /
  Dilectam* capitalized → "the Beloved" (0027 @1228D, 0028 @1231A and @1232B, 0029 @1236C,
  @1237A, @1237B). At 0029 @1237B both appear in one sentence — *Assistenti autem **Dilectae**
  … **dilectus** ait* — and the English keeps the capital where Migne does.

only** ("Not every rain is called *imber*, but only the wintry"), while the lemma and the rest
of the gloss read "rain." Logged rather than marked.

## 6. Renderings flagged for a reader, no marker taken

- **0025 @1221D** *inofficiosum membrum* — "a member without office." The legal sense
  (*inofficiosus*, "undutiful") is possible but the context is the body's service to the head.
- **0027 @1228B** *ut qui Patrem videt, et **vivendo Patrem** cuncta contuetur in Patre* —
  *vivendo* with an accusative object is odd; rendered literally ("living the Father"). No
  emendation offered.
- **0027 @1229D** *et arctam **inchoans** viam* — a nominative participle whose subject could be
  the Lord or those he leads. Rendered with the same ambiguity ("and, beginning the narrow way").
- **0029 @1236A** *Vineae vero Domini **et una est**, et multae sunt* — plural subject, singular
  verb, then plural. Rendered "of the vineyards of the Lord there is one, and there are many";
  no English exponent for the mismatch (Pattern 9).
- **0029 @1237D** *velut evaserit* — "shall have as it were let them go"; the verb is
  intransitive in classical use and the object is supplied by the sentence. Rendered without
  emendation.
