## 2. `[var: …]` markers taken — three, all outside the Song

1. **0031 @1242C · Matt. XXII, 30.** Migne prints *cum jam non nubent homines, **neque nuptum
   tradent***, against Vulg. *neque nubent neque nubentur*. The divergence is one of **agency**:
   the Vulgate's second limb is passive ("nor **be given** in marriage"), Migne's is active ("nor
   **give** in marriage"), which puts the men in the position of those disposing of brides rather
   than of those disposed of. Rendered as printed; `[var: Vulg. *neque nubentur*, "nor be given in
   marriage," passive]`. (Migne also drops the Vulgate's closing *in caelo*; not marked — no sense
   turns on it and the note locates the verse.)
2. **0032 @1244B · Rom. VIII, 38.** Migne prints ***Scimus enim*** *quia neque mors, neque vita…*
   where the Vulgate has *Certus sum enim*. Person and modality both move: Paul's personal
   certainty becomes the community's knowledge — and Foliot is at that moment arguing about what a
   whole ORDER of souls may say *cum Apostolo*, so the plural helps his argument, which is exactly
   why the reading must not be silently corrected. `[var: Vulg. *Certus sum enim*, "For I am sure"]`.
3. **0032 @1246D · Matt. VII, 7.** Migne prints *Petite, et **accipietis***, against Vulg. *Petite,
   et dabitur vobis*. Migne's reading makes the asker the agent of receiving; the Vulgate's makes
   the gift the act of another. The whole paragraph turns on this triad (*petitioni nil negatur,
   quaestioni nil clauditur, pulsationi promptius aperitur*), so the first limb is load-bearing.
   `[var: Vulg. *et dabitur vobis*, "and it shall be given to you"]`.

All three contents are **phrase-length**, per the merge note: a one-word content trips Pattern 14's
mirror test whenever that word occurs anywhere in the Latin twin. All three cleared `verify-english`.

## 4. ⭐ ***ferculum*** — rendered **"litter"**, and the corpus was consulted first (as instructed)

**What the corpus says.** *ferculum* appears in 11613 at 0033 (×1), 0034 (×6) and 0035 (×1); this
range holds all but one. In **11550** (Richard on the same book, already englished) it was
**kept in italic Latin at all eleven occurrences** — and that was right *there*, because Richard
**etymologizes it**: *ferculum a **ferendo**, quia **fertur** de loco ad locum*, and an English word
sharing no root with "bear" would leave his gloss explaining nothing. That is the Song ruling's
case 2 (*stylus*/*epistylium*, *murenulae*/*ferculum*).

**Foliot does not do that.** His gloss is functional, not etymological: *Surgens enim e lecto Rex,
ferculo se suscipit, ut de loco feratur ad locum* — the King rises from the bed and takes to the
*ferculum* to be borne about; it has silver pillars, a golden *reclinatorium*, a purple ascent, and
an *area* in the middle paved with charity. **Nothing in his argument depends on the word's root**,
and every clause of it construes in English with "litter". So the ground that forced italic Latin in
11550 is simply absent here, and the Song ruling forbids importing 11550's answer anyway: *each
commentary stands on its own; do not harmonize a lemma across works.*

**Chosen: "litter"** (also Douay's word at Cant. 3:9). ⚑ **This is deliberately different from
11550's *ferculum*, and both are correct as they stand** — exactly the *murenulae* / "chains of
gold" situation the ruling names as needing no action. Do not sweep either.

Consequential renderings in the same passage, for the merge:
- *reclinatorium* → **"reclining-place"** (not italic Latin, and not Douay's "seat"): Foliot's gloss
  is *quasi reclinatorium ex auro sapientiae composuit*, a place to lean, with no etymology argued.
- *media … charitate constravit* → **"the middle parts he paved with charity"**, neuter plural, held
  fixed through *« Media, » id est, area quae in medio ferculi est* and *media namque charitate
  constrata sunt*. Rendering *media* as a singular "the midst" would break the second re-quotation.
- *area ferculi* → **"the floor of the litter"**.

## 5. ⭐ *virgula* — **"little rod"**, because Foliot argues the diminutive

Douay gives "a pillar of smoke" at Cant. 3:6. **That rendering cannot be used here**, because
Foliot's gloss is built entirely on the diminutive: *quae **non virga, sed « virgula »** recte
dicitur* — twice, at 1249C — *quia recta et extenuata, agilis quoque compunctio cordis
intelligitur*. With "pillar" the page would read "not a pillar, but a pillar", and the two
occurrences of « virgula » inside guillemets would be expounding a word not on the page. Rendered
**"a little rod of smoke"**, with *virga* as "rod", so the antithesis survives. The same policy
governs *lectulus* → **"little bed"** against *lectus* → **"bed"** (0034 @1252B sets the two side by
side: *Surgens enim e **lecto** Rex*, of the *lectulus Salomonis*).

## 7. Defect markers taken — one

- **0032 @1243D — `[sic: *perfecto*]`.** Migne prints *ibi **perfecto** luxus exuberat*. *perfecto*
  is a real Latin form (dat./abl. of *perfectus*) with **no grammatical slot in the sentence**: there
  is no noun for it to agree with and no construction that takes it. The near-certain reading is
  ***profecto***, "assuredly," the intensifier the clause wants. This is the dangerous class named
  in the wave-1 notes (a real word takes no automated flag), and it is the same shape as 1192A's
  `[sic: *suum*]` for *sum*. **7a⁗ check applied**: the quarantined run carries no negation, no
  conjunction and no verb — deleting it leaves *ibi luxus exuberat*, which is complete — so nothing
  needed supplying in plain words beside the marker.

## 8. `[cj: …]` DECLINED — one site, and the reason is that a second reading construes

- **0031 @1241D — *illi soli cui commendata sunt, nobis cum **noluerit** aperienda, commendamus*.**
  First read, this looks like the classic negation defect: sense seems to want *voluerit*, and
  "to be opened to us when he shall be UNwilling" is the opposite of the argument. Pattern 18's
  2026-08-15 extension would then admit it, since the word that went wrong is a negation.
  **Declined, because the printed text construes without emendation**: *aperienda* is a gerundive
  serving as the object of *noluerit* — "we commit them to him alone to whom they have been
  committed, **since he has not willed that they be opened to us**." That reading is coherent, is
  continuous with the preceding *ne tentemus ingredi, nec … praesumamus aperire*, and is what the
  English now says. A `[cj:]` here would have glossed away a good sentence. **Logged rather than
  passed over silently, because the alternative reading is the sort a blind reader will propose.**

- **0030 @1239D — *quam ad Deum tendentem … impedire valeat*.** The relative *quam* is picked up by
  participles with no finite verb of its own. Rendered "the Church, her that tends toward God…",
  keeping the anacoluthon visible rather than converting it to a clause.
- **0030 @1240C — *de allophylis*** rendered **"from the Philistines"**. *allophylus* is the
  Vulgate's own word for the Philistines through 1–2 Kings; this is translation, not substitution.
- **0030 @1238D — *coelestium novem ordines, hominemque eis adjunctum, et parem eis per gratiam
  **factam***.** Feminine *factam* against masculine *hominem*. Pattern 9 — Latin gender has no
  English exponent. No emendation, no marker.
- **0032 @1246D — the three-part gloss on Isa. XXVIII, 20.** Migne's order is « *pallium* » …
  « *utrumque* » … « *operire* » … *non potest*. English wants the verb before its object, which
  would reorder his quoted words. **Kept in his order** ("the « cloak » … « both » … is not able to
  « cover »"), fronting the object, so that the guillemet sequence on the English page matches the
  Latin twin position for position.
- **0034 @1251B — *quod verbo « contexunt, » hoc manibus exercere intelliguntur*.** *contexunt* sits
  inside guillemets, as though quoted, but no source is given and the sense wanted is closer to
  *detexunt* ("unweave") or simply "profess". Rendered literally, "that which they « weave together »
  in word". **No marker**: the word is real, it construes, and the sentence asserts nothing false —
  it is merely odd, which Pattern 18 explicitly does not admit. **Flagged for the blind read as the
  site in 0034 most likely to be judged differently.**
- **0031 @1242D — *pallio terrae noctis obducuntur*.** Three genitives in a row with no obvious
  governor; rendered "are drawn over with the cloak of the night of the earth", following the
  printed order. Recorded because a smoother "with the earth's cloak of night" would be a reading,
  not a rendering.
- **0033 @1249A — *« Quae » igitur « ista haec, »*** with no copula. English cannot omit the verb;
  rendered "« Who » therefore « is this she, »", which supplies only the copula English grammar
  requires and leaves both quoted fragments in their printed places.
- **0034 @1251D — *hostis … cui poenas meruit, impunis evadit*.** Rendered "the enemy … escapes
  unpunished from him at whose hands he has deserved punishment", which is the only reading the
  printed cases allow; recorded because *cui* invites the opposite binding (Pattern 7a mechanism 5).
- **Em-dashes: none in this range.** Migne prints none; commas, colons and full stops carried the
  sense everywhere it was tested.
