# Cruces — 11613 (Gilbert Foliot, *Expositio in Cantica canticorum*), chunks 0035–0039

Columns 1253C–1268A. Column + printed reading + note. Divergences from the Vulgate are listed
whether or not a `[var: …]` was taken; where a call was declined the reason is given.

Conventions inherited from wave 1 and used throughout this range without re-deciding them:
**`amica` = "friend"** · **`pulcher`/`pulchra` = "beautiful", never "fair"** · `sponsa` = Bride /
lowercase *sponsa* in a lemma = "bride" · `sponsus` = Bridegroom · Migne's own capitalization of
*Dilectus*/*dilectus* mirrored (1267C prints lowercase *dilectus*, rendered "beloved").

## 1. The named Song lemmata and their divergences (7a″ deliverable)

### 0036 @1255C — 1. *Quam pulchra es amica mea, quam pulchra es! oculi tui columbarum.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. IV, 1a):** identical. Migne's only departure is **segmentation**: he breaks the
  verse after *columbarum* and sets *Absque eo quod intrinsecus* and *Capilli tui sicut greges
  caprarum* as separate lemmata. Not a variant; **no `[var:]`**.
- Rendered "How beautiful art thou, my friend, how beautiful art thou! thine eyes are those of
  doves." — *amica* = "friend" per the wave-1 ruling; *pulchra* = "beautiful"; **the `!` rides as
  printed** (Pattern 8), and the chunk's `!` count is 1 in both twins.
- "Thine eyes are those of doves" reproduces the rendering already fixed at 0020–0024 for the same
  bare genitive at Cant. I, 14, so the two sites agree inside the work.

### 0036 @1255C — *Absque eo quod intrinsecus.* — ⚠ THE LEMMA IS TRUNCATED, AND FOLIOT'S OWN GLOSS PRINTS THE FULL FORM
- **Vulgate:** *absque eo quod intrinsecus **latet***. Migne's lemma stops at *intrinsecus*, with no
  verb; four lines below, his gloss quotes « Absque eo quod intrinsecus **latet**; » and the whole
  exposition (what lies hidden *within* the dove-soul) rests on *latet*.
- **`[var:]` DECLINED, and for the mechanical reason recorded in the merge notes for 0009:**
  Pattern 14 forbids a marker whose content appears verbatim in the Latin twin, and the received
  reading is printed in this very chunk as Migne's own gloss. This is a second instance of that gap
  — *a work quoting itself can diverge from itself* — and it strengthens the case for the ruling
  Wilson has been asked for.
- **What the English does instead:** the truncation is rendered visibly. The lemma reads
  "*Besides that which within.*" (no verb supplied, Pattern 8/7); the gloss and the second
  occurrence at 1257B read "« Besides that which lieth hid within; »". A reader meets the defect
  on the page even though no marker could carry it.

### 0036 @1256C — 2. *Dentes tui sicut greges tonsarum … et non est sterilis inter eas.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. IV, 2):** *… omnes gemellis fetibus, et **sterilis non est** inter eas.*
- Migne inverts to *non est sterilis*. Word order only; the negation is present and carried
  ("and there is not one barren among them"). **No `[var:]`.**
- **Internal re-quotation drift, recorded:** the gloss glosses *gemellis **fetibus*** ("twin young")
  with *gemellis ipsos ditant **fructibus*** ("enrich them with twin **fruits**"). Foliot has
  slid from offspring to produce. Rendered as printed in both places, distinct words kept distinct;
  no marker, because the second is his own exposition and not a re-quotation of the lemma.

### 0036 @1256D — 3. *Sicut vitta coccinea, labia tua; et eloquium tuum dulce.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. IV, 3a):** identical. Re-quoted at 1257A as « Eloquium tuum dulce. », held to the
  lemma rendering ("Thy speech is sweet"). **No `[var:]`.**

### 0036 @1257A — *Sicut fragmen mali punici, ita genae tuae, absque eo quod intrinsecus latet.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. IV, 3b):** identical. Re-quoted at 1257B as « ut fragmen mali punici: » and
  « Absque eo quod intrinsecus latet; », both held to the lemma. **No `[var:]`.**
- ⛔ **Homograph check:** *mali punici* here is the **fruit** (pomegranate) in every one of its five
  occurrences in this chunk, governed by *fragmen* / *malum punicum Ecclesia est*. Nothing in 0036
  is *malus, -a, -um* "evil".

### 0037 @1257C — 4. *Sicut turris David collum tuum … omnis armatura fortium.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. IV, 4):** identical. Re-quoted at 1257D as « mille clypei, » and glossed as
  *fortium armatura* twice. **No `[var:]`.**

### 0037 @1258A — 5–6. *Duo ubera tua sicut duo hinnuli capreae gemelli, qui pascuntur in liliis. Donec aspiret dies, et inclinentur umbrae.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. IV, 5–6a):** identical but for Migne's punctuation (*hinnuli, capreae gemelli*).
  **No `[var:]`.**
- **The lemma word held fixed through the gloss:** *aspiret* is rendered "**breathe**", not the
  conventional "break", precisely because Foliot re-uses the verb — *cum dies judicii
  **aspiraverit*** at 1259A, "when the day of judgment shall have **breathed**". A "break" in the
  lemma would leave that gloss expounding a word not on the page.
- Re-quotations « ut duo hinnuli capreae, » (1258D) and « pascuntur in liliis, » (1259A) both held.

### 0037 @1259C — 7. *Tota pulchra es, amica mea, et macula non est in te.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. IV, 7):** identical. **No `[var:]`.**
- **The negation is carried twice**, in the lemma and in the re-quotation at 1259C (« all
  beautiful, and there is not a spot in thee, »), in the same words both times.

### 0037 @1259D — 8. *Veni de Libano, sponsa mea … de cubilibus leonum, de montibus pardorum.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. IV, 8):** identical. **No `[var:]`.**
- **Two internal re-quotation divergences, both recorded, neither marked:**
  1. @1259D–1260A the gloss quotes *Coronaberis de **capite** Amana* (matching the lemma) and then
     immediately *corona de **cacumine** Amana* — head → summit. Three Latin words for the top of a
     mountain occur within two lines (*capite* · *vertice* · *cacumine*) and are kept visibly
     distinct in English as **head · top · summit**, on the *laeva*/*sinistra* precedent.
  2. @1260B the gloss re-quotes « De cubilibus leonum, **et** de montibus pardorum. » where the
     lemma has no *et*. Conjunction only.
- ⚠ **Guillemet imbalance MIRRORED, not repaired:** @1259D Migne prints
  *cum dicitur, Coronaberis de capite Amana; »* — a closing guillemet with no opening one. The
  English reproduces the orphan close in the same place. This is the whole of 0037's 28-open /
  29-close asymmetry, and both twins now carry it.

### 0037 @1260C — 9. *Vulnerasti cor meum, soror mea sponsa, vulnerasti cor meum.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. IV, 9a):** identical. **No `[var:]`.**
- **The repetition is rendered as a repetition**, in identical words, here and at every
  re-quotation in 0038 (@1261D the half-line « Vulnerasti cor meum, soror mea sponsa; »; @1262D the
  bare « Vulnerasti cor meum; »; @1262D the doubled form again in full). Foliot's argument at
  1262D–1263A *is* the doubling — he counts the two woundings and asks for a third — so any
  elegant variation would destroy the gloss.

### 0038 @1263A — 10. *Quam pulchrae sunt mammae tuae, soror mea sponsa! pulchriora sunt ubera tua vino …*
- **Vulgate (Cant. IV, 10):** identical. **No `[var:]`.** The `!` rides as printed, twice (lemma and
  the re-quotation « quam pulchrae sunt mammae tuae! » at 1263A).
- ⚠ **A deliberate flattening, declared:** Migne prints **two** Latin words for the breast in one
  verse, *mammae* and *ubera*, and the gloss uses them as plain synonyms in a single sentence
  (« quam pulchrae sunt mammae tuae! » *id est, doctrina **uberum** tuorum*). Both are rendered
  "breasts". *ubera* was already fixed as "breasts" at 0037's lemma 5, no argument anywhere in the
  range turns on the difference, and the alternative ("paps" for *ubera*) would have split a word
  the work treats as one. **Flagged rather than silently done**, in case the merge finds a site
  where the two collide.
- Migne's `?` at 1263A closes what is in sense an exclamation (*… et sustentationem parvulorum?*).
  **Carried as printed** under Pattern 8, recast as a direct question English can hold ("how
  beautiful is the teaching … ?"). Not 8b: the Latin is already direct.

### 0038 @1263B — 11. *Favus distillans labia tua, sponsa.* / *Mel et lac sub lingua tua.* / *Et odor vestimentorum tuorum sicut odor thuris.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. IV, 11):** identical; Migne divides the verse into three lemmata. **No `[var:]`.**
- Re-quoted at 1263B as « Favus distillans labia tua, sponsa » **Christi** — Foliot runs his own
  word straight out of the closing guillemet into the quotation. Rendered so: "« A dropping
  honeycomb, thy lips, O bride » of Christ." Guillemet positions unchanged.

### 0039 @1264B — 12. *Hortus conclusus soror mea sponsa, hortus conclusus, fons signatus.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. IV, 12):** identical. Re-quoted at 1264B in full, and at 1264C as « hortus
  conclusus » and « Fons signatus; ». **No `[var:]`.**

### 0039 @1264D — 13. *Emissiones tuae paradisus MALORUM punicorum, cum pomorum fructibus.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. IV, 13a):** identical. **No `[var:]`.**
- ⛔ **The work's homograph at its sharpest, and it was decided from the syntax at every one of the
  nine sites in this chunk.** *malorum punicorum* / *mala punica* / *Malum punicum* are the **fruit**
  (genitive plural of *malum punicum*, governed by *paradisus* and glossed *sub rubeo cortice
  multitudinem granorum claudit*) — rendered **pomegranates**. *pomorum* / *poma* beside it is the
  second fruit-word, rendered **apples**, and the distinction is load-bearing: at 1265B Foliot
  *contrasts* them (*Mala namque punica sunt, qui … sanguine laureati discedunt: illi vero fructus,
  qui solum poma dicuntur* — the pomegranates are the martyrs, the apples are the confessors). A
  single "evil" anywhere in this chain would destroy the argument and pass every check.
- **Where *malus, -a, -um* really does mean evil in this range** (checked, and rendered "evil"):
  0035 @1253D *Egressiones enim istae **malae** sunt*; 0037 @1259B Col. III, 5 *concupiscentiam
  **malam***. Neither is near a fruit.
- ***emissiones*** rendered "**sendings forth**" rather than "plants/shoots", because Foliot glosses
  the noun with its own verb three times (*fructus **emittit*** · *odorem virtutum … **emittunt*** ·
  *nec solum mala punica **emittuntur** a te*). An unrelated English noun would leave the gloss
  explaining a word not on the page.

### 0039 @1266B — 15. *Fons hortorum, puteus aquarum viventium quae fluunt impetu de Libano.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. IV, 15):** identical. **No `[var:]`.**
- ⚠ **Guillemet imbalance MIRRORED, not repaired:** @1266C Migne opens a quotation at « Fluminis
  impetus … and opens a **second** one at « Quae fluunt cum impetu de Libano … , closing only once.
  The English reproduces both opens and the single close in the same places; this is the whole of
  0039's 27-open / 26-close asymmetry.
- Note the third form of the phrase inside four lines: the lemma *fluunt **impetu** de Libano*, the
  psalm-tail *fluunt **cum impetu** de Libano*, and the gloss « cum impetu, » *id est impetuose*.
  Rendered "with force" throughout so the gloss lands; the added *cum* is not a sense change and
  took no marker.

### 0039 @1266D — 16. *Surge aquilo, et veni auster, perfla hortum meum, et fluant aromata illius.*
- **Vulgate (Cant. IV, 16a):** identical. **No `[var:]`.**
- **Internal re-quotation divergence, recorded, unmarked:** at 1267B the gloss quotes « Fluant
  **ejus** aromata, » for the lemma's *aromata **illius***. Same possessive in English; the two
  Latin pronouns are indistinguishable in translation (Pattern 9, no English exponent).

### 0039 @1267C — *Veniat dilectus meus in hortum suum, et comedat FRUCTUS pomorum suorum.* — `[var:]` TAKEN
- **Vulgate (Cant. IV, 16b):** *… et comedat **fructum** pomorum suorum* — singular.
- Migne's lemma prints the plural *fructus*, **and his own gloss eight lines below re-quotes the
  singular** (« ut comedat **fructum** pomorum suorum, »). So the plate diverges from the received
  text and from itself at the same site.
- **`[var: Vulg. has the singular, "the fruit of his apples"]` taken on the lemma**, and the two
  readings are rendered as printed in their two places (lemma "the fruits of his apples", gloss
  "the fruit of his apples"). Written as a **phrase**, per the wave-1 finding that a one-word
  `[var:]` content trips Pattern 14's mirror test.
- *dilectus* is **lowercase** in Migne here and is rendered lowercase "beloved", mirroring his own
  varying capitalization rather than normalizing it.

## 2. Divergences in the non-Song scripture (same rule, same scrutiny)

### 0035 @1255A — Joan. XX, 28: *Dominus meus, Deus meus* — `[var:]` TAKEN
Vulgate: *Dominus meus **et** Deus meus.* Migne drops the conjunction, leaving the two titles in
bare apposition. Rendered as printed ("My Lord, my God"), with
`[var: Vulg. couples the two titles, "my Lord and my God"]`.

### 0035 @1255B — Joan. XVII, 3: *ut VIDEANT te VERUM Deum* — `[var:]` TAKEN
Vulgate: *ut **cognoscant** te **solum Deum verum***. Migne prints *videant* for *cognoscant* —
seeing for knowing — and drops *solum*. This is not cosmetic: the whole paragraph is about the
daughters of Sion **seeing** the King (*ad hunc videndum … videant et attendant*), so the altered
verb is doing work in Foliot's argument, whoever introduced it. Rendered as printed, with
`[var: Vulg. reads that they may know thee the only true God]`.

### 0035 @1253C, 1253D — the two misplaced note-citations, kept verbatim
`[n: (Gen. IV, 8)]` is printed at the end of the *diademate quo coronatus est* clause, one sentence
**before** the Cain material it belongs to; `[n: (Gen. XXXIV, 1)]` closes the Cain sentence and
belongs to the Dina material that follows. Both **kept exactly where Migne prints them**, contents
untranslated. Per the merge notes: do not "correct" these, and note that an *Ibid.* elsewhere may
depend on the printed sequence.

### 0035 @1254A — Gen. XII, 1–3
Migne prints *et in te benedicentur* for the Vulgate's *atque in te benedicentur*. Conjunction
only, no sense change, **no `[var:]`**.

### 0035 @1254A — Gen. XXVIII, 10 and the narration around it
*Bersabe* / *Haram* for *Bersabee* / *Haran* are spellings (Pattern 9, not defects). The
*lapidem **magnum*** ("a great stone") is Foliot's own narration, not a quotation — Gen. XXVIII, 11
has no *magnum* — so it takes nothing. The quotation proper (*Et benedicentur in te…*, Gen.
XXVIII, 14) agrees with the Vulgate.

### 0037 @1258C — Deut.: THE PERSON OF THE SPEAKER IS INVERTED — `[var:]` TAKEN
Migne prints *Prophetam **suscitabit vobis Deus** … similem **mei** … ponet verba **sua** … ipse
**ultor existet***: God spoken **of**, in the third person. The Vulgate (Deut. XVIII, 18–19) has God
speaking **as himself** — *suscitabo … ponam verba mea … ego ultor existam* — and *similem tui*.
`[var: Vulg. has God himself speaking in the first person throughout, "I will raise them up a
prophet … I will be the avenger," and "like to thee"]`.
⚠ **The decisive control is inside the work:** 0038 @1260D–1261A prints the SAME verse in the
**first person** (*suscitabo … ponam verba mea … ego ultor existam*, *similem tui*), under the same
`[n: (Deut. XXVIII, 18, 19)]`. So this is a single-site divergence in the plate, not the author's
habit — exactly the *Nigra sum ET formosa* shape already found at 1184A. Both are rendered as
printed, and the English of the two passages therefore differs, deliberately.

### 0037 @1258C and 0038 @1261A — the note itself is wrong, and is kept
`[n: (Deut. XXVIII, 18, 19)]` is printed twice for what is **Deut. XVIII, 18–19**. Kept verbatim
both times. Recorded here, not "fixed": per the merge notes, agreement between agents is not
evidence, and a following *Ibid.* may depend on the printed reference.

### 0037 @1258C — Jer. XXXI, 22 — `[var:]` TAKEN
Vulgate: *creavit Dominus novum super terram: **femina** circumdabit virum* — perfect tense. Migne:
*Faciet Dominus novum* — future. The verse is being adduced as a **prophecy of the Incarnation**, so
the tense is the point of citing it. Rendered as printed, with `[var: Vulg. puts the verb in the
perfect, "the Lord hath created a new thing upon the earth"]`. The *mulier*/*femina* substitution is
not separately marked: English has one word.

### 0037 @1258C — Dan. IX, 24 — `[var:]` TAKEN
Migne: *cum venerit Sanctus sanctorum, cessabit in vobis unctio.* **This is not the Vulgate in any
part.** Dan. IX, 24 reads *ut … ungatur Sanctus sanctorum* — the Holy of holies is **anointed**;
there is no ceasing anointing in the verse. The form Migne prints is the medieval testimonium
against the Jews. Because he **does** print a locator here, the received reading can be named
without our supplying the identification (the 0004 @1160C precedent, which correctly declined a
marker where Migne printed none). `[var: Vulg. at this place says rather that the Holy of holies may
be anointed, and knows nothing of an anointing that ceases]`.

### 0037 @1258C — Isa. VII, 14 and Ezech. XLIV, 2
Isaias is simply truncated after *pariet Filium* (no *et vocabitur nomen ejus Emmanuel*):
abridgement, **no `[var:]`**. Ezechiel is abridged in the middle (*quoniam Dominus Deus Israel
ingressus est per eam* absent) and prints *transiet* for *transibit*: abridgement and a tense form,
**no `[var:]`**. Both negations (*non aperietur*, *vir non transiet*) are carried.

### 0038 @1260D — Ps. I, 1 INVERTED, deliberately, by the author
*quae dudum abiit in consilio **bonorum**, et in eorumdem via stetit, et in cathedra **prudentiae**
sedem sibi firmavit* — the psalm's *consilio impiorum … via peccatorum … cathedra pestilentiae*
turned positive. This is Foliot's own rhetorical inversion, not a quotation and not marked by Migne
as one; **no marker**, rendered as printed. Recorded because it looks like a corruption and is not.

### 0038 @1263D — Matth. XI, 28
Migne drops *omnes* (*Venite ad me, qui laboratis*). One word, no sense change, **no `[var:]`**.

### 0038 @1262C — Gen. III, 15 re-quoted with the words transposed
0037 @1258B prints *Inimicitias ponam inter te et mulierem*; 0038 @1262C, calling it *principali et
prima prophetia*, prints *Ponam inimicitias*. Word order only; both rendered "I will put enmities",
so the two passages agree in English as they agree in sense. **No marker.**

### 0039 @1265C — the Sirach citation is INLINE ITALIC, not a note
Migne prints *(Sirac III, 20)* inside the quotation, in italics, where every other citation in this
chunk is an `[n: …]`. **Reproduced exactly as printed**, verbatim and untranslated, in the same
place. It is deliberately NOT converted to `[n: …]` — the marker positions are matched positionally
across the pair and a conversion would desynchronize them. (Runbook item 4 also applies: this is a
lemma-and-gloss commentary, so no Pattern 4 `[f:]` tagging.)

### 0039 @1265C — Luc. XIV, 31 for Luc. XIV, 11, kept verbatim
The note attached to *Qui se humiliat exaltabitur* is Luke **XIV, 11**; Migne prints **XIV, 31**
(the king going to war). Kept verbatim, unmarked, per the standing rule on his wrong locators.

### 0039 @1266C — Ps. XLV, 5 and the second opening guillemet
See §1 above. The psalm text itself agrees with the Vulgate; the defect is the unclosed quotation,
which is mirrored.

## 3. Defective type, and the calls taken on it

### 0035 @1254B — « Aaron » where the sentence wants an ablative of manner — **MARKER DECLINED, and this is the one to look at**
> *quanam « Aaron » filiabus Sion consulitur egredi, ut Regem gloriae in diademate suo contemplari
> valeant et intueri?*

*quanam* is a feminine ablative interrogative with **nothing to agree with**, and « Aaron » — the
patriarch's name, in guillemets, in a paragraph that has nothing to do with Aaron — stands where
that noun should be. Rendered literally, "by what « Aaron » is it counselled to the daughters of
Sion to go forth…?", with the guillemets and the `?` as printed.

**Why no `[cj: …]`, and I want this looked at rather than passed over.** Pattern 18's form admits
exactly one conjecture, and there are two good ones, of different kinds:
- ***egressione*** — "by what **going forth**". The whole paragraph is a taxonomy of *egressiones*
  (Cain's, Dina's, the lying spirit's, against Abraham's, Jacob's, Peter's), the immediately
  preceding sentence ends *…poenitentiae et amoris passibus accedens **egressio***, and the
  immediately following clause answers *non ea utique qua Cain egressus est*. Contextually this is
  much the stronger.
- ***ratione*** — "by what **reasoning**", the ordinary idiom for *quanam*, and palaeographically no
  worse.

Choosing between them would be a guess dressed as a reading, and `[cj:]` is additive precisely so
that it never has to be one. **The conjecture is recorded here in full so that a reader of the
plate can settle it.** Note also that Migne's guillemets around the word suggest his own editors
already regarded it as suspect (runbook item 5's neighbourhood), which is a further reason not to
stack a marker of ours on top.

### 0037 @1259A — *umbrae hae **igorantiae*** — non-word, carried
*igorantiae* for *ignorantiae* is not a Latin word, so Pattern 7's non-word class applies: it is
**carried untranslated in italics**, "these shadows of *igorantiae*". The conjecture (*ignorantiae*,
certain — the phrase answers the lemma's *inclinentur umbrae* and the gloss's *quae nos ad praesens
impediunt*) lives here and not in the text.

### 0038 @1262A — *quo contemplatur **aeternitas*** — `[cj:]` TAKEN
*contemplatur* is deponent and active in sense, so the printed nominative *aeternitas* makes
**eternity the one doing the contemplating**, in a sentence whose whole subject is the Church's two
eyes and what each of them looks at (*unus quo intuetur proximum, alter quo videt Deum*). The word
is a real form, so nothing is broken for `[sic:]` to wrap, and the faithful English asserts
something Foliot did not — Pattern 18's exact case. Rendered "wherewith eternity contemplates
`[cj: *aeternitas*; read *aeternitatem*, "wherewith eternity is contemplated"]`".

### 0038 @1261A — *Dei Spiritu Deo dudum « munitos »* — **MARKER DECLINED**
*munitos* ("fortified") sits oddly with the dative *Deo*, and *unitos* ("united to God") would suit
the run of the sentence, which is about the elect being *conjuncti … et coadunati … unus cum eo
spiritus*. **No `[cj:]` taken**, for two reasons: "fortified **for** God by the Spirit of God"
construes without asserting anything false, so Pattern 18's test is not met; and **Migne has already
flagged the word himself with guillemets**, which under runbook item 5 is a reason not to stack our
marker on his. Rendered "long since « fortified » for God by the Spirit of God", guillemets in
place. Recorded because a plate reader may want to test *unitos*.

### 0037 @1258B — *qua terram « videbat » serpens insidians* — **MARKER DECLINED**
Another word set in guillemets by Migne himself. The clause construes as printed (the serpent, lying
in wait at the heel, "saw" the ground by that outermost part), oddly but without a false assertion,
so it is rendered literally and left alone. Same reasoning as *munitos*; recorded together with it
because **three suspect words in this range are all in guillemets** (« Aaron », « videbat »,
« munitos »), which may be a habit of Migne's editors worth knowing about at the merge.

### 0039 @1265C — *et **sin** cum nardo quae humilis est* — `[cj:]` TAKEN
*sin* is a real word ("but if"), and it opens a conditional that **never closes** — the sentence runs
straight on into *hoc innuens, ut princeps … adhaereat*. The word that went wrong is a
**correlative**, which is the class the 2026-08-15 extension of Pattern 18 explicitly refuses to
excuse on grounds of incoherence. Rendered "and but if with the spikenard
`[cj: *sin*; read *sit*, "and let it be with the spikenard"]`". ⚠ ***sic*** ("and so with the
spikenard") is the competing conjecture and is nearly as good; *sit* was chosen because it supplies
the main verb the sentence is missing, where *sic* leaves it verbless. The alternative is recorded
here so the choice is visible and reversible.

### 0036 @1256C — a missing full stop, preserved
*sancti … ad superna ascenderunt Sequitur:* — no stop between the sentence and the formula. **No
mark supplied** (Pattern 8): the English reads "…have ascended to things on high There follows:".
It looks like our error and is not.

### 0036 @1257B — a full stop inside a quotation frame, preserved
*Hae tamen genae sic sunt. « ut fragmen mali punici: »* — Migne stops the sentence before the
quotation that completes it, and starts the quotation with a lowercase *ut*. Both preserved:
"Yet these cheeks are thus. « as a piece of a pomegranate: »".

### 0038 @1261B — a space before a semicolon, preserved
*quibus « vivere Christus est, et mori lucrum » ; Deo dicentes assidue:* — the spaced semicolon is
Migne's and is reproduced.

## 4. Vocabulary decisions inside this range (for the merge diff)

- ***capra* vs *caprea* — kept distinct.** The lemma at 1256B reads *greges **caprarum*** ("flocks of
  **goats**"), but Foliot's gloss on it, and his gloss on *de monte Galaad*, both use ***capreae***
  ("**roes**"), and 0037's lemma 5 has *hinnuli **capreae*** ("young **roes**"). Two Latin words,
  two English words, on the *laeva*/*sinistra* precedent; the slip from one to the other is Foliot's
  and is now visible to the reader instead of being smoothed away.
- ***ferculum* = "litter"** (0035 @1253B, recapping Cant. III, 9–10). ⚑ **Merge check:** the
  *ferculum* exposition proper falls in chunks 0030–0034, which this agent did not translate. If
  that range chose another word (palanquin, couch), one must yield — this is a single word inside
  one work, so the within-work rule applies, not the across-works rule.
- ***lectulus* = "little bed"**, matching the 11 occurrences already in 0000–0029.
- ***emissiones* = "sendings forth"** (0039), reasoned above.
- ***mammae* and *ubera* both = "breasts"** (0038), declared above as a deliberate flattening.
- ***Sequitur:* = "There follows:"**, matching the 33 occurrences in 0000–0029.
- **Second person:** every Song lemma in this range addresses one Bride, so *thou/thy/thine*
  throughout (Pattern 17, number not reverence). The plural *vobis / vestrorum* inside the
  Deuteronomy proof-text at 1258C and 1261A correctly turns to "**you** / **your**" inside the
  quotation, and the singular *similem tui* / *mei* stays singular — the number changes with the
  Latin, mid-quotation, exactly as Pattern 17's worked case requires.
