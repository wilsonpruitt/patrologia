# Cruces — Gilbert Foliot, *Expositio in Cantica canticorum* (PL 202, 1147A–1304D)

**What this file is.** The working editorial record for the English of Foliot's exposition
of the Song of Songs: every place in the work where Migne's plate is defective, where his
text diverges from the received one, or where the reading is uncertain — given by column,
with what he prints, what the English does with it, and why. Declined calls are kept here
with their reasons, because a place a reader already examined and let stand is worth as
much to the next reader as a place that was marked.

**What kind of work this is, and why it shapes the notes.** The *Expositio* is a
lemma-and-gloss commentary. Migne prints a verse or half-verse of the Song in italic as a
numbered lemma, and Foliot then expounds it for anything from ten lines to two columns,
re-quoting his own lemma inside guillemets as he goes — often four or five times, and
often not in the same words. **Most of the textual interest in this work is in those
re-quotations, not in the lemmata:** every named Song lemma in Caput III, IV, VI and VII
agrees with the Vulgate word for word, while the glosses beneath them print the same
clause three and four ways. So an entry below will frequently record that a lemma is clean
*and* that the plate diverges from it eight lines later.

**How to read the entries.** They run in one ascending sequence by column, 1147A to 1304D,
grouped under the work's own chapters — not by the order in which they were written, and
not in batches. A reader arriving from a work page with one column in hand should be able
to find that column and stop. Each entry opens with its column and (where it helps) the
book or lemma at issue, then states the printed reading, then what was done. The markers
named in them are the edition's standing apparatus:

| marker | what it means |
|---|---|
| `[sic: …]` | Migne's type is broken or the word is not a word; the printed run is carried untranslated inside the marker, the conjecture lives here in the cruces, never in the text |
| `[cj: …]` | the printed word is a *real* word, so nothing is broken — but the faithful English asserts something the author is arguing against. The gloss is **additive**: Migne's word keeps its English and the conjecture stands beside it |
| `[var: …]` | Migne's scripture citation diverges from the received text in a way that changes the sense. The English renders what he prints; the marker names the received reading |
| `[d: …]` | dittography — the plate sets a run twice; both are printed and the repeat is wrapped |
| `[nt: …]` | one of Migne's notes that is a *statement* rather than a locator, so it is translated |
| `[ed: …]` | the edition's own voice, for a hole in **our** digitization — never for a hole in the plate |

**Three things this file does not do.** It never repairs the page: a construable but wrong
reading is rendered literally and the conjecture is recorded here. It never corrects one of
Migne's `[n: …]` citations, however plainly wrong — his notes ride verbatim, and a note that
sits in an *Ibid.* chain is untouchable, because the chain resolves through it. And it does
not harmonize this commentary with any other commentary on the Song; each stands on its own.

## Work-wide conventions

Fixed once and held through all 53 chunks. Where two readers of different parts of the work
reached different answers, the collision and its resolution are named — the resolution, not
just the winner.

### The Song's vocabulary of address and love

- **`amica` = "friend"** — 26 instances, whole-work scope. **This was the largest collision
  in the merge and it was decided by Foliot, not by us.** Two ranges fixed "friend" from two
  independent passages neither could see: at 1196A the gloss turns on the etymology
  (*illam **verbo amicabili** convenit … dicens, « Amica mea »*), and at 1231C Foliot puns on
  the word himself (*ut **amica** nominum positio totum leviget*) and calls the Bride's
  friends *amicos* at 1221C in the same breath as the vocative. A third range fixed
  **"my love"** on the sound but different ground that *amica* must stay a THIRD word,
  distinct from *sponsa* (Bride) and *dilectus* (beloved), with the vocative carrying the
  Bride/Bridegroom gender English loses. **"My love" is not wrong about the vocative; it is
  wrong about who decides.** A rendering that cannot carry Foliot's own pun makes his gloss
  unreadable, and "friend" keeps the third term just as distinct. Merged to **friend**
  everywhere, vocatives included ("my friend" where Migne prints *amica mea*). The masculine
  twin settles with it: at 1281C *talis est **Dilectus** meus, et ipse est **amicus** meus*
  puts both words in one line, rendered "such is my Beloved, and he is my friend"; and the
  work's last lemma, 1303C *amici auscultant*, is glossed *fidelis **amicitiae***, so the
  friendship sense is what Foliot expounds at the end as at the beginning. **Never "bride."**
- **`sponsa` = Bride · `sponsus` = Bridegroom**, with Migne's own capitalization mirrored —
  he varies it, and the English varies with him (lowercase *sponsa* in a lemma reads "bride").
- **`dilectus` = "beloved" · capital `Dilectus` = "the Beloved"**, again mirroring Migne's
  own varying capitalization, occurrence by occurrence, and never normalizing it. He prints
  both cases of the same phrase within a page — 1205B's capital *Dilectus* against 1205C's
  lowercase in the identical clause, 1294A's lowercase re-quotation of a lemma he capitalized
  eight lines above. Foliot's ordinary participial *dilecta* is "beloved" without promotion.

### The praise-words — four Latin adjectives, four English ones

This was the second collision, and it took three ranges and a freed word to resolve.

- **`pulcher` / `pulchra` = "beautiful", never "fair"** — *pulchritudo* is glossed
  continuously for four columns (1207A–1210C), and "fair" would leave the gloss expounding a
  word not on the page. **`pulcherrima` = "most beautiful"**, deliberately unharmonized where
  Migne's re-quotations drop the superlative (see 1194B).
  ⚠ **One partial's vocabulary table (chunks 0010–0014) lists `pulcher` = "fair".** No
  *pulcher* is rendered "fair" in the shipped English of those chunks — the two "fair"s there
  are ordinary prose — so the table is a stale entry, not a divergence in the text. Named so
  that a later sweep does not go hunting.
- **`decora` = "comely" · `decor` = "comeliness"** — Douay's word, and the precedent inside
  this work is Foliot's own prose at 1194C. The gloss depends on it at 1283B
  (*unde patientiae **decorem** obtinet*) and again at 1292D.
- **`speciosa` = "fair" ("my fair one")** — this is the resolution of the three-way collision.
  One range had fixed *speciosa* = "comely one" at 1236C; a later range needed "comely" for
  *decora*, which is glossed in three separate places and could not move. The word that
  freed the deadlock is **`species` = "fairness" / `speciosus` = "fair"**, fixed at 1281A on
  1210B's precedent (*Haec forma, haec species, haec pulchritudo* → "This is the form, this
  the fairness, this the beauty"). With *speciosus* = "fair", *speciosa mea* reads **"my fair
  one"** and all four adjectives stay distinct across the whole work.
  ⛔ **This is a live repair, not a settled fact: the shipped English at 1236C still reads
  "my comely one."** See the open items at the foot of this section.
- **`formosa` = "beautiful"** — the word of *Nigra sum, sed formosa* (1183C) and its fourteen
  re-quotations. It shares its English with *pulchra*, which is a flattening; the two never
  meet in one clause (*formosa* belongs to Caput I, *pulchra* enters at 1194A and takes over),
  so nothing in the work asks the reader to tell them apart.

### The two hands

- **`laeva` = "left hand"** (a hand, answering *dextera* "right hand", which is what the
  gloss at 1221C–1222D argues about: *Dominum laevam pariter habere et dexteram*);
  **`sinistra` = "on the left"**, the bare substantivized adjective with no "hand" supplied,
  because Migne prints no *manus* in the Proverbs III, 16 proof-text and Proverbs is
  describing what Wisdom holds on each side. Two Latin words stay visibly distinct without
  inventing a distinction of sense. `dextera` / `dextra` = "right hand" in both places, since
  Migne prints the same word in both.
  ⚠ **The ruling was made at 1221C and the earlier columns predate it.** At 1173A–1175A the
  two Latin pairs run through the argument about the two breasts and the two Testaments, and
  that range's own note records the decision it made instead — "English has one pair of words
  for two Latin pairs, and the alternation is recorded here rather than invented in the
  translation." So the work carries a declared flattening in Caput I and a declared
  distinction from Caput II onward. **Not silently harmonized at the merge**: the two
  passages argue different things, and no gloss in Caput I turns on *sinistra* against
  *laeva*. Named here because it is the kind of thing a careful reader will find.

### The homograph the work turns on — *mala*

⛔ **`malus, -a, -um` "evil" · `malum` "apple" · `malus, -i` (f.) "apple tree" ·
`malum punicum` / `malum granatum` "pomegranate" · `pomum` "apple".** Every instance in the
work was decided from case, gender and governing verb, never from context alone, and the
decisions are tabulated range by range in the entries below. The distinction is not
pedantry: at 1265B Foliot **contrasts** the two fruits (pomegranates = the martyrs, apples =
the confessors), so a single wrong reading in that chain would destroy the argument and pass
every automated check. The two senses collide inside one eleven-word clause at 1297D
(*ut sibi mala junguntur in malis, et nullis a te potuerunt malis averti*, rendered
apples–apples–evils) and Foliot slides across the homograph six times in four lines at
1298D–1299A. **No apple occurs before 1220C**; Caput I is clean, and every *mala* in it is
"evil".

### Other terms fixed across the work

- **`ferculum` = "litter"** — reconciled across the 0034/0035 seam, and the two readers
  agreed independently. The exposition proper is at 1252B–1253B, and Foliot's gloss is
  **functional, not etymological** (*Surgens enim e lecto Rex, ferculo se suscipit, ut de loco
  feratur ad locum*), so nothing in his argument depends on the word's root and every clause
  construes with "litter" (Douay's word at Cant. 3:9). ⚑ This is deliberately different from
  11550, where Richard *etymologizes* the word (*ferculum a **ferendo***) and it therefore had
  to stay in italic Latin. Both are right as they stand; each commentary stands on its own.
- **`capra` = "goat" · `caprea` = "roe"**, kept distinct wherever they meet — the lemma at
  1256B says *greges **caprarum*** (goats) and Foliot's gloss on it says *capreae* (roes).
  The slip from one to the other is his, and it is now visible instead of smoothed away.
- **`mammae` and `ubera` both = "breasts" — a DECLARED flattening**, not a silent one. Migne
  prints both Latin words in one verse at 1263A and the gloss uses them as plain synonyms in
  a single sentence (« quam pulchrae sunt **mammae** tuae! » *id est, doctrina **uberum**
  tuorum*). "Paps" for *ubera* would split a word the work treats as one. Stated here because
  a reader collating the Latin will see two words and one English word.
- **`custodes` = "keepers" · `vigiles` = "watchmen" · `custodiunt` = "keep" ·
  `circumeunt` = "go about".** ✅ **Checked at the merge and there is no collision**: these
  are two different verses two chapters apart, and Migne agrees with the Vulgate in both —
  Cant. III, 3 (1247A) is *vigiles qui custodiunt*, Cant. V, 7 (1275D) is *custodes qui
  circumeunt*. They must stay visibly distinct because the Song says two different things and
  Foliot glosses them differently (at 1247A the *vigiles* are angels who teach the law of
  confession; at 1277A the *custodes* are the Church's prelates and the *custodes murorum*
  are the angels). The shipped English of 1247A reads "watchmen" throughout, as it must.
- **`mater` = "mother" · `genitrix` = "her that bore her"** — Foliot argues the distinction
  twice (1284C *Haec vero genitrix, eadem quae et ejus mater est: diversitate tamen verborum,
  diversa denotat*; 1299A), so collapsing them would leave those sentences expounding a
  difference not on the page.
- **`charitas` = "charity", always** — never "love", which belongs to *amor* and *dilectio*.
  The distinction is load-bearing at 1154C: *Amor namque in Deum accensus, charitas est* is
  only a statement if the two words differ. *amor* and *dilectio* are both "love": they are
  synonyms in this work, and inventing an English distinction Foliot does not make would be
  the mirror of harmonizing a lemma.
- **`lectulus` = "little bed" · `lectus` = "bed"** — the two stand side by side at 1252B.
  **`virgula` = "little rod"** against *virga* "rod", because Foliot argues the diminutive
  (*quae **non virga, sed « virgula »** recte dicitur*, twice at 1249C); Douay's "pillar of
  smoke" would make the page read "not a pillar, but a pillar". **`areola` = "bed"** (of
  spices) and *not* "little bed", because there the diminutive is **not** argued.
- **`racemus` = "berry" · `botrus` = "cluster"** (1284A, *ex multis racemis botrus unus*).
  **`species` = "fairness"**, **`electus` = "chosen"**, **`tornatilis` = "turned"**,
  **`guttur` = "throat"** (never "palate" — the gloss apostrophizes *O beatum guttur*),
  **`suavis` = "sweet"** beside `dulcis` "sweet", **`pacificus` = "the peaceable one"**,
  **`Sulamitis` = "Sulamitess"**, **`sexaginta` = "sixty" and `octoginta` = "eighty"**, never
  "threescore" — Foliot's gloss is arithmetical for a full column (*sive sexies decem, aut
  decies sex*), and "threescore queens" glossed by "the number sixty" would explain a word the
  page does not print. **`campus` = "plain" against `ager` = "field"**, and
  **`villa` / `oppidum` / `civitas` = "village" / "town" / "city"**, all three ranked in one
  sentence at 1295B.
- **Latin kept visible where the gloss IS the etymology** (the *stylus*/*epistylium*
  precedent): *murenulae* (1199B), *monilia* with *monendo* (1289B), *collum* with
  *colligando* (1198D), *imber* against *pluvia* in the defining sentence only (1232D),
  *aurora* / *hora aurea* (1285B), *ager* / *agi* (1294D), *femina vel femora* (1289C),
  *ubera* from *ubertas* (1173A), *serum* gathered *sero* (1173C), *theoria* beside
  *contemplatio* (1152D), *Acoluthus* and *Sirasirim* (the epistle and 1150A),
  *pomum* beside *malum* at 1213D, and the Greek **θεοτόκον** at 1267B with Foliot's own
  Latin gloss translated after it, as he wrote it.
- **Second person is NUMBER, not reverence** (Pattern 17), throughout. The corpus's cleanest
  worked case is in one sentence of the 1638 dedication — *Amplissima Dominatio **Vestra*** →
  "**Your** most Ample Lordship" (plural) against *Amplitudini **Tuae*** → "**Thy**
  Amplitude" (singular); Young switches and the English switches with him. The daughters of
  Jerusalem take "you"; the Bride, the reader and God take "thou". The number changes
  mid-quotation where the Latin changes (1258C, 1261A).
- **Heads.** `CAPUT PRIMUM.` → `CHAPTER ONE.`, `CAPUT II.`–`CAPUT VIII.` → `CHAPTER II.`–
  `CHAPTER VIII.`, following the sibling Song commentary 11550/10804 and the corpus majority.
  Migne's numbered section heads pass through unchanged — **including his two lemmata both
  numbered "5." in one chunk at 1290D and 1291D**, which are reproduced and not renumbered.
  `(cont.)` is our chunk division and is carried untranslated.

### ⬜ Open at the merge — two items the merge could not close from the files

1. **The 1189A seam — RESOLVED 2026-08-17, and it was wider than one seam.** Cant. I, 6 (*Indica mihi, quem diligit anima mea, ubi pascas, ubi cubes in
   meridie*) is the lemma of one chunk and is re-quoted and glossed in the next, and the two
   were written by readers who could not see each other:

   | | at 1189A (the lemma-bearing chunk) | at 1192D–1193A (the gloss) |
   |---|---|---|
   | *quem diligit anima mea* | whom my soul **loves** | whom my soul **loveth** |
   | *ubi pascas* | where thou **feedest** | where thou **pasturest** |
   | *in meridie* | **in the** midday | **at** midday |

   **Resolved to the left-hand column, and applied work-wide.** *quem diligit anima mea* is
   not a two-chunk problem: it is printed **thirteen times across four chunks** (0014 ×4,
   0015 ×1, 0032 ×4, 0033 ×4), and three ranges had settled it two ways — nine sites read
   "loveth", four read "loves". All thirteen now read **"loves"**, and 1193C reads "in the
   midday" with its lemma at 1189A.

   Two independent grounds, neither of them taste: **the lemma governs its own
   re-quotations** (the rule already applied at the 0048/0049 seam), and 0014 carries the
   phrase's FIRST lemma — Cant. I, 6 precedes 0032's Cant. III, 1. The corpus register agrees:
   across 11550 and 11066 the running prose runs *loves* 68 / *loveth* 8, *says* 480 /
   *saith* 3. `translation-style.md` PERMITS traditional wording where the quoted Latin matches
   the standard text; it does not require it, and it cannot license one work saying both.

   ⚑ **How it was nearly missed, which is the part worth keeping.** A first pass grepped for
   "pasturest" and "at midday", got almost nothing back, and came within one edit of
   recording that this entry was stale and the divergence imaginary. It was not: 1193C
   abridges *ubi pascas* away entirely (so "pasturest" was never going to appear), and the
   surviving "at midday" sat deep inside a 400-word line that the search window truncated.
   **The apparatus said the site was there and the search said it was not — and the apparatus
   was right.** Same failure as the *Martyrs of Palestine* case: an empty grep is not absence.

   Both are internally consistent and they cannot both ship. **This merge did not choose**,
   because choosing is a translation decision and the fix is an edit to the chunk files, not
   to this record. The evidence in hand: the lemma-bearing chunk's own gloss runs on feeding
   (*pastorem*, *pascit Deus*, *Spirituale pabulum*) while keeping "pasture" free for
   *pabulum*; Migne's Latin agrees with the Vulgate here, so the scripture rule permits the
   traditional cadence, and Douay reads *feedest … in the midday*; 11550, the nearest control,
   renders the same verse *at midday*. **Whichever is chosen must be applied in both places.**
2. **1236C *speciosa mea* still reads "my comely one"** in the shipped English, against the
   ruling above that frees "comely" for *decora* and gives *speciosa* "fair". One line of
   English has to move for the four praise-words to be distinct across the work.

## ⬜ STANDING ITEM 1 — THREE SENTENCES WITH NO APODOSIS. It is a class, not an accident.

**1159D · 1269D · 1299C.** Found by three readers who could not see one another, in three
separate parts of the work, each rendering the sentence **ending where Migne ends** and each
deliberately taking **no marker**. Two made it worth watching; three settles it: *this plate
drops limbs.*

- **@1159D** — *et ut homo de manu Domini bonum sine fine meruit, cum a veritate recessit.*
  The *ut* clause has no *sic* clause. The argument requires "as man deserved good without
  end …, **so he deserved evil without end** when he departed from the truth", and the second
  limb is simply not on the plate.
- **@1269D–1270A** — *Cui cum in corde vigilavit ad intelligentiam, qui sibi septem annorum
  futuram copiam; et septem annorum inediam declaravit.* Two relatives (*Cui … qui*) and no
  principal verb: the limb that should say Joseph was the one who watched in heart for him is
  absent.
- **@1299C** — *Haec enim **si** cohaerentia sunt, cor deliberans, et brachium id quod corde
  deliberatur exsequens.* The protasis is complete, the two participial limbs are complete,
  and there is no main clause. The very next sentence starts a fresh conditional (*Quae si
  ambo memoriale … tenent … cohibebunt*) which **does** have its apodosis.

**Why none of them is marked.** `[ed: …]` is the edition's voice for what **our**
digitization lost, and nothing here is lost on our side: at 1299C the TEI, the Latin chunk
and the plate all stop in the same place. There is no marker class for a lacuna the *printer*
left, so the English simply stops where Migne stops and the argument visibly breaks off.

⬜ **For Wilson.** Three independent sightings is a measurement, not an impression, and the
question is what a reader should meet. The options, none free: leave them silent (current
behaviour — the argument stops and nothing says so); log all three here (done, and cheap and
honest, but invisible to a reader of the text); or open a marker class for a **printed**
lacuna as distinct from a digitization loss. ⚠ The last is a real extension of the apparatus
and would need a sweep of the other 105 works, because nothing has ever counted them. **Do
not open it casually.** Note also that all three were found by *translating*, not by any
check — no verifier in the pipeline can see this class.

## ⬜ STANDING ITEM 2 — "MIGNE AGAINST HIMSELF", and what Pattern 14 cannot say

The work quotes itself constantly, and it diverges from itself when it does. **The apparatus
has no vocabulary for that.** A `[var: …]` must open by naming an EXTERNAL witness
(Vulg. | LXX | Gk | Heb. | Douay | Vet. Lat.) — the validator rejects anything else — and it
must not carry content that appears verbatim in the Latin twin, which is exactly what a
self-quotation guarantees. So every one of these sites lives here, and **the reader of the
page sees nothing**.

**Fourteen sites, and for each the question that actually decides it: would it earn a marker
ON MATERIALITY?** That distinction is the one the 1199D repair established, and it must not
be skipped: *"re-opening the content with `Vulg.` would have made the marker validate while
asserting a materiality it doesn't have… **A marker that validates is not thereby earned.**"*
Passing the validator is a floor, not a warrant — the same shape as "a flagged passage is not
a checked passage."

⚑ **The mechanism, diagnosed and bounded.** The blocker is **the LENGTH OF THE OVERLAP with
the Latin twin, not self-quotation as such.** The proof is 1270B, whose `[var:]` **survived**
the mirror test on exactly the shape that blocked 1176B's — because Migne's own re-quotation
there stops short of the full received phrase, so the overlap was partial. A second face of
the same mechanism: **a `[var:]` whose content is a single Latin word will trip the mirror
test whenever that word occurs anywhere in the twin**, which for a common word (*Deus*, *rex*)
is nearly always. Two markers in this work (1215A, 1216B) had their contents rewritten from
single words into phrases purely to clear the test, readings unchanged — **so the apparatus
silently dictated the marker's prose.** Every later `[var:]` in the work was written
phrase-length for that reason.

⚑ **The proposal, for Wilson, in two parts.** (a) Compare a marker's content against the
**sentence** it sits in rather than the whole chunk. That keeps the protection the test exists
for — we still cannot put a `[var:]` round our own words — while unblocking the case where a
work quotes itself twenty lines later and diverges. (b) Consider whether the witness list
should gain an **internal** term (something like `Migne @<col>`) so a self-divergence can be
stated in the apparatus instead of buried here. ⛔ **Do not change the validator without
Wilson: it is the check that keeps a marker from claiming our prose is Migne's.** And argue
the case on **1176B or 1194B**, which earn a marker on materiality — never on 1199D, which
was mechanically blocked *and* unearned.

across 400 TEI files: ZERO notes begin `F.` or `f.`** — the conventional *fortasse* form. The
layer is absent from the whole PL corpus we build on, not just this work.

conjectures — precisely the apparatus that resolves cruces.** 1254B is the worked example: we
declined to conjecture at a site where Migne had already published his conjecture. Every
`[cj:]` this project has ever declined *may* have had an answer printed at the foot of its own

## ⬜ STANDING ITEM 3 — THE PLATE-READ QUEUE (original entries, now answered above)

Four sites where our files cannot settle the question and one look at PL 202 can. In priority
order.

1. **@1303C — *Centum* against the lemma's *ducenti*.** The lemma prints *ducenti*, two
   hundred, and the gloss's first limb correctly renders it ("by the two hundred, who keep the
   fruits thereof") — and then the very next sentence divides them and speaks of
   « **Centum** vero qui supersunt », "the hundred that remain." Two hundred split into a first
   group and *a hundred* remaining. **Deliberately unmarked:** a number is neither broken type
   nor a negation nor a correlative, so no existing pattern reaches it, and `[sic:]` cannot
   wrap perfectly good type. The conjecture is that *Centum* stands for a second hundred, or
   that the plate has dropped a *C*. **The cheapest decisive read in the work.**
2. **@1202D — *pallium suum* for Gen. XLIX, 11's *pullum suum*.** The colt vanishes and a
   cloak takes its place — and *pallium* has just been correctly glossed two lines above, from
   the other half of the same verse, as *vestem exteriorem*, so the printed reading makes the
   verse say *pallium* twice. **The work gives no independent witness either way**: Foliot's
   gloss (*ad quem asina alligata est*) covers only the she-ass and never the colt, so it is
   compatible with what is printed. A `[var:]` ships on the received reading; the plate decides
   whether Migne's compositor or Migne's copy is responsible.
3. **@1254B — *quanam* with no noun to agree with, and two good conjectures competing.**
   *quanam « Aaron » filiabus Sion consulitur egredi…?* — a feminine ablative interrogative
   with nothing to agree with, and « Aaron », in Migne's own guillemets, standing where that
   noun should be, in a paragraph that has nothing to do with Aaron. The two readings:
   ***egressione*** ("by what **going forth**"), much the stronger contextually — the whole
   paragraph is a taxonomy of *egressiones*, the preceding sentence ends on *egressio* and the
   following clause answers *non ea utique qua Cain egressus est*; and ***ratione*** ("by what
   **reasoning**"), the ordinary idiom for *quanam*, palaeographically no worse. **Two competing
   good readings is exactly when Pattern 18 says decline and log**, which is what happened.
   A plate read decides it; a vote does not.
4. **@1304D — the possibly-missing colophon.** The work ends mid-thought on *conquiescit.*
   with no colophon, no *Explicit*, no *Amen* and no closing note. ✅ **This was verified at
   the SOURCE, and the check is the point:** `sources/pl/tei/11613.xml` closes straight from
   that word into `</p></div2></div1></body>` — no `<trailer>`, no `<closer>`, no trailing
   note, no further `<pb/>`. **Nothing was dropped between source and chunk**, which is the
   thing our files can actually settle. ⚠ What they **cannot** settle is whether Migne's plate
   carries a colophon that Corpus Corporum dropped, and a twelfth-century Song commentary
   ending with no formula at all is unusual. Worth one look, on the principle that **where our
   text lacks something the genre always has, suspect OUR files first.**

⚑ **A locating heuristic worth testing while the plate is open.** Three suspect words in one
range — « Aaron » (1254B), « videbat » (1258B), « munitos » (1261A) — all sit inside **Migne's
own guillemets**, and all three `[cj:]` calls on them were declined. That may be coincidence at
n=3, but if it holds it locates defects: the guillemets mark where his compositor was setting
quoted matter, which is where type most often went wrong. **Test it before believing it** — the
corpus is its own control.

## The dedicatory epistle and the Preface — 1147A–1150C

Patrick Young's 1638 dedication to William Juxon, Foliot's letter to Robert, and the
*Gilberti praefatio*, before the exposition proper begins.

- **@1147A** — *amico suo charissimo*, Foliot to Robert. An ordinary masculine *amicus*,
  rendered "friend"; not the Song's *amica* and not "bride". Recorded because the count of
  *amicus* against *amica* is what the whole-work ruling rests on.
- **@1147A (the epistle) — `[sic: *Reverendissim*]`.** The vocative has lost its final *-e*
  in Young's dedication. It sits inside an already-italic run, so it takes no second italic
  marker.
- **@1147A (the epistle) — *Acoluthus* kept in italic Latin.** Young means the companion piece
  printed with the *Expositio* in 1638 ("for to go forth into public alone, without a
  companion, was neither seemly"); "acolyte" would carry a liturgical sense the sentence does
  not have.
- **@1147A (the epistle) — Pattern 17's cleanest worked case, and both halves are in one
  sentence.** *Amplissima Dominatio **Vestra*** → "**Your** most Ample Lordship",
  grammatically plural; *Amplitudini **Tuae*** → "**Thy** Amplitude", grammatically singular.
  Young switches; the English switches with him. Number, never reverence.
- **@1148A · Cant. VIII, 6** — *Fortis est ut mors dilectio*, where the Vulgate has *quia*
  before it. Simple abridgement; no `[var:]`.
- **@1149A · Gen. I, 2 — `[var: Vulg. *inanis et vacua*]`.** Migne prints *terra autem erat
  **informis***, the Old-Latin/patristic form. Marked because Foliot's *etc.* stops the
  quotation immediately after, so the divergent word is the last one he prints and the reader
  has nothing else to go on.
- **@1149A · Exod. I, 1** — *ingressi sunt **in** Aegyptum* for the Vulgate's *ingressi sunt
  Aegyptum*. Preposition only; no marker.
- **@1149A — an unstated subject that inverts the sense.** *haec, quos irae filios, dictante
  justitia, perfecerat, in libertatem gratiae … reformat.* The subject of both verbs is
  grammatically *haec* (= *dilectio*), which makes **love** the maker of the sons of wrath;
  the sense wants *justitia*, which the ablative absolute has already used up. Rendered with a
  neutral "it", preserving the ambiguity rather than choosing.
- **@1149D — *Seorsum **vos**, abstractus a **vobis***.** The one plural second person in
  Gilbert's own prose, addressed by the soul to the world's goods she is dismissing. Rendered
  "you"; everything else in his prose is *tu* / "thou".
- **@1150A — `[sic: *implieetur*]`** for *implicetur*, *ie* for *c*. It is the clause's only
  verb, so the sense stands in plain words beside the marker: "be not **entangled**
  [sic: *implieetur*] in these things".
- **@1150A — *Sirasirim*, twice, left as printed.** Foliot's transliteration of the Hebrew
  title; he glosses it himself in the next breath (*quod « Canticum canticorum » appellatur*).
- **@1150A ff. — *Parabolae* against *Proverbia*: a real distinction, not a synonym.**
  Solomon's first book is « Parabolarum » here and *Parabolas* / *Parabolarum volumine*
  throughout the following column, and the lemma at 1150D is *Parabolae Salomonis* — all
  rendered **"Parables"**, because the gloss is about the book's title. But at 1152D Migne
  writes *jam edocta **in Proverbiis***, rendered **"in the Proverbs"**. Foliot uses both names
  for one book; the English keeps both, because collapsing them would conceal that he does.
- **@1150B — *Animam igitur, vel Ecclesiam*: the sentence that sets the work's allegory.**
  *Ecclesia* = Church and *anima* = soul are fixed from here, and they meet head-on in this
  clause. Migne prints *ecclesia* lowercase through 1159B–C and capitalized elsewhere;
  followed, not regularized. Note also lowercase *sponsum* here against the capitalized
  *Sponso*/*Sponsum* of 1153B–C — again followed.

## Caput I — 1149C–1211D

### Lemma 1, *Osculetur me osculo oris sui* — 1149C–1172B

- **@1149C · lemma 1 · Cant. I, 1** — *Osculetur me osculo oris sui.* **Agrees with the
  Vulgate exactly**; no `[var:]`. Fixed English: **"Let him kiss me with the kiss of his
  mouth,"** held identical through **twenty-one** further re-quotations. Only the terminal
  mark varies, and it varies **because Migne varies it**, so it is carried each time: `?` at
  1149C and 1153B (both inside guillemets); `:` at 1149D, 1154A, 1163A; `;` at 1162D (in the
  *acsi dicat* frame); `.` everywhere else. The lemma is also fragmented into its own words
  twice — « *Osculetur* » alone at 1163A and « *Osculum* » alone at 1163C — rendered
  « Let him kiss » and « A kiss », matching the fixed rendering word for word.
- **@1151A · I Cor. XV, 46 — a missing *non*, checked under 7a and DECLINED.** Migne prints
  *Prius namque quod animale est, deinde quod spirituale*, where the Vulgate states this by
  **negation** (*sed non prius quod spiritale est*). Nothing is asserted here that the Vulgate
  denies: the abridgement converts the Vulgate's own negative construction into the
  affirmative it entails, and Foliot's gloss (first the animal, then the spiritual) is what
  both forms say. **Recorded because a reader comparing the two will notice the missing
  *non*** — and because a dropped negation is the one class that must never be passed over
  silently.
- **@1151B · Heb. XI, 6 — `[var: Vulg. *inquirentibus se*]`.** Migne prints *quod
  **diligentibus** se remunerator sit* — seeking becomes **loving**. Load-bearing in a
  commentary whose entire argument is that love, not inquiry, reaches God; the gloss two
  sentences later builds on *amor*.
- **@1151B and @1163A · Heb. XI, 6 — the same verse printed two ways within one work.**
  *sine fide placere Deo est impossibile* here, *sine fide Deo placere impossibile est* at
  1163A. Word order only. **This is Migne's inconsistency, not ours**; both rendered as
  printed, and the English differs in nothing but the same word order.
- **@1151D · Gen. I, 28** — *universis quae moventur* for Vulg. *universis **animantibus** quae

- **@1152A · Eccle. XII, 1** — *Memento **Domini** Creatoris tui* for Vulg. *memento creatoris

- **@1152A · Eccle. XII, 13** — *hoc est omnis homo* for Vulg. *hoc est **enim** omnis homo*.
  Declined deliberately: Foliot glosses the clause three separate ways at 1152C and none of
  the three turns on *enim*.
- **@1152B–C — *amor* / *dilectio* / *charitas*, and why only one of the three is fixed.**
  All three are in play here and at 1154C, where Foliot varies them for style within two
  sentences. *charitas* is **always** "charity", because *Amor namque in Deum accensus,
  charitas est* is a statement only if the words differ. *amor* and *dilectio* are both
  "love"; they are synonyms here, and inventing an English distinction Foliot does not make
  would be the mirror of harmonizing a lemma.
- **@1152C — the lemma *mandata ejus **observa*** re-quoted as « mandata **servare** ».**
  Both rendered "keep the commandments": the verb is synonymous and the gloss turns on
  *timere*/*amando*, not on the verb of keeping.
- **@1152D — *theoria* kept in italic Latin.** Foliot has *contemplatio* twelve times in the
  same chunk and builds an argument on it (*amorem contemplatio suscitet*); rendering
  *theoria* "contemplation" too would fuse two words he keeps apart at the very moment he is
  defining one of them.
- **@1153B — an imperfect subjunctive among perfects.** *Sponsum prorsus **nesciret*** in a

### Lemmata 14–16, *Ecce tu pulchra es* to *laquearia nostra cupressina* — 1206C–1211D

- **@1206C · Luc. XXIII, 34 — `[var: Vulg. *dimitte illis: non enim sciunt*]`.** Migne prints

- **@1206D · lemma 14 · Cant. I, 14** — *Ecce tu pulchra es, amica mea, ecce tu pulchra.*
  **= Vulgate.** Rendered with *amica* = "my friend" per the ruling. "Thine eyes are those of
  doves" is the fixed rendering of the bare genitive here, and the same rendering is held at
  1255C.
- **@1206D ⭐ and @1209B — the gender English cannot carry, and what carries it instead.** The
  two lemmata stand consecutively and are grammatically opposed: *Ecce tu **pulchra** es, amica
  mea* (feminine, the Bride) and *Ecce tu **pulcher** es, Dilecte mi, et decorus* (masculine,

  vocative Migne prints and by nothing else** — *amica mea* can only be the Bride, *Dilecte mi*

  reinforces it independently at 1209C: *Sic amans amanti pari respondet elogio*.

  in it. **The familiar Douay cadence is exactly the pressure that had to be resisted here.**

## Caput III — 1243C–1255B

⚑ **Every Song lemma in this chapter agrees with the Vulgate.** The three `[var:]` markers
taken here are all on *other* books — Romans, Matthew, Matthew again — which is the shape to
expect once the lemmata themselves are clean: **the commentary's own text is stable and the
proof-texts adduced round it are not.**

- **@1243C · lemma 1 · Cant. III, 1** — *In lectulo meo per noctes quaesivi quem diligit anima
  mea; quaesivi illum, et non inveni.* **= Vulgate** (the Vulgate sets `:` where Migne sets `;`
  — punctuation only, carried as printed). *lectulus* = "little bed".
- **@1243C — a full stop that breaks the sentence in two, and the English is as broken as the
  plate.** *Exoravit haec Sponsa, vel fidelis quaecunque anima. Dilectum suum, ut coelum non
  deserens…* — the stop leaves *Dilectum suum*, the accusative object of *Exoravit*, stranded at
  the head of the next sentence. Rendered with the stop where he prints it: "…has entreated. Her
  Beloved, that, not deserting heaven…". **Not marked: no type is defective and nothing is
  missing. Deliberately visible.**
- **@1243D — `[sic: *perfecto*]`, and it is the dangerous class again.** *ibi **perfecto** luxus
  exuberat.* *perfecto* is a real Latin form with **no grammatical slot** — no noun to agree
  with and no construction that takes it. The near-certain reading is ***profecto***,
  "assuredly", the intensifier the clause wants. **A real word takes no automated flag**, and
  this is the same shape as 1192A's `[sic: *suum*]`. The quarantined run carries no negation, no
  conjunction and no verb — deleting it leaves *ibi luxus exuberat*, which is complete — **so
  nothing needed supplying beside the marker.**
- **@1244B · Rom. VIII, 38 — `[var: Vulg. *Certus sum enim*, "For I am sure"]`.** Migne prints
  ***Scimus enim***. **Person and modality both move**: Paul's personal certainty becomes the
  community's knowledge — and Foliot is at that moment arguing about what a whole ORDER of souls

  Item 3.
- **@1303C · lemma 13 · Cant. VIII, 13** — *Quae habitas in hortis, amici auscultant: fac me
  audire vocem tuam.* **= Vulgate.** ⭐ ***amici* = "the friends"** — the *amica*/*amicus* ruling
  arriving at its last site in the work, **and here it is doubly right: the gloss glosses the word
  with *fidelis **amicitiae*** at 1304A, so the friendship sense is what Foliot expounds.**
  *habitas*, *tuam* are singular — **"thou"**, throughout the lemma and every re-quotation.
- **@1303D–1304A — a re-quotation printed BARE, and left bare.** The lemma is re-quoted three
  times, twice inside guillemets (« Quae habitas in hortis, fac me audire vocem tuam. » and the
  split pair « Amici » … « auscultant; ») and once with **no guillemets at all**. **Guillemets are
  1:1 and none is supplied.**
- **@1304A · lemma 14 · Cant. VIII, 14** — *Fuge Dilecte mi, et assimilare capreae hinnuloque
  cervorum super montes aromatum.* **= Vulgate** except that the Vulgate punctuates *Fuge,
  dilecte mi* and Migne prints ***Fuge Dilecte mi*** — **no comma, and a capital *D*. Both are
  carried:** the English lemma reads "Flee my Beloved" with no comma, while **both** gloss
  re-quotations (1304A, 1304C) print the comma and read "« Flee, my Beloved. »". **No `[var:]` —
  a comma is punctuation and the capital is the standing mirroring convention, not a reading.**
  ***caprea* = "roe", never goat**; *hinnulo cervorum* = "the fawn of the harts", held identical
  in the lemma and in both gloss re-quotations.
- **@1304B · Matth. XXV, 34** (*Venite, benedicti Patris mei…*) **carries no `[n:]` at all**,
  where the *Ecce Sponsus venit* two lines above does. **Verbatim Vulgate. Not a defect — Migne
  simply does not note it — and recorded only so a later indexer knows the omission is his.**
- **@1304C — « aromatum. » is a ONE-WORD guillemet unit.** Migne quotes the single word he is
  about to gloss. **Kept as its own unit, "« of spices. »", not merged into the surrounding
  sentence.**
- **@1304D ⛔ — THE WORK ENDS HERE, on *conquiescit.*, with no colophon, no closing formula, no
  *Explicit*, no *Amen* and no editorial note.** ✅ **Verified against the TEI, not inferred from

  dropped by a chunker: `sources/pl/tei/11613.xml` closes *…certissima Sponsi sui promissione

## Checked and deliberately NOT reported

So that a later reader knows these were looked at rather than missed.

- **Column-band arithmetic.** The bands are not four per column, and single-band jumps
  (1192D → 1194A, 1197A → 1197B, 1206C → 1206D → 1207A, 1253C → 1268A) are normal. Anchor
  **sequence** — not merely count — was diffed against the Latin twin at every chunk boundary in
  the work; no text is broken mid-word or mid-construction at any anchor.
- **Marker parity, both directions, counted rather than eyeballed.** Column anchors, `[n:]`
  notes, « », `?`, `!` and `## ` heads were counted against each Latin twin before and after
  writing, **including the eleven deliberately mirrored guillemet imbalances** (1180C, 1184B,
  1205B, 1213C, 1217C, 1217A, 1227A, 1227C, 1240A, 1243A, 1244D, 1251A, 1259D, 1266C, 1268B,
  1271A, 1272B, 1301A). Where an English chunk's guillemet count looks unbalanced against the
  body, the extra `«` is in the `incipit:` frontmatter field, copied verbatim from the Latin
  twin — **a known measurement artefact, not a defect.**
- **No `[f: …]` tags anywhere in the work.** This is a lemma-and-gloss commentary, not a
  florilegium. The four constructions that might have tempted a tag — the inline italic
  *(Joan. XV, 5)* at 1217B, *(Hebr IV, 12)* at 1230D, *(Sirac III, 20)* at 1265C and
  *(Matth. IV, 17)* at 1274C — are each **reproduced verbatim, untranslated, in place**, on the
  `[n: …]` principle.
- **`(cont.)`** on every repeated chapter head is our chunk division, carried untranslated.
  Migne's numbered section heads (`## 5.`, `## 6.`, `## 10.` and the rest) pass through unchanged.
- **Medieval spellings** — *lethale*, *charitas*, *coelum*, *seculi*, *foenum*, *thymiamata*,
  *Bersabe*, *Haram*, *seraphin*, *abcessum*, *abcessisse*, *adolescentulae*, *exultabimus*,
  *contepescat*, *conviventia* — translated normally, no italics, no crux.
- **Paragraphing follows the plate exactly** throughout, including where a paragraph break falls
  mid-argument.
- **Em-dashes: none in the work.** Migne prints none; commas, colons and full stops carry the
  sense everywhere it was tested.
- **Negation fidelity was checked clause by clause across the work**, and the count holds: every
  *non* / *nec* / *neque* / *nisi* / *nullus* / *nihil* / *nunquam* the Latin prints appears in
  the English, and **none appears that the Latin does not print.** The dense sites are named
  where they fall — six *non*s in one sentence at 1242A, five *nulla*s at 1252A, five *neque*s at
  1231C, four in the gloss at 1248B, five negatives in Rom. VIII at 1287A, the three *non*s of
  Psalm 1 at 1302D, and the two apparent polarity defects at 1151A and 1241D, **both of which
  were examined and both of which construe.**
