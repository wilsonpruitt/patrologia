## PROEM ON THE SONGS.

[0181]

[0181C]A certain king of kings and lord of things (and if anything worthier or more excellent can be named, whereby his power and powerful excellence may be commended) obtained a region of wondrous and unheard-of greatness, in which he decreed to make no portion divided off for another. It seemed indeed agreeable to his good pleasure that his kingdom should remain undivided to himself; and that none there should be reckoned greater than he, or even his equal, who by an equal power, rebellious and insolent, might be hurried away into an affront. A certain part of that region is held to be loftier, brighter, more honourable, which seems the worthier for his more frequent presence; the other part far lower, more rugged, dark, to its own Lord perhaps not gracious by an equal merit. But in that part over which [0182C] he presided above, a great order of many princes attended upon him, who had received a command to serve their lord with bowed neck, and with unwearied zeal to modulate praises consonant with him. Wherefore he also made them chief and conspicuous by a wondrous and admirable dignity, and, as it were, by a certain secondary power upon the throne of the kingdom; so that they might preside in excellence over all who lived in the whole kingdom, and might themselves lie subject to their own Lord with worthy reverence. One of them, who seemed to himself not inferior to the rest, who rejoiced in the benefit of comeliness and honour he had found, conceived something lofty and rash against his lord; and seeking greater things, judged that he would be like him. And in this very many out of so great a number of great ones consented with him, who [0183A] presumed to think themselves equal to the highest of all; and he at once despoiled them all of the glory conferred upon them, and, despoiled, destined them for the lowest part of that kingdom. For in the highest part he suffered nothing repugnant, nothing unfitting, nothing troublesome, nothing at all dishonourable to linger; but the evil once arisen he presently cast down by a severe judgment, and set the evil ones far from the serene glory of his countenance. But they, being set far off, did not even so cease from malice [n: (Sap. XII)], but infected the dwellers of that outermost part with the venom of their own wickedness, and forced them to become rebels against the lord of them all, and drove them by threats and by force, by wiles and by blandishments, into a like injury. For there that highest king, by a counsel which he himself knows, suffered the evil to run wild and grow strong, nor did he at once restrain each evil man with the hastened sickle of punishments; [0183B] but, though he could either by a nod alone forbid that they should be done, or presently avenge each hurtful deed once done, he seemed wondrously patient to forget them as they went on to worse. So in a short time, as much by the prompting of the greater as by the consent of the lesser, that whole lower part of the kingdom is depraved, and all flesh corrupted venerates its king and lord neither out of fear nor out of love; but him whom, being present, it both does not see with the eye and, with a malevolent heart, now does not feel, to his commands (if perchance it sometimes hears them) it does not consent. Sometimes indeed, looking down upon them from on high, he destined his messengers for them, and speaking in many parts and in many ways, called back those who had gone astray through error to better things and to things next to salvation; but very many of them, entangled in the hard and long-lasting bonds of the vices, [0183C] were softened neither by his warnings nor by his promises. Only a very few, to whom the spiritual teaching was made brighter and more pleasing, or whom perhaps natural reason and understanding admonished, had by a sounder counsel a humble regard toward that their King, and, good men among the evil, worshipped the Lord according to place and time. Yet in so many spaces both of places and of times men of this kind were found so rarely that, since — as was said before — all flesh had been corrupted, that king could easily know his own by name everywhere among the nations, whereas the multiplicity of the evil could now be comprehended by no number of men. And this endured not only for many years, but for many ages of years, in which against their king and against his law the [0183D] people hardened their neck. All of them guilty, and by the merit of their guilt *103* introduced at the last into the eternal darknesses of the prison, save for the fewer who had before been brought back into his grace. But throughout the whole series of times in which the people was found to be held in the aforesaid error, that king had purposed to have mercy on them at the end of times; in the last age to impart his grace more abundantly to the wretched, whom he saw perishing as much through ignorance as through malice. He chose a wondrous and sufficiently marvellous manner of showing mercy; but the reason why he should choose this rather than another cannot be clearly comprehended, save that he saw it more pleasing to his own good pleasure, and for this reason no one rightly wise doubts that it is better. But this mercy which he was to show to the erring people in the last days, and the manner of showing it whereby he was to fulfil it, he willed to be revealed in [0184A] diverse nations as well as places and times to very few, whom by an ampler grace he willed to be called *seers* or prophets. And they, with the eye of the mind unveiled, willing to open the mystery to the rest who did not know it, nevertheless took pains to open it in figures and riddles; and preaching to the peoples the benefits of the aforesaid king that were to come after, they brought forth many things indeed by tongue or by pen, but obscure ones, so that they might neither, after the manner of the envious, wholly conceal the king's grace, and yet might not divulge the holy mystery to the unbelieving who were unworthy of it. But some of them committed these things, understood and foreseen, to a remembering writing, and so transmitted them to those who were to come long after in the succession; that not only those then present, but rather those coming after, might read true things in the Scriptures, which the [0184B] understanding of those who went before foresaw and did not begrudge to those who should come after. And among those who wrote one after another, a certain man puts himself in the middle, whose knowledge and teaching ecclesiastical authority does not set behind the rest; who, perceiving by a prophetic spirit the secrets of things to come, brought them forth by a wondrous kind of writing to the knowledge of the rest. That highest king of whom we speak, having no end nor beginning of existing, having not by grace but by nature to persist unchangeably; whose strong power it is given to none to have leave to resist, but to whom he wills, and how he wills, a welcome possibility of standing by: he had a Son of the same nature, substance, majesty, coeternal, coequal, of one power through all things. Him he had appointed to send to that depraved and pestilential nation of men, that among them and in the midst of them he might make a lingering dwelling, [0184C] and might take from them a certain virgin to himself by a nuptial covenant, whom nevertheless, above the law of marriages, he would keep entire in mind and body as a virgin. For he saw that by the benefit of marriage anger, brawling, hatred, and rivalry are called away far off, and joy, fairness, welcome fellowship, peace, and love are called back; and that to this not two alone, but many thousands together are called together; and that men who are diverse and (what is more wicked) adverse are effectually drawn on to will the same thing and to be unwilling for the same thing. He saw that nations diverse in tongue and in manners, regions sundered from one another by land, by sea, by much distance between, are alike allied by the reconciled marriage of one bridegroom and bride, and that from then on and thereafter they obey, cultivate, love, and venerate one another. Finally, by the benefit [0184D] of marriage the longed-for offspring of sons is acquired, and among them a welcome brotherhood of minds thrives; and as both brothers and cousins are multiplied, natural love and affection grows beyond measure, and thus the primal usefulness of the nuptial bond is extended to more. In this way, then, or rather in a far more excellent way, he purposed to call back his enemies, and, taking a bride from among them, willed to turn them into friends; so that when men should render the right of brotherhood to the bridegroom of their sister, they might, having found a fitting degree, advance in loving toward the eternal. For the Father, who was appointing to send the Son to this, and the Son, who loved to obey the sender in all things, is God immortal, impassible, invisible by nature, whom no visible creature could either see or hold visibly; and for this reason, among men, the very Son of God [0185A] willed to be received into the virginal bridal chamber of the bride, so that, proceeding thence as a bridegroom, he might be seen and held in nuptial form and habit. And before this should really come to pass, some, as has already been said, foresaw it in the spirit, and left it set down in their writings, some obscurely, some more obscurely. One of them is found to have described the mutual affections of the Bridegroom and the Bride more expressly, and to have woven, as it were, a tale subtle and honourable, spiritual and modest. That man knew that singers, men and women, are employed at temporal marriages, and that by them measures of song and various praises are set forth; that they touch with a taught hand the lyre, the cithara, the symphony, and weave a concordant melody with sackbut, pipe, and psaltery, so that in all these they are found becomingly to rejoice with the nuptial [0186A] chamber, and to admonish the other guests to rejoice together. Formed wondrously by that form or example, our scribe followed up this notable bridal chamber with epithalamic praise: he composed a new and unheard-of drama after the likeness of the comic poets, who, as they speak, bring on the various persons of the stage. This kind of writing antiquity before him certainly did not know, but from him, as it seems, posterity took it up far below: while the schools of the Greeks that followed after, because they could not sufficiently grasp what he wrote mystically and of what, at least transferred that same manner of writing to their own vanities and follies.
