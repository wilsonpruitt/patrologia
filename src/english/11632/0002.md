## EXPLANATION OF THE TITLE OF THE SONG OF SONGS. Now the title of this work is: *Songs of songs of Solomon.*

[0185]

[0185B] *104* Since in this little work he wrote nothing but praises of the Bridegroom and the Bride in concert, he superscribed in the very title Song, or Songs, so that at the very forefront the prudent reader might by that reminder be prepared for the praises of the bridal chamber, and, loving them, might modulate them not by the [sic: *molodia*] of the voice, but by the concert of manners. But because many both before and afterwards are found to have brought forth Songs in their writings, yet not to have described the praises of this bridal chamber in them so excellently; for the commendation of these and for their distinction from the unequal rest, he set down here not only Songs, but Songs of songs. For just as both the Holy of holies and the Sabbath of Sabbaths are more eminently exalted above holy things and Sabbaths, and ages of ages are preferred in dignity and glory to ages; so, since in the writings of the ancients [0185C] there is much commendation of Songs, something ampler and far more sacred is promised and contained by the Songs of songs. And lest the writer of this work be unknown to its readers, and, the writer being unknown, the authority of the writing be lessened, he pointedly set himself to make mention of its author, plainly naming Solomon in that same title. *Songs,* he says, *of songs of Solomon.* [var: Vulg. *Canticum canticorum Salomonis*, singular] This Solomon was held to be a king and a king's son, and for this reason he rightly venerates the highest King, the King's Son; describing with how great a grace of peace his marriage shines out, and with what quiet and glory the noble Bride lies hidden in his bosom. Furthermore, as the Interpreters say, by the name of Solomon *peace* is signified, and his peaceful kingdom is designated by the testimony of Scripture; whose warfare an Egyptian hand did not succour as though he were weak, [0185D] but rather from Dan even to Beersheba Satan, that is the *adversary*, did not meet him. « He had, he says, peace on every side round about, from Dan even to Beersheba [0186B] [n: (III Reg. IV)]. » And below: « Rest, he says, my God has given me round about, and there is no Satan nor evil encounter [n: (III Reg. V)]. » [var: Vulg. *requiem dedit Dominus Deus meus mihi per circuitum*] By whom is signified he who by his own power made both one, who set himself as a corner-stone to the Jews and to the Gentiles coming from opposite sides [n: (Ephes. V)], who, coming from the bosom of the Father, nevertheless did not cast off heavenly things, who coming to the unjust made salvation and justice near to them. He who gathered into one, as by a bond of concord, the earthly and the heavenly, whom not so much the quality of their place as the inequality of their merits had long sundered from one another; when in the bridal chamber of the Bride he celebrated a spiritual marriage, by which he wondrously joined the divine and the human nature in a unity of person. Rightly, therefore, is the describer of this work called Solomon, that is, *the peaceable* [0186C], hinting that a song of this kind is not worthily sung by anyone unquiet, lest anyone think that he has attained the contemplative or theoretic summit of knowledge who has not first been carefully instructed in the moral and the natural. For the more effectual teaching of which, he, being about to write this book, is found to have sent two others before it, and in each to have described each matter with vigilant care; having in the first that which they call ethics or the moral, in the second physics, which they assert is called natural. In the first he teaches the untaught, and composes us to elegance of manners, and while composing them he frequently interposes the Father and the Son; moreover, in the [sic: *tilulo*] also he calls himself a king, a king's son, so that both the dignity of a father and a royal fear may compel us. *The proverbs,* he says, *of Solomon the son of David, [0186D] king of Israel.* In this book he implants a fatherly discipline in the untaught and the little ones, asserting that he who does not hear falls the more heavily into ruin; but that he who understands [0187A] possesses his own helm, because an obedient man ought rather to speak victories than to be silent [n: (Prov. XXI)]. But in the second he teaches a man well composed and endowed with an age now mature, that every creature under the sun lies subject to vanity; and that in the same state no begotten thing abides, but everything that is begotten tends by nature to perishing. *105* And since those things which are of the world are not going to remain in a stable degree, but by an inevitable law their appearance and figure passes away, he probably hints that such things are not to be clung to, but that something else ampler and nearer to salvation is to be sought. But because it is a rare and arduous thing in some manner not to see visible things — that is, to cling to visible things by no affection of the mind — in the title of this book also he called himself a king, a king's son, [0187B] and, as though by right of command, addressed the assembly, that is, the Church. *The words,* he says, *of the Preacher, the son of David, king of Jerusalem.* He willed indeed that those whom the timely sweetness of teaching does not sufficiently allure should be shaken and driven by the untimely authority of a king, and that it should promote them to a more advanced place of natural knowledge, so that they might judge contemptible whatever they know to be unstable by the privilege of a temporal course. In the third volume he makes no mention of royal power, but, that being now buried, he names Solomon simply; showing that they who worthily come to this reading, lulled in the peace that is due, seek the vision of the Bridegroom alone. This reader indeed, having utterly despised what he understands to be transitory, and having learned by every diligent care what is oppressive, now with a freer spirit obtains the contemplative place, [0187C] from which the presence of the Bridegroom shuts out whatever unfitting and harmful thing comes upon him. In these volumes, then, he comprehends moral, natural, and contemplative knowledge; in the first of which the son lays hold of a father's discipline, not of money; in the second he grasps that all things passing under the sun are vain; in the third, having forgotten what is below, he stretches himself wholly into the divine and the heavenly. In the first every untaught man is formed in good manners by a father's watchfulness; in the second the visible world is disfigured for him by the desert of its mutability; in the third that lower man is reformed with a wiped face, and, brought into the sanctuary, is conformed by a welcome likeness to him whom he sees. In the first Abraham hears him commanding, hastens to go out, is prepared for moral [0188A] obedience [n: (Gen. XII)]; in the second Isaac digs wells, seeks waters, searches out natural things [n: (Gen. XXVI)]; in the third Jacob sleeps upon a stone, is present among angels, contemplates divine things [n: (Gen. XXVIII)]; he sees God himself, as he says, face to face, and is saved. In the first he feeds the future bride, still untaught and a little one, with moral precepts, with the rules of discipline, with a saving binding, and shuts her in more strictly and wraps her about; in the second, now grown up, he frees and looses her from the frauds of suitors and from the bonds of deceivers; in the third, now marriageable, the Bridegroom claims her alone for himself alone and joins her to himself, to whose chaste commerce no unchaste person joins himself. Rightly, then, is that first book, the executor of precepts, called ethical or moral; the second, the pursuer of visible things, physical, [0188B] natural; the third, the attainer of invisible things, contemplative, theological, spiritual — the later it is in order, the more principal in a worthier excellence. In it Solomon saw and foresaw that a certain virgin was to be born of the royal stock, one who would please the highest King and his Son far beyond all others; in whom the Son of God would dedicate to himself a temple, a tabernacle, a bridal chamber, that in it he might celebrate a marriage certain to some beforehand, but utterly untried by all. And lest anything honourable or fitting should seem to be lacking to that marriage, he says that with the Bridegroom the friends of the Bridegroom, and with the Bride maidens, are at hand; and so, with four persons as it were speaking to one another, he orders a spiritual epithalamium, by whose frequent approach and withdrawal the nuptial drama is woven. And the virgin whom Solomon sees thus to be loved by the Son [0188C] of God, and to be specially chosen for this ministry, or (if it please better) mystery, is she who is noble in birth, notable in manners, beautiful beyond the daughters of men, wise in letters, humble in spirit, glorious in merits; she, I say — that I may at last name her — is Mary, sprung after Solomon from his posterity, who joyfully occupies the first part of this drama. For whereas many before the virgin spoke very many things here and there about a marriage of this kind, and followed out its heraldings and its prior approaches, Solomon in this book brings forward nothing of theirs into the middle, but suddenly brings on the aforesaid virgin speaking.
