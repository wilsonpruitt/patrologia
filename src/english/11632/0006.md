## CHAPTER V.

*For his breasts are better than wine.* [var: Vulg. *ubera tua*, "thy breasts" — the second person, which the commentary at once expounds and which the lemma is re-printed with later in this same chapter] Rightly, she says, did I long for thy coming far beyond the time promised; rightly did I ask that the kiss of thy coming be given me, because I knew and perceived — not led by the sense of the flesh, not taught by my own spirit but by the divine — that thy breasts are far better than wine. Since in the Bridegroom's kiss she had felt not the breasts but, as it seems, the lips, and for that reason ought consequently to have commended the lips and not the breasts, what does it mean that here, making no memorial of the lips, no word of them, she makes mention, as though by an erring simplicity, of the breasts? For indeed it is not [0196D] bridegrooms' breasts but brides' that are wont to be commended, since it stands fast that children are not suckled at the breasts of men; and as we count worthy of praise those things which we have known to be fruitful, so those same things rightly go without it which remain idle and without fruit. But it is in some manner naturally implanted in every one, that setting aside what is wholly untried by him he turns himself to that which is more familiarly known to him, and takes for his example that which is not uncertain to his art or office. So the sailor, when he speaks, calls all prosperous things gentle breezes, a level sea, a strong sail, oarsmen and a keel; but adverse things, rushing winds, a swelling strait, waters running down into the bilge. The soldier [0197A] too, wordy in almost everything he loves, draws his examples from what belongs to the soldier's office, and scarcely anything sounds in his mouth but lance, sword, shield, helmet or breastplate; and though a degenerate fear sometimes renders these things hostile to him for fighting, yet for speaking they are friendly by familiar use. What, again, do the scholarly clerics speak of but books, or the authors of secular letters? What do the more religious sort bring forward but the testimonies of the divine letters? What, in fine, do we poor men speak of, but the things that have been impressed upon us by poor authors or poor guardians — we, I say, to whom both the dignity of nobles and the abundance of the rich is inaccessible? Therefore, because the things of women are familiarly known to the Virgin, at whose breasts little infants grow to boyhood: [0197B] speaking to the Bridegroom, she assigned to him simply what belongs to women — nay rather, she compared his fruitful grace to breasts. And in this she withdraws us also from the literal sense of the words, hinting that they are big with a spiritual understanding, when she attributes to the Bridegroom what is the easier to find in the bride the closer it lies, and which she knows perfectly well belongs to him only for the sake of the comparison. *For thy breasts are better than wine.* The Virgin knew indeed, and had read in the books of the ancients, that righteous Job, stripped of his substance, bereft of the offspring of his sons [n: (Job I)], wounded with a hard scourge from the sole of the foot to the crown *(Job II)*, and moreover wearied by his wife's most grievous reproach, at last complains, sorely enough and wretchedly enough, and turns it over again, that death coming beforehand at the opportune [0197C] time did not release him from such great evils. Why, says Job, did not I, wretched, die and breathe out my life in the womb; when I came forth from the belly, why did I not at once end my life by a worthy peril; why, to the increase of misery, was I strengthened by the placing of knees under me, why, to the heaping up of the same, was I at last ( *111* ) suckled at breasts [n: (Job III)]? » I judge that the breasts at which Job grieves and complains that he was suckled — counting it far better that he should first have been weaned from his mother — poured into him no health-bearing milk, no life-giving nourishment, but the venom of the asp, or the wine wherein is luxury. Of which you have read: « The wine of them is the gall of dragons, and the venom of asps, which is incurable [n: (Deut. XXXII)]. » That wine, or that venom, is the very persuasion and teaching which the serpentine depravity of the perverse sows abroad and spreads, [0197D] which makes its hearers drunken and seduces them, and turns them aside and leads them from faith into error, from justice into injustice. Wherefore in Egypt, as the psalm bears witness [n: (Psal. LXXVII)], the Lord smote the vines of the wicked, and in his canticle Moses arraigns the vine of the Sodomites [n: (Deut. XXXII)]; because Egypt and Sodom mix for those who recline at their table a poisonous wine — a form of perversity, a doctrine of malice, the crooked road of injustice. On the other side, in Jeremiah the sons of Jonadab earned praise [n: (Jer. XXXV)], who, having heard their father's precept, did not drink wine of this kind, while their minds, not made mad by the sense of seducers nor by a ruinous consent, kept the rule of truth according to their father's mandate as well as [0198A] his example. Heretics have, and false Catholics have, a wicked wine, by which, their mind lost and their foot broken, they fall into diverse errors either of faith or of morals; and in like fashion they corrupt and lead away their little hearers. The Jewish people also has, and the Gentile has, a wine by which each of them, wickedly made drunk, is held fast and darkened in wretched error; and now and then the one drags and goads the other into headlong ruin. Of which sort a certain poet says beautifully: . . . . *there met me a procession worth*
*the telling; a drunken old woman was dragging along a drunken old man*.


Judaea and the Gentile world are not undeservedly said to have grown old, since it is agreed that each was a people from ancient time, [0198B] and each of them is found not to have been content with its own ceremonies, but often to have strayed aside into the other's error. Did not the Gentile world, ill drunken, seem to you to be dragging the Jewish people along, when in whatever ways it could it urged them to worship the gods; and that most wretched people was held by a like drunkenness, and followed the leader who went before like an ox led to the slaughter? Elias, seeing a procession of this kind coming to meet him, could not bear it, but either laughing was kindled with anger, or being kindled hastened to deride the same as it met him, so that he might strike the erring leaders with a mocking chastisement that they should not go before, and the straying hearers with a chastising word, that they should not follow after. « Elias, says Scripture, coming to all the people, said: How long do ye halt between two sides? If [0198C] the Lord be God, follow him; but if Baal, follow him [n: (III Reg. XVIII)]. » And below: « Elias, he says, mocked the prophets of Baal, saying: Cry with a louder voice, for he is a god, and perhaps he is talking, or is in an inn, or on a journey, or at least he sleepeth, that he may be awaked [n: (ibid.)]. » [var: Vulg. "he mocked *them* (illis)"; the naming of the prophets of Baal is not in the received text] Nor are the Jews only seduced by the pestilent persuasion and error of the Gentiles, but the Gentiles too are sometimes drawn into the superstitions and follies of the Jews; and Judaea, grown old in evils, does not allow the wretched Gentile to be loosed from his chain, but, herself ill drunken, goes before him who is drunken, blind before blind, even to the last ditch. Their procession the Lord execrates in the Gospel, not so much in derision as in censure, and threatens them with a woe to come, as terribly as he does truly, so that when they have heard his hard and [0198D] truthful warnings, both the leaders who go before and the followers may be either amended or left without excuse. « Woe to you, he says, Scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites, who go about the sea and the dry land to make one proselyte; and when he is made, you make him the son of hell twofold more than yourselves [n: (Matth. XXIII)]. » [var: Vulg. *quia circuitis*, "because you go about"] Paul too, beholding a procession of this kind, seemed not to laugh, but, burning with zeal, tenacious of the right, impatient of harm, to shrink from it, so that with a free voice he did not spare even Peter, who seemed the first and the worthier among the apostles, because in this procession he was held to be the one going before in a timid error. For this man was unwilling that that man should be given the turbid wine to drink, and that a crowd equally turbid should follow him ill drunken, lest from this procession not only [0199A] laughter, or derision, should be begotten, but the truth of the Gospel too (which is not lawful) should suffer an unworthy peril. « When Cephas was come, he says, to Antioch, I withstood him to the face, because he was to be blamed [n: (Gal. II)]. » And below: « When I saw, he says, that they walked not uprightly unto the truth of the Gospel, I said to Cephas before them all: If thou, being a Jew, livest after the manner of the Gentiles and not as do the Jews, how dost thou compel the Gentiles to live as do the Jews [n: (ibid.)]. » Paul, noting that both the leader and those who came at his heels were made drunk with the cloud of error, and that among them the evangelical truth was not kept purely enough, counting it a grave offence and not bearing that it should go unpunished, broke forth at once into the midst, and with the rod of correction stretched out interrupted that blameworthy procession. And this the same Apostle judged worthy of the telling, [0199B] and speaking to the Galatians he set it forth again among other matters pleasantly and from memory — perhaps so that not only the Galatians but we too, by his teaching, might know both what the greater owe to us and what we owe to the greater. For the lesser owes no consent to the vices and errors of the greater; but if something grave and intolerable be detected in him, he must needs reprove it out of hatred of the fault, out of zeal for a more gracious justice, taking the more diligent heed that he insert nothing of swelling insolence or rancour. And the greater ought not to disdain the lesser's admonition with a swelling brow, nor to strike back at one who worthily corrects him as though by the privilege of authority; but with a kindly ear to obey the monitor, or rather *112* his admonitions, if only it be clear that they truly [0199C] and humbly serve charity. It is beyond doubt over against this wine — by which either Cephas or anyone else goes astray wandering, and in the procession either goes before or follows after, tottering with a lame step — that the Virgin says and asserts the Bridegroom's breasts are better, knowing by experience that they are more pleasing not only to outward hearing but to inward taste as well. The breasts of the Bridegroom are his form and his doctrine, his example and his word, his life and his speech, with which he cherished and nourished his little hearers as with milk, and fed and strengthened the greater as with bread and solid food. For this indeed he was born a child, that he might show himself suited to little ones, and he advanced in age, that he might show himself a man to those further advanced, in all that he did and said leading and teaching the little ones or the greater, according as they made themselves the readier to take in small things or great. « For coming forth from his [0199D] chamber, beautiful in form above the sons of men [n: (Psal. XLIV)], » [var: Vulg. Ps. 18:6 *tanquam sponsus procedens de thalamo suo* is here fused with Ps. 44:3 *speciosus forma prae filiis hominum*, whose word order is inverted] rich in the incomparable fullness of saving doctrine, he willed first by the merit of his form to please not only the Virgin but us also, and afterward by word and doctrine to teach all who should prove docile. For because we are unwilling to show his word an obedience worthy enough of it — whose face at first we are deservedly compelled to shrink from — Jesus began, as you have read [n: (Act. I)], to do and to teach, so that by the enticing appearance, as it were, of his deeds he might call and lead us on attentive and well-disposed, and having sweetly allured us might strongly bring us into his lecture-hall. With the right breast, as it were, in doing great things, he showed himself worthy of commendation, and caught in the snare of his form those who looked [0200A] the more diligently; with the left, in saying great things, he makes those whom he already holds learned and blessed hearers, but those who despise him he by no means leaves unpunished, though he teach them. Who would not call these breasts of the Bridegroom better than ruinous wine? Who, unless he be drunk with wine, would contradict the Virgin who asserts it? — since those who are drunk with the wine of the damned, and those who pour it out with them, do not so much run as fall blinded; but those who are drunk with the Bridegroom's breasts, are blessed? « Blessed is he, he says, whom thou shalt instruct, O Lord, and shalt teach him out of thy law [n: (Psal. XCIII)]. » [var: Vulg. *Beatus homo quem tu erudieris*, "Blessed is the man whom thou shalt instruct"]
