## CHAPTER VI.

*For thy breasts are better than wine.* In the Bridegroom's breast, indeed, the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are laid up, which are utterly hidden from the prudent and the wise of this world; and he who conceals their secrets from so [0200B] many who are somewhat grown, and for so long a time, now reveals them to little ones, coming forth from his chamber by the goodness which is his own. For through the ages that ran their course behind us those incomparable treasures lay hid in him, but when the flesh of the Virgin had been assumed they burst forth marvellously, like swelling nipples out of his breast, and poured forth not dry drops but streams and rivers, and shed themselves with full horn upon all who worthily drew near. Wherefore the Virgin too, after she has received the kiss, does not straightway turn aside from the Bridegroom as though bidding farewell, but leans back upon his breast for the sake of profiting; and applying the drawing-vessel of a pure mind the more capaciously the more greedily, she is given drink, she is filled full with the welcome abundance of the breasts and their flowings. There she thirsts with a deep longing for knowledge, there for wisdom, hidden by a profound counsel [0200C] from very many, and feels it revealed to her more openly; and now, as she consulted not the angel, so neither the prophets who went before, but leaning alone upon him alone she draws, as Job has it [n: (cap. XXVIII)], and draws to herself wisdom out of hidden places. I am deceived, if that disciple of the Bridegroom also, whom he loved, did not understand that wisdom and knowledge flow from his breast, when, placed at the Supper, he acquiesced not so much in the wine and the material feast as he rested with a welcome reclining upon the breast of Jesus, between those very breasts. There he saw the Word abiding in the most secret bosom of God the Father [n: (Joan. I)], he saw the Word made flesh in the virginal chamber of the Bride his mother; he saw the treasures of wisdom and knowledge, luminous with a brightness plain to him, dark to the prudent and wise of this world under a certain veil spread over them. [0200D] He saw, I say, and did not begrudge to reveal them, not to all but to the faithful, and to commit them to a remembering script with the swift pen of a scribe, so that the understanding hearer might love to aspire to the secrets of that same breast, and from his own poverty of merits to breathe again in the presence of those treasures. To this breast there came not Porphyry, nor Aristotle, nor Socrates, nor Plato, nor the first, nor the second, nor that third Cato falling from heaven; nor in fine anyone who, blinded by malice, shrank from the newness of grace, holding and retaining the oldness of his own knowledge or wisdom. Whose knowledge is proclaimed by a truthful judgment not to heal but to puff up [n: (I Cor. II)], and whose wisdom is judged to be gross foolishness with God; [0201A] in which nevertheless they remain fixed, by I know not what sense of the truth, seducing themselves by a certain privilege of antiquity. One of whom says: *Let those who make haste* — so he says — *drink the new must; let the jar pour out for me the*
*ancestral unmixed wine.*


Not the must of the new grace from the Bridegroom's shining breast, but choosing rather that the old wine of error be poured out for him from a muddy vessel, whose sweetness seems to him the more acceptable and the more perfect for himself, the more both use has confirmed it and ancestral antiquity has sanctioned it. But that new must, which cannot be borne by old vessels, let those drink who make haste, who love to gaze upon the Bridegroom coming forth from his chamber; who, warned by angels after the manner of the shepherds, come hastening, and him whom they see, [0201B] whom they love *113* they do not press down with silence, but announce with a hasty proclamation [n: (Luc. II)]. To whom none was found to hasten sooner, none more perfectly, than she who deserved to prepare for him at his coming the chamber of her own womb, who reclining upon his breast drew and felt his knowledge and wisdom to be more gracious, better in sense, in judgment, than the error of the philosophers.

## CHAPTER VII.

*For thy breasts are better than wine.* The Bridegroom's breasts may also be called his mercy and his truth, which it is agreed work in a more special way in the Bride and mother virgin; with which the law given through Moses can neither be nor ought to be made equal, though it may be judged good and, in its own time, necessary. [0201C] « For the law is good, if a man use it lawfully [n: (I Tim. I)]. » [var: Vulg. *Scimus autem quia bona est lex*, "But we know that the law is good"] Yet in the law there is found a certain harshness of wine, which works wrath and does not bestow grace, which will have a tooth taken for a tooth, an eye for an eye [n: (Exod. XXI)], the striker struck back and the other cheek not offered [n: (Matth. V, Luc. VI)]. The law indeed commands many things, commands great things, but it does not bring the obscure into the light; if it brings forth anything vital, it draws a clinging chaff over it, it burdens blind minds, it weighs down hard necks and blunts them, it does not pour in the mildness of grace, the sweetness of the Spirit. The law duly gives or promises temporal things according to desert, it torments the offender, it does not remit the penalties deserved; it works a severe judgment, a heavy justice, it knows not mercy, it is not appeased freely and without price. Who would suck such [0201D] a grape, and his teeth not be set on edge the harder? Whose taste and palate would not turn the more bitter at such a draught? — where wrath reigns without mercy, and judgment is exercised, whereas mercy is worthily exalted above wrath and judgment? [n: (Jacob. II.)] And so the Virgin, marking the commandments of the law which in a manner deceived the people, since without the spirit of grace they laid down hard and unbearable things, praises the Bridegroom's breasts, which received her more graciously and more sweetly: « Me, she says, O Lord, according to the Psalm, me thy mercy and thy truth have upheld [n: (Psal. XXXIX)]. » [var: Vulg. *misericordia tua et veritas tua semper susceperunt me*, "thy mercy and thy truth have always upheld me"] Mercy it is by which the Lord of the heavens willed to enter the nuptial chamber, having an unbegotten Father, and willing to have a virginal [0202A] mother. Truth it is by which he consummated that will in a happy work, when « the Word was made flesh [n: (Joan. I)], » and God as man dwelt marvellously in the virgin. Mercy it is by which he did not compel the world to undergo the penalties it deserved, but for the blotting out of them chose this time, these nuptials, this virgin. Truth it is by which he happily made bright the chosen time by his coming, and by which, the Spirit working, he made the chosen virgin fruitful. « Mercy, he says, and truth have met each other [n: (Psal. LXXXIV)]. » That the virgin might be made worthy, mercy ran before; that she might become a mother, truth straightway ran to meet her; and there is not, as with us by the desert of guilt, a parting or a running away of the virtues, but in her there is their welcome coming-together, their meeting. Running before, mercy prepared a place for the Bridegroom who was to come, [0202B] and fencing harmful things far off, adorned the chamber with the flowers of virtues; and when it was adorned, truth likewise presented itself, which ineffably made God man in the virgin according to his purpose. God's mercy, therefore, toward the Virgin is his readier will and affection; and truth is the fulfilling or effecting of that will; since not merits but mercy makes the virgin gracious, and truth renders her, already full of grace, glorious. For you have read what is written and foretold: « For God loveth mercy and truth, the Lord will give grace and glory [n: (Psal. LXXXIII)]. » In the Virgin God loves to work mercy and truth, when he wills that she who is freely chosen should truly glory of grace, when he purifies her with the incomparable gift of spiritual [0202C] grace, and gladdens her with the singular privilege of a special glory. « He hath regarded, she says, the humility of his handmaid; and from henceforth all generations shall call me blessed [n: (Luc. I)]. » [var: Vulg. *ecce enim ex hoc*, "for behold, from henceforth"] In the regard of humility she would have merciful grace commended; in the proclaiming of blessedness she would have true glory signified, because both the humble handmaid becomes rich in the gifts of grace, and, city of the great King, we worthily say of thee, glorious. [sic: *Pecte*] therefore the Virgin says the Bridegroom's breasts are better than wine, because no antiquity whatever ever conferred on any, nor did the censorious harshness of the law bring this with it, that mercy should come into a virgin, that truth should be born so that she might be made full of grace, and might rejoice in an incomparable glory.
