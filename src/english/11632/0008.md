## CHAPTER VIII.

*For thy breasts are better than wine, fragrant with the best [0202D] ointments.* The Virgin, reclining in the bosom of the Bridegroom, leaning back upon his breast, resting between the breasts, set apart from things outward, acquiescing in things inward and more secret, feels wonders, tastes sweet things — not with the palate but with the experience of a rejoicing heart — and rejoices in her sense to be flooded with the sweet fragrance of sweet things. For the breasts of the Bridegroom hold not a simple drink of milk, but ointments, wondrous confections, the seasonings of medicinal things, which, gathered and compounded *114* of exceedingly precious spices, were foreknown to few and fully shown to none in all the ages or days behind us. The inward abundance of them is not drawn by any little ones and sucklings; but the Bride, [0203A] a virgin fuller of grace, greater in advancement, experiences it in his very presence, in whose chamber that compounding or mixture of ointments was itself made — a mixture that would profit her first, far and more amply, but us little ones less and later. For when mercy and truth, as has been said above, had run to meet each other, they prudently and carefully gathered certain spices, as it were, which, brought from opposite quarters, they drove together into one little bundle, and by the perfumer's art reduced them into most sweet ointments. For between God and man an ancient discord was in play, in that the injustice of man was joined by no agreement to the justice of God: and the Bridegroom coming brought justice, gave peace, and made her that went before and her that followed to be joined in a welcome kiss: « Justice, he says, and peace have kissed *(Psal. [0203B] LXXXIV)*. » For when, by the justice conferred, he made him just who had been unjust, the discord gave way which had long grown old between the just and the unjust, and at once peace succeeding — because in the justified it perceived nothing but what was just — gave and received the kiss of the long-desired justice now found. To God therefore, who is just, the just man had peace, and by a marvellous assumption God was made man: God, I say, assuming, giving his own justice to the assumed, yet with the vigour of his own justice as the assumer not consumed. Further, as mercy and truth meeting each other make a confection or an ointment, so justice and peace kissing each other make none the less a certain seasoning, whereby health, or beyond doubt a healing salve, is given to him whom the Samaritan shall have mercifully lifted [0203C] onto his beast [n: (Luc. X)]. In short, in the chamber of the Virgin they came together as in a little mortar, and in a most welcome coming-together compounded an ointment, or ointments useful enough: the Father's precept, the Word's presence, the overshadowing of the Spirit; an obedient ear, a firmer faith, and the flesh of the Virgin. Of these spices in an earthen vessel there is made no rustic confusion, but by a learned hand and the physician's art a certain contusion, and when they have been distributed in fitting number and measure and weight, there are precious ointments, both mitigating pains and restorative of health. The compounding of which is of so great a fragrance, doubtless, or scent, that the Bride, to whom the benefit of a fuller grace is given, is delighted and marvels at the odour-bearing sweetness of the ointments, which are not only [0203D] good, but beyond doubt the best of better ones. For although before the law, and under the law, very many things were found which by the merits of a devout faith are medicinally turned into ointments, which God, ever loving honours, is read to have smelled, and is believed to have been many times delighted with a most welcome odour of sweetness, yet these of which we speak are found by experience to be the best of all that went before, being nearest the chamber, or rather being found to be the chamber itself. Into the earth of that chamber God sent spittle from his own mouth [n: (Joan. IX)], and in a manner wonderful enough made clay like none above or below, whose touch and anointing restores the light denied from the ancient time of birth, and whose sweet fragrance ineffably soothes the sense of smell. This sweet fragrance Isaac is believed to have felt beforehand from far off, [0204A] and, taught through the Spirit, to have foretold of it in a mystery, when his son Jacob presented himself to him in very good garments, and a most sweet odour of the garments breathed upon his father's nostrils: « Behold, he says, the smell of my son is as the smell of a full field, which the Lord hath blessed [n: (Gen. XVII)]. » To this field is given the incomparable sanctity of a new blessing, and for this reason, consequently, an unfailing fullness of flowers, and fruits, and odours, which Isaac, perceiving from afar, marvels at exulting; the Virgin, feeling it near at hand and experiencing it in the thing itself, gives thanks ineffably. And from her and through her the sense of that same fragrance, however thin, comes to us, scarcely at last arriving, because it finds us set far off, and having found us has made us as happy as we are glad at its coming — not, however, by our own [0204B] merit, but by the intervention of that same Virgin. For although ointments of this sort were compounded in the virgin, yet they were not held with her within narrow bounds, but with abounding grace they came down to those further off, and anointed us both with an odour-bearing touch and with a health-bearing odour. And in that she says ointments in the plural, not ointment, she hints that in us not one kind of evil singly has been found, but our misery has procured for itself many languors, many infirmities, which the physician at his coming has carried away with medicinal ointments. « Truly, says Isaias, he hath borne our languors, and he hath carried our infirmities [n: (Isa. LIII)]. » [var: Vulg. *dolores nostros*, "our sorrows"] Truly he bore and bore away, carried and carried off our languors, our infirmities, which, being many and various, demanded the touch or [0204C] fragrance of no single ointment; but different ones needed different remedies of ointments. But we who, labouring under much sickness, have no leisure for learning what is healthful, whom either dullness of wit or the torpor of loathing presses down, so that we bring little diligence to them; we, I say, know not what infirmity needs what ointment: and having experienced manifold diseases, we are darkened in the knowledge of curing. But that physician of ours, alone presiding within his own workshop, prepares for each sickness a fitting medicine, and by his craftsmanship he does not wish the sick to be present everywhere, nor that the art or the knowledge should pass over to them, but that it should profit them. Nevertheless, so far as anyone may experience in himself *115*, for whose healing the physician [0204D] loves to impart his care, he seems to have brought forth to us three ointments one after another, and by them to have conferred on us who are sick an opportune remedy.

The first, biting and rough, shook us the more harshly, and, anointing as it were from without, straightway struck a certain heaviness into the inward parts, and troubling and dislodging the disease long grown in, increased it, and presented it, as it were, all at once to sense and to knowledge. And when it was presented, the wicked mind raged and groaned, scarcely bearing itself, while it turns over the newest things and the old, and, pricked and stung, wrapped itself round with hard recollections of iniquities, and rolled itself down among the thorns and briars of thoughts accusing, or even defending, it. Thus the troubled mind began at once to abhor thy foulness, and by the desert of its own vileness [0205A] to be the more vehemently displeasing to itself; by the hatred of its own self to bear witness to certain new things and contrary to the old, and by a changed judgment to detest what had pleased it before. This fragrance of the ointment, after the manner of a draught, brought in a shuddering fear, by which, when the passages of the veins had been purged, it led us on to a healthful vomiting, so that, spitting out with free disdain or loathing the former blandishments of the vices, we might truly reckon as losses what before were falsely thought gains. That penitent showed himself touched by this odour, who, labouring in his groaning, washed his bed with tears, whose eye was troubled at the regard of his faults by the Judge's fury, and who confessed by a healthful vomiting that he had grown old among his enemies [n: (Psal. VI)]. For odour and stench do not agree well together, nor do they abide in one seat, but when, the nostrils being loosed by grace, the odour of the precious [0205B] ointment is let in, the stench of the vices, impatient of its company, is sent out; and with the greater vehemence this healthful potion is poured in, the larger the flux and the looser the outlet by which the worthless filth goes forth. For that odour is « living and effectual and more piercing than any two-edged sword, and reaching unto the division of the soul and the spirit, of the joints also and the marrow, and a discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart [n: (Hebr. IV)]. » And for this reason, when with the physician's devoted care it has been pressed upon anyone in season, out of season, when it has entered as powerfully as mercifully the wounded borders of the sick man: no passage of the veins, no lurking-place or recess of the marrow, no place at last from the sole of the foot to the crown is inaccessible to it. It runs therefore and runs about, and going round after the manner of the swallow [0205C] it ranges everywhere, it searches out all deep and hidden things with a censor's office and keenness, and whatever it finds contrary to life, liable to fault, given over to vanity, it sweeps together with a strong broom, and having swept it together casts it out, preparing a place for health.

The second ointment, further, breathes more healthfully, gives the odour of a gentler benefit, brings back the hope of living and brings in love as well, so that the sick man, feeling himself relieved by the laying down of a heavy burden, may now hope for better things, and in hope may love to work. At this odour the spirit that languished before revives, and with returning appetite longs for the food of life: refreshed with the food of grace, it rejoices that the necessary strength is restored, and that the hands hanging down and the feeble knees are strengthened [n: (Isa. XXXV)]. Thereupon, embracing with a ready affection the gift of strengthening grace, he goes out to his [0205D] work, holding welcome the labourer's exercise; and stretches out watchfully to labour the hands unfolded from their torpor, he hears and hearkens, that he may go and bring forth a more gracious fruit. Forgetting the things that are behind, he gives his effort the more attentively to virtue, he chastises his body and worthily brings it into subjection [n: (I Cor. IX)]; and the things which before seemed bitter or perhaps impossible are, on the contrary, reckoned as easy as they are healthful. Hence he embraces fastings, vigils, continence, patience, and judges it fitting to be ruled and made subject by the counsel of his elders; for love of the right he renounces the pleasures of the flesh — nay, even his own wills — that he may subject his body and himself to God. For God wills this order to be kept in us, that thus, within a just law and the bounds set for them, single things be restrained: [0206A] so that the spirit, commanding the flesh, may drive and bring it under itself, while itself obeying its greater is one spirit with God. Who, wretchedly set in the filthy hovel of this body, who, wrapped round with so many stains or snares of malignant spirits, could so set out, loosed from the threats or ambushes of enemies meeting him, if he had not been anointed with this ointment of strengthening grace? Who would not recoil from the entrance at the very bolt of exceeding difficulty thrust against him, or would not fail in his going forward at the manifold onset of impediments; if he were not touched by the odour of this ointment so sweetly, so effectually, that as he began, so also going forward he should not be broken? In fine, whoever rightly examines the course of those who go forward knows that whom the Father loves as a Son, he rebukes and chastises [n: (Hebr. XII)]; [0206B] nor does he wish him to glory in the obtained benefit of the promised inheritance whom he has not first suffered to be scourged with many tribulations of sufferings. But if by the impulse of vices, or of scourges, the just man gives way a little and totters, the odour of this ointment meeting him revives him the more quickly: and so great a sweetness does not endure that he should be crushed and fall into failure, but with a hand set under him it cherishes him and promotes him the more diligently to a fuller advance. Wherefore threatening evils do not draw him back utterly from what he has begun, but grace mercifully fashions his labours and struggles into a precept: and him whom it permits to be cold and to sweat, by the counsel of its own pity it strengthens after the sweat with the health it has promised again, and makes him stronger.
