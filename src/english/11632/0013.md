## CHAPTER XIII.

*Draw me; after thee we will run in the odour of thy ointments.* [var: Vulg. *in odorem unguentorum tuorum*, accusative — "toward the odour"; Migne's ablative *in odore* is the reading Philip expounds throughout] Though the Virgin is raised to so great an excellence that by the gift of grace she is advanced to the Bridegroom's kiss, from this indeed she takes to herself gladness and a spiritual confidence, yet of her own strength she presumes nothing. For as she did not conceive the willing of good by her own merit, so she did not receive the accomplishing of it save from another: [0214C] and from him from whom she has all whatsoever she knows to be useful and desirable to her, from him she asks to be drawn after him, to whose service she has devoted herself. For she neither is, nor intends so to be, nimble that she should run before, striving only for this, and judging this to suffice her, that she should run behind; and to this very thing, by her own judgment, she does not prevail unless she be drawn, unless she be not dragged back by the burden of her own infirmity; unless she be driven forward by the sweet Spirit of grace to the things that are before, and not driven back by the wicked spirit to the things that are behind. From him therefore she asks to be drawn after him, not unwilling, but hindered still by the fetter of corruptible flesh, whose fortitude, though it be already perhaps firm enough in a prompt spirit, yet the flesh by a certain natural heaviness is somewhat infirm. [0214D] Him therefore whom she loves the prompt spirit burns to follow, wishing above all that the flesh may not retard him more than is just, but that, as the virgin's spirit loves to cleave to God, so the flesh may be able to wrest nothing injurious from that same spirit. For a prompter will provokes and lifts up not a few to stronger things, but the same men a carnal infirmity calls back and softens; and by a heavy softness either casts them headlong into failure, or at the least in no wise suffers them to be advanced to perfection. And for wretches of this kind, whom their wretchedness draws back with a softer bond, what is needful but that they be drawn and attracted by a stronger spirit, so that, the softness of him that wickedly draws back being pressed and oppressed, the pitying hand of him that draws on may at length prevail? « Therefore, he says, have I drawn thee, pitying thee [n: (Jer. XXXI.)] » There is also that drawing whereby the flesh is not now compelled [0215A] to obey, but which is found rather to soothe the spirit itself; a drawing, I say, far sweeter, of a certain inward delight, when the spirit is delighted by the taste and the love of the truth it has tasted. And in place of this the Bride and Mother wills to be held by a grateful longing, and by it to be sweetly moved forward to the things that savour of the spirit; and to be frightened away from their sweetness by no disquietudes of temporal things, but to be wholly swallowed up by the serene face of him whom she loves. And if a face of this kind be shown to one that loves, even for a little, if *121* to it, once shown, an eye purified by right judgment be directed, both the shower and the beholder are bound together by the bond of so great a love that he who loves is drawn on to the beloved by an indissoluble knot. Drawn, I say, not by violence, not by molestation, not by fear, not by hatred of the drawn or of the drawer, but by love; by that drawing whereby [0215B] the nursling is drawn after the nurse, whereby a son follows his father or a daughter her mother — nay, by that drawing whereby he that loves faints away into the beloved, the more graciously as the more sweetly, while he desires to obtain, and insatiably covets to desire. Whereof by Hosea: « I will draw them, he says, in the bonds of charity [n: (Ose. II)]. » And he that is drawn answers: « My soul hath cleaved after thee [n: (Psal. LXII)]. » So our Virgin chooses and asks to be drawn after her beloved, that he, namely, may by his grace give effect to the loving and the following; and that with affectionate love the Bride after the Bridegroom, the Mother after the Son, may kindle, and that not flesh to flesh, but the soul to God may the more strongly cleave. For as she recalls that she was weighed down by the loss of a certain death, whose unhappy soul had once cleaved to the pavement, so this Virgin, because after the Bridegroom's [0215C] kiss she loves nothing but life, desires to be drawn after him through whom she may live — not unwilling, but eager in affection, fervent in spirit, unencumbered in merits. And since, being thus drawn, thus helped, it is not expedient that she tarry, assuredly she will not follow him that draws her with a limping footstep, but with prompt pace, with agile step, with hastened foot she will run, and to him for whom she sighs she will make speed by an unencumbered road. *We will run,* she says, *in the odour of thy ointments.* She says in the plural: *We will run;* though in the singular she had set before: *Draw me.* If thou shalt have drawn me to thee by a special affection and grace, that thou mayest make me pleasing and blessed by the nuptial kiss, that thou mayest both cleanse and make fruitful thy bride by a princely spirit, that by a new and untried manner I may rejoice in a virginal childbirth; if me, she says, [0215D] thou shalt have thus drawn, then not I alone by a spiritual gift, but with me the maidens shall rejoice by a certain general benefit, so that they may follow me, who run before, more devoutly according to their own measure, and may be declared to run with me together after thee, whom they love. And so give me first, give me chiefly, the kiss; draw me by the bond of charity, grant me to know, grant me to feel the ampler grace of thy goodness; and this shall not profit me only, nor shall I be found to run alone, but we shall run, and all that love shall come to thee alike, though not with equal running. By the example indeed and by the merit of the Virgin, who is found to have run first and more perfectly, the apostles and their successors believe that they have found grace: who, having put off as it were the tunic of a wrinkled eld, grow young like the eagle, not with feathers renewed, but with manners [0216A] renewed. The Virgin was drawn and attracted by a certain thong granted to none besides, whereby with a heart utterly untiring she cleaved to the Bridegroom and Author; led upward and carried up, the Holy Spirit overshadowing her so strongly, so sweetly, that she conceives God, nature setting aside the right ingrained in it. And he who is conceived is himself he whose word runs swiftly, who, leaping upon the mountains, skipping over the hills like a giant, outruns all the rest [n: (Cant. II)]; and to the following of him the Virgin weighs that she must needs be drawn, whom she does not lay hold of save when her native slowness has been laid aside. But who is either found readier to be drawn, or is rendered nimbler for running, than the Virgin, whose flesh is wholly lightened of the load of sin, and by that lightening drawing is wondrously made one with God that draws? And so the Virgin, being thus drawn — which she knows to have been granted to no other [0216B] — now runs, now advances, now presses on the more hastily to what she has desired: teaching and leading the fervent maidens to perfection, though none, however nimble, comes to an equal advancement. Perhaps for this reason the Virgin asked in the singular that she be drawn, but affirmed her running to be not alone but together with the rest, because in choosing her especially for his Mother the Son of God drew her to himself, yet she did not draw to herself alone the fruit of this work. For from the fact that she was singularly exalted to the prerogative of motherhood, no small profit was conferred upon the maidens that follow; while they studied worthily to imitate that same Virgin, and each according to her own measure ran to love the Bridegroom. « They ran, he says, both together, but John did outrun Peter more swiftly [n: (Joan. XX)]. » [var: Vulg. John 20:4 *et ille alius discipulus praecucurrit citius Petro* — the Vulgate does not name John] And another: [0216C] « So I run, he says, not as at an uncertainty [n: (II Cor. IX)]. » *We will run,* they say, *in the odour of thy ointments.* The odour of the ointments is the sweetness of spiritual gifts, which when any of the elect have perceived by a keen scenting, not grudging it to the rest but rather rejoicing in their company, they hasten with eager course to come to it, or rather into it, bearing no delay, and scarce deferring to find what they have already sensed beforehand. You know that it belongs to dogs by nature that what they do not even see with their eyes they are granted to sense beforehand with their nostrils; and if they have been worthily trained by hunting use, they run this way and that, they hasten, they bay, they cry out, until they have overtaken it — to such as these Paul says: « So run that ye may obtain [n: (II Cor. IX)]. » But if any one, keener of nostril, has sensed some anointed thing or ointment, with such greediness [0216D] of the odour he has drunk in does he follow the allurement, that neither promises, nor threats, nor death at last, nor life, can call him back from it, and he reckons all things loss that he may gain only the thing he covets. But how great are the ointments, when weighed in their own fullness, where they are kept under key and in a chamber beneath the perfumer's hand — ointments which, before from afar they have been found present and in fullness, so hold men's hearts intent by the sweetness of their odour? How great is the fullness of them that already refreshes those present, whose odour flowing outward so works upon them that drink it in, that it makes them, with the soles of their feet made firm, not only to come but, out of hatred of heavy delay, *122* to run? Who will grant to us, set far off, to sense this odour even a little? Who will bestow a stronger step upon our weakened feet, [0217A] upon our enfeebled knees, so that by pitying grace we too may some day be found running, or at the least may step by step follow from afar the maidens that run before? Happy are they to whom the Bride and Mother signifies that she will give her fellowship, with whom she asserts that she will run in the odour of the ointments; who in truth so runs with those same maidens that she runs before them, and, while they still remain outside, herself more swiftly and more familiarly runs within — which, signifying, she subjoins:
