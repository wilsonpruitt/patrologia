## CHAPTER XV.

[0219A]

*We will exult and be glad in thee, mindful of thy breasts above wine.* Turned, or rather turned back, to the maidens, she announced her joy to them, and by that announcement invited them to run the more constantly: and this being briefly accomplished, she straightway called back both her word and her face to the Bridegroom, because she judged it sweeter to speak to him than of him. Whoever loves the more tenderly, though he gladly converse with others about him whom he loves, holds it far more grateful to speak to him, if perchance the choice be granted; and speaking of him, he loves to commend him to his hearers, while speaking to him he rejoices to taste his grace. And so the Bride, speaking of the Bridegroom, seems to do it for the sake of the maidens themselves, that she may exhort them, namely, to love him whom she loves; [0219B] because her love of him is so great that it fears neither rival nor concubine, nor does she count it her own loss if any other be provoked to love him. Nay rather, that joy which, loving, she wills to have in her beloved, she reckons to be made greater if it befall that others also obtain a share in him: and therefore, quickly referring herself to the Bridegroom, with whom she holds it grateful to converse more often, she asserts that she rejoices not over her own advancement alone, but over theirs together with hers. *We will exult,* she says, *and be glad in thee.* It does not suffice one that loves to proclaim her joy in a single word, but she doubles it, that by doubling she may be able the more expressly to intimate its abundance; and to the intimating of it, though she seem to unfold the same or like words at some length, yet she will not be able worthily to unfold as much as she feels. Or perhaps in that she says: *We will exult,* [0219C] she signifies the joy which inwardly she does not hide, which she rather so pours forth outwardly in voice or in any work whatever, that to the eyes of beholders the kind of her love may be shown, so that whoever worthily attends may be kindled to love in like manner. « Thy justice, he says, I have not hid in my heart; thy truth and thy salvation I have declared [n: (Psal. XXXIX)]. » But because every dissembler sometimes does or says very many things which, though their appearance be good, yet the inner conscience gainsays them, the Virgin adds: *And we will be glad in thee;* signifying that joy which, God being witness, lies hid in the secret of the heart, when, answering to what is shown outwardly, the heart within grows cheerful. Which the prophet seems to designate in a single verse, and in it to assign, as it were, each to each more distinctly: calling that [0219D] gladness which is contained in secret, but that exultation which is exhibited by outward tokens. « For this, he says, my heart hath been glad, and I have exulted under my tongue [n: (Psal. XV)]. » [var: Vulg. Ps 15:9 *et exsultavit lingua mea*, "and my tongue hath rejoiced" — Migne gives the first person and adds *sub*, the very word the exposition turns on] And elsewhere: « Let the heart of them rejoice that seek the Lord [n: (Psal. CIV)]. » And that: « Deliver me from blood, O God, thou God of my salvation: and my tongue shall exult thy justice [n: (Psal. L)]. » From these places it is gathered that between being glad and exulting there is this difference, that to be glad belongs to the heart, to exult to the work; because both by affection and by effect ought every just man to rejoice in him from whom he hopes to obtain both present grace and future glory. Although exultation is sometimes assigned to the heart also, and one is declared not only to be glad but to exult in secret, so that exultation may seem to differ little or nothing from gladness, [0220A] since, like the one, so the other is asserted to be inward. « To him, he says, I cried with my mouth: and I exulted under my tongue [n: (Psal. LXV)]. » [var: Vulg. Ps 65:17 *et exaltavi sub lingua mea*, "and I extolled him under my tongue"] Under the tongue, he says, that is, in the heart, as below in this Canticle: « Honey and milk are under thy tongue. » And Anna in her Canticle: « My heart hath exulted in the Lord [n: (I Reg. II)]. » And this same Virgin Mary in hers: « My spirit hath exulted in my [sic: *Desalutari*] [n: (Luc. I)]. » In that therefore she says: *We will exult and be glad,* by the doubled word she signifies a heaped-up joy, which she understands that both she and the maidens will receive, and that by the gift of a more bountiful grace they will rejoice as much over things to come as over things present. For to godliness, which deserves to be brought into the aforesaid storerooms, the Author of the storerooms promises the joy of the life which now is and of that which is to come [n: (I Tim. IV)]; and by the giving of the things promised [0220B] he already bestows the present in the thing, and the good things that follow in hope, whereby, the cloud of sadness being wiped away, the heart of them that seek the Lord is soothed. Moreover in the present he advances knowledge, refreshes and makes serene the conscience; in the future he wholly estranges both from all darkness and from all disquiet: and that reality, *124* and this hope, warns to love, gives to run to him, and grants that he who runs faint not, but rather advance until he come to that same. Accordingly the Bride, saying: *We will exult and be glad,* subjoins: *in thee;* so that when, whether from things present or from the good things that follow, exultation or gladness is given us, none of us may glory in himself, but in the Lord; and that, forgetting himself, who has of himself nothing but death and sadness, he may with tender affection call to mind him from whom is life, and — that I may say it better — who is life. *Mindful,* [0220C] she says, *of thy breasts above wine.* With thy breasts, she says, thou givest suck, thou nourishest, thou refreshest the thirsty, while thou givest, while thou sustainest with free gifts them that love thee: while thou grantest that they who love thee, having obtained thy grace, be made perfect by a desirable advancement. Who, having relegated their substance and cast away the purse of their own depravity, rejoice in the hundredfold recompense of voluntary poverty; and being by the present benefit and by the milk of the left breast made stronger, are by the gift of the right breast now made more certain of receiving the reward of eternal life. Breasts of this kind are above wine, that is, better than wine, because present perfection and the promise of things to come are offered to the just more profitably and more graciously than are the tribulation and anguish that are inflicted upon any of the wicked, who both in present things are tormented by a wounded conscience and in the future await an irremediable punishment. [0220D] « Tribulation, says Paul, and anguish upon every soul of man that worketh evil [n: (Rom. II)]. » And Jeremiah: « With a double destruction, he says, destroy them, O Lord [n: (Jer. XVII)]. » [var: Vulg. Jer 17:18 *et duplici contritione contere eos*, without the vocative] This wine, harder than gall, of a most bitter taste and savour, this deadly cup of the Lord's wrath or fury, all the lost drink, by the merit of a more worthless life — they who do not attain to the Bridegroom's breasts, full of grace and of a more gracious nourishment. « Thou hast drunk, says Isaiah, at the hand of the Lord the cup of his wrath; even to the bottom of the cup thou hast drunk, and thou hast drunk even to the dregs [n: (Isa. LI)]. » [var: Vulg. Isa 51:17 *usque ad fundum calicis soporis bibisti*, "the cup of dead sleep"] And expounding what this cup is, he says below: « There are two things that have come upon thee, wasting and destruction, and famine and the sword [n: (ibid.)]. » [var: Vulg. Isa 51:19 inserts *quis contristabitur super te?* between *tibi* and *vastitas*] Than this wine or cup the Bridegroom's breasts are far better, which promise [0221A] or exhibit more gracious things both to the Bride and to the maidens, whom by the hand of mercy he sets far from wrath and fury, far from the heavy judgment, and whose longing both present perfection and the recompense that follows satisfies. *Or we shall be mindful above wine,* that is, we shall be more mindful of the breasts than of the wine. He calls wine, as it seems, the transitory suffering of this life, which upon them that sigh brings the injury of no slight annoyance from the eternal, while the body that is corrupted and the necessary habitation of earthly things weighs down the soul, and presses down the mind with the burden of very many thoughts. And though a weighing-down of this kind is to any just man either destructive or purgative of vices, when God burns and chastises him whom he loves with the heavy but useful cautery of scourges, yet because it pricks, and a draught of this kind grows bitter [0221B] in the palate of him that tastes, everyone who burns to draw heavenly things the more sweetly wills that it be set far from him. Whereof the royal Prophet: « Thou hast given us to drink, he says, of the wine of compunction [n: (Psal. LIX)]. » The harshness of penalties, of which neither the poor man nor the richer is found free and immune, since even an infant of one day experiences it by a heavy law, is a troublesome pricking and a potion suited to healing, bringing in the hateful but plainly fruitful bitterness of discipline; which every wise man, in respect of itself alone, diligently rejects, but in view of the fruit that follows judges must be patiently borne. And though present experience compels him to remember it, yet a more perfect fruit and a sweeter hope warn him to forget it: and he is more gratefully mindful of spiritual goods and of a salvation that will longer endure, than of the bitter potion and [0221C] of a compunction that will more quickly pass. Whose grace seems to be attributed not to all, but to very few, whose foot is found worthily set upon a rock; that an unbending uprightness may carry the firmly established man up to the things above, and that by the merit of uprightness he may receive an ampler and more perfect certainty. Therefore there follows: *The upright,* etc.
