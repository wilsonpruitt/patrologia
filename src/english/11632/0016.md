## CHAPTER XVI.

*The upright love thee.* For he who does not go down to the lowest things with bent knee and crooked ham, but hangs himself upon high and arduous things with fervent longing; who is not bowed down according to the promptings of malignant spirits, that to them the wished-for ease of passing over him may be afforded — he assuredly is upright, and is judged to hate what he rejects, and, abhorring the things forbidden, is approved as loving the forbidder. [0221D] Finally you have: « Ye that love the Lord, hate evil [n: (Psal. XCVI)]. » He hates evil who does not incline to it the drawing-vessel of his heart, who does not cast down his eyes into the deep, or into the earth [n: (Dan. XIII)]; who does not bear with an even mind him that exhales a foul smoke from the dunghill of this life, who afflicts the nostrils with its incense, blinds the eyes, discolours the inward face. But by hatred of evil — which he detects to be tending into the deep — loving better things and things agreeable to salvation, he stretches heart, hands, eyes upward; and not only, though pressed and oppressed by a wicked burden, does he not bend down to the lowest things, but, raised up to the things above, he cleaves to them inseparably with his whole longing. And because those things stand and by an unbending uprightness are directed to *125* standing, it is certain that they become upright who are raised up to them by advancing merits; [0222A] and as they that make idols or the images of the nations are made like to them [n: (Psal. CXIII)], so they are conformed to heavenly things who cleave to their uprightness. And heavenly things, which are also not unfittingly called heaven or the heavens, which are prepared for the Lord as a house or a habitation, a place, a seat, a throne, do truly embrace the Lord himself, and him that sits therein, with affectionate longings, and cannot but love him whom they contemplate ruling mightily and reigning sweetly within them. « Our Father, he says, who art in heaven [n: (Matth. VI)]. » And: « I have loved, he says, the beauty of thy house, and the place of the habitation of thy glory [n: (Psal. XXV)]. » And: « The heavens declare the glory of God [n: (Psal. XVIII)]. » And: « The Lord, in heaven is his seat [n: (Psal. X)]. » And: « Heaven is my seat [n: (Isa. LXVI)]. » And many things of this kind you will find, [0222B] which assert that God dwells in heaven or in the heavens, and presides over seat, place and house with gracious good will: and the mutual gratefulness of the seat and of him that sits is brought about no otherwise than if, grace granting it, uprightness answer to uprightness. For God, being upright, wills to have those like himself, nor to be loved save by those who love to cleave to his uprightness — whom nothing but love makes to cleave to that same uprightness, and nothing but uprightness makes truly to love. Which the Virgin Mother perceiving: *The upright,* she says, *love thee,* because they who do not pervert their intention by the desire of the flesh, but with a willing heart turn themselves to him who is supremely good, it is certain that they set out upon a straight way, upon a royal way, and, advancing rightly, obtain the Good which they desire. Finally: « God is good to Israel, to them that are of a right heart *(Psal. [0222C] LXIX)*. » [var: Vulg. Ps 72:1 *Quam bonus Israel Deus, his qui recto sunt corde* — Migne drops the exclamatory *Quam*, "How good"] For they that follow the serpentine windings and the meanders of a crooked depravity do not taste how sweet, how delightful is the rule of the divine goodness, because to that uprightness which is good, which is just, which is supremely praiseworthy, the crookedness of malice or of injustice neither agrees nor cleaves. But he who with free horror suspends himself from earth and earthly things, and, seeking the things that are above, lays hold of them rather with an upright heart than with his hands, feels indeed that great and greatly praiseworthy is the greatness, equally great and magnificently to be commended the multitude of the divine sweetness — whose sweetness, though report perceives it, yet by an inward sense no man experiences it save he who is found like to the good God by uprightness of heart. And uprightness of this kind [0222D] the Lord seems to set in order by a fitting advancement, and to invite the apostles to it in the Gospel: willing them to be made not only readier to seek after it, but also more certain of the order of that very thing which is to be sought: « Let your loins, he says, be girt about, and lamps burning in your hands: and you like to men who wait for their lord [n: (Luc. XII)]. » [var: Vulg. Luke 12:35 *praecincti*, "girded"; Migne here prints *percincti*, and prints *praecincti* himself at 0223B below] This prudent craftsman of a discriminated uprightness, setting things in order, first lays as it were a base, he casts the foundation of the work: and the base being laid, he sets up the firm shaft of the column, and places upon it the capital, the ornament of that same uprightness. By the loins indeed is understood the will or the affection of this life, the concupiscence of the flesh, the acquiring of the will's desire: and for this reason the censure of divine [0223A] justice wills the loins to be girded, since it wills us to be restrained from all things not only that are not lawful, but that are not expedient. And to set aside harmful things, and to abstain from the things of concupiscence, to forbid carnal appetites their completing effect, to restrain loose motions with the strong girdle of chastity — this is a certain solid base, and the beginning of holiness. For whosoever seeks to obtain uprightness of living must first take care to abstain from things unlawful and unprofitable, so that when he has laid the base of the continence that must needs come first, he may confidently set upon it the weight of the fabric that follows. Perhaps for this reason, to that man who laboured with a grievous feebleness of the feet, who sitting at the temple gate begged an alms of them that entered in, Peter, moved with mercy, gave no help of money, because a mighty grace, [0223B] not gold, had given the Apostle nothing, nor silver, but by a mighty word he restored soundness to the lame feet, and that man, his soles made firm, walked leaping and glad [n: (Act. III)]. And rightly did Peter first impart soundness to the feet, and that man consequently, walking with his soles made firm, leapt up; because first the infirmity must needs be brought to an end, and, this base being laid, the fabric rise the more robustly to advancement. Accordingly, when he had said in the Gospel: « Let your loins be girded [n: (Luc. XII)], » he subjoined: « And lamps burning in your hands [n: (ibid.)]. » For when a man has with due horror cast off the works of darkness, in an order just enough there follows the kindling of the lamps, namely that before men his holiness may shine, and that like a pillar of fire the uprightness of his manners and of his justice may stand forth. [0223C] The lamp in the hands signifies brightness in works, when upon the base of the continence whereby you abstain from the things that are not expedient, you apply an eminence of works, an uprightness of manners, and after the manner of an unbending column you study to remain steadfast and immovable, and to be pre-eminent in life and manners over all beneath you. But because it does not suffice that a man shine with great works, unless through them he aim to please not men but the Lord, he adds: « And you like to men who wait for their lord [n: (ibid.)]. » The intention or expectation whereby you do not court a transitory reward, but with ever-wakeful care await your Lord from above, is a certain fortification of the aforesaid uprightness, and like [0223D] the capital of the column, a commendable ornament. For a column is bare and unfitting if it be not *126* adorned by a capital set above it, and insufficient is that uprightness which is not held fast by the expectation of the Lord; whereby, as by a summit, it surpasses and transcends all the things that are of the world, and after the manner of Jacob's ladder lays hold of the heavens from above. Whence Paul: « Our conversation is in heaven, whence also we look for the Saviour [n: (Philipp. III)]. » There the Apostle asserts that he converses, whence he loves to await the Saviour of himself and of all; and whatsoever is by worthy judgment declared upright and stable, is by this awaiting, as by a certain roofing, brought to completion. Wherein the Bride and Mother, being far more fully instructed and having attained that same uprightness by an ampler advancement, loves the Bridegroom, [0224A] and is joined to him in chaste commerce, learning by experience that to her who loves rightly his embrace is not denied. For because, according to the prophet, upright is the Lord our God, and in him no iniquity is held [n: (Deut. XXXII)], with a countenance assuredly gracious he beholds the Virgin's equity; and in her the strength of his kingdom holds a realm peaceful enough, whose uprightness agrees with so great an equity of hers. To her indeed the same Prophet says: « The rod of direction, the rod of thy kingdom [n: (Psal. XLIV)]. » The Virgin herself also is in the Scriptures for this reason, as I judge, called a rod, when she is figured in them by a mystical speech, so that by the name of the rod may be understood not only the flowering beauty of the Virgin, but no less her clean and fruitful uprightness. You know that Aaron's dry rod put forth leaves on the second day [n: (Num. XVII)], and, unaided [0224B] by natural fomentations, brought forth nuts by a wondrous newness: wherein it is doubtful to none that the uprightness of the Virgin is figured, who, nature standing astonished, and knowing no man, is brought forward to the bearing of a son. Isaiah also, beholding a rod that should come forth from the root of Jesse [n: (Isai. XXI)], knew that a Virgin should be born of the stock of Isai; but him who by an incomparable process ascends from her as a flower, he did not weigh what better thing to figure than her son. In this very drama the angelic Spirits marvel, when they behold that same Virgin ascending through the desert like a wand of smoke [n: (Cant. III)]; namely for this, that the Virgin, forsaking the things that are beneath, stretches herself more freely into the things above, and, treading the earth with her purpose behind her, lays hold of heaven by her longings. But because one comes not to this uprightness [0224C] save by file and labour, nor is entrance made into the kingdom of God save through many tribulations, as Scripture testifies [n: (Act. XIV)], the Virgin wills that the maidens be not scorched by present annoyances, but in the pressure of temporal things be refreshed by the consideration of spiritual. For the maidens might be moved at this, that she who seems already to have been led to the Bridegroom's kiss, and by her own testimony to have been brought into the storerooms of the King, nevertheless still labours under pressures, is wearied with a wretched burden, and, though she boasts herself beautiful, has her face accused of foulness. Whereupon the Virgin seems anxiously to forestall them, and, that they may weigh each thing worthily, to instruct them more diligently — because both the foulness that appears is to pass away with a swift course, and a beauty unbending in its state remains to follow after.
