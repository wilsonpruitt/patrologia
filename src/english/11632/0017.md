## CHAPTER XVII.

[0224D]

*I am black, but comely, daughters of Jerusalem, as the tabernacles of Cedar, as the skin of Solomon.* [var: Vulg. Cant 1:4 *sicut pelles Salomonis*, plural, "as the curtains of Solomon"] The blackness which fouls and infects the comeliness of this Virgin is the adversity of the present life, which weighs upon her and afflicts her against her will, which does not suffer the joy of the Spirit, the grace of a peace begun, to be brought through to the harbour of a consummated beauty. For upon the Bride, fervent indeed in Spirit and sighing after continual peace, aspiring in the Bridegroom's sight to a perfect beauty, there come fear, peril, the annoyance of hurtful injury, tribulation and anguish, grief of the inward parts, the sword of passion — which upon her face, which sometimes a spiritual gladness, the light of grace, floods over, spread instead a cloud of sadness, a shadowy blackness. For neither to the Virgin herself [0225A] did the present life give continual peace and unfailing joy, but she grieved and groaned, harassed by many annoyances, and in the number of her days she brought forth white pebbles in such wise that yet among them very many blackening ones were not lacking. Indeed the heathen, since they distinctly number and weigh their sad and their prosperous days, that they may comprehend with sure knowledge which outweigh which, as with white pebbles they mark the series of prosperous days, so with the blackness of pebbles they mark the sad and adverse. Black therefore is the Virgin, when she feels herself afflicted with temporal annoyances, when she sees him whom she bore crucified by mockers; and him whom she knows to be present to the angelic spirits as their author and Lord, and in the bosom of God the Father to be God in equal glory, made flesh in her and of her, she sees now made subject [0225B] to the power of his persecutors, with many assailants and no defender of him at hand. Black is the Mother, when she beholds her own flesh, which she loves not by vice but by nature, hard pressed in her son by an untimely death, when whatever ill he suffers at the Father's bidding, by the circuit of a natural sling, redounds pitiably into the Mother's heart. Black is the Bride, when she hears the Bridegroom, even after he rose from the dead, blasphemed by his own countrymen; when she sees his preachers, the apostles, scourged; when she considers that whatever she herself knows of him, or the holy oracles declare, *127* is a sign which the rebellious and unbelieving contradict. In all which, who shall weigh with what mourning, with what sadness her mind wastes away, with what wrinkles it is drawn together, with what pallor her face withers: since the sorrow which inwardly weighs down [0225C] and afflicts the mind, the countenance for the most part displays, and the outward foulness of the flesh sets forth? Which Solomon asserting says: « A sorrowful spirit drieth up the bones [n: (Prov. XVII)]. » Sadness therefore, the bones being dried up, blackens and disfigures the Virgin, and, joy being set far off, seizes and usurps for itself the first place, nor suffers the present form to rejoice in a fairer one, since the body that is corrupted darkens it with a blacker dye. Nor is it a wonder if the Bride, made more sorrowful, confesses herself black and deformed, since in this she is not unfruitfully made like the Bridegroom whom she loves, who, for that he is weighed down with annoyances while he is held by corruptible flesh, is by the testimony of the Scriptures declared to have no comeliness and no beauty. « There is, he says, no beauty in him, nor comeliness [n: (Isa. LIII)], » and: [0226A] « We have seen him, and there was no sightliness [n: (ibid.)]. » But he who has no comeliness, no beauty, is understood to be black and deformed; so that the Virgin is not to be accused, if she be conformed to him in blackness, since blackness is to be assigned to neither's ignominy or shame, but the present state of living, given over to wretchedness and toil, renders them deformed. From the bearing of which Christ willed not to be free, but as to all others, so to the Bride and Mother he offered himself for an example, that the Virgin might not herself shrink from seeming black and deformed, but in this also might patiently show herself conformed to the Bridegroom. Finally, below in this epithalamium the Bride says of him: « His locks are black as a raven [n: (Cant. V)]. » Because therefore he is made swarthy and grows black like a raven, the Virgin in no wise blushes to confess herself black also; but lest anyone [0226B] should reckon that very blackness to be the more grievously burdensome the longer it lasts, having made mention of it the Virgin straightway asserts herself comely. *I am black,* she says, *but comely.* Though I suffer penalties, she says, by the merit of human depravity — penalties which in the present furnace are vexed and purged by the cautery of a transitory heaviness — yet beneath this burden a form lies hid, a beauty is nourished, which justice makes glad and an unfailing length makes firm. The body indeed is dead because of sin, but the spirit liveth because of justice [n: (Rom. VIII)]. [var: Vulg. Rom 8:10 *spiritus vero vivit propter iustificationem*, "because of justification" — Philip's exposition below repeats *propter justitiam*] And though the manifold death of penalties, she says, afflicts my bodily life by the merit of the first sin, and daily fastens me, as I die, upon a heavy gibbet: yet that inner man, rich before God, is renewed from day to day, lives because of justice, is adorned with the merits of the virtues. And the more the present [0226C] wretchedness discolours the outer man, so much the more does the love of things to come advance and adorn the inner; to whom both here and now the grace of loving heavenly things gives strength, and, when strength is consummated, promises glory and salvation. « O Lord, says the Prophet, in thy good will thou hast given strength to my comeliness [n: (Psal. XXIX)]. » He who is beautiful in form above the sons of men, by the privilege of his own will, the brightness of eternal light and the mirror of the divine majesty, by his own will imparted strength also to his Mother and Bride, whose comeliness he himself, who had given it, being King and Lord, desired. To whom also, speaking below, he says: « Show me thy face; thy face is fair [n: (Cant. II)]. » And she, as she is called black and deformed by suffering temporal evils, so is she called comely or fair [0227A] by affectionately loving spiritual things, whose inestimable comeliness so affects her that loves it that it makes the blackness of her that suffers to pass over into whiteness. Therefore the Prophet: « O Lord, he says, I have loved the beauty of thy house [n: (Psal. XXV)]. » And the Lord in the Gospel to them that love him: « Your sorrow, he says, shall be turned into joy [n: (Joan. XVI)]. » As therefore affliction renders her black, so love renders the same woman comely: and the former indeed belongs to the present time and is more quickly to have an end, but the latter, though it began in time, yet in no time shall it cease. Whence also the Virgin declared herself to be, not rightly comely and black, but black and comely, because with her blackness held the first place, while it was granted to tribulation to rage against her with looser reins, and both by its coming [0227B] and by its going to come before the beauty that was to be. Of which John says: « Death shall be no more, nor mourning, nor crying, nor sorrow shall be any more, which former things are passed away [n: (Apoc. XXI)]. » [var: Vulg. Apoc 21:4 *quia prima abierunt*, "for the former things are passed away"] This heap of evils therefore came first to the Virgin's heart, but found in it no dwelling or stable place; nay, as it importunately came before her beauty that was to be, so it was compelled to depart when that later beauty more fully came upon her. The Virgin's beauty is, when with fervent spirit, desiring the face of God, she anxiously wipes with her hand the stains of her inward face; when she clears her eye, cleanses her conscience, makes serene her heart, when with severe judgment she sets far off the assaults of them that clamour against her; when at last with a pure and agile mind she is caught up into ecstasy, when she comes to [0227C] the throne of majesty, unapproachable to the slow and the negligent. Whereof he that sat upon the throne subjoins: « Behold, I make all things new [n: (Apoc. XXI)]. » The Lord makes new when with the hand of mercy he wipes away the oldness of our inner man, when he knocks upon ignorance, heals the conscience, directs the will; when, the error of falsehood being wiped away, he grants us to speak truth with our neighbour, when he more gently pours in the delight of that same truth, when at last, to confirm the grace of her inward beauty, he hides us from the tumult of men in the hiding of his own face. She therefore who, exposed to threshings and sufferings, reckons herself black and deformed, the same, hidden in the hiding of the divine face, rejoices to be made conformed to it; and her whom the furnace of tribulation *128* renders shadowy, as it were, with its own soot, [0227D] the ray of contemplation by its outpouring makes bright and comely.
