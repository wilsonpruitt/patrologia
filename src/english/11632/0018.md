## CHAPTER XVIII.

*As the tabernacles of Cedar, as the skin of Solomon.* [var: Vulg. Cant 1:4 *sicut pelles Salomonis*, plural, "as the curtains of Solomon"; the whole chapter expounds the singular skin] Because she had called herself black and comely, she shows in what manner she is black and comely. *Black,* she says, *as the tabernacles of Cedar, comely as the skin of Solomon.* Cedar is interpreted *darkness.* Tabernacles are called, not stable houses, but movable huts, which are set up either by shepherds in the pasture or by soldiers on campaign, and, being placed for a time, are not made firm upon a stable foundation; but when it is convenient they are carried elsewhere without loss. The Virgin therefore not unfittingly confesses herself black as the tabernacles of Cedar, hinting that the condition of this life, by whose bonds she is still [0228A] held, grows dark indeed with much dye or cloud of annoyances, and grows bitter with many stings and prickings of sufferings; whose hateful and shadowy bitterness is nevertheless not founded in an abiding state, but passes after the manner of a tent, and, like a guest, does not linger. Affirming which, Job: « After darkness, he says, I hope for light [n: (Job XVII)]. » And the Lord in the Gospel: « Sorrow, he says, shall be turned into joy [n: (Joan. XVI)]. » For that which the Virgin grieves at, that which she groans at, that which she suffers of the annoyances of temporal life, passes away and is turned into the light of spiritual joy: nor does she complain so much of the presence of a night that will pass, as she rejoices in the sure hope of a light that will remain the longer. Strengthened by the gift of that hope, she cares little for the momentary pressure, while she conforms a pure and untroubled mind [0228B] to the glory that shall come upon her: and, the frost or old age being wiped away, as it were, by which for an hour she is drawn together and sickens, she blossoms again with a glad spirit, and grows young after the manner of an eagle. Whereof Solomon beautifully: « A rejoicing mind, he says, maketh a flourishing age [n: (Prov. XVII)]. » For whose inner man's age would not flourish, whose broken oldness would not grow young by the benefit of the rock — in whose conscience pure faith, secure hope, and abiding love come together, and all these in all things constantly obey God alone? Accordingly the Virgin is rightly called comely as the skin of Solomon, who, the sense of the flesh being cast off, as it were knows not the annoyances of a passing suffering: whose conscience continual obedience, and whose obedience a cheerful conscience, renders commendable and serene, adorns her countenance, and declares her face a stranger to the filth of dust. [0228C] A skin indeed is drawn off from a dead animal, is separated from the flesh by a scraper rather of iron than of flesh: but once separated, it knows no more the hurts or sufferings of the body; struck, it does not grieve; an annoyance being inflicted, it does not sicken. Finally it is shown pliant, tractable, and apt for various uses, if only the labour and industry of a prudent craftsman work upon it: and if a careful craftsman love to bestow his pains, it is found the readier to obey in innumerable ways. So too the Virgin is separated from the things that are of the flesh by that strong scraper which is proclaimed to be sharper than any two-edged sword [n: (Hebr. IV)] and to reach unto the dividing of the spirit and the soul; and she, drawn and drawn off by that iron, as it were knowing not the affections and afflictions of the body, [0228D] loves to forget them, and, being stretched out unto the things that are above, to remember chiefly those. Whom that highest craftsman, designated by the name of Solomon, yet of far ampler benevolence, wisdom and power, drawn off from bodily affection, so fitted to his own spiritual good pleasure, so formed, anointed with the oil of grace, unto all things that were worthy, that no rebellion, no contrariety at all was had in her, but she was rendered, after the manner of a skin, nimble to obey in all things. « Behold, she says, the handmaid of the Lord; be it done unto me according to thy word [n: (Luc. I)]. » You see that, the message being heard, according to his word the handmaid gives herself over to the Lord, and in nothing does the thing formed gainsay its former, nor the shard the potter; but the throne of Solomon is made subject to the command of him that presides over it, nay, the heaven of heaven, his seat, is stretched at the pleasure of him that sits upon it. [0229A] For to this end God stretched out the heaven like a skin, that that prophetic word might stand: « Heaven is my seat [n: (Isa. LXVI)]; » and that other: « The Lord, in heaven is his seat [n: (Psal. X)]. » But who more worthily, who can more fittingly be called heaven, than the Virgin who deserved to be adorned with so many stars of virtues, whose varied brightness mitigates the troublesome darknesses of this life, while it calls back the errors of the unskilled multitude to knowledge? She herself brought forth the moon that should shine out for those darkened in the night, when she exalted the Church, that it might show itself luminous to all: when by form and by doctrine she instructed the holy apostles, whose manner of life imparted a necessary light to the dwellings of the lands. Finally the Virgin herself, or that heaven, brought forth the greater luminary, which conferred upon a languishing world a fuller saving [0229B] remedy: which, because he is God alone, and no other can be made equal to him, has worthily been called the Sun by a certain singular privilege. So the Virgin alone and no other bore so great an offspring; that wondrous heaven in wondrous wise brought forth the sole Sun, who thus fashions day for them that watch, when by his presence he lights up and floods our air, and thus strikes night into the drowsy, when, the mass of earthly sins being set between, he hides himself. But this Sun alone among all things, and above *129* all things luminous, beautiful in the light of his countenance and in form above the sons of men, if he loves by his grace to make fruitful lands heavy with so great a weight of their own, how much rather does he make heaven abound with ampler gifts? Which heaven he is read to have stretched out like a skin, wherein he seems to have shown its nimble obedience, [0229C] because the Virgin, who is reckoned comparable to heaven, nay commendable above heaven, in nothing, not even a little, struggles against the good pleasure of him that commands. I indeed, as I see, am not a skin in the Lord's hand, who am found hard and rebellious toward him that calls and commands, who to his voices and warnings give not open ears, but stop them; and if perchance it fall out that I hear, that I may not obey what I have heard, I harden my heart. But if sometimes, being visited, penitent with a softened heart, obedient to his commandments, I promise perseverance in them; when I am now entering upon the correcting of my own manners or of my people's, behold, a wind rushes in, thunders, tempts, destroys, and I break off what I had begun. For that worst tempter heaps up many darts for himself, by whose infliction he either terrifies the mind more roughly, [0229D] or more gently soothes and unnerves it: and the flesh leads the vanquished spirit into its own right, and, that I may become wholly flesh, the love of carnal things seduces me. Who therefore shall deliver wretched me from the body of this death? [n: (Rom. VII 24.)] Who shall separate me from this flesh by the strong sword of the spirit, that, slaying the flesh and resigning it to the beasts and the birds of Babylon, I may be found skin alone, and be stretched out at the command of Solomon? But if to me, or to any like me, that gift is not yet granted, to whom the bridegroom's chamber and the sanctuary of God still seems unapproachable, yet the true Solomon did not defraud our virgin of that grace, but formed her, after the manner of a skin, to his every pleasure. For her, drawn off from the concupiscence of the flesh and from the hurtful affection of depravity, he lifted up on high by the hand of mercy unto purity: and, [0230A] the desires being scraped away, he spread over her, as it were, varied colours and a starry beauty; nay, he brought in the merits of the virtues and heavenly grace. Which truly the virgin willed should not lie hid from the daughters of Jerusalem, not knowing how to grudge to her familiars the grace she had found; so that to those whom the virgin's blackness can trouble, if it be too negligently regarded, both a worthier cause of that same blackness and a briefer duration of it might be shown.
