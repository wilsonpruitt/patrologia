## CHAPTER XIX.

*I am black, but comely, daughters of Jerusalem.* She names as daughters those whom a little above she named maidens, wherein she designated both their tender affection and their fervent desire; and because daughters, quickened to hear more serenely and to see more studiously, attend more subtly and love more tenderly the praises of a commended beauty: [0230B] You, she says, whom no stolid dullness holds back from understanding, attend and see how commendable is my beauty; you whom the confused blindness of Babylon does not darken, but whose eyes the grace of an inward vision enlightens and unveils. Whence also I pointedly call you daughters of Jerusalem, by which name I the more diligently compel you to see, so that when you hear and mark that you are called by such a name, you may know that a name ought not to be empty, but rather to be derived from the thing. You are therefore truly daughters of Jerusalem if you see, if what is darkly said you intelligibly grasp, if what outwardly sounds harsh you reckon as a shell or as chaff, and what within is sweet and vital you long for, as the kernel, the grain, the fine flour. Finally, know that I myself, who invite you to understanding [0230C] and suffer you neither to be darkened by ignorance nor to wander in gloom, am rightly called Jerusalem by the merit of seeing, to whom it is plain that these divine mysteries and heavenly sacraments are revealed. And when after the manner of childbearing I lead you out of the darkness of ignorance, when with study and labour I bring you through to the light of truth and knowledge, when with affectionate solicitude I form you by more perfect rules of living, what do I do but, in a mother's stead, conform you to my own bowels, or rather to my own manners? And indeed to one of you the Bridegroom says: « Behold thy mother [n: (Joan. XIX)]. » Whose daughters you are by doing well, that is, by not considering my blackness with an over-searching attention — whose cause is profitable, and whose end is already near at the door — lest perchance by too much curiosity you run into a present [0230D] stumbling-block, if you attend to this only, that I am black, more than is just. Accordingly she says: *Do not,* etc.

## CHAPTER XX.

*Consider me not, that I am black, because the sun hath discoloured me.* [var: Vulg. Cant 1:5 *Nolite me considerare quod fusca sim*, "that I am swarthy", and subjunctive; Migne prints *nigra sum*, black and indicative, which is the reading the chapter expounds] See, she says, that you fix not carnal eyes upon my burdens, that the adversity of a shattered will beget no stumbling-block for you, that your consideration be not childishly astounded at my annoyances, as though it were not fitting that my manner of life should grow bitter with tribulations. Have, I pray, as befits daughters of Jerusalem, understanding, and weigh the cause of this thing rather than the effect; what is seen by all, I will to be as though not seen at all by you, and what lies hid from the unwise, I will that you the more wisely *130* behold. That I am black, that I am sorrowful, [0231A] that a cloud of momentary hurt flies about me, that the waters enter even unto my soul and the sword of passion passes through: this the true Sun has done, he by his own counsel has brought me to this, he has spread over my face a dye or blackness of this kind. That Sun, in whose absence a fouler and misshapen night presses in, and all the variety of things is found discoloured and formless; but in whose presence the sky shines, the earth rejoices, its surface is rendered manifold, and the very face of things is made in some sort conformed to what is comely: that Sun, I say, who by the regard of his countenance refreshes and adorns all things, by a certain absence of his own discolours me for a little. For from those who weigh me down he absents himself by a strict judgment, but to me who endure he does not yet present himself for a kiss — perchance that they may advance to the worse by wickedly inflicting [0231B] evils, and I on the contrary to the better, by patiently bearing annoyances. Finally, a prudent virgin, in the letter, greedy of preserving or increasing her beauty, sometimes lays a fillet manifoldly upon her face, that under the veil the beauty desired may advance, and may appear of greater grace when it has been nourished under a medicinal covering. And truly her lover (if only his uprightness be not inexperienced in such things) does not despise a virgin whose face is covered, knowing that whence her comeliness for an hour grows dark by the veil drawn over it, thence, when it is afterwards taken away, it shines out the more pleasingly. So I too am black, wrapped in the dye of a certain veil, and while I live in the flesh not yet wholly loosed from annoyances; because that Sun of mine, who [0231C] wills me to be still in the hovel of this night, wills me not to be altogether free from its injuries. For the place and the time in the midst of which you still see me are not, by a certain implanted law, of full peace or of rest — which peace not even my Bridegroom himself chose to obtain in them by the privilege of his power, but, as is known to you, willed rather to fear and to be weary [n: (Marc. XIV)]. And he who, pressed by many discomforts, willed at the last to undergo death, wills that I too, set here, should not lack the same sufferings, which I bear patiently in his memory and love, being sure indeed that he not only wills but consequently also does. For he who does not yet honour my face with a full colour discolours it, as I seem to see, by his delay; and he who wills me to be blackened by a burden that will the sooner [0231D] pass away — what he disposes to be done, that very thing I do not refuse to do. Since therefore in this wrestling-school my patience the more fully strives, I pray, daughters, that the consideration of these things scandalize you not, and that you attend not to what I suffer, lest your weakness so take offence, but to who it is that wills me to suffer, the knowledge whereof commends you. Nor is whatever I suffer for his sake worthy of scandal, but it is a greater suffering to lack sufferings apart from him; because he who, ill-happy, is granted to be excluded here from the scourge, grieves as a wretch hereafter not to be included in the number of the elect. But my Bridegroom, who himself also endured to be black in the present, wills that same blackness to be in me as in all his own; who, when the fitting time shall have come, will take away this colour, and will change mourning into joy, the dye into whiteness. Whence [0232A] what was said, *The sun hath discoloured me,* can be taken for this, which is (he shall discolour) — the past, namely, for the future; because he who made the things that are to come was indeed about to do that in the Virgin which the virgin, being the more sure, asserts him already to have done: for what the Virgin already foreknows to be future concerning her glory, taught by that Spirit whereof she is full, she does not foretell, does not demand, but, secure of its fulfilling, rejoices and affirms it already fulfilled — which faith and hope possess, and only the fact murmurs back as unfulfilled. Do not, she says, judge my blackness worthy of consideration, as though either it hurt while present or could linger longer, because « I passed by, and lo, it was not [n: (Psal. XXXVI)], » nor is a trace of it to be found; and for you too it shall now be no more, if you will, to pass by in like manner. For that true Sun who bestowed his light [0232B] upon this world, who is risen for us to this end, that he may draw his own out of the deep, will the sooner abolish my dye or blackness with a gracious hand, and will utterly remove wrinkle and spot, grief and every tear. Nay, that I may seem to speak better and far more surely, he has already removed them, has already cherished my sick mind with the wished-for remedy, because by his grace he has already advanced me to such certainty that I hold as done what my understanding has foreknown is to be done next. But as, when he who consoles me by his benefit is set before you, your consideration is not worthily weighed down by the presenting of my blackness, so it is expedient that they who press me hard with their malice should know that their way also shall perish, and that they themselves shall not rise again in the counsel of the just [n: (Psal. I)]. And who that is rightly wise would weigh or [0232C] consider with heavy care whatever they inflict, whose power is quickly to pass away? and would not rather make little both of them and of the annoyances inflicted by them, when he detects in them nothing firm, nothing stable? *131*
