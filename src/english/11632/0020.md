## CHAPTER XXI.

*The sons of my mother have fought against me, they have set me a keeper in the vineyards: my own vineyard I have not kept.* They who ought to have spared her, and to have defended the Virgin from the injuries of others, to run against them that laid hands upon her, to fight back against her assailants, to give their life for her, by their own death to preserve their sister, on the contrary assail her, and rejoice to harass her with persecutions. And, affection being shattered, the law wounded, that right utterly broken which the law of nature has appointed to be kept even among brute beasts, the mother fears not to persecute the daughter, [0232D] the sons the sister: they molest, tear, torture, disembowel; in nothing do they show mercy. Judaea, or the Synagogue, I judge to have stood as the Virgin's mother, who according to the law of the flesh is believed to have borne her, and whom with maternal solicitude she cherished in a tender bosom until the years of advanced youth, and imbued with legal ordinances. But the sons of the mother, in flesh as in manners, are the Jews, blind leaders of the blind, Pharisees more by malice than by knowledge, who, forgetful of the brotherly covenant, the more grievously afflicted their sister when they fastened her son to the cross rather with viperous tongues than with iron nails. Of whom the prophet Amos: « They have not, he says, remembered the covenant of brethren *(Amos I)*. » For of this covenant, whereby brothers ought to be the more affectionately bound to a sister, they, sick with a venomous [0233A] perfidy, would not more soundly be mindful; but as though strangers to the stock, set far from nature, they render her rights neither to a sister nor indeed to a human being. And who of sound head would think soundly of Judaea, who is a mother and yet loves not, even as the fables report of Medea, to which mother there was no love of her sons, no care, but, kindled with hatred, she took them out of the way by an untimely death? As Medea, so Judaea strives to render false that argument which nature has woven for the safeguard of a debt of love — an argument which not only the modern loquacity of disputing clerics winnows and affirms, but also the sagacity of the heathen, tried from of old. If she is a mother, they say, she also loves. But because Medea was a mother and yet loved not, she put off, as they say, the nature of a mother rather than neglected it; and since [0233B] even a brute beast is held by a natural affection toward its own young, she is convicted not to be a mother who is not softened even by a beast's feeling. For the rest, Solomon, upon whom God bestowed wisdom, the assessor of his own throne, by this argument proves that harlot to be the mother who chose that her own infant be given alive to the lying woman rather than, at the king's word, be cut asunder by the sword that was brought. « She said, he says, whose son was alive, to the king (for her bowels were moved over her son): I beseech thee, my lord, give her the living child, and do not kill it. On the contrary the other said: Let it be neither mine nor thine, but let it be divided. The king answering said: Give this woman the living child, and let it not be slain; for she is its mother [n: (III Reg. III)]. » And elsewhere it is written: « No man hath hated his own [0233C] flesh [n: (Ephes. III)]. » But Judaea loves to obey neither the testimonies of Scripture nor the commands of nature, but to rage against her daughter with an incredible hardness; and, overstepping the rights of the laws, she spreads abroad and practises a most evil fury of mind, whose sons likewise no more considerate regard restrains from malice. For they are those who, like their mother, neither fear God nor reverence man, who with obstinate malice deny that man is God, and think it does not befit his majesty and deep counsel that he should will and be able to come down to bear our miseries. They indeed assert that the mother of Christ conceived of Joseph, and that she remained a virgin neither in conception nor after childbirth; and whatever is venerably preached either of her integrity [0233D] or of her son's majesty is by their testimonies disowned as feigned and frivolous. If a virgin, they say, has borne, she first lay with a man. Whence also they persecute her son not for a good, as they say, work, but for blasphemy [n: (Joan. X)], and endeavour to scrape his memory from off the earth: namely because, among those who are mere men, he takes to himself something more, and, being man, himself presumes to make himself God. At the last, malice pressing on, they adjudge the innocent to death, they accuse him as one not paying due reverence to the law and to the king: they shut him in with bonds, smear him with spittle, heap buffets upon him, hand him over to the judge, and the judge to the soldier; an unlovely clamour resounds; and they crucify him, these with clattering tongues, those with nails. And what do all these things do but rave [0234A] against the Virgin, sound the trumpet furiously, wrestle against the walls of her patience? They assail and storm the tower of her constancy with a heavy ram, they shake it unmoved, but by the interposing of grace they do not take it. And it is to be noted that, although both Gentiles and Jews rage against the Virgin, though Pilate and the soldiers do what the Pharisees demand in words, yet here, where the battle is recounted in a prophetic spirit and in a nuptial song, the Virgin makes mention of her own only, and no mention is made of outsiders. *The sons,* she says, *of my mother.* Why this, save because, as you know, by right judgment he troubles the more who dishonours when he ought to have shown deference, who (when in his bosom, as a friend's, rest was to have been hoped for) hands over to the hands of an enemy the one he ought to have cherished? For when an enemy curses, the cursing is *132* borne the more patiently; [0234B] when foe rages against foe, he deserves either not to be blamed, or not to be blamed harshly; when a stranger does not succour a stranger with generous good will, as he wins no praise, so neither does he incur much reproach. But the man of peace, the man of one mind, the man familiarly known [n: (Psal. LIV)] speaks the more unjustly, so that he offends, when a friend or a neighbour draws near against me more harshly, when, instead of loving me, he venomously detracts and assails, when he shows evil for good, hatred for love: whence have I to bear it, whence to be able to act patiently, and not rather to complain that the faithless man rages fraudulently? Being silent therefore about the Gentiles, the Virgin complains of the Jews, and their malice is for the present brought into the midst — who, being the sons of her mother, so grievously offended the Virgin, and repaid her with battle for defence, [0234C] with hindrance for help.
