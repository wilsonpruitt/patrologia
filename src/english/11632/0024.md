## CHAPTER XXV.

*Show me where thou feedest, where thou liest down in the midday.* Having asked where the Bridegroom feeds, the Bride adds the asking where [0240B] he also lies down in the midday; touching upon the custom of shepherds, who, when they have given their labour to the pasture in the first part of the day, at midday turn aside and lie down for the sake of rest. Since therefore foxes are known to have dens, and the birds of heaven nests [n: (Luc. IX)], into which very many wretched men, with blind brow and erring foot, tumble down: the Virgin wills to know more surely whither the Son of man chooses to turn aside, and where he would lay back his own head for the sake of quiet. For the season admonishes, when the world is sultry and burns, when with a keener sun the midday sky glows white, that the provident Shepherd should turn aside into the shade and rest, and that the little flock, fleeing to him, should lie hid at ease. The sun is sultry, the heat falls upon the minds of the wicked, when the wicked heap of vices flows together upon them: when avarice, when lust, when [0240C] ambition lords it over them, when with the burning of such things their inward face is blackened. In this midday therefore, whose heat that world cannot bear to endure, that Holy One to whom nothing unclean pertains — whither he turns aside, where he pauses, where he finds it welcome to find a chamber — I myself, if it might be done, would wish to know, not so much by knowledge as by experience. For when pride lifts us up in the goods either of nature or of grace, when a clinging anger inflames us, envy torments us, lust makes us soft; when prayer is heavy, divine reading is loathed, when the word of the preacher, the ear being closed after the manner of the asp, is not heard: it is to be feared lest, weighed down by this heat and unseasonable distemper, that shepherd, forsaking us, flee away beneath a convenient shade. With a heavy and parching sun the world set in [0240D] wickedness was sultry, and with the midday burning Egypt or Ethiopia laboured, when that Holy Spirit overshadowed the incorrupt Virgin, and prepared in her for God the Son of God a welcome covert of shade [n: (Luc. I)]. The same burning, or distemper, had lain hard enough upon the Pharisees, and with a looser rein had been spread abroad among the Jews, when Christ, forsaking them, turned aside to a few apostles, and gathered them like a hen under the shadow and covering of her wings. But when even unto the cross the Jews and the Gentiles raged against him at midday, and some with the tongue and others with the hand poured forth the venom of a boiling wrath, he himself, not so much with the foot as with the affection, more with the mind than with the eye forsaking those whom he despised, saw standing beside the cross his Mother and the disciple whom he loved [n: (Joan. XIX)]. And *136* when he had received the vinegar, [0241A] which being tasted rather than drunk he thus fulfilled the mystery, upon these, whom the Spirit overshadowed, he rested the more graciously as he rested the more sweetly; and while the sun was sultry without, whose rage filled him full, bowing his head toward these, and delivering up his spirit, he fell asleep. Thus, no longer bearing the midday and the raging heats of the Crab or of the Lion, at the sixth hour consummating the agony, or the mystery, of the Passion, he turned aside and loved the shade of the Spirit, the serene lying-down of the tomb, to which the stone set against it was no hindrance for one that should rise again. « For he lay down as a lion [n: (Gen. XLIX)]. » [var: Vulg. Gen 49:9 *requiescens accubuisti ut leo*, second person, "resting, thou hast lain down as a lion"] Whom indeed the tomb was able to hold as long as he willed, but, his will fulfilled, he was not held back by the barrier of the stone; nay rather, for rising in the morning the divine power rendered him strong as a lion, and equally for rendering requital at midday to them that raged. [0241B] Nor do I judge it unworthy if we reckon that the same Bridegroom lies down in you also, if we declare that he finds even in us some little rest: since both to serve him and to soothe him is our will, by the gift of his grace, although for them that will the power is not yet wholly free. But if we ourselves, or perchance any others, should judge that it does not sufficiently befit us to ascribe to ourselves that whereto all our betters scarcely avail to come: it is to be feared lest on the contrary we distrust more than is just, and by a degenerate distrust incur an equal peril. Between the two therefore let not Daedalus, but the Father of spirits teach us to fly; let him rule the unknown way, let him be willing to give the grace of journeying, lest we be plunged in mud and dregs while, after the likeness of the earth, we keep to the things nearer to carnal men, [0241C] or lest we be pressed down from glory while after the fashion of [sic: *lehari*] we foolishly assail the higher things. For the Bridegroom lies down in us, if we are proved to love him, if with diligent zeal we make ourselves ready for his service: if whatever clamours against him and gives offence we hasten to wipe away with broom or with razor, if the secret of our heart, for love's sake, we adorn with the flowers of love. For nothing does he desire more gladly, nothing more eagerly, than to be loved, namely when we truly embrace him with a singular love, when whatever the world offers we cast far away as refuse for his sake, when in the world or in ourselves we love nothing beside himself. Finally John [n: (Cap. XIII)] declared that he reclined upon the breast of that same Bridegroom, because he, above the rest, loved him as he reclined: nor is any other, as you know, said to recline [0241D] in that same place, save he whom Jesus, by frequent testimony, is not silent that he loves. And rightly it is read that not Jesus lay down in John's bosom, but John in Jesus' bosom, because it is believed that we did not love him first, but that he first loved us: and where a man truly understands that he is loved, there he rejoices to recline, and to be delighted with a sweet reclining. For Christ before his passion understood that John was weak, as the rest also were, and that he himself was loved less than sufficiently by the disciple whom he loved, because throughout that time neither John nor Peter either willed or was able to undergo death for him — which, before his Spirit was received after the resurrection, none of them deserved to obtain. But for the rest, Christ was found so to love those whom he chose, and his charity toward them so constrained him as he loved, that for them [0242A] he willingly gave his soul unto death, and those whom he held in his bosom he defended from death by dying. But after his resurrection, appearing to the same disciples, he breathed upon them [n: (Joan. XX)], and by a most serene breath assigned to them the pontifical office of binding and loosing, when a little after the vehement Spirit inflamed them with fiery tongues [n: (Act. II)], and unbendingly strengthened them against the threats, the entreaties and the bribes of the world; and that death's turn which he first willed to undergo for them, they themselves, not wrapped about with the ropes of avarice but with a prodigal bounty, rendered back. In these, and in such as they, the Bridegroom must not undeservedly be believed to lie down, with whom he finds nothing whereby his rest can be hurt: in whom devotion is vigorous, discipline glows the more devoutly, where the furnace does not smoke, but the whole workshop of love utterly burns. For because the Bridegroom, [0242B] as has been said before, desires to be loved, and love is a fire, those whom he beholds to be kindled and to burn with love, in these, as upon a little bed, he wills to lie for the sake of quiet. And on this account he is said not to lie down save at midday, when the sun, nearer to heaven, is removed farther from the earth, when hotter than its wont it sends forth purer rays, when with a greater burning it affrights the broader fields of the aether. And perhaps he feeds in the morning, when the troublesome mist of the nights now withdraws, when the sun, necessary to one that rises to his works, goes before him; when its rolling wheel strains upward with labouring horses, and, knowing not how to stand in one place, does not aim to journey and to advance for itself. Through which space of time the sun affords the day a useful nourishment, whereby the day, cherished and promoted, gradually receives [0242C] increase, until the sun, hastening and arriving at mid-heaven, is fixed, where, now higher in degree, it is compelled either to stand or to descend. So indeed, when to you, brethren, sitting in darkness, the Sun of grace brought the morning light, the night's error being laid aside you hastened to lay hold of discipline; and the more fully he spread out for you his brighter rays, the more gladly you labour and take to yourselves the ampler advances. While therefore with watchings, fasting, sweat and cold you chastise the contrary body, while, devout and humble, you bind your patience with the ropes of obedience, and in regard of the commandments banish your own will; while you labour to work the food which perishes not, but which abides, and with untiring zeal eat the labours of your own hands: assuredly with you the Bridegroom feeds along with you [0242D] *137* and labours, even as the devout lover and getter of wisdom indeed prays: « Give me, he says, wisdom, that she may be with me and may labour with me [n: (Sap. IX)]. » For in feeding there is indeed a life-giving fruit, but beyond doubt with labour; the labour is a little thing, made bearable by the more welcome fruit: and for this cause, when the just man invites patience along with himself to labour, he loves not so much the labour itself as its better fruit. Accordingly the Virgin desires the more anxiously to know not only where the Bridegroom feeds, but also where he lies down, that she may avail to come to his more welcome state; because, although while he feeds he gives us no middling advance, yet while he lies down and rests he carries us forward to perfection more graciously and more surely. When therefore, the body being chastised, the murmur of the passions lulled asleep, the labour of temporal actions not wholly laid down but for an hour [0243A] set aside; when prayer, devotion, the rumination of the reading you have heard makes you serene within, and the grace of the divine vision snatches you up, hung from the earth, as it were on high; when (though hurriedly and for a little) you seem to be present among the angelic spirits, and, beholding after some fashion the glory of the Lord, you say with Peter: « It is good for us to be here [n: (Matth. XVII)]; » when with the love of such things you are sweetly sultry as with a fire, and a Sun brighter than the sun floods you with the ray of his own brightness: then indeed in you the Bridegroom feeds, and is delighted with a friendly rest, rejoices in such a midday, and day is glad in day, light in light. For as the wicked enemy, the author of evil, the prince of ruined Babylon, loves the deep darkness of night and the cold of the north wind, so the Bridegroom who is God, unfailing light, burning fire, putting the welcome presence of himself [0243B] far from the unlike and the unworthy, lies down and reposes in those whom he perceives to be burned with the fiery torch of divine love, whom, snatched into ecstasy, he beholds to shine with the midday light, and not to be burned up. Who, forgetful of things present, are intent upon heavenly things alone, not content with the dawn or the morning sun and the works of them that are beginning, but are carried up to the perfect day and the fervent midday, one spirit with God, not made perfect only by reason of the flesh's fellowship as yet. Happy is he who, forgetful of the things behind, deserved to be thus stretched out to the things before, and, loathing the morning's mildness, to be thus kindled with the midday's advance and an exceeding fervour; to hold the bridegroom in his bosom, to embrace him with affection as with an arm, to fasten his eyes upon the sun, and not to be turned aside to the carcases of this flesh. Happy, I say, if yet [0243C] he fears not the fall of this benefit, if the midday of this heat and of this splendour alike inclines not to its setting; if the flesh draws him not back, and the still weaker law of nature, which cannot yet bear the glory of a happiness that shall abide. Happy therefore is that day whose midday goes not down, in whose fiery light the Bridegroom ever abides and reclines: to which whosoever shall have come, he perseveres in it with steadfast step, and, its good being found, affirms that nothing is better, nothing more delightful. In which the holy angels ever behold the face both of the Father and of the Son, and the souls of the saints are not held estranged from the sight of him; which souls, snatched away already from the wicked world and from the prison-house of the flesh, are received with the angels into the quiet chamber of that same Bridegroom. There a fuller and more perfect midday is found, where the day declines not, and [0243D] is not ended by the oncoming of evening; and there dawns not save one only day, neither advancing nor failing, whose splendour is not made swarthy, whose splendid heat does not grow lukewarm. Accordingly no man in this life fully receives such a midday, but foretastes it; there he is fully satisfied, where delay, being shorter, does not straiten, where the love of all men toward God burns continual and unremitting, and he himself who is loved gratefully lies down in all and rests. And so upon earth he feeds — in him alone, being accepted, to whose account he sets down justice, he pours in the bounty of grace; with whose good works, as with herbs and flowers, he refreshes his own will; whose manners he accepts, whose equity he loves to see; in whose merits he rejoices, whose virtues he embraces, whose holiness he receives; for whom the morning day and the sun shine with an imperfect light, while he labours and advances, [0244A] because labour is fashioned in a precept [n: (Psal. XCIII)]. But in the heavens the Bridegroom lies down, where the day rises not with a dim light, but as at midday the sun glows red with an advance already ripe, whose warm splendour, and heat equally splendid, have nothing imperfect, but with unmoving pole, as in a kind of solstice, they stand perfect. In the day therefore the day, light in light, in the sun the sun lies down sweetly and rests, because the just man in the kingdom of his Father shines bright as the sun [n: (Matth. XIII)]; and the Bridegroom, the sun of justice, receives in him a chamber and a rest welcome to him in this, that it is not straitened in space, not troubled by the assault of annoyance. Accordingly the anxious Virgin not undeservedly asks that it be shown her where he feeds, where he lies down whom she loves with a singular love, that when it has been shown, she may safely and gladly follow thither, lest (if it be shut) [0244B] she be borne wandering after the flocks of the companions.
