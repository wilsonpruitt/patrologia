## CHAPTER XXVI.

*Lest I begin to wander after the flocks of thy companions.* He is Christ's companion who wills to be reckoned by his name, who says that he is his, who chooses to profess his faith; who announces his word, who preaches the way of truth; who joins himself to Christ, either in the reality or in the image of holiness. But because there are many who rejoice not in the true *138* reality, but are coloured with the image, who by affection and by work detest what they build up by word or by countenance: these the Virgin willed to call not a flock but rather flocks, knowing that the singular name is more fittingly attributed to the just. Whence below in the Song: « Thy hair as of goats, thy teeth as a flock of sheep [n: (Cant. VI)]. » [var: Vulg. Cant 6:4–5 *capilli tui sicut grex caprarum*, "thy hair as a flock of goats" — the singular *grex* Philip's argument turns on is absent from the first clause as printed] And in the Gospel: « Fear not, little flock *(Luc. [0244C] XII)*. » The flocks therefore of the companions are the multitude of them that believe falsely, in whom there is a show of piety, but no virtue, no strength of works; in whom the ostentation of the Christian religion is apparent, in whom the preaching or the doctrine of Scripture sometimes sounds forth, but the life savours not rightly, the discipline of their manners is not fragrant enough. Who for the most part wound and seduce the consciences of the weak, and, turning aside from the path of justice, lead them into the broad room of error, and by open converse or by hidden counsels or by worse examples corrupt good manners, and either take away from them the narrow way of religion utterly, or at the least break it off for a little. After seducers of this sort the most prudent Virgin wills not to be borne, but to discern wisely who [0244D] ought to be preferred to whom, lest, if she take Nicolas for Stephen and Judas for John, she be held like to them, or be twofold a daughter of hell. Which evil the Virgin knows to be wrought at the instigation of the malignant spirits, whom iniquity set and removed far from the seat of the heavenly ones, while they beheld not the divine excellence with an eye humble enough, but, affecting an unapproachable likeness, fell from heaven after the manner of lightning. Who, knowing that they shall never return thither whence they had fallen, but that men made of clay shall ascend by the working of grace and by merits, corrupted with malice, envy and are tormented with a wondrous grief, and with utmost straining do hurt to those to whom they behold this grace to be given; since therefore they have an untiring mind for envying, and, according to a certain heathen, a thousand arts of hurting are theirs, as much at midnight [0245A] as at midday they stretch out their nets, and either by an arrow in the darkness, or by a midday assault, they seize the incautious and the wretched [n: (Psal. XC)]. The arrow flying in the darkness, which strikes grievously while it is unknown, is the hidden suggestion whereby our mind is soothed, when, a kind of veil being spread over it, it promises itself either no wound or a venial one, and, as with a little wound, lets in a deadly poison to the wretched heart. But the midday assault is the open malice of the assailant, when the vehement shamelessness of the malignant one pours out all its strength, when it grievously casts us down from the throne of patience, of obedience, of continence, or perchance, when we resist, threatens and sets before us losses, perils and martyrdoms more evidently. But if perchance the malignant spirit find any man stronger, so that he fears not its terrible threats, [0245B] nor its keener onset, it lays aside its threats, is silent about perils, brings nothing in violently, but turns itself fraudulently to a certain show of religion: for perceiving that watchings, prayers, abstinence, fasting, the labour of the hands are pleasing to some, it does not resist but presses on, and exhorts them first to abide more strongly in these things; and, lest they be outstripped in them, to guard themselves the more anxiously each against the other; and then, not content with the due time, not to keep the measure regularly appointed. While therefore anyone, fervent in spirit, attempts to be no less than the rest, and does not hold back with the bridle of moderation the loosened horse of desire; while he forestalls the solemn vigils, that he may pray more devoutly or ruminate somewhat more sweetly out of the Scriptures; while he fasts and abstains, lest he should as it were burden his belly with meats that will more quickly pass away; [0245C] while, being still vigorous in body, he strains beyond the labour of the hands that is due; while for these things, or for the like of these, he stretches himself out with looser reins: there meet him on this side and on that the blasts of vainglory, the love of praise, a presumptuous self-esteem, contempt of others, rash judgment, a harsher and hurtful reproof. For condemning the rest, whom he beholds to keep a looser measure as though they were lukewarm, he now reckons himself, as being more strongly fervent, better than all men; and the more inconsiderately he burns for greater things, the more, hurt by the wind of pride, he withers the more wickedly and is the more foully emptied out. For him whom fervour drives headlong, a vanity brought in beneath receives, and a certain show introduced deceives his conscience, which, feigning a good thing by a false light, blunts and darkens his eyes, while he works the more subtly who transfigures himself into an angel of light. [0246A] By whose work this wretched man in no wise reckons himself deluded, but rather as flooded with a kind of ray of the midday sun, in which, while he attends only to the surface as to a conspicuous flower, he hopes for a surer fruit, and does not detect the flaw in the root. Seduced therefore by him, he now wanders after the flocks of the companions, while, as though working well, he is defrauded by the craft of the malignant spirits, whom in some sort he finds to be Christ's companions and fellows, while he reckons that he pleases Christ, and is nevertheless found to please them. After companions of this sort the more prudent Virgin wills not to stray, nor to be defrauded by their midday show of good, but to detect their cunning with a keen-sighted eye, and to find Christ in his own light and in a midday that does not deceive. I will, she says, to find thy light, a true light, [0246B] useful and serene, lest, deceived by error, I run after another's light; lest the falling Lucifer falsely take to himself the likeness of Michael, lest Satan offer himself to me in the form and appearance of Gabriel. I will, she says, not to be seduced by the midday assault and demon, not to be led away by their blandishments and deceiving pretence, who lie in calling themselves thy companions, who, rejoicing in the name of angels, enjoy not the angelic blessedness. *139* Lest therefore the aforesaid light and day of these men should flatter me guilefully, show in what man or in what angel a rest welcome to thee is had, which with thyself glows red and flames, not only in the day, but in the midday, being perfect, neither begun in the morning nor growing toward evening by a downward course.

[0246C] Hitherto the Bride, the Virgin, has spoken of him and to him whom she more tenderly loves, and has followed out her love and desire in continuous speech: now she takes up silence, yet not omitting speech altogether, but breaking off a little for the sake of hearing. And so let us too in this place take up silence and rest awhile, and at last set an end to this first book, since it also asks that a limit be set to its own length, and our weakness asks that the labour begun be now, at least for a little, laid aside. But to the setting forth of that which the Bridegroom vouchsafed to answer the Virgin, if the day shall have dawned, I will take care to turn back my resting pen; if that Holy Spirit shall vouchsafe to come and teach me in silence what may please him that I should speak rather than be silent.
