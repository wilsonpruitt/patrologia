## CHAPTER III.

[0250B]

*If thou know not thyself, O fair one among women, go forth and go thy way after the footsteps of the flocks.* [var: Vulg. Cant 1:7 *o pulcherrima inter mulieres*, superlative, "O fairest among women" — the same head lemma is printed *pulcherrima* at 0245D] The Bridegroom, not willing to promote the Virgin by degrees to advances, teaches her how she may deserve to obtain the grace of advancing: namely, if the things she has so obtained she so lay up with herself, as in a hidden place, that the guileful enemy may not lay his pitchy hand upon them even for a little. For he often lays a hurtful touch upon the better goods of a greater grace of holiness, when he pours over them a ruin, the wicked mist of a darkening vanity; when either with the heavy blast of pride he overwhelms and hardens a mind endowed with gifts, or at the least with a light breeze he somewhat darkens the beauty of a tender face. For it is difficult for any Virgin to behold herself endowed with the better gifts, and either not to be shaken by the battering-ram of proud [0250C] vanity, or not to be moved by a blast however slight: to feel herself excellent in form and praiseworthy in countenance, and never with a bent mind to consent to one that praises her the more often. Whence it is necessary that she who wills not to be touched by pride should not set a mirror before herself, lest, when she has seen herself fair, she should perhaps set herself before the rest: nay rather, that she may not consent even for a little to the flattering deceiver, she should offer her face, unknown to herself, to be known by her chaste lover only. And so the chaste lover, willing that the Virgin should please none but himself, wills that she should not have knowledge of her own face; he wills that the Virgin should not know herself, should not marvel at her own beauty, lest, marvelling or praising, she should glory in herself rather than in the Lord. That angel would have been held happy, if [0250D] in some sort he had not known himself, when in the delights of paradise he outstripped Lucifer in splendour; happy, I say, if he had not been proud of the things he had received, and, being founded by God, had not thought himself God's equal. Far better, far more happily, Abraham knew not himself, when, as though not knowing himself, he sought divine converse, when, forgetful that he was held great in merits and more illustrious in life, by a prudent counsel he confessed himself to be nothing but dust and ashes. « I will speak, he says, to my Lord, whereas I am dust and ashes [n: (Gen. XVIII)]. » Happy also king David, who, because he wills not to know the letter of the law which he had learned, setting little by his own, deserves to enter into the power of the Lord; and, not establishing his own, serves God's justice with bowed neck, and, forgetful of his own things, remembers only the things that are the Lord's. [0251A] « Because I have not known letters, he says, I will enter into the powers of the Lord; O Lord, I will be mindful of thy justice only [n: (Psal. LXX)]. » On the contrary, that wretched Nabuchodonosor, who put not the knowledge of himself away from himself, who knew himself rich, most powerful, endowed with various successes, who attended more to himself commanding than to him through whom kings command, and, beholding himself great, set little by the author of his own greatness. « Is not this, he says, the great Babylon, which I have built in the strength of my might, and in the glory of my beauty? » [n: (Dan. IV.)] But if anyone shall object that some are found to have gone over their own illustrious works, in which nevertheless Scripture does not judge that they were reprehensible: let him attend to the time, let him discern the cause *142* and in whom this is found to be done; and let him know when [0251B] and why it is found to be as lawful as it is necessary. For when anyone perceives that he is the more grievously constrained to suffer or to do evils, if he wills to be plucked out of them by prudent counsel, as feet out of a snare, he can, nay he ought, lovingly to recount the goods which he has already obtained, that by the memory of better things he may be able to remove the evils that press upon him. Hence it is that Job, when he was shaken with so great a weight of evils and with their number at once, and was urged by his wife's insistence to curse God at last, calls back to memory and goes over the goods which he had already received; and the heap of evils did not wrap him, who was mindful of good things, in the stains of impatience. « The heart of the widow, he says, I comforted, and I was clad with justice: and I clothed myself with it as with a garment. I was an eye to the blind, and a foot to the lame; I was the father of the poor. And when I sat [0251C] as a king with his army standing about him, yet I was a comforter of them that mourned [n: (Job XXIX)]. But now the younger in time deride me [n: (Job. XXX)]. » And above: « If we have received good things at the hand of the Lord, why should we not endure evils? » [n: (Job. II.)] [var: Vulg. Job 2:10 *si bona suscepimus de manu Dei, mala quare non suscipiamus?* — *Dei* becomes *Domini*, and "receive" becomes "endure"] The good man did not think his own good things worthy to be pressed down in silence, when so great a manifoldness of evils straitened him on every side, so that the sweet remembrance of good things might render light the burden of the pressure that lay upon him — as long afterward the Scripture of a certain Wise Man admonished: « In the day of evils, he says, be not unmindful of good things [n: (Eccli. XI)]. » To this end avails, as I judge, that saying which the letters of the Gentiles so favourably extolled, and, as worthy of memory, handed on in writing to posterity, which [0251D] some assert that one of the seven wise men of Greece uttered, while others rather affirm that Apollo answered it in an elegant oracle: « Know, he says, thyself, O man. » Because the wise men of the world knew that man consists of a rational soul and a body, of which they asserted that he has the one in common with the gods and the other in common with the beasts, those of them whose doctrine was sounder preferred reason to the body, and by that judgment, detracting from the beasts, they deferred (as was more fitting) to the gods. And so they willed that man should be ruled by reason going before, and that the bestial flesh should be subjected to the rational spirit; and, the spirit being carried up, that man should be taken away from among the beasts, and, if not in place, at least in merit, be transferred into the fellowship of the gods. [0252A] For to this end, and on this account, they reckoned man to have been made, and that this difference lies naturally between man and beast: that the beast freely gapes after earthly things, while man is bidden to aspire to heavenly things — which the uplifted face given to man also seems to indicate. Of this thing Apollo wills that man should prudently be mindful: to know whence he comes, and whither by reason's desert he ought to journey; to forget what is beneath and behind him, as though given over to sleep; to wake up, by the nimbleness of his powers, to that which is before. But because very many turn aside and stray from the path of reason, who after the manner of beasts are plunged in the filth of earthly action, who entangle and wrap their hands in grievous offences, who cast their feet into voluntary sins and do not absolve themselves from the crimes laid against them, of whom scarcely any is found who lives according to man, [0252B] nay, according to the philosopher, is found to live as a swine or an ass: therefore the seer cries out, does not permit the tripod to be silent, the oracle sounds, a censorial voice sends forth its doctrine: Great Apollo thunders, and, the lightning being loosed, wills to be feared; he wills that horse and mule, swine and ass be frightened away from error and ignorance; he wills that all the better sort be admonished to honest knowledge, and that his own hearers be advanced to the understanding of reason's excellence. « Know, he says, thyself, O man. » Of which sentence Paul was not unaware, when he was constrained to suffer grievous things, when, tenderly compassionating the weakness and the scandal of the Corinthians, he was set afire, lest he should be weighed down more than is just with another's load, or perhaps that he might seem worthier to be heard by them that were weighed down: he has recourse to knowledge, professes that he knows himself a man — one who serves not [0252C] the flesh, but who glories to be grafted into heaven. « I know, he says, a man in Christ caught up even to the third heaven; and I know such a man, whether in the body or out of the body, I know not, God knoweth, that he was caught up into paradise, and heard secret words which it is not lawful for a man to utter [n: (II Cor. XII)]. » And he added: « For such a one will I glory [n: (ibid.)]. » Do you see that Paul knows himself to be a man lifted up to the third heaven, to visit paradise, and to glory for so great a grace? To this end, namely, that the Corinthians should not defer more to false apostles, and prefer guileful teachers to his doctrine and his merits. Otherwise, if this cause or necessity, or one like to it, had not been, which forced a wise man to this as it were unwisdom, [0252D] assuredly he would have remembered that saying of the Gospel spoken to his fellow-apostles, and would have held it, as worthy of memory, in speech as in conscience: « When you shall have done all good things, say that we are unprofitable servants [n: (Luc. XVII)]. » [var: Vulg. Luke 17:10 *cum feceritis omnia quae praecepta sunt vobis*, "all things that are commanded you" — Philip's gloss immediately below expounds *omnia bona*] In which saying it is given to be understood that no one is proved to do all good things, unless the saying of this be added to the rest of his good things: and he does not colour the heap of his good things with a surface worthy enough, who is not ignorant of it for the sake of avoiding vanity. That therefore you may know the Bride to be perfectly holy and humble, the Bridegroom teaches that this ignorance is in her, who, while she hastens to the rest which the son does not yet give but promises, does not commit the things which she already obtains to a boastful [0253A] memory. *If thou know not,* he says, *thyself, O fair one.* For in that he says Si, it is not a dubitative but an affirmative conjunction; as that poet: « Ye gods, if there be any piety in heaven. » And so the Bridegroom says: *If thou know not thyself, O fair one* — which indeed is difficult, but yet *143* is a man — *Go forth.* But go forth whence and whither, save from thy contemplation to thy work? Go forth from the consideration of thy own beauty to the forming of the beauty of others; as though neglectful of thyself, be anxious for very many: form the disciples, teach the apostles the things which thou hast more perfectly known of me; stoop to John, whom as a son thou, a mother, hast received. *Go forth,* I say, *and go thy way after the footsteps of the flocks.* They who preside over flocks walk after their footsteps, and are known to have the care of them before [0253B] their eyes; lest any should stray, lest any should sit down weary, they lead their watchful eyes about, they rejoice with the healthier, and every weak one they carry back upon their shoulders or lead home. It is therefore a mark of power to preside over flocks, and the apostles are admonished to sit beside the Virgin as she presides, that the Virgin may command them, and as a mother may bear the care of all, and that they may hear their mother, as the Gospel affirms. « Woman, he says, behold thy son [n: (Joan. XIX)]; » and to the disciple: « Behold thy mother [n: (ibid.)]. » For when the care of John is delegated to the Virgin Mother, the care of the rest is not, as I judge, banished from her; but what is more specially assigned to one for the sake of a certain mystery is designated as generally necessary for all. For neither does John alone receive Mary and venerate her whom he has received, nor does the Virgin [0253C] embrace John alone with the affection of a devout care, but, loving all, she is zealous to show herself useful to all, to show herself pre-eminent in doctrine and imitable by all. « All, he says, were with one mind continuing in prayer with Mary the mother of Jesus [n: (Act. I)]. » [var: Vulg. Acts 1:14 *hi omnes erant perseverantes unanimiter in oratione cum mulieribus, et Maria matre Iesu, et fratribus eius* — "with the women" and "and his brethren" are absent] And so the Virgin is admonished to go away after their footsteps, that she may search out their goings with a diligent eye, that she may investigate the more anxiously what they do, whither they tend, by what way they pass; that she may advance what is worthy of praise, and may not pass over the wanderer either, but chastise him. The flocks are the apostles, and they who are gathered to them by faith and by works, who, imbued with the evangelical sacraments, are separated from the error of the ignorant, who, venerating the Virgin and believing her word, sighing after the eternal pasture, hold in contempt the things that are of the world. [0253D] To whom it is said through Ezechiel: « And you, my flocks, saith the Lord [n: (Ezech. XXXIV)]. » Whom perhaps he therefore willed to call flocks in the plural number, because the apostles, being bidden to preach the word of the Gospel to the nations that should believe, being divided one from another under the Spirit's leading, chose diverse parts of the world, in which by their zeal in preaching they gathered many flocks and assemblies of men, and many churches. Who, although they are diverse in place, in time, and in the merit of their virtues, yet, while they all come to the catholic faith and the evangelical doctrine, many flocks are brought back into one, and very many into one Church: and all are united in the undivided sacrament of faith and of baptism.
