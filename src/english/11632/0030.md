## CHAPTER VI.

*Thy cheeks are beautiful as the turtledove's.* In the cheeks [0256C] it is agreed that the Virgin's beauty appears the more, in which a snowy colour is found to blush faintly beneath; nay, whiteness and redness seem to envy one another, while each strives to hold alone the surface of the cheeks. Or, if it please better — nay, because it does please — when whiteness pours itself over the whole face with a large bounty, and redness overspreads itself upon the cheeks with a thin sprinkling: they smile upon one another, and there comes to be a certain friendly fellowship of colours, while out of the snowy the rosy, and out of the rosy the snowy, is made the more pleasing. And so the cheeks are coloured with the Virgin's commendable comeliness, which are adorned by a grace in them not confounded but diffused; in which the ruddy colour, or the sanguine redness, is joined to whiteness, and the one does not disdain the other, but with a throat ever [sic: *a vida*] is satisfied. The Virgin's whiteness [0256D] is the cleanness of her flesh and her virginal integrity, which no stain, no bodily corruption harms; which, being the more safely fortified, grows not foul at the robber's violent onset, being the more diligently shut in, grows not pale when the blast blows more harshly. But the redness which is becomingly coupled to the snowy colour is the fervour or the heat wherewith the inward mind rejoices to burn; which, since as in a furnace it can scarcely contain itself within, seems to appear without in works as upon the cheeks. For how greatly the Virgin burns inwardly with spiritual love, she cannot fully express without by bodily work; but out of the abundance which her inward conscience comprehends, a kind of ruddy spark shows itself without upon her face. By this redness and whiteness I judge the Virgin's cheeks to be called beautiful, whose beauty her lover [0257A] delights to gaze upon; and the beauty which, by the grace of humility, he wills to be unknown by the Virgin, as was said before, he himself, not grudging, loves to impart to her in fitting praises.

Nor is it to be thought that he brings any hurt to that beauty in that he makes mention of the turtledove among her praises, because the turtledove, though it be believed to be of no merit and of no holiness, yet affords a type of modesty and a pattern of a modest chastity. For having obtained one mate it knows no other thereafter, and keeping faith to that one alone it blushes to know another: and if perchance that one be lost, not even so does it wish to have another; it grieves as a widower, nor will it sit upon a green branch. How great a love, how great a faith, how great a desire of chastity, how holy and imitable an appearance of chaste honesty does it set forth, in that as one present so one absent it loves [0257B] singularly whom it has chosen, into whose fellowship it once brought itself back with faith for a go-between? To which turtledove can she better, can she more worthily be compared than that Virgin of virgins in whom God willed to be made flesh, when already she was caught up with her whole desire to love him singularly, so that, abhorring every man, she was troubled even at an angel's entering? And when her son was born, no concupiscence of man harmed the mother none the more, but the bearer clave to him she bore, the Bride to the Bridegroom, with a love not to be loosed, in such wise that no carnal onset should thereafter bring loss to her chaste spirit, nor should she thereafter feel any blandishment of another love; cleaving to heaven alone, she knew not the frail pavement, and so loved the flesh in her son as the sacrament in the Bridegroom. To the Bridegroom indeed with firm faith [0257C] she loves to cleave, and to show herself to him as a chaste virgin to her one husband, in whose bodily presence for some time she rejoices, and in whose spiritual love and commerce she is proclaimed blessed. « Blessed art thou that hast believed, » says Elisabeth [n: (Luc. I)]! But when he was as it were lost — that is, when, dying and rising again, he disdained human fellowship, and returned thither whence he had come, that is, to the Father's right hand — she, abhorring the public gaze, with chaste heart and modest face, remains a widow, groaning after the manner of the turtledove. And because she had not the Bridegroom, whose presence had been green and delightful, keeping faith to him absent she now sat upon no wood but a dry one; nor did another Bridegroom preside over her with a like privilege, though another perhaps stood by her as a son by the grace of ministering. Wherefore, as I judge, [0257D] when Christ, set upon the cross, commended his mother the Virgin to John who stood by, and carefully made over the disciple to her service, he expressly did not say: Woman, behold thy bridegroom; and to the disciple: behold thy bride, but he said: « Woman, behold thy son; and to the disciple: behold thy mother [n: (Joan. XIX)]. » For in many sons a mother's fruitfulness, with wedded faith unharmed, is commended, but in many bridegrooms chastity is accused of intemperance: and therefore, that the Virgin might be kept for one husband even in his absence, no other Bridegroom, but another son, stood by her in the present. Finally the other John, greater than whom there hath not risen among them that are born of women, understood this mystery, as he plainly shows to all; who, though he might be believed to be the Christ, who was the Virgin's own Bridegroom, yet the more evidently declares that he is not the Christ, is not [0258A] the Bridegroom. « You yourselves, he says, bear me witness, that I said, I am not the Christ. He that hath the Bride is the Bridegroom. But the friend of the Bridegroom, who standeth and heareth him, rejoiceth with joy because of the Bridegroom's voice [n: (Joan. III)]. » You see that John, loving *146* to measure himself with a worthy eye of consideration, while others lied concerning him, himself prudently chose not to lie; and knowing his own bounds, does not assert himself to be the Bridegroom but the friend, lest by usurping a falsehood he should rather make himself an enemy. And so to the Virgin there is no Bridegroom but Christ, who in her womb celebrated wondrous and incomparable nuptials, and made her fruitful not by male coition but by the divine Spirit, by whose presence the same Virgin ever grows green, and at his greenness the loving virgin grows glad. [0258B] For so long as the bridegroom is with her, she cannot but rejoice, in whom she sees that her own flesh has no stain of flesh; who in the bridal chamber of her womb was made one flesh with her, in a union having indeed no vice, but opportune for the salvation of all. And because so spiritual a commerce felt nothing contrary in the joining, assuredly it brought no vice into what was conceived; and Christ was born not, like the rest, a son of wrath also by nature, but he lived with clean flesh, to die by an impulse not necessary but voluntary. Finally, about to die: « If in the green wood, he says, they do these things, what shall be done in the dry? » [n: (Luc. XXIII.)] The Bridegroom calls himself the green wood, whose root in a hidden conceiving is not gnawed by the little worm, whose stem, that is, his life, is not held by rottenness, however slight; the spreading of his words [0258C] as of branches withers not at a hurtful blast, but, to conclude briefly, the whole substance grows green with an inborn vigour. To whom that better applies which Paul says: « And if the root be holy, so are the branches [n: (Rom. XIII)]. » Rightly then is any other man called dry wood, whose conceiving is not sufficiently purified, and whose conversation is assailed by vices; who of himself or by himself germinates not, is not made fruitful with a just germ, unless, by the Bridegroom's bounty, the dew of divine grace be poured upon him from above. Therefore the Virgin, so long as the Bridegroom is with her, rejoices in green wood, but when he is absent she reckons every wood dry; nor does she find another wherein the turtledove's foot may rest even a little, which withers not at the touch, whether original or actual, of vices. [0258D] Showing therefore that she grieves for the Bridegroom's absence, while she neither has him whom she loves nor wishes to have another in a like place, she sits in the dry, and groaning she shows herself a widow, and both by her posture and by her groaning represents the form of the turtledove. Nor is it to be thought that from this her beauty is deprived of its grace, in that a widow's sadness compels her to sit and to groan, because as worthily in the Bridegroom's presence the Bride's face grows glad, so it is not foreign to grace if in his absence it wastes away. And so he rightly commended the Virgin's cheeks as beautiful like the turtledove's, to whom, both present and absent, the Virgin kept the faith she owed, who, knowing him alone to be a man endowed with a deity united to him, gave herself wholly and devoutly to him with modest chastity.
