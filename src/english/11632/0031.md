## CHAPTER VII.

[0259A]

*Thy neck, as necklaces.* The Bridegroom, praising the Virgin, uses comparisons unheard of, and, as the first face of them pretends, not sufficiently choice for expressing the likeness, since neither cheeks seem to suit a turtledove nor a neck a necklace, unless a careful reader take pains to find the hidden fitness. A band is knotted to the neck, that he who has been bound may be held, and, being held, may be led away hence thither, thence elsewhere, otherwise, at the pleasure of him that holds him; and if the leader-away be malevolent, he that is led away is wretchedly taken captive, and, his liberty lost, is made bondsman to the rule of the stronger. Whereby the pitying Lord once perceived a certain Virgin pressed and oppressed, who, deceived and enticed, at the first despised not fraudulent blandishments; [0259B] but afterwards, offering her neck to the bands, she took no care that she was led to ignominy, and, forgetting her inborn liberty, turned herself aside to a hurtful servitude. Seeing her and pitying her, the Lord invites her back to that which was lost, warns her not to suffer herself to be held any longer by another band, and, that she who with the loose bar of shamefastness let the corrupter in may be loosed from his band, bids her hear a chaste and saving monitor. « Loose, he says, the bands of thy neck, O captive daughter of Sion [n: (Isa. LII)]. » Thou, he says, who once wast worthily called daughter of Sion, when, seeing God with the mind, thou wast seen of him with a kindly return; from thy neck, whereon no precious stone, no gold now glitters, no necklace, but a corrupt mind, the heat of the flesh, a youthful desire has defiled it; loose, I pray, the bands, that what is foul, prostituted to an unpermitted baseness, [0259C] may be wiped clean with a strong hand and be restored, set free, to honesty. With that filth the neck of this our Virgin, whom we have in hand, was not fouled, nor grew hard with a stubborn neck, with an untamed heart; but the Bridegroom rubbed it clean with a soothing voice and a soft hand, and upon it, rubbed clean and made smooth, laid a light burden and a sweet yoke. Whereof also you have received a commandment: « Take up my yoke upon you, and learn of me, because I am meek and humble of heart [n: (Matth. XI)]. » The Virgin's neck therefore is her meek humility, a humble meekness accustomed to the laws of obedience; whose form or example the Bridegroom himself willed to afford, when, obedient to the Father, God willed to appear as man in a virgin's womb. Whose obedience the Virgin understood to be worthy enough and to be imitated, and, a lover of this pattern, [0259D] lifted not up her neck swellingly, but hearing with the hearing she bore the yoke — not that which Adam laid heavy upon his sons, but that which, being light and sweet, more graciously eased the neck of her that bore it. *147* For as, according to the heathen, that appearance is no honour but a burden which will hurt the wearer, so on the other side that appearance is no burden but an honour which will adorn; and assuredly Christ's yoke burdens not him that loves it, but honours him, dishonours not the neck of him that obeys, but adorns it. How great a comeliness of mind seems to you to shine out in the Virgin's words, how great necklaces — not dewlaps — to hang from her neck; who, at the angel's word whereby she is forewarned that she shall bear God, confesses herself not a noblewoman of royal stock but a lowly handmaid? Nor indeed was it new and sudden for her to obey [0260A] the divine warnings, who for very many days before had been accustomed to serve God; so that with faithful purpose she vowed herself to remain a Virgin also, for which nevertheless she had neither a commandment in the Scriptures nor a pattern among her forerunners. By the teaching of his Spirit alone, who graciously presided within, she was disposing to keep her chastity unviolated; and she deserved not to be cheated of her hope and desire, nay — what is greater and more wonderful — to be made fruitful, such a purpose abiding. But by a new kind she both remained Virgin and was fruitful, whereas before and after neither has she who was Virgin been fruitful, nor has she who was fruitful remained Virgin: of so great a prerogative she willed to speak or to think nothing lofty, nothing unseemly, nor to dissent from him whom she knew as a Master meek and humble of heart. And so no man ever so bore the light burden and the sweet yoke, [0260B] no other ever so conformed himself to the obedient Bridegroom, as she who furnished him with the garment of her own flesh, whose obedient neck seems, to them that consider worthily, an ornament.

Finally it is she who is found so to be beneath Christ the head, that there is no doubt she is above the rest of the body; and she seems to hold the middle place between the head and the body, nor can the Bridegroom's sons reach to the Bridegroom save with the Mother between. When she, the Bride, is present to us, it is good to cleave to him, but when she is absent the members cannot cohere with the head; to us set far below, the Son of God has come down through her, to whom our infirmity ascends not save through her. She first, she more blessed, receives a great [sic: *munos*], whereby many and diverse are made as it were one; whereby the two [0260C] walls have been united by one faith, by one baptism, and, running together to Christ, have leaned upon him personally. Finally she commends us to the Bridegroom and Son by her intercession between, invites us to pray, admonishes him to attend; the Bridegroom's mother and handmaid, the empress of us all, she joins the disjoined, holds fast the joined, a mighty and effectual mediatrix. For seeing us dry, and that we have no wine at the wedding, and knowing that without wine the wedding pleases not worthily enough; pitying them that were to perish of thirst, she hastens anxiously to come before them, and confidently to approach the Son for the failing of the wine. The Mother of Jesus, he says, saith to him: « They have no wine [n: (Joan. II)]. » And perceiving that the humble intercession of a loving prayer is not made void, but comes to profit for us, nay for all that sat at meat, she says to the servants: [0260D] « Whatsoever he shall say to you, do *(ibid)*. » And when they, according to the Virgin's word, inclined their ear to the command, the waterpots are filled with water, and clarified wine is rendered: by a thing worthy of a miracle our tastelessness is turned into savour, torpid cold into fire, a more negligent contempt into love. A good neck, a good intercession, a good mediatrix at last, who joins those whom Eve, the hurtful separatrix, had disjoined; who invites the Son to bounty and us to obedience, and brings it about that we omit not to render service, nor he to confer benefit. Nor is it beside the point that the Bridegroom praises that same neck as necklaces, which bear the ensigns of matronly wealth and of honourable chastity; which, made of gold and stones with learned art, [0261A] with a preeminent appearance adorn the neck, cover the breast, and close the double hem of the garment. You know that by gold understanding and wisdom are for the most part signified, and by stones virtues and works are figured; and the craftsman sets the stones in the gold and orders them diligently, when he works strong things and disposes his works wisely. And rightly did the Bridegroom mark out the Virgin's neck by the comparison of necklaces, because the humble meekness wherewith the Virgin obeyed to bear the yoke the more sweetly the longer she bore it, or the prerogative of merit whereby, being nearer to the head, she went before the remaining members of the body, whereby she interceded with the Bridegroom as an effectual mediatrix for his sons; glitters with the gold of wisdom, is made solid with the stones of works, and is adorned by the craftsman's hand with a worthy coupling of virtues. For who more strongly, who more wisely bore the yoke that is sweet to him that loves it, who loved [0261B] more fervently, who obeyed more graciously him that sweetly disposes all things, than the Virgin in whom the Word coeternal with the Father, the Wisdom of God, willed so to be clothed with flesh that, remaining what he was, he vouchsafed to be found in habit as a man? [n: (Philip. II.)] But the Virgin's prerogative, strengthened by the gift of this mystery, closed like a golden necklace the hem of her garment from above; since it confers upon the Virgin not only comeliness but also defence, that within the bosom of the flesh she may not feel the fraudulent robber. For as from the mind, so also from the flesh the Virgin drove back everything hurtful; nay, the shameless robber knocked not at the bosom of her rest, seeing and envying that it was fortified from above with wondrous grace, so that no entrance could anywhere be found for any malevolent one. And for this cause perhaps the Bridegroom named not a necklace but necklaces, [0261C] wherein he signified a manifold, or at least a twofold, prerogative of grace: namely that whereby the Virgin's Spirit faints with tender love toward her salvation, and that whereby the flesh, now tamed, lusts nothing against the spirit. In *148* Paul, as he says [n: (Rom. VII)], two laws struggle against one another, whose spirit as well as flesh work not more fully the things that they will; serving the law of God with the mind, he is believed already to have one necklace, but with the flesh serving the law of sin he prevails not yet to obtain the other. By how much therefore Paul is the more wretched, who is still snatched into diverse things, whose Spirit wills the things that are above, and whose insolent flesh wills the contrary; by so much is the Virgin the more blessed, to whom a twin necklace is already given, whose Spirit cleaves to God, whose flesh wrestles not [0261D] against the Spirit. Blessed, I say, is she who, fortified with necklaces, admits no unchaste robber, who keeps the enemy far from the doors of her virginal modesty, who suffers not even the shameless dogs, the vices of the flesh, so much as to bark at her gate, from which the loving Bridegroom willed to make her chamber serene. Nevertheless he did not so utterly banish the other assaults from that same chamber, nor deny some kind of access to them that rushed in harshly; wherein, although the cruel malignity of him that brings them deserves to be reproved, yet the humility of her that suffers them contracts not guilt to itself in like measure.
