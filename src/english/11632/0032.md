## CHAPTER VIII.

*We will make thee muraenulae of gold, vermiculated with silver.* The *muraenula* is a fish which, nourished in the waters, bears many times to be beaten and driven on by their billowy flood, [0262A] yet rejoices not to be driven to death by the impulse of the water; shaken by the tempest it fears not to be swallowed into the deep, and, putting its tail (as is said) in its mouth, it folds itself into a round; taken by the fisherman's art it enriches the tables of them that feast, and by a pleasing savour invites us to the desire of it. Such a fish the Bridegroom perhaps seems to promise to the Virgin, were it not more manifestly shown in the promising; for he utterly forbids us the understanding according to the letter, when he says that this same fish is of gold or of silver. Finally he does not say: We will give thee — which could be understood of eating — but: *We will make for thee,* which is understood of having; because the fisherman indeed gives *muraenulae* but does not make them, whereas the Bridegroom not only gives his own gifts but also makes them. For his are the [0262B] gifts, whose author makes them precious.

And so in the estate of this life, not stable but billowy, in this sea exceeding horrible, and, when faith is lost, full of shipwreck, the Virgin feels herself beaten with many annoyances and tribulations, but not weighed down unto failing and impatience. For the Bridegroom works in her a certain fortitude as it were of fishes, whereby, standing in the midst of the waters, she is not loosened by the manifold inundation; and, having called upon him for whose love she burns, by whose inextinguishable heat she is held, she dreads not the flood, is not swallowed up by the deeper waters. « Save me, O God, he says, for the waters are come in even unto my soul [n: (Psal. LXVIII)]. » And worthily thereafter: « Let not the deep swallow me up [n: (ibid.)]. » The restless depth of the waves, the flowing manifoldness of tribulations, cannot wonder enough [0262C] that they hasten to be gathered against the Virgin as with a battle-line drawn up; and yet by no assault, by no onset do they prevail to break her that resists, that persists unharmed among the waves after the manner of fishes. They drive, they drive not out; they wrench, they wrench not away; they shake, they shake her not out of the state of solid firmness; they are astonished, beholding the Virgin of so great a weight; whom, as a new grace, an incomparable beauty, an unheard-of prosperity does not lift up, so a hard tribulation, the persecution of the malignant, the flowing heap of waters does not soften. For abiding in the flesh, she was in spirit not carnal but golden, that is, rich with the gold and treasure of spiritual wisdom; retaining in herself nothing soft, nothing fluid, nothing of unskilled dullness, but solid and shining, of grave understanding, [0262D] of an exquisite treasure of fortitude. For in the furnace of tribulations, at the smell of the fire, she was not caught beforehand after the manner of chaff, nor was she found like hay, nor like stubble, but like the finest gold [n: (I Cor. III)]; and her, being prudent, whom the inundation of waters sank not into the depth of the sea, the furnace of fire befouled not with soot but wiped clean. For the greatness of tribulations found in her no scruple, no stumbling-block whereto any hurtful or unseemly baseness might cleave, but when floods and fires passed in emulation through her or over her, the floods assuredly found her a *muraenula*, and the fire found her golden.

But in that the Bridegroom declared that he would make not a *muraenula* but *muraenulae*, he signified, as I judge, not one virtue but very many, which the good [0263A] craftsman forged with the learned hand of his power, and, compacted with wondrous workmanship, prepared for the Virgin's adornment and defence. Further, the *muraenula*, bringing its tail back to its mouth, is then seen to make of itself a circle or a ring, when the end of a virtue begun with good intent is bent back to its beginning; when the Virgin's patience, for the sake of him whom she loves, chooses to bear whatsoever grievous things, and no adversity prevails to remove [sic: *cam*] from her purpose. But another *muraenula* may be called her obedience, whereby, from the time that she was saluted by the angel, she is found so given over to the heavenly commands that, according to Gabriel's word, the Spirit working, being faithfully joined to one Bridegroom and rejecting every other, she was intending to please him alone who sees in secret. And who can speak or think of that *muraenula* anything that shall seem to you sufficiently to befit her praises, [0263B] when, namely, with no man for a go-between, the angel's word is made to her — he calling her blessed among women, *149* she declaring herself a handmaid; he pledging that she, unknown of man, shall now be the mother of the Lord who is present [n: (Luc. I)], she, yielding faith, yet answering herself humble; bearing a new man who with God in the beginning was God [n: (Joan. I)], she, uttering nothing lofty, nothing swollen, remains abject and humble? There is also a greater and a more excellent one: that *muraenula* which is named love, or charity, which, sitting upon a throne, holds princedom over the rest with a kind of royal sceptre; which, preeminent above the dignity of faith and hope, brought over them with a noble sovereignty, proceeds the more effectually, rejoices the more securely, being led into the Bridegroom's very chamber. Out of *muraenulae* of this kind, as out of certain meshes, a cord [0263C] or a chain is woven together, more solid indeed than gold and a stranger to the filth of the dust; forged in the furnace of tribulation by the learned hand of the goldsmith, reduced neither by the waters into softness nor by the smoky fire into ashes. Does it not seem to you that Paul compacted diverse *muraenulae*, and wove them, being compacted, into one cord or little chain, to which the pressure of tribulation stood as furnace and anvil, out of which by a wondrous workmanship came forth the golden graciousness of the *muraenulae*? « Knowing, he says, that tribulation worketh patience, and patience trial, and trial hope, and hope confoundeth not, because the charity of God is poured forth in our hearts [n: (Rom. V)]. » You see therefore that out of the furnace and the anvil of tribulation the bronze of patience is brought forth, which constitutes the root [0263D] and immovable foundation of the cord or chain; whereupon leans the loftiness of the virtues that follow, which the beauty preeminent in the gold of charity consummates. And this is charity unfeigned, and a worthily commendable connexion of virtues, which the pleasing mingling of silver vermiculates and adorns; when the cord of *muraenulae* is so laboriously knit together that a vermiculation of silver is found inwoven in it. Vermilion, which is a red colour, or the little worm which is of one colour with vermilion, signifies Christ's passion, whereof mention must needs be made in all things that we do, so that when we think that we serve him, obey him, love him, we may diligently call to mind those things which he unworthily suffered for us. His garment indeed grew red for us, as Isaias testifies [n: (Isa. LXIII)], and his raiment was fouled as of them that tread in the winepress; [0264A] in whose type you know that a commandment was of old promulgated, that a red heifer should be sacrificed for sins [n: (Num. XIX)]. The red heifer is he who says in the Psalm: « But I am a worm and no man; the reproach of men, and the outcast of the people [n: (Psal. XXI)]. » Of this worm and this outcast, of this reproach, he ought the more diligently to be mindful who desires the more perfectly to obtain the *muraenulae* promised to the Virgin, because the golden connexion of virtues gives not sufficient adornment if the red little worm and the silver be not inwoven. Finally the little worm is bent back into a ring and a manifold circle, that in it the worthy perfection of the aforesaid remembrance may be figured; and being cut off and divided into two separate parts it still lives, signifying him who, being struck upon the wood of the cross, slept a little while, but straightway waking [0264B] « now dieth no more, death shall no more have dominion over him [n: (Rom. VI)]. » In whose faith and love and rightly to be loved remembrance the Virgin lived, when the persecutor harshly fixed the sword not so much in his body as in her soul; and when the Son's death came even unto the Mother's tender heart, yet the Mother's faith and love found not death. Was not he dead who escaped death by a timid denial [n: (Marc. XIV)], and he who, shaken with fear, leaving the linen cloth, fled naked; and they who, all forsaking him, entered the more hastily upon flight, and held the signification neither of the little worm nor of the silver. For silver, being bright and sonorous, is said to hold the form of speech, whose chastity in the furnace, whose testing in the fire, fears not to render a voice; which, having nothing of lead, nothing of tin, grows not dark [0264C] in the burning, but, passing through unharmed, grows glad more brightly and more sonorously. You have it that the word of the Lord is a chaste word, silver tried by the fire, proved of the earth, purified seven times [n: (Psal. XI)]. [var: Vulg. Ps 11:7 *Eloquia Domini, eloquia casta*, plural, "The words of the Lord are pure words"; the singular *eloquium* is the word Philip goes on to expound of Peter and of John] And by Isaias; « If thou pass through the fire, he says, thou shalt not be burnt; and the flame shall not hurt thee [n: (Isa. XLIII)]. » [var: Vulg. Isa 43:2 *cum ambulaveris in igne, non combureris, et flamma non ardebit in te*, "when thou shalt walk in the fire, thou shalt not be burnt, and the flame shall not burn in thee"] Peter's speech in the fire is found not sonorous, but sordid after the manner of lead [n: (Marc. XIV)]; and John's speech is found not bright enough, since the one answered not a good word, a truthful word, to them that upbraided him; the other, taking to flight, hid himself under silence; having read, but neglected, that which is written: « Take not the word of truth utterly out of my mouth [n: (Psal. CXVIII)]. » And again: « And I shall answer a word to them that upbraid [0264D] me [n: (ibid.)]. » But the Virgin, embracing Christ's passion diligently with the arms of faith, presses not down her love of him negligently under silence; but what she, believing, loves, she evidently preaches when the time requires, as truly loving, so confidently bringing forth the word of faith. Worthily therefore is she given the special adornment of *muraenulae*, which are vermiculated and adorned with approved silver, since to her perfected virtues the Bridegroom interposes a diligent remembrance of his own passion; which the Virgin puts not off, both to carry through in her manners and to bring forth in her words. Such an adornment the Bridegroom asserts that he has not made for the Virgin but will make, showing that in this life that gift is not present to her but future, because while the Virgin labours and sweats and strives strongly in the contest, not [0265A] the completing of the wayfaring, not joy, the receiving of it for her, but the promise gives of the crown. For here, as upon an anvil, the mesh is beaten, a promissory perfection is drawn out, and, grace prospering, it is bent back that it may become completive, that the tail *150* may cleave to the head, that the end may be conformed to the beginning, and that the perfective conforming may be made firm with a stable rivet. In type of this Moses commands the head and the tail of the animal to be offered, hinting that a spiritual conversation should bend back the end to the beginning, that the mesh may be made whole, that the ring be made, that the *muraenula* be rounded again, that an action well begun be consummated with a like ending. Which, because while the Virgin lives here she obtains it not yet more fully but awaits it, to her that awaits the Bridegroom casts up no fear or distrust; but rather comforts her expectant mind with hope, cherishes it with a promise, makes it secure; asserting that he will do whatsoever [0265B] befits her perfection. Nor let it move any that he says not: *Muraenulae of gold* I will make *for thee,* but *we will make.* For although here the Son alone seems to speak or to promise, yet he alone works not the effect of the promised gift, but it is agreed that the Father works with the son, and the holy Spirit equally, whom it is certain are separated from one another neither in nature nor in work. « My Father, he says, worketh, and I work [n: (Joan. V)]. » And again: « Whatsoever things the Father doth, these the Son also doth in like manner [n: (ibid.)], » and there is no doubt but that the Holy Spirit doth them also. Of whom the Son says in the Gospel: « We will come, he says, and will make our abode with him [n: (Joan. XIV)]. » The same, speaking to his virgin Bride: *Muraenulae,* he says, *of gold we will make thee, vermiculated with silver.* And she:
