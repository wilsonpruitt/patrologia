## CHAPTER IX.

[0265C]

*While the king was in his repose, my nard gave forth its odour.* The praises being uttered wherewith the Bridegroom commended the Virgin's cheeks and neck, the promise of the *muraenulae* being made whereby he comforted her girlish mind, he now seems as it were to give place and to withdraw from the Virgin's sight; but not going far from her mouth and her love, he is held fast by her grateful remembrance. For the Virgin, turning to those maidens who tarry with her, who with anxious hearing await their mutual discourses, rejoices to speak of the Bridegroom and to commend his excellence, and to proclaim what his excellent benevolence has conferred upon her. *While the King was,* she says, *in his repose.* My Bridegroom, she says, is not base-born, not [0265D] plebeian, not of a viler impotence, but the son of the highest king, and himself a king of no lesser excellence; from the Father's session, from the royal seat, from the hiding of an unapproachable greatness, where the angels see his face and unwearied desire it; he willed to come hither to the earth for the salvation of perishing souls, and rested in my bridal chamber with the delightful love of the nuptials: in my womb, I say, that King gladly reposed, and such a repose pleased him well enough that reposed therein; and whereas in the foxes' holes and in the birds' nests he had no pleasing rest for himself, he complained not that he had found anything in me that should displease his repose. But reposing there he filled me wondrously with his grace, while, keeping me a virgin, he abolished the Virgin's barrenness; and his effectual fire burned me up for a holocaust, and not the reek of flesh but a most sweet odour of ointments filled the whole [0266A] house. For when in this his repose that majesty more sweetly leaned back its head, and God the Son of God inclined himself into my womb to take flesh; my virtue received no mean increase, a larger advance of grace, and was made as nard, fragrant within with vigour, giving without a fitting odour.

Nard, according to the letter, is a lowly herb, of a hot nature, of a richer juice, thick with leaves, mighty in odour, fit for the compounding of spices. By which not unfitly is figured the virtue of the virgin Mother, which before the Bridegroom's coming seemed small and known to God alone; but when he was present it gave the sweetness of its odour far and wide, pouring out to them that were without the benefit of an ampler knowledge. While the Bridegroom reposed in [0266B] the Virgin's womb, the just Joseph wonders at an unknown mystery, but, the Angel revealing it, he smells the magnificence of the sacrament [n: (Matth. I)]; understanding that the Virgin was not corrupted, nor had conceived of another womb, but had received a wondrous conception by the gift of the Holy Spirit. This odour Elisabeth seems none the less to have perceived with a prophetic nostril, when the Virgin, already great with child, is read to have gone up into the hill country, at whose saluting voice the little John leaped in the womb [n: (Luc. I)]; and with great dancing the whole dwelling of the mountain resounded like a ringing echo. For Elisabeth felt, and the infant in her womb felt, the repose of the incarnate Word, and smelled the sweet fragrance of this precious nard; the priest with his voice, the infant with his motion, applaud the Virgin as she comes, they extol her as she salutes them, they render worthy service [0266C] to her that goes before them: « Whence is this to me, says the priest, that the mother of my Lord should come to me [n: (ibid.)]. » And he adds concerning the Son: « For behold, as soon as the voice of thy salutation was made in mine ears, the infant in my womb leaped for joy [n: (ibid.)]. » With whom rejoicing the Virgin great with child so loves to rejoice together, that yet, like nard, she is found to remain humble, and, being fuller in grace and richer in an ampler merit, the young woman ministers with dutiful diligence to the elder. For she willed to tarry three months with the priest, holding it fitting to converse for that time with the aged matron, to take care of her, to bring *151* help, to sit the more anxiously by her as she travailed, to have compassion on her pains, to still her groans, to show a pleasing solace. Nor did the Virgin afford to the woman in childbed only the solace [0266D] of an attendant, but, as some think, she performed as it were the office of a midwife; not that the Virgin, being unskilled, had been accustomed to such a ministry, but that, being meek, she might show a ready mind toward the woman in childbed; and that, first holding and beholding the child as he was born, she might render him better, whom she knew to be the prophet of the Most High, the forerunner of Christ who was to be born [n: (ibid.)]. Elisabeth sees the Virgin's humility, and considering it she marvels; with the Spirit of prophecy she smells the humble excellence of this nard: she sees that wondrous bush, to be proclaimed to all ages, burn unharmed [n: (Exod. III)], and the nard, hot by nature, nay fervent in spirit, not consumed; she feels that a richer juice is in the humble nard, that in the Virgin's womb a purer fount of mercy gushes up; she sees the humble nard [0267A] made thick with leaves, and the Virgin abound in words of modesty, in saving doctrine. Of all which, although the Virgin's nard shows not yet a fuller effect, yet little by little it gives an odour to some that have sound nostrils, so that even with a slight perception they may perceive how great a virtue is in the Virgin, how great a grace of virtue, and how great a hope of health there is for the sick in the aromatic nard. For whoever shall have felt in himself a disease or a languor, if with greedy nostril, if with due love he shall have perceived this odour; not only shall he not grieve to be weighed down, nor to fall away into failing, but he shall rather rejoice to be assuaged and to pass over into advancement.

Finally the very odour of the nard is found to receive an advance, when with the process of time the Virgin imparts a greater knowledge of herself; when, after the birth of the Son, who advanced in wisdom [0267B] and in age, she is known to be his mother with the incorrupt chastity of a Virgin [n: (Luc. II)]. For when the Word in flesh, God in man, was conversant among men, and by many was called man only, whether out of ignorance or out of malice; yet very many knew him with a truthful judgment to be God and man, and believed that as before the birth so after the birth his Mother was a Virgin. *While* therefore *the king was in his repose,* that is, when God, being great in man, showed himself as lowly, and, hidden from the unbelieving, lying secret from them that were without, appeared only to his own chamber-servants, *my nard gave forth its odour,* that is, my light under the bushel, my virtue in the dark, lay not hid, but became known to Peter as well as to the rest that had a true faith. Whence to him asking: « Whom do men say that the Son of man is *(Matth. [0267C] XVI)*; » and again: « But whom do you say that I am [n: (ibid.)]. » Peter answered: « Thou art Christ the Son of the living God [n: (ibid.)]. » He, asking concerning himself, called himself my Son of man. Peter, having drawn in the odour of the nard, affirmed him the Son of the living God; understanding that my Christ is so held to be a virgin's son, that rightly he is proclaimed the Son not also of a man, but of God. This odour the Gentiles perceive not with sound nostril, nor the Jews, who, not knowing me a virgin, deny him to be the Son of God; and derogating from the Mother, to whom a Father was altogether wanting for the joining, they weigh not the unspeakable begetting both of the begetter and of the begotten. Therefore with stopped nostrils, with blind sight, with deaf hearing, with impious hands they afflict me, who, the odour not being perceived, [0267D] crucify Christ as a mere man; whose unsound judgment the centurion rightly contradicts, when, having seen the earthquake, he says that Christ is the Son of God: « Indeed, he says, this was the Son of God [n: (Matth. XXVII)]. »

But much more amply did my nard smell sweet, my virtue shine out, when the King reposed upon the royal throne and upon the seat of rest; wherein, removed from earthly things, he found a more pleasing place, and, far from the envious, suffered neither injury nor labour. For dwelling in this world and set in a life subject to death, wherein he seemed as it were forsaken by the divine protection, he suffered so many and so great things, walking through the straits of a harder path, that this is rightly judged a dwelling not of rest but of labour. Wherein: « God, my God, he says, why hast thou forsaken me? [n: (Matth. XXVII.)] » And whereof: « I have laboured, he says, with bearing [0268A] [n: (Isa. I)]. » And Jeremias: « O Lord, he says, thou hast given them a shield of heart, thy labour [n: (Thren. III)]. » [var: Vulg. Lam 3:65 *Dabis eis scutum cordis, laborem tuum*, "Thou wilt give them a shield of heart" — future, and with no vocative] But when, dying, he had fulfilled the laborious journey, death being triumphed over, and rising again immortal had ascended to the heavens whence he had come: he sat at the right hand of majesty on high, being made now so much better than the angels, by how much he obtained above them a hereditary name [n: (Hebr. I)]. When upon this seat or royal throne that King possessed the peace that was owing to him, and, reposing in the Father's session, rejoiced with a coeternal glory; the virgin's nard was found to smell more copiously and more fruitfully, and virginal fruitfulness to shine out the more graciously the more richly. For when the Spirit was sent and came down in fiery tongues, when suddenly a vehement sound came from heaven upon the disciples [n: (Act. II)], the blindness of ignorance and the strait [0268B] of fear are driven far from them, and they utter as prudently as confidently the things they had known of Christ. For assuredly the Virgin had recounted to them in a long series from of old concerning the Bridegroom's kiss and the nuptial covenant, and, the law of the body not being observed, concerning the bodily conceiving and birth; and how he that assumed and he that was assumed had been united personally, the one not degenerating in what he assumed, the other not consumed by the glory of him that assumed: God and true man, Christ had advanced in wisdom and in age, and at last both laid down and took up his own soul with a free power. These things and much more, which it is not easy to recount in the present place (especially since they are so many that they can scarcely be contained in a book), the virgin through many years before took pains many times to explain to many, whom the same Bridegroom willed *152* to attach the more familiarly to himself. All which, [0268C] because when the Spirit was sent from the heavens they were more fully understood, were with a fuller understanding the more gratefully received; and the things which, while the Bridegroom was labouring and mortal, had pleased less being covered, now that he is immortal and reposing in rest, please more amply being uncovered. The virgin's nard therefore gives to the faithful its own odour, that is, the odour fitting to it, when her virtue and holiness show a fuller knowledge of themselves; when, the Bridegroom reposing at the right hand of the divine majesty, the knowledge of the aforesaid truth becomes more fully known and grows the more sweetly sweet. I wonder whether that woman was not seen to signify this very thing, who willed to present a pound of ointment of precious spikenard to Christ as he reclined [n: (Joan. XII)], which with due reverence she poured upon his feet, and with a larger bounty upon his head, [0268D] and that happy house of Simon was wholly filled with the odour. Whereof Jesus himself, bearing witness: « She hath wrought, he says, a good work upon me [n: (Matth. XXI)]. » Truly a good work, nor can a better easily be found, when Jesus, reclining among his guests, rejoices that his feet and his head are anointed with spikenard; that his humanity is not pressed down under the silence of ignorance, that the summit of his divinity is lifted up by the proclaiming of a fuller knowledge. Which our virgin took pains to commend not so much by her words as by her manners, to proclaim it commendable as much by living as by speaking, lest she should seem, like a wordy soldier, to dispute of war and victory, who, being delicate, willed not to bear the burdens of the camp, the encounters of such things.
