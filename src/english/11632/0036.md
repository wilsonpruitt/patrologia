## CHAPTER XIII.

*Behold thou art fair, my friend, behold thou art fair: thine eyes are of doves.* Thou, he says, who before all women art a friend to me, an enemy of all malice, of all foulness. Behold, that is, not only with all the just in the life that follows, but far more praiseworthily than all the rest in the present: *Behold,* I say, *thou art fair,* strong with a certain singular beauty, wherewith none of women, neither earlier nor later, can be compared. For it is fitting that he who is beautiful in form above the sons of men should have to himself such a one as by a singular grace displays a special beauty, whose face no unworthy and degenerate foulness may darken, but [0274D] love may conform her, the beloved, to him by whom she is loved. But when he had said: *Behold thou art fair, my friend;* what is it that he repeated: *Behold thou art fair?* What means this, fair and fair? Perhaps he repeated it to this end, that none should doubt of the aforesaid beauty, but that the assertion following might confirm the testimony set before; because it is both proclaimed that every word stands in the mouth of two witnesses [n: (II Cor. XIII)], and it is recorded that the authority of the law and of the prophets hangs upon two precepts [n: (Matth. XX)]. Some say that he therefore called the virgin fair twice, that he might mark her as having had a double beauty; who, conceiving and bearing, wondrously continues a Virgin, and in so great a prerogative truly avers herself humble, whereas for the most part [0275A] any woman is found either humble, but corrupted, or somewhat prouder, if perchance she has been uncorrupted. Worthily therefore fair, whom no hurt of integrity has disfigured, and again fair, whom an incomparable humility has adorned; so that the little flower of virginity renders her that is humble in spirit the brighter, and one humble in spirit renders her that is bright with the virginal flower the fairer. But I (not prejudging those who, it is agreed, have irreproachably thought this) judge that the Bridegroom, by repeating, expressed something proper to a lover, who, tenderly loving him by whom he rejoices to be loved, says pleasing things, and it does not suffice him to say once *156* what is loved. For the force of love has this familiar and proper to itself, that when it has enticed a man with the bond of a dearer affection, it stirs him up to speak gracious things of that which he loves, and, [0275B] unless they be repeated, the same things do not seem to him fruitful enough. Love admonishes the lover to speak the more willingly, and, that he may express his affection, to repeat the word the more often; nor does this reading seem to the lover to be required more than is right, even if, according to the poet, it has been ten times repeated. Whence David also, when he would markedly express the love which he had had toward his son, and could not press down with silence the grief of his death, went mourning and with covered head, forgetful of the royal dignity, and speaking of his son repeated the same speech the more often: « Absalom my son, my son Absalom [n: (II Reg. XVIII)]. » The same in the Psalm: « God, my God [n: (Psal. XCII)]. » And elsewhere: « May God, our God, bless us; may God bless us [n: (Psal. LXVI)]. » You see that to speak once what pleases [0275C] a lover is not thought sufficient, but the tenderer affection rejoices to repeat what it had said; because whatever can be said, he that is given over to love does not yet esteem it enough, in comparison with that which he grasps in his mind, inexpressible in words. For this cause the Bridegroom, setting himself to address the Virgin with soothing words — in that, as you have heard, he willed to call her fair a second time — strove by the repetition not so much to commend her beauty (which is great) as to intimate to his hearers the affection wherewith he is held toward her. But because very much beauty shines out in the eyes of girls, in her praise he asserts that she has the eyes of doves, which he wills to be understood, as I judge, not those of this head, not carnal, but the spiritual ones of that inner man. [0275D] Doves have certain properties implanted in them from nature, which generation transfers by an unspeakable law from the former to those that follow; wherein they are reported to differ far from the other birds, upon whom these gifts of nature are not all of them bestowed upon all. For they are said to have seven natures, or powers of nature, whereby they both seek what is useful and are kept back from what is hurtful; which to set forth by name and to compare with the virtues of holiness (being perhaps about to speak of them at another time) I now defer for brevity's sake. Finally the Holy Spirit was seen to appear in a dove [n: (Luc. III)], intimating to us that he has no small likeness with her, since he himself is found marked with seven principal gifts, and she is marked, as has been said before, with the pleasing sevenfold of nature. When therefore the Bridegroom declares the Virgin's eyes to be [0276A] of doves, he commends in her the seven gifts of graces; not the perishing light of the head, but the beholding powers of the mind, discerning of just and unjust rather than of days and nights. And rightly does he praise her whom he considers, thus conformed to his own Spirit, to have the sevenfold grace of virtues; from whom driving far off the drowsiness of the flesh and womanish torpors, he bestowed on her spiritual watchfulness and dove-like eyes. Indeed the Spirit himself rested more abundantly upon none of women than upon that same Virgin, in whom he rested sevenfold, since what bodily doves obtain figuratively from nature, he gave the whole of it to her in spirit, in truth rather than in figure. Whence rejoicing, and not ungrateful for the Bridegroom's praises received, she calls him also fair, with a praise nonetheless [0276B] doubled; and she rejoices to render the like to her praiser, and thus by an answering praise their mutual love is strengthened.

## CHAPTER XIV.

*Behold thou art fair, my beloved, and comely.* Because thy holiness has bestowed beauty upon me, my heart being cleansed, from this I have known that in thee also there is much beauty; for if deformity held me while my eye was not yet wiped clean, thy bright form would in no wise appear to me. But now, marked after the manner of doves with spiritual eyes, I attend more keenly how polished thy face is: and I see that there is in thee a form fairer than the sons of men, whom very many see only as not having beauty or comeliness. For the raven-like sight, which is pressed hard with a foul blackness, [0276C] beholds thee only as deformed, only as a man; and, held by error, it assigns thee to a judgment unworthy enough, while it says that thou art John, or Elias, or one of them of former times [n: (Matth. XVI)]. But to me, whose faith is not shattered, whose sight is not raven-like, but to whom the sevenfold grace and the dove-like form are at hand: far off is thy mortality of infirmity, far off is foulness, and the incomparable beauty of both natures shines sweetly forth. Thou assuredly art fair with a wonderful and singular Godhead, endowed with a substantial unity together with the Father and the Spirit: having with them the same natural excellence, a coeternal greatness, a coequal kingdom and power. Wherein none of the gods is found like to thee, nor is any other God marked with so great a prerogative [0276D] of beauty; because, although the personal distinction gives to understand that the Father is one and the Holy Spirit another, yet the natural substance renders him one God, and not another. Whatever therefore of beauty the Father and the Spirit are known to have, my beloved, I perceive with sure faith to shine back in thee: and this it is that entices me to love thee and to choose thee as my beloved, to follow thee, and not to desist, until I attain and come through to what is perfect; and because the loftiness of thy divine beauty is not hidden from me in the *157* present, the wonderful beauty of thy humanity also pleases me not slightly: whereby thou art so held a man among men, mortal among them that are dead in sin, that although thou sufferest the penalty, yet thou art not burdened with the guilt of mortals. Which comeliness none of the sons of Adam has deserved to obtain from [0277A] nature, because no man's conception, no man's begetting lacked a fault; but a singular cleanness, a special excellence, adorned thee alone, whom alone, conceived of the divine Spirit, a virginal womb brought forth. But this thou dost not manifest sooner, nor more fully, to any of men than to me, my beloved; whom thou dost so honour with a singular merit that I bring thee forth, God and man, in sure truth: mother of the Bridegroom, Bride of the son, with a wondrous and incomparable chastity. To me therefore thou art fair, to me especially thou shinest forth comely, who art made known both as God in man, and as a man Holy above men; whom, as Son, the equality of nature conforms to God the Father, and whom, as man, the law of sin and the general conception of men does not darken. Fair, I say, thou art, because thou art the brightness of eternal light, the mirror without spot of the divine majesty, the splendour of the Father's [0277B] glory, the figure of his substance, the image of truth [n: (Hebr. I)]; light of light, the rising sun, bringing to the world the morning day, day of day, lying down at noon, knowing no evening setting. When, caught up in ecstasy and lifted in the Spirit above visible things, I consider thee so fair with the cleared edge of my mind, then, as if forgetful of myself, I cannot not love thee, whom I behold to be such; I burn to commend thee, my beloved, and thy beauty.
