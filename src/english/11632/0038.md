## CHAPTER XVII.

*The beams of our houses are of cedar; our panelled ceilings of cypress.* By houses he means the churches of the faithful, the congregations of the just, the assemblies of the cloister, in which all the simpler sort can be reckoned stones, but the greater and more prudent are more fittingly called beams and panelled ceilings. The beams, set in order over the building after the manner of a vault, and distributed from either side of it one against another, below indeed [0280A] embrace the breadth of the building with arms as it were outstretched; but above, with heads as it were joined, they are knit together with a solid nail. But the panelled ceilings are laid beneath the height of the beams, and are stretched out from one wall to the other in a spread length; and they confine and bind those same walls with a certain binding as it were, lest by a hurtful shock they should decline from their straightness or *159* stature. By the name of beams the order of prelates is not unfittingly figured, whom, hung up from earthly things, the loftiness of their merits commends; who, bearing the troubles of showers and of winds, that is, the hard assaults of the lost, cover and protect from these the tender manners of their subjects. Who, raised toward the things above, all narrow themselves to the beholding of that one thing necessary, and, bound the more strongly by the nail of love, [0280B] while they see, cleave the more augustly, and taste the more sweetly; judging it better to be with Christ [n: (Philip. I)], while they contemplate his excellence, but yet deeming it needful to live in the flesh for the brethren's sake. Who therefore lift themselves up with a subtle desire to relish the things that are above, and condescend with kindly care to visit their neighbours; and, gathering them under their wings, they cherish and protect them as a hen, and, having quickened them to the virtues, fortify them by examples and by doctrine. And these indeed, that they may be commended by a worthier merit of virtues, are declared beams of Cedar, whose part they bear; because Cedars, as they say, incorruptible and tall by nature, are strong with so great an odour that serpents are said to shrink from it. Those therefore are worthily found to be advanced to the height of prelacy [0280C] who, lofty in merits, love to behold heaven with desire; whose works are ever strong with a steadfast virtue, that they may not rot; whose repute is so great that all the malevolent shudder at it. Nor is it far from prelates that the Cedar is said not to be burned up by fire, bearing the figure of those who are not to perish in the burning of the passions; whose wonderful fortitude knows not to be broken by the concupiscence of the flesh, nor does their most sweet odour turn to ashes at the odour of fires. But the panelled ceilings, which are also said to be of cypress, signify those whose discipline is strong and preeminent among the rest; by whose strong work and obedience of a perfect will a pattern of persevering is given to the lesser ones gathered in the Church. The Cypress too is itself declared incorruptible and odour-bearing, [0280D] and moreover it is without knots, and no scruple is found in it; rightly signifying him who, readier and smoother by a divine gift rather than by nature, obeying without delay, never kicks back at the joining. For in some men the virtue of obedience is so knotty, so teeming with murmuring, so scrupulous with so many complainings, that a command as it comes does not walk before it upon a smoother way, but for the most part stumbles into contempt or into a graver wrath. But those to whom it is given to be strong with the smoothness of the cypress, being without knots and polished, rejoice in the discipline of obedience; they love whatever the discretion or doctrine of prelates shall command, nor does a sudden fall of impatience set itself against the command. By these as beams and panelled ceilings spiritual houses go forward, are covered, are fortified, and avoid ruinous [0281A] and carnal falls; their holy and wonderful beauty is strong, because, according to that which was said, holiness becometh the house of the Lord [n: (Psal. XCII)]. But the Virgin willed to declare these houses ours to this end, that she might show that she has fellowship with the Bridegroom before the rest; because what is the Bridegroom's, the Bride reckons ours by nuptial right, and as Christ, so also the Virgin is careful for the spiritual profit of us all. Marking therefore that not only she herself but her own also have profited spiritually, and that prelates and subjects have come to the likeness of the cedar and the cypress, she exults and invites the Bridegroom to rejoice in these also, and, leaving the restless, to be delighted with the pleasing rest of the houses. ## CHAPTER XVIII.

[0281B][CAP. II.] *I am the flower of the field, and the lily of the valleys.* If the Bride's little bed, he says, is green with flower, glows with a virginal comeliness; if it shines and is fragrant with spiritual adornment and beauty; if, rich with a royal culture, it seems to smile upon them that worthily consider it — what has it that it may truly assert it has not from another? For neither has it of itself that it should be glowing with a flowering comeliness, nor does he flower whom the Bride declares flowering, save by a flower; and for this cause let him that flowers not glory in himself, but in the flower, since assuredly he has not of himself that he should flower, but of the flower. But if whosoever flowers desires to know more attentively whence he flowers, lest a foul ignorance should discolour the comely and flowering one: I am God, the Son in the Father, the light dwelling in light inaccessible. *I,* I say, have been made *the flower of the field,* having an unwearied beauty. The root of Jesse has budded, and [0281C] a flower has come forth from the root, nor did it will to lie hid in a chamber under a bushel, or in a bed under a coverlet; but it was made the flower of the field, that it might be made known more plainly to all, and that whosoever knows not one so conspicuous might not be worthy to be excused. For I who from eternity was God hidden, that I might not be seen, coming down from this mountain, the field received me that I might be held; nor did I enter the gates and walls of cities, nor again did I climb a steep mountain, lest either I might be unknown, or might not be the more easily laid hold of by him that knows. For if the strong man went up to the mountain, the weak would remain excused; but in a city the citizen would keep off the stranger; *160* and thus, injury prevailing, the one would plead that the other was the readier, and Habacuc [n: (cap. I)] would accuse me of silence, the wicked devouring one juster than himself. I [0281D] therefore willed to be a flower in the field and not in a farm, which assuredly the countryman claims for himself by hereditary right; which he breaks up with the plough, furrows with the share, digs zealously with the hoe, because it answers fruitfully to nothing but the work of hands. But the field is properly assigned to no man, it knows not the tiller's hand, it is sown without its surface being broken; but quickened by the sun, drenched with heavenly rain or dew, it grows warm and buds, and with the process of a fitting time it laughs with flower. Nor is it unfitting if the Virgin of Virgins be asserted to be signified by this field, in whom no man, no carnal commerce, works anything; but, her flesh being whole, a spiritual rain coming down as upon a fleece, she is made fruitful and buds, and rejoices in a flowering or snowy comeliness. For this field, which no greedy workman has hurt, no rustic hand has ploughed; [0282A] no hurtful swelling has raised it up, but a diffused humility has levelled it; I am the flower, coming forth assuredly not by human zeal, not by tillage, but brought forth by a heavenly gift, endowed with an incomparable birth. I am the flower wherewith the pleasing levelness of this field is adorned, nor does it flower of itself, but by my gift is thus blessed; and therefore, although the Virgin worthily rejoices at so great a benefit, yet let her glory in me and not in herself, in the flower and not in her that flowers. For although the flower does not send forth fruit as soon as it is born, yet, being about to give it at the opportune season, it already promises it; and as no other flower is found mightier than mine, or than me, so neither does any man make trial of a better fruit in another. But lest I should seem to have mercifully provided for the Virgin alone, and to have grudged my mercy to any lesser ones, I who was made the flower of the field, [0282B] endowing the Bride with a special privilege, have flowered in the valleys also — with much indeed, but not with an equal benefit.
