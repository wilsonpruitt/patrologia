## CHAPTER XXI.

*As the apple tree among the trees of the woods, so is my beloved among the sons.* The Bride, hearing herself praised with the Bridegroom's worthy praises — from whom she has it that she is worthy to be honoured [0285D] with the heraldings of praise — lest she should seem ungrateful, hastens to render the like to her praiser, not being ignorant that reciprocal praises are pleasing to a lover. Whom she rightly enough willed to compare to an apple tree, which it is agreed shines out among the trees of the wood not so much more loftily as more excellently; for since the trees of the woods bring forth no fruit that can be suited to human eating, in the apple tree there is good fruit to be found, the best refreshment. For Christ is a tree whose strength sustains no harm, whose leaf yields not to a wind raging the more harshly [n: (Psal. I)]; whose all things prosper, and in its time it shall give fruit, wherewith it now invites those that hunger, and hereafter shall satisfy them. Christ is the tree of life to all who strongly lay hold on him, who hang themselves up with a strong desire to take his fruit; who strive not to lay hold with a wandering sense on whatever other things [0286A] shall meet them, since they mark not that from these hang the fruit of life, the health-bearing apples. I believe all men can be named trees of the wood, who, though they have the leaves of words, lack without doubt the saving fruit; because in them there is not salvation, there is not life, there is not an unfailing force of nature, but a certain barrenness of virtues, a guilt of the flesh by the fault of their begetting. For there is not so anything in them, as also from them, that is vital, commendable, gracious; there is not anything in one man that is saving to himself, fruitful to the rest; because whatever is in any man — if yet anything worthy and necessary be found in any man — whosoever truly makes trial knows it to be not of his own, but of Christ's. But Christ, a man among men, is not unfittingly compared to an apple tree, who, conceived [0286B] of the Spirit, is not accused of a barren nature; whose root is harmless, whose thicker shade hides us from the hurtful heat, whose fruit is more wholesome, whose most sweet odour spreads itself far abroad. Finally these three things can alike be found in the apple tree, nor is there another in the woods that rejoices to mark itself in like manner with these three; but in the apple tree especially shade, fruit, and odour are coupled by a natural binding, and from him who is God and man they proceed with a spiritual abundance. With which he refreshes, feeds, and soothes those who received him, who, piety being received, deserved to be called and to be sons; who, because they betook themselves from darkness to the light of faith, by a happy change passed over from trees of the wood into sons of God. Whom Christ marking, sons of God and heirs, is not confounded to call them brethren, to love them as coheirs [0286C] [n: (Matth. XXV)], that among many brethren he himself, the firstborn, may stand out with a princely honour, and that he who was once the only son may rejoice in a number of sons now plural. For he himself, God of God, was born the Father's only-begotten; but born man of a virgin, likewise his Mother's only-begotten; but, taking counsel for the elect, he opened the bosom of grace more bountifully, and those whom nature had denied him he made brethren by the benefit of adoption. Such brethren or sons the Son of God and of man himself so excels, as the fruit-bearing apple tree surpasses the trees of the woods; because both the fruitfulness of the apple tree *163* is set before their unfruitfulness, and no man whatsoever (however just he may have been) is compared to Christ. Understanding which, Peter, when the centurion deferred to him more than was right, and gave to a mere man what religious devotion owes to Christ [0286D], did not rashly usurp to himself what he knew was not his own, but declared who he was, how barren, how unlike the apple tree. « Cornelius, he says, falling down at his feet, adored him. But Peter lifted him up, saying: Rise, I myself also am a man, even as thou art [n: (Act. X)]. » [var: Vulg. Acts 10:26 reads *Petrus vero elevavit eum*, and the verse ends "I myself also am a man"; the added clause "even as thou art" is not in the received text] And below: « Announcing, he says, peace by Jesus Christ; he is the Lord of all [n: (ibid.)]. » You see that Peter neither wills nor endures to be adored by Cornelius, showing that a mere man ought not to be so honoured; giving to understand that this privilege is owed to Jesus Christ, that he alone, a man among men, should be venerated with divine obeisance. I deem that neither does an angel will such obeisance to be shown to himself, and, though greater than man, yet he is found to reverence man; in whom he now perceives [0287A] something new and untried by all generations past, hidden from the ages, uncovered in the fulness of our time. Whence, when John, rejoicing that very many things were shown him by the angel, and willing to venerate the shower of them with a worthy obeisance of humility, as he thought, fell at his feet, willing to show him the honour owed to greater ones, the angel, measuring himself worthily, was seen to shrink from it. « After I had heard, he says, and seen, I fell down to adore at the feet of the angel who showed me these things, and he said to me: See thou do it not [n: (Apoc. XXII)]. » And above: « I fell down, he says, before his feet, to adore him, and he said to me: See thou do it not, for I am thy fellow-servant, and of thy brethren that have the testimony of Jesus. Adore God [n: (Apoc. XIX)]. » The angel wills not [0287B] to be made equal to Christ, the servant to the Lord, nor the unfruitfulness of the woods to be compared to the fruitful tree; but as the apple tree is agreed to be preeminent among the trees of the wood, so the angel knows Christ to be preeminent among the sons. Finally the angels too are sometimes named sons in the Scriptures, who are proclaimed always to see the face of God the Father; and according to Job the sons of God are read to have come to stand before the Lord, among whom that wicked angel Satan also is read to have been present [n: (Job I)]. Moses also: « He appointed, he says, the bounds of the peoples, according to the number of the children of Israel [n: (Deut. XXXII)]. » calling the angels children of Israel. Which another translation more evidently intimates, saying: « He set the bounds of the nations, according to the number of the angels of God. » For by some it is believed that God chose out of the nations as many to be saved [0287C] as there are found angels to have remained when the others fell; so that as many sons out of men may be prepared for him to see him, as are contained equally as sons among the angels that already see. But among these sons the Bride's Beloved is preeminent as an apple tree, because as in a mere man, so also in an angel there is no salvation; but in Christ, who, though less than the angels by reason indeed of the suffering of death, yet is far more exalted by reason of the union of God and man. Therefore when the angel had said to John. « See thou do it not, » he adds the cause, saying: « Thy fellow-servant his own, and of thy brethren that have the testimony of Jesus, adore God. » I, he says, am worthily the servant not only of God, but of God made man; the fellow-servant of thee and of them who by faith and by work bear testimony to Jesus; nor does it befit a servant to take away from his Lord his own [0287D] rights, since rather every creature ought to defer to the Creator. Do thou therefore adore not an angel, but God — God, who took not hold of an angel that he might save an angel, but took hold of the seed of Abraham that he might make him safe; God, who, emptying himself and taking the form of a servant [n: (Philip. II)], was found in habit as a man; according to which manhood, although he was found less than the angels, yet he is reckoned not much less, but a little less, if what Scripture declares be taken with an undeviating sense. « Thou hast made him a little less than the angels, says David [n: (Psal. VIII)]. » And Paul: « Him, he says, who was made a little lower than the angels by reason of the suffering of death [n: (Hebr. II)]. » Christ assuredly consists of God and man, but man of soul and [0288A] body; and the soul is the greater part of man — greater, I say, not in bulk, but in dignity and excellence, in that man was made in the soul to the image and likeness of God. But the body is the lesser, that is, the inferior part of man, according to which part Christ is found less than the angels, in that the angel's nature is not clothed with the wrapping of flesh that must die; but that immortal Spirit, strong in understanding, endowed with reason, may remain in that which it was made without the hurt of death. The spirit of man also is declared to be of the same or of a like nature, itself also endowed with reason, having a beginning, and not held by the narrowness of an end; but, poured into a body which is begotten corrupted with an original fault, by a wondrous and pitiable enough law it is weighed down by that body's deserts. Whence you have read: [0288B] « The body that is corrupted weigheth down the soul; and the earthly habitation presseth down the sense that museth upon many things [n: (Sap. IX)]. » The soul therefore, which is of the same or of a like nature with the angel (for creatures of this kind are as it were of one genus by the merit of reason), first indeed discolours its native beauty from the fellowship of the flesh, but afterwards, by the assent of the will, slips into the ruin of vices. But because the flesh of Christ, conceived of the Spirit, contracted no original fault to itself, it drew not the soul poured into it to a guilt which it had not known; but as both the conception and the converse kept the one unpolluted, so creation and the Creator's condescension rendered the other free from every *164* stain. But the soul, whose nature immortality, understanding, and reason so ennoble, and which neither [0288C] a prevailing incorporation nor any defilement dishonours — such a soul as Christ is to be believed to have assumed into a personal unity with himself, nor is he that assumed believed to have consumed the dignity of that which was assumed — than such a soul, I say, neither an angel nor any creature whatsoever is greater; he alone is greater, to whom, as Creator, no creature can be made equal. According to which soul Christ ought not to be esteemed less than the angels, which soul, strong with an inborn dignity, could be depraved by no vice; and although the maker did not confer this not-being-able upon the thing made in the making, yet in assuming he conferred it upon this soul by assuming. For what neither an angel nor a soul obtains by the privilege of its condition, by force of nature, the Creator conferred upon the creature to whom he willed, when he willed; and he who had first conferred upon the angels that none could fall further, or fear it, [0288D] afterwards gave to the soul which he assumed, that a holy confirmation should equally remain in its footing. It remains therefore that Christ be asserted less than the angels according to the flesh only, which, as was said before, is judged the other, the lesser or inferior part of man; and therefore he is rightly read to be made less, not much less than the angels, lest it should seem to be according to the whole man, but a little less, that mention may be known to be made of the flesh alone. But guilt has made us wretches much less than the angels, whose flesh conception and whose soul incorporation has so infected, that, guilty on both sides, we deservedly accuse our begetting, held by a hard law, in that the fault has been turned into nature. Whence Paul: « We are, he says, by nature children of wrath [n: (Ephes. II)]. » [var: Vulg. Eph. 2:3 *et eramus natura filii irae*, past tense: "and we were by nature children of wrath"] And [0289A] although the burden of guilt did not weigh down the good angels, but grace better confirmed them who were well created, yet among them, as also among men, Christ is to be believed to be preeminent, and, as the apple tree among the trees of the wood, so to obtain the primacy among sons of this kind. The angels indeed venerate Christ not as God only, but as God made man, in whom they contemplate the fruit of salvation and the protecting shade; through whom they see, and do not envy, that men pressed and oppressed by the heavy bundle of damnable offences are converted and return to the saving excellence of merits. Which neither crops, nor a ram, nor a calf, nor finally man or angel could work, nor was there another name given wherein we should be saved; but life so complains of faintness, and health of loss, that for [0289B] fruit or for healing every creature is ineffectual. Because therefore the barren wood did not give life, fruit, the efficacy of a mighty medicine, nor the craft of any workshop; and to a sick world they that promise are already hateful by the merit of their delay: there arose the tree of life, a fruit-bearing apple tree, in the midst of paradise. Whose sweeter shade, driving far off incitements, whose fruit freely refreshing those whom oppressive hunger had hurt, not only brought man back to the fellowship of the angel once lost, but also raised human nature above the angelic dignity. For the angel beholds our nature now made one with God in Christ, and, considering its fruit, worthily confesses himself barren; because he who was not able to restore either a falling fellow-angel or a lost man ought not to have compared himself to Christ, who was able [0289C] to do all things that he willed.
