## CHAPTER XXIII.

*The king brought me into the wine storeroom: he set charity in order in me.* [var: Vulg. Cant. 2:4 *introduxit me in cellam vinariam*: no *rex*, and *cellam*, "wine cellar," for the printed *cellariam*] Whom he names king, and why, I recall that I said above, when the Virgin remembered that she had been brought into the king's storerooms; which, though they are worthily reckoned filled with many riches, are nevertheless not [0293A] recorded to be storerooms of wine. But now the Virgin declares that she is brought into the wine cellar, hinting that with the passing of time she is led on to an advancement of conscience; wherein she not only rejoices already over the gift of grace received, but is made drunk with an incomparable gladness as with abundance of wine. For if that most excellent cup of which you have read [n: (Psal. XXII)] makes drunk any just men you please, so that, the Spirit falling from heaven, it makes them talkative as men wet with new wine [n: (Act. II)] — of what merit do we judge the virgin to be, in whose womb God hid himself, and to whom, brought in, the whole wine cellar pours itself out more graciously? How could she who was brought in fail to have a mind made drunken, for whom no narrower cup, no wisdom mingled wine in a bowl, but an overflowing cellar, a house of wine, put forth a spreading richness and flooded her more richly [0293B] as the rush of a river floods a city? For the Most High, willing that his own proper tabernacle should be adorned, that a holy place holier than the rest should be graced with worthy virtues, did not bring out little gifts to her with a grudging hand from his inner sanctuary, but more graciously brought her in within the secret places of his sanctuary. Where the one beloved is joined to the one lover alone, and out of that fountain, or in it, draws waters with a fuller joy [n: (Isa. XII)]; by which, washed, she is made white; by which, drinking, she more sweetly grows glad, is made drunk with joy, and warms mightily as with wine. But this drunkenness does not disturb the mind, does not confound the virgin soul, does not darken the memory, does not take away knowledge but pours it in; it thinks no evil, deals not perversely, loves equity, stirs up no anger, brings in no hatred, nourishes love, [0293C] sets charity in order [n: (I Cor. XIII)]: *He set,* she says, *charity in order in me.* Of no small merit is she, of no small holiness in fine, in whom not only charity but also the order of charity is pre-eminent; and while in very many others love is diffused, in her especially the diffusion abides unconfused. For most men, though they love, yet do not keep order in loving, but either darkened by ignorance or weighed down by a heavier negligence, *167* love more what should have been loved less, or less what should have been loved more, or set in the last place what was worthily to be set first. But the Virgin, prudently taught in all these things, and hindered by no stains of negligence from fulfilling what she knew, loves herself, as is worthy, in the Lord, [0293D] loves, as it is written, every neighbour as herself [n: (Matth. XXII)]. And among those whom faith or nature makes neighbours, whom the authority of the written law commands to be loved with measure, the prudent Virgin knows how to discern whom she should embrace with less and whom with more love, to whom she should defer less, whom she should reverence with greater diligence. And lastly, in Christ also, whom she conceived of the Holy Spirit and brought forth, in whom a personal unity coupled God and man, the true Mother so loved both with a discerning judgment that charity did not err, which that same Son set in order in her. For first she loved God, whom she knew to be the Creator of things heavenly and earthly, and the most orderly lover of the things he had created, united to true flesh and in a wondrous manner made man — she loved him with her whole heart, [0294A] with all her strength, without measure. For in this love no measure whatever is bidden to be kept, and no bound; and how much God is to be loved, no writer has taught, nor Holy Scripture; but whoever is found to have expressed the manner of loving more subtly is seen to have taught not how much, but out of how much, he ought to be loved. « Out of thy whole, he says, heart, and out of thy whole soul, and out of all thy strength [n: (Luc. X)]. » This is the measure — if measure it be — wherewith the Virgin mother rejoices to love God in Christ; the depth whereof human speech cannot clearly express; the sweetness whereof, though a more fervent soul may somehow experience it, yet one who may unfold it in words is not easily to be found. Out of all therefore that she had, that she could, she loved in her Son; to the incarnate deity she applied [0294B] her mightier power, her readier affection; to every creature whatsoever she preferred the Creator with worthy reverence, and held God united to man more pleasing than all things. But the man, whom she knew to be her own virgin flesh, conceived without man, born without any groan of his mother, and by nature and natural binding to be her own neighbour, she loved as herself, as she had read it worthy to do. For since no man hates his own flesh [n: (Ephes. V)], as you recollect that you have read, although he may know that some fault or other has been present to that same flesh — how greatly must we believe that the Virgin mother loved the Son, in whom fault had nothing, and nature was astonished to have lost its ingrown right? For the Virgin in conceiving bore no embrace, no coupling; in bearing she felt no pain of body, no wounding; [0294C] the original guilt, which of old had been turned as it were into nature, being driven far off grieved, and could by no means take hold of such a begetting. Of old the law of sin was wont to come before every woman about to bear, and drove both mother and son like tributaries to the usury; upon all alike that were born it stamped the striking of its own coinage, nor was there any so happy as to escape this pressure. The Virgin alone, bringing forth in wondrous manner a singular firstborn, in conceiving and in bearing did not undergo the general sentence; she rejoiced to have such a neighbour, nay, such a Son, in whom the injury of the origin could obtain nothing of its own. This neighbour therefore the Virgin [sic: *reete*] loves as herself, and, if it be lawful to believe it, she must be said [0294D] to love him more than herself, because she judges that so great a man, above every man, is to be loved, and to be embraced more tenderly and more eagerly than herself as well as than all others. And what by the Spirit's teaching she understands, and understanding loves, must be done, by the working of that same Spirit she is not powerless or ineffectual to fulfil; and therefore the Son, the man whom in a manner unspeakable she brought forth out of herself, as she understands that he is to be loved, so she can love, and does love, more than herself. We wretched ones assuredly either cannot grasp with the understanding the things that are perfect and highest, or, for fulfilling them, have not a perfect and effectual grace; and therefore perhaps God, pitying our infirmities and ourselves, commands us for the time to love any man as ourselves. But the Virgin, full of God, rich with the gift of a fuller grace, [0295A] is carried up into the hill country and to a step of loftier merit; and the man whom she knew to be born of her materially, hindered by no stains of origin, clean in flesh and by the Spirit united in kind to God who is inaccessible to all, she loves above all whom she understands to be endowed with sparer merits and grace, holding it worthy not only not to prefer herself to him, but not even to compare herself with him in love. This order of loving he who is loved himself inspired, and in the loving Mother he set a chief charity in order, so that her whom in his own birth the Son had wondrously granted to be both a virgin and fruitful, he might in a manner no less wondrous render, if not equal in merits, at least second. For inferior to Christ alone, she is pre-eminent above all the rest in the light of holiness; but holiness is made bright by nothing save the excellence [0295B] of charity, which, the more subtly and expressly it grasps the necessary order, the more perfectly and effectually it shows itself Christ's neighbour. This benefit Christ works in the wine cellar, into which the Virgin, being brought, rejoices to burn with the fire of charity; judging nothing more gracious, nothing more delightful, than to be aflame; nothing in the present more wholesome than to be smitten, or to languish, with the heat of charity. Whereof she adds: *Stay me up,* etc. *168*
