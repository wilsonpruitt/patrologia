## CHAPTER XXIV.

*Stay me up with flowers, compass me about with apples, for I languish with love.* They who love unlawful things, and are not yet reckoned to have possession of them, are declared to sicken and to pine away by the putting off of their desire; and their [0295C] ruined mind runs into the troubles of languishing, if the fulfilment of their vow does not come to the aid of their wretched will. Whence Ammon, who greatly loved Thamar the sister of Absalon, when he could not yet fulfil what he had evilly coveted, fell sick; and despairing that the wickedness he sought after could be brought to effect, he went day by day, wasted with leanness, toward his failing. « It came to pass, he says, that Ammon the son of David greatly loved Thamar the sister of Absalon, and pined away for her exceedingly, so that he fell sick for the love of her. To whom Jonadab: Why, said he, art thou thus wasted with leanness, O son of the king, day by day? And Ammon said: « I love Thamar the sister of Absalon [n: (II Reg. XIII)]. » If he sickens and pines away who is weighed down by an unlawful love, if he who is pressed by the desire of Venus is wasted day by day, how are we to believe that the Virgin mother, loving [0295D] the Bridegroom, sickens, she who fails toward her salvation not by the incest of Venus but by the heat of a chaste wound? For she loves, and in loving, as by a wound, she slips into a kind of failing, while she does not yet lay hold, and yet so strains the edge of her mind toward what is perfect that, not content with what she has, she pours out her whole desire upon what is to be had, and as though she obtained nothing, so she burns to cleave to the things to be obtained. Reaching therefore toward what she wishes for, and not yet feeling it consummated, she languishes and fails, and cannot obtain what she desires; she seeks after it and awaits it, and still complains that she has not the effect of it. What is healthier than this languishing, what more languid than this health, could the Virgin experience, at least in this life, who, unwilling that anything savouring of earth should be found within her mind, wished to cleave with unbending embraces to God alone, [0296A] and was still constrained, perhaps for the sake of others, to remain here? She wished, and was not able, unbreakably to enjoy God, to be loosed from the heavy body, not to be worn down by labour, to rejoice in jubilee; judging it good to enjoy invisible goods, when as yet, so it seems, she must needs be held by things visible. And so to one thus willing and not able, her converse in the flesh is somewhat burdensome, yet assuredly to the maidens who are to be nourished it is not a little fruitful; because the example and teaching of the Virgin profited the holy apostles, while through her among them the order of life and the discipline of manners flourished. And she, marking that she is put off from that which she loves the more, wishes some consolations to be brought to her waiting; and the maidens who live with her she invites to advancement, that seeing it she may bear her own unfinished state, as it were, more patiently. [0296B] If, she says, the presence of a perfect rest does not yet receive me, if faith cannot yet pass over into the rule of vision; if impossibility languishes, because possibility is not yet consummated; if I am afflicted with groaning so long as imperfection is not healed by perfection; I wish at least that you meanwhile be advanced to fitting profits, and by my word as well as by my example deserve the same blessedness along with me. If that highest and discerning provider of things wills me to be here longer, if by a deep counsel he wills my presence to profit you; if he puts me off from glory, I do not accuse the good pleasure of him who puts me off, and so long as it shall please him I do not refuse the labour laid upon me. Do you meanwhile, while there is time, come together to the bed, as it were, of my sickness, and stay me up with spring flowers, [0296C] that is, with your good affections, as I labour under a desire deferred; put far from you the old dryness, the harshness of winter; and with a flowering diversion and a springtime solace warm again one who is languid with the heat of love. For it will be no small consolation, no slight refreshment, to one who burns; it will be reckoned a poultice, or oil, or a binding, to one who is sick, if, the winter frost being driven far off, you flower with the beauty of spring; if after the flowers of affections you bring forth the riper fruits of works. A good tree indeed is graced in the welcome season of spring with the flowers of its beauty, and not content with that grace is afterwards laden with the fruit of works; wherewith the country husbandman, who had laboured in it, seeing himself compassed about, rejoices that the labour he bestowed is repaid with a fruitful gift. So [0296D] the Virgin also wills that the maidens who are with her hold the place of a good tree, and, set in the garden of the Bridegroom, wills them to flower with faith and desire; and with the passing of time to abound in good works, stayed up and compassed about wherewith she may await the Bridegroom the more richly, the longer she awaits him. For the more shall deserve to profit by her word and example, and shall be held in her company when the Bridegroom shall have come, the richer and the more festive shall she herself appear to him at his coming; and not only for her own merits, but for theirs also, shall she obtain a greater glory. Rightly therefore she desires and demands to be stayed up with flowers and compassed about with apples, since she does not yet know God face to face; that meanwhile she may rejoice over the advancement of those whom she instructs, and, when the time shall have come, may glory the more over them and with them. And she asks not only to be stayed up [0297A] with flowers, but also to be compassed about with apples, because with a flower and no fruit no hungry man can be filled; and although the latter of these necessarily comes after the former, yet the flower is a pleasant plaything to the sight, but assuredly is found not sufficient for the eating. And indeed, *169* as without virtue the appearance of piety is not fruitful, so faith without work is read and understood to be idle *(Jacob II)*; and affection does not suffice, which, if it have time and place, is not brought through to effect; and a good understanding is to all, not to them that have understanding, but to them that do it [n: (Psal. CX)]. *Stay* therefore *me,* she says, *up with flowers, compass me about with apples,* that I may rejoice when I have heard you say: « We ought to obey God rather than men [n: (Act. V)]. » And this: « We cannot but speak the things which we have heard and seen [n: (Act. IV)]. » Let me rejoice too when I have felt [0297B] you going rejoicing from the presence of the council [n: (Act. V)], because you were accounted worthy to suffer reproach for the name of Jesus. *Stay me up,* I say, *for I languish with love,* not with an injury of the head, not with a palsy of the limbs, but with love; not with the straitness of a fever, not with any trouble of the body, but with love; than which nothing more burning, nothing more vehement, can anyone experience, while what is loved is tasted, and is seen and not held, is believed to be present and is ardently sought, and cannot be found.
