## CHAPTER XXV.

*His left hand is under my head; and his right hand shall embrace [0297C] me.* The left hand of the Bridegroom under the head of the Bride shows him present, but the right hand that shall embrace rather than embraces hints that he is absent; so that while a sad complaint is drawn out over his absence, there is good cause to rejoice over his presence, such as it is. For when he had said, *His left hand is under my head,* he subjoined, *and his right hand shall embrace me,* not: embraces me: hinting thereby that the benefit of the left hand is already held in present possession, but that the present embrace of the right hand is not yet had. And because the Virgin had said that she languished with the burning of love, that it may agree with her languishing she hints by the words that follow that she is lying down, since she declares the left hand not to be laid upon her head but placed under it; and it is certain that a comforting hand is placed under one who lies, not under one who stands. And so in the Gospel the servant of the Centurion [0297D] is declared to lie; and if you ask the cause, it is that he is palsied and tormented. For whom that same Centurion hastens by a messenger sent to come before the Lord, praying that he would deign with his most mighty word to help him as he lay. « Lord, he says, my servant lieth at home [n: (Matth. VIII)]. » Why does he lie? Palsied, he says, and grievously tormented. What wilt thou have done for thee? « I am not, he says, worthy that thou shouldst enter under my roof; but only say the word, and my servant shall be healed [n: (ibid.)]. » Elsewhere too we read that a very great multitude lay in five porches, and no cause is put in but the various sickness of their bodies; who wait for an angel to come down into the pool after the accustomed manner to move the water, so that he who has come down first after the moving of the water may, his body being washed, feel the healing. [0298A] « In these, » he says, porches, « there lay a great multitude. » Why it lay, he sets forth: « Of them that languished. » Why in the porches, he adds: « Of them that waited for the moving of the water [n: (Joan. V)]. » If therefore the multitude, if the Centurion's servant are declared to lie, in that they are held by languishing or by palsy, it is not out of tune that of the Virgin, who declares that she languishes by the violence of love, it should be understood also that she lies, which belongs to one who languishes. And to her languishing and lying the Bridegroom does not absent himself longer, but the beloved presents himself to his beloved with a welcome kindness; and lest love should languish too hardly, and languishing bear a harder delay, he puts his left hand under her head, softer than a pillow or any couch. The left hand of the Bridegroom (as some assert) is temporal prosperity, strength, riches, beauty, birth, learning in letters; and whatever [0298B] in this world seems for a time to smile upon us, which no man can obtain unless God give it. Which things are for that reason quite fitly called the left hand, because in this present life such things are not denied to the evil as well as to the good; and those things above all are sought after with all their striving, which are set at the left hand of the strict Judge in that true and severe judgment. All which things the Bridegroom, being present, puts under the head of the Bride, when he sets her holy mind above all things transitory; and what every perverse man with corrupt judgment reckons the highest allurements, the Virgin presses down and despises, judging them loss for Christ. For as the man who craves such things exults and dances in the getting of them, so — nay, far otherwise — the wise Virgin rejoices in the despising of them; and whereby she pleases the Bridegroom, in that at his regard, as at a head [0298C] set over her, she spurns all the rest, thereby the other displeases him, while he lays himself down under those same transitory things. But the right hand of the Bridegroom is that more fruitful and living sweetness of grace which the animal man, blinded in heart, does not perceive; but he alone knows it, nay the Spirit of him, immortal, to whom the spiritual unction gives the understanding which it teaches for knowledge. This therefore the Virgin, set as yet in this body, already tastes; but lest she take it in with a widened bosom, the presence of the flesh cramps it for her; this she perceives, and having perceived it rejoices to be greatly delighted; she is delighted and hungers, and hungering is not yet able to be filled. This delight assuredly has here a beginning but not an end; it pours itself into them that love, to them that desire it it *170* promises an end; but to them that worthily persevere it gives that [0298D] end whose beginning knows no trouble, whose consummation knows no end. Beholding which the Prophet: « Delights, he says, at thy right hand even to the end [n: (Psal. LI)]. » Which right hand, since to one who loves and waits it is not yet more fully conferred, but the present life, as it were the left hand, the more troublesome as it is the longer, is set before it: this she feels and does not consent to, but by the excellence of her mind rises above the enjoyment of it; that other she hopes for, and affirms as to come, which, though not in time, yet by its own worth is already preferred. *His right hand,* she says, *shall embrace me.* He who has not wholly denied me his presence or his coming, while he has brought into contempt those temporal things which are given by him, shall be seen to present himself to me far more fully and far more delightfully, when his right hand and unfailing life shall deign to embrace me.

[0299A]There are those who would have another sense found in the left hand set beneath: namely that there are signified the spiritual things which divine grace wills to bestow upon the Bride; wherewith, while she lives here, the Bridegroom loves to mark her out, and to soothe with their sweet refreshment one who labours under the sun. For because she confessed that she languished with the heat of love, and, held by languishing, seemed, as was said above, to lie; and that the languor of one lying down should not, with the delay pressing, sink into weariness, the Bridegroom puts his left hand under her head, that upon it she may lay herself down in her languor. The Virgin grieves indeed that the sight of her Maker is not yet more fully granted her; she wishes to be dissolved, for that sight is inaccessible to all mortals; under the heavy delay her inner mind languishes and lies down as though wearied; and firm faith, a surer hope, an unwearied charity put themselves beneath her. The welcome fellowship of these virtues, [0299B] or their fellowly grace, their present carefulness, their faithful keeping, their spiritual consolation, when it does not suffice that the Virgin be compassed about with flowers or apples, fulfil for her languid mind, for her head as if wearied, the office of a bolster. While the time weighs upon me, she says, while the days are evil, let me here rest a little; while I languish with love, while I lie in languor, let me lie hid in your lap. Here is my rest, here my dwelling, until at last I grow old in a hundredfold, not of years but of merits; and when that is fulfilled, let the long languor give way — I shall be made young as the eagle. For from that time and thenceforth, after the poet's manner, I shall count my years like Nestor upon my right hand; nay, passing over from the left to the right, after the Prophet, I shall watch from the morning; in the morning I shall stand watching, and shall contemplate him whom I have desired, or rather my own desire; and under his right hand, [0299C] when his own glory appears, I shall be satisfied [n: (Psal. XVI)]. Then no longer shall the left hand, that is, the consolation of present virtues, be put under my languid head, but the right hand and the eternal sight of God shall be conferred in fulness; which shall embrace me standing and seeing with dearer arms, shall surround me wholly, and nothing anywhere shall be left languid. But meanwhile, while I await the coming glory of that right hand, I will not despise the grace already present, nor the solace of the left; but lying hid in it I shall be glad according to the place and the time; and when faith shall have passed into vision, and hope into the thing itself, I shall glory. And the Bridegroom, considering the Virgin so provoked with love, and lulled asleep in the soft lap of the left hand under the hope of the right, speaks thus: *I adjure,* etc.
