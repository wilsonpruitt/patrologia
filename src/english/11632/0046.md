## CHAPTER XXVI.

[0299D]

*I help you, O daughters of Hierusalem, that you stir not up, nor make the beloved to awake, till she herself will.* [var: Vulg. Cant. 2:7 opens "I adjure you" (*adiuro*, with r); the printed *Adjuvo* means "I help." Migne prints the adjuring form both in the tag that closes the preceding chapter at col. 0299C and when the verse returns at 0302A] The Bridegroom, speaking to the maidens, shapes himself to our manners, and after the manner of men calls them to witness rather hardly, and adjures them; knowing that negligence or forgetfulness sometimes shuts and stops the ears of the weak, and that the transgression of commandments does not go long unpunished. Warning them therefore lest he be thought to command negligently, and lest they should not take diligent care to keep a commandment as though it were a very light one, he binds them more hardly and puts in an oath, that upon those in whom infirmity works sloth the sacrament may strike fear. I will not, he says, that the beloved, under whose head I have put the spiritual left hand, to whom, resting in grace, I have given to look for the eternal glory; I will not, he says, that you call her back [0300A] to business or to temporal care, until she herself shall have seen that the same care is made wholesome for you. For she knows better when she ought to be free for spiritual leisure or for spiritual business, when she should take counsel for you by word and by temporal foresight; when she should read more devoutly, pray more urgently, meditate more closely, when she should turn herself back to you, of whom she is not held forgetful. And when she shall have seen her return and her presence to be needful for you, she who assuredly loves not so much to be set forth to you as to profit you, she will do a kind of injury to her spiritual leisure, and will change necessary acts into voluntary ones. I will therefore that you bear evenly with her return to you until she will it; for she will will it when she shall have seen a fitting time to press upon you: knowing that your business must not be utterly let go, and that her own leisure [0300B] must indeed be held to chiefly, yet must sometimes be broken off. They who in the Church are set over the rest in care and office are instructed by this teaching, if they will attend to it more diligently: namely that with the highest zeal they should give diligence to spiritual leisure, and should not leap back from it at the blandishments and enticements of things temporal. For the most part we are so hindered toward spiritual things by such cords of *171* negligence, and are found so lukewarm, or rather so cold, toward them, that we make more haste to run about, to hear, to see, to be full of words, judging nothing more grievous, nothing more troublesome, than to sit, to read, to write, to meditate. And, to heap up the evil, if we have seen any unwilling to cleave to worldly business, spurning running about, speaking more sparingly, abhorring rumours; giving their labour to reading, sweating more gladly at meditation, and, if they have understood anything, committing it to a remembering writing: [0300C] these, with drawn-in lip, hooked nostril, and lifted brow, we mock at; and, since stones are lacking, with a slanderous tongue we stone them. Why is this? unless because we do not hear the warnings of the Bridegroom wherewith he adjures the daughters of Hierusalem, or rather, hearing them, with malice running to meet us we in no wise obey; doubtless drawing upon ourselves the wrath of him who, a Judge more severe, hates and pursues them that detract! « The man that detracted, he says, against his neighbour, him did I persecute [n: (Psal. C)]. » Yet nevertheless it is proper to those who are seen to preside over the rest in the Church, not to lord it over the clergy, but to show themselves a pattern to their subjects; not to entangle the mind with outward cares or with worldly business, but with a mind bent down to serve spiritual studies; not to be present willingly at the causes of men of the courts, nor at the [0300D] contentions of worldly men, but to be at hand with a fitting purpose for the study of letters and for spiritual leisure. Let their whole will betake itself to those better things; let necessity, not pleasure, bring them back to these lower ones: in the former let them be hidden, that they may diligently draw the hidden things of wisdom; to the latter let them be brought back, patiently, for the sake of their brethren and neighbours. But they who wish to detract from such men, and to draw them back from their spiritual purpose, let them fear the warnings of the Bridegroom, whereby both the Bride is helped and the daughters of Hierusalem are adjured; because their studies will not be hurt by our envious murmuring, and the indignation and the animadversion of the strict Judge will pursue the wicked detractors. But what is this, that the daughters of Hierusalem are adjured by the roes and the harts of the fields, as though such animals were known [0301A] to be feared or loved, so that by a mention made of them more diligently those women might be more attentively moved to hold the bidding they have heard? And who would believe that the commandment of one so adjuring should be kept the more carefully, and not rather be reckoned useless and altogether frivolous, since the reverence of any oath scarcely holds a man, if that by which he is adjured is reckoned of no weight or moment? But in this play, although carnal and contemptible things seem to be named, yet in them a sounder understanding loves to search out spiritual things; and abhorring what the literal surface sounds and clamours, it holds great only that which the spiritual content shows to them that seek it. To the roe, then, as men say, this is implanted by nature, that she is not content with plains and valleys for her pasture; but she seeks the lofty tops of the mountains [0301B] with the utmost care, as though nowhere else she would find a sufficiency to please her. The wide breadth of the fields, the low and sunken valleys, granted from the beginning by the laws of nature to all cattle everywhere, she passes by with a kind of disdain; she goes to rocks inaccessible to the rest, and there she loves to feed delightfully, to take leaps confidently, to make unwearied stay. Whereby, not unfittingly, that virtue can be figuratively signified which, not content with transitory goods, is zealous to gaze upon the highest and heavenly things; which, going beyond those things in which the common infirmity of many is seen to linger, both wills and is able more graciously to embrace certain steep places inaccessible to lesser men. Which, forgetting the troubles of the present life as things put behind it, engrafted among the companies of the angels, nay, going forth in desire beyond the angels, [0301C] having climbed above the flatness of things below, is caught up by the love of things above, and hanging from the highest peaks is fed with the sweetness of things eternal. But the hart, which with hunger and a fitting greed devours serpents and venom, which the other animals abhor and judge alien from their food, signifies that virtue of the mind which loves to swallow up deadly sins, and to bring an eating diligence to the destroying of them. For there are some who do not indeed reach the excellence of contemplating, but who gird themselves with a strong sword to destroy vices; who, as it were the food of bread, devour the people of the lost, and by a wholesome eating bring to nothing the force of offences. And when the fraud of them that assail has stuck in these men like an arrow, they compel it by an art quickly sought out to leap back; and they do not suffer the hurtful iron long to have mastery in the wound, [0301D] but when it is drawn out they grow strong again by a saving medicine. But because it is agreed that this virtue belongs to men that are humble, whom it is agreed to be not rough with wickedness but smooth with the good will of their manners; who, though they have done great things, yet say that they are unprofitable servants: therefore he says harts of the fields, namely such as strongly embrace the broad commandment of the twofold love, and run the way of the commandments when they are enlarged in heart and mind. And lastly, when harts set themselves to pass through [0302A] the deeps of waters, in that very passage they so set themselves before one another in order that the head of the one that follows is laid upon the haunches of the one that goes before, and that same one in like manner puts itself under another that follows. But he who has set himself before them all, and is stayed up by no forerunner, when he is hindered by weariness from bearing the labour of swimming and of carrying, leaving him who is placed beneath, hastens to betake himself to the last place, whom he knows to be still free and not bearing another that follows. So, bearing one another's burdens in turn, they busy themselves to fulfil the law of Christ [n: (Galat. VI)]; imitating a building in which stone loves to hold up stone; whose holding up the affection of charity works, *172* by whose strong ship they are drawn out of shipwreck and led to the harbour of their will. *I adjure you,* he says, *O daughters of Jerusalem, [0302B] by the roes and the harts of the fields.* You, he says, whom it beseems to be daughters of Hierusalem, that is of peace, without which the sight of God can in no wise be present to you, by those virtues whereby you both tread down vices and love rewards, I will adjure you, that you presume not, by a counsel led astray, to go against the sounder desire of the Virgin. By those virtues, I say, which are lifted higher in searching out and contemplating God, and by those likewise which are stretched more widely into the love of a neighbour, I adjure you, lest you be found to assail with troubles a neighbour who is at rest, and to fight against God, in whose left hand she reclines. For this rest of hers is useful and welcome to her who rests, and not unwelcome to me who know more fully what is expedient for whom; and therefore I will both that she, while it is time, give diligence to quiet, and that you who are weaker be kept from [0302C] murmuring or from judging. Who will grant me not only that I stir not up nor make to awake any that sleep, nor be found guilty of the commandment by rashly judging them that rest; but that I too may rest in the left hand of the Bridegroom which gives its grace, and may lie hid from the heat and the whirlwind and the rain in the hiding place of his tabernacle? For happy am I if I may so deserve to die, so to sleep; if the Bridegroom deign to soothe me with the benefit of this death, with the grace of this sleep; if the daughters of Hierusalem be forbidden to hinder this, so that I be not compelled to leap back either to the troubles of life or to the watchings of guilt [n: (Num. XXIII)]. Happy am I, I say, if my soul, dying the death of the just and fainting, may long for the courts of the Lord [n: (Psal. LXXXIII)]; if it may fall asleep either in the left hand of the Bridegroom or at least on the knees of the Bride; if it may learn by experiment how sweet [0302D] that sleep was to the Virgin, how delightful — she from whose kiss the first book took its beginning, and in whose sleep let this second receive its end. For I judge it worthy that for the present my pen should not hasten to pursue the rest, but that, while the Bride sleeps, it too should lay itself down a little; and when she shall have willed to awake, and charity shall constrain her, watchful, to speak, my pen shall gird itself again, so far as its strength allows, to a labour interrupted rather than laid down.
