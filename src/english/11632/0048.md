## CHAPTER III.

[0304A]

*Behold, he cometh leaping upon the mountains, skipping over the hills.* The Virgin burns to look more nearly upon him whom she loves, seeing and not envying that his presence is not held unprofitable; and him whom she first perceives to be coming she announces to any that will hear, that at the word of her who announces the hearer may run up and run to meet him that comes. For neither is she moved with envy if abundance of him be given to others also, if the beloved in his coming offer himself to be seen by the rest also, that he may be loved; whose love reconciles lovers and puts into them a spiritual consent, so that [sic: *signa*] may burn the sooner and may not fear to have an emulator, not a rival. Therefore here too the virgin hides not the presence or the coming of her beloved, but announces it to the daughters, wishing that it may come to the profit of all — him whom for this cause she proclaimed a little above to be gracious in beauty, [0304B] that she might consequently show him fruitful not to herself alone but to the rest also. She says above: « Behold thou art fair, my beloved, and comely [n: (Can. I)]. » And a little after: « As the apple tree among the trees of the woods; so is my beloved [n: (Can. II)]. » But whom there, announcing him fair, she plainly intimates to be not absent, why here does she proclaim him coming as though he were not present, since there she did not say that after his presence was granted he had withdrawn, so that she must needs say that he had drawn near again? But, as is known to one that has experience, it is a property of lovers or of love, that he who loves and desires to be held by the bond of a straiter affection should sometimes withdraw himself, that he may draw his beloved after him and be anxiously sought; and sometimes return, that he may caress her and be the more eagerly held. Now he both goes and returns, and is said to be to us now absent, now present, [0304C] he whom a sounder faith believes never, nowhere not to be at hand; because he who is always and everywhere present by the privilege of an uncircumscribed majesty is yet called absent when he withholds, and present when he bestows upon us the grace of his goodness. Now therefore the Virgin rejoices and announces that he is coming, marking that in his coming there is no delay, that he finds no obstacle; but that, leaping upon the mountains, he passes over the hills that meet him, that so he may be seen to reach the appointed place by a swift advance. But the Virgin, speaking mystically, willed to call the good angels mountains, who, it is certain, shine with the excellence of a primal holiness; who, created sublime, fell not by the blast of pride nor by the impulse of avarice, but by the merit of persevering, or by grace alone, [0304D] advanced to an unswerving firmness. In whom God leaps, when he keeps not the hidden things of his counsel with himself alone, but shows himself to them wonderful in his counsels over the sons of men; when what he purposes to work among men in the process of time he vouchsafes upon his heavenly throne to reveal all at once to the angels. For the salvation of men, which God willed to work in the midst of the earth, distributing it through diverse places and times by a saving dispensation and providence, he willed that the angels in heaven should [0305A] first know all at once, that by their ministry he might afterward impart his benefits to the earth. « Are they not all, he says, ministering spirits, sent to minister for them who receive the inheritance of salvation? [n: (Hebr. I.)] » God therefore, coming to the Virgin, in whom he works a wonderful mystery, leaps first in the angels, to whom a swift and full knowledge is presented; he makes known to Gabriel the things which he, being instructed, may afterward carry to Mary: God wove the prophecy for the angel all at once, but the angel for the Virgin in order: « Hail, he says, full of grace; behold thou shalt conceive and bring forth a son [n: (Luc. I)], » etc. God leaps also in the angels, when not by himself but through them he undertakes to instruct us, to give warnings of salvation, to impart any benefit whatsoever; and when, to teach us or to rule us, [0305B] they are found to go forth suddenly or to leap, he may be said to do this, whom, it is agreed, they that do it obey. « I heard, saith Daniel, the voice of a man between Ulai; and he cried and said: Gabriel, make this man to understand the vision [n: (Dan. VIII)]. » And to Mary asking: « How shall this be done, because I know not man [n: (Luc. I)]? » the angel says: « The Holy Ghost shall come upon thee [n: (ibid.)]. » But whether God be asserted to leap in the angels, that is, to the angels, or perchance to men through the angels, that he may bestow upon them with an abundant gift his own presence and the presence of his own; yet he skips over the hills, when he baulks them of the grace of that same gift and enlightens them not with the knowledge of himself by a like benefit. But the hills are the demons, *174* who, it is agreed, are borne above earthly men [0305C] by the privilege of nature, yet by the desert of their fault are not to be reckoned with the spirits above; who, having fallen from among them, grow great no more upon an equal summit, and having slipped down to men, swell against them the more wickedly. From whom God hides the mystery hidden from the ages; he reveals not the grace of his coming, the mystery of great godliness; but passing them by, whose good nature the depravity of pride dishonours, he manifests himself and his own wisdom to better ones, whether angels or men: « Wisdom, saith Paul, we speak among the perfect; which none of the princes of this world knew [n: (I Cor. II)]. » And to Micheas, prophesying more truly, Sedecias the son of Chanaan said: « Hath then the Spirit of the Lord left me, and spoken to thee? [n: (III Reg. XXII.)] » Plainly it is so. The Spirit of truth leaves and skips over [0305D] the proud and the lying, but the humble in spirit he saves, and teaches them by the gift and presence of his goodness; to these he hastens as a giant rejoicing and suffers himself to be felt more present; those, given over to forgetfulness, he flies past with a light bound and admits them not to the knowledge of himself. Nor is it repugnant to a fitting sense if the Virgin also be said to have mountains and hills in herself, that her loftiness may be commended in spiritual goods and in temporal; of which the spiritual are rightly compared to mountains, and the temporal to hills, which, though great, are yet not equal to the spiritual. The mountains indeed in the Virgin are chiefly the gifts of those virtues by which, or for which, we know it to have been granted to the same Virgin that she should worthily be pre-eminent above the rest in the Church upon a lofty height, and worthily [0306A] please him who is truly called the mountain of mountains. Her love is a mountain, her humility a mountain, the integrity of her chaste purity is a mountain, in which the Bridegroom leaps, that is, shows himself present by the magnificence of his divine majesty; into which, according to the angel's word, the fulness of grace [n: (Luc. I)] wholly pours itself, that in these the incomparable loftiness of the Bride may stand out and rejoice. But that her love was much — not one to whom, offending, indulgence forgave more, but one to whom, loving, the grace of the beloved committed more — she herself intimated a little above in this drama, saying: « His left hand is under my head, and his right hand shall embrace me [n: (Can. II)]. » And she set the merit before it: « Because I languish with love [n: (ibid.)] » But her humility, of what excellence, of what merit it is to be believed, she herself also shows, and no doubt at all is to be had, that that regard of God [0306B] chose her that he might look upon her, or that the Virgin believed herself chosen, so that she answered concerning it the more confidently: « Because he hath regarded the humility of his handmaid [n: (Luc. II)]. » Lastly, the integrity of her purity, as in the flesh so also in the heart, was not hidden from Isaias, and an angel long after confirmed the old prophecy; and since nothing like it was heard before nor after, it is agreed that it was fortified beforehand by the excellence of a singular holiness: « Behold, they say, a Virgin shall conceive and bear a son [n: (Isa. VII; Luc. X)]. » Mountains of this kind he, coming and leaping, willed to take away from the virgin and to bestow, and the same the Virgin took pains to render back and to offer to the bestower: in these he rendered himself present to her with a bounteous hand, with full grace; in the same she rendered herself pleasing to him beyond all women [n: (Psal. XLIV)]. Lastly, [0306C] the daughters of Tyre are read to entreat the King's countenance with gifts, and here he is presented in the mountains, that is, in most lofty [sic: *vul*] gifts; and seeing these his own gifts and his own presents, by which as by singular things he exalted the Virgin, rendered back to himself, he gave himself wholly to them, as it were, with a gracious leap. But the hills which he, leaping, is said to skip over are those gifts of the Bridegroom which, though great, he willed to impart to the Virgin; yet in these the sum of religion, the perfection of life, does not consist, but a comeliness shines forth which, being fitting to the person, does not attend religion as an inseparable companion. For beauty, birth, knowledge, which flourished in the Virgin with a certain excellence, were suitable to comeliness, but were not so necessary to salvation that without them it is not sometimes found among some that the supper is led, nor does any man's holiness, in their absence, [0306D] despair of being brought into the joy of its Lord. But the Bridegroom, willing that nothing comely, as nothing necessary, should be lacking to the Virgin, granted her to be holy in inward goods and elegant in outward: to those bringing himself wholly, nor forsaking them, since they were to abide with a stable footing; these regarding in passing, inasmuch as by the privilege of nature they were to pass away. Passing these by, or skipping over them, he despised them not with disdain or hatred; but, them, shining with a transitory bloom, he beheld not with a fixed gaze; comparing with better things those which present grace exalted and the end has injured, he rather transferred himself to those, and clave not to these that have an end. The elegance of the countenance has an end, the beauty of the body is not held by a stable thong, the glory of parents passes, the line of descent is not long held for a great thing; prophecy shall cease, as you have read [n: (I Cor. VIII)], knowledge [0307A] shall be destroyed; these things and the like of them he who gives them abhors not, but beholds as one passing by. For not chiefly for these is the Virgin proclaimed commendable, but rather for those things which an incomparable holiness works; in which, as upon mountains, he in his coming wills to leap, and these, of unequal merit, to skip over as hills. He indeed is the highest bestower of goods and the lover of all that he bestows, in whom all equity and no iniquity is found; distributing many things to his own, he measures not all by one measure or weight, but discerns each severally and imparts to each a worthy diligence.
