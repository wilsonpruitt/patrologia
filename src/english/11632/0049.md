## CHAPTER IV.

*My beloved is like to a roe and to the fawn of harts.* [0307B]Very many are not led with a fitting subtlety to that which must be done, but are either held by negligence or seduced by ignorance; and when the foot cleaves too slow, or the understanding that goes before is darkened, they neither desire what is worthy nor bring things prosperous to their effects. Which kind of pestilence I judge untried by none among men, but from the first parent it has been inevitably implanted in all his posterity, so that for the most part they either see not, or are not able to go thither where they might rejoice to find life and light, both their own and their people's. For what befell the first parent, first offending, by vice alone and not by nature, is in us that come after, far more wretched, both by vice and by nature; and by a law wretched enough we now either will not, or cannot, deliver ourselves from stains of this sort; and Paul is all too [0307C] truthful: « By nature, » saith he, « we are children of wrath [n: (Ephes. II)]. » [var: Vulg. Eph. 2:3 *et eramus natura filii irae*, past tense — "and we WERE by nature children of wrath"] Would that thou wert, Paul, a man not having a spirit, and wert rather speaking a lie [n: (Michae. II)]; would that thou wert not led by another law of the flesh against the law of the spirit; although thou, now rightly willing but not yet more fully able, reckonest that there is no condemnation to thee, because whatsoever thou so willest and canst not yet fully do, God accounts as done! But I, wretched, far inferior to Paul, whose eye grows dark, whose spirit languishes and with languor bends toward its end — a vicious nature working, a natural vice prevailing — bound by the law of the flesh, obey not the spiritual law. When therefore someone reads me the new Gospel, presses upon me the old prophecy; when upon the ten-stringed psaltery David more often sounds his psalmody; when [0307D] Paul goads me, urging me with harder warnings to go forward, that worthily following those set over me I may likewise go before those set under me: I, of a hard neck, what am I but an ass at the lyre, and, the goad availing nothing, an ox heavy with natural weight standing toward wrath? For taking up the psalm and the harmony of Holy Scripture, the new as well as the old, and being bidden to give the timbrel, to render a concordant symphony, I know not how but to bray, to make an ill-sweet melody sound harshly; I will not follow Debbora [n: (Judic. V)], nor imitate Moses or Mary [n: (Exod. XV)]. Unhappy man that I am, upon whose soul the body that is corrupted has pressed this brand, whose sense, intent upon many things, the earthly habitation has so weighed down [n: (Sap. IX)]; whom an asinine dullness makes blunt [0308A] and stolid, and a sluggish sickness like a good man's makes slow and going backward. Over against him, happy is that man, conceived of the Spirit, whose mind the burden of the flesh blunts not, whose willing — never anything but good — no impossibility beats back; who with a sagacious understanding contemplates things uncertain and hidden from the rest, and with an effectual power works whatsoever he will. A happy man, I say, who knows not the dullness of the ass nor the sloth of the ox, who alone by an inborn power grows effectually strong both toward subtle things and toward remote; who prevails for both, having a sight likened to the roe's, and like also to the fawn who with swift course passes more speedily to what he desires — as they report who have known the various natures of beasts. For there is in diverse creatures much diversity of natures: the roe, though slower than the hart, [0308B] yet sees more sharply, and what may suit or perchance may not suit her as she feeds, she beholds and discerns more subtly. Lastly, she is said to have within her a certain humour, wherewith if a man prudently anoint the eye, it shall be sharpened to see the better; and so not only does she prevail by the sharpness of a readier sight, but the anointing of this inward humour gives to us also the grace of seeing. Who is worthily like to her, save he to whom every will speaks and the hidden thing of the heart lies open, that Bridegroom and beloved of the Virgin, from whom nothing is hid; who with a strict and sharp scrutiny judges and discerns the causes of all, and far removes and severs the evil from the best, the good from the worst? He indeed, alone a man among men, beholds the eastern regions and the western at once, and from beholding them is hindered neither by distance nor by obstacle; [0308C] whom in the searching of things a false appearance seduces not, and whom neither error nor forgetfulness draws away from a true judgment. Lastly, in him is found that humour fitted for medicine, as he knows far better who makes trial of it not so much by knowing as by feeling; which if any man, more greedy of seeing, pour diligently upon his eye, blindness is removed, and blearedness spreads no cloud over the sight. The humour is the Spirit of Christ, the water of wisdom, the fountain of water springing up, the virtue going out from him for the healing of the poor woman that besought it [n: (Luc. VIII)]; his is that water of Siloe, wherein he who had not sinned that he should be made blind washed, not without fruit, his own eyes first smeared with spittle [n: (Joan. IX)]. But the fawn of harts is borne over the level of the fields with a rapid course, and is preferred not only to roes but to harts riper in age; and while a fitting [0308D] strength is at hand for him, an agile body, an age still younger, he spurns barriers, overtops the thickets, leaps the hedges, knows no bounds. So indeed he cares not, fears not the bite of dogs, the snares of them that lie in wait, the traps that meet him, but outflies whatsoever pursuers; and when he is believed present, sooner than one can say it he absents himself; when he is reckoned absent, by a sudden coming he presents himself. To him this beloved of the Virgin is not unfittingly likened, who, being God, is not weighed down by the burden of the flesh he has taken, but wills and is able to leap over all things that displease *176*, and to come to whatsoever he will, straightway when he will. He runs past the cords stretched for a snare [n: (Psal. CXXXIX)], he past the stumbling-block set beside him; he ran not, with slipping or slippery foot, upon the snare hidden [0309A] in the way wherein he walked [n: (Psal. CXLI)]; he outruns with far mightier merits the fathers of whom he was born according to the flesh; from us that are slower he is now withdrawn according to his own will, now runs to meet us. But he is withdrawn when with a sight like the roe's he regards us not so much in the face as in the secret of the conscience, and detects hearts ill-wounded and reins hardly framed; when he considers us empty of merits, heavy with vices, ungracious in manners, and for this cause judges us unworthy of his presence, that is, of his grace; whose present grace when we feel not within us, and, while he watches, sleep heavily under the shadow of vices; when we reckon that he the more negligently forgets our excesses, that, being intent elsewhere, he journeys further off — [0309B] then, we not foreseeing it, sudden as a fawn he presently runs upon us, punishes vices, condemns the vicious, if yet mercy run not before judgment. Very often too, when, our vices being cast off, as we think, we sweat at better pursuits, and for these rightly look for the blessed hope and the propitious coming of the Lord, our understanding growing dark we set bitter for sweet and darkness for light, and cleaving to the goods we know more than is just, we chastise not worthily enough the evils we know not. And so, when offending in many things we feel not our own ignorance, being beyond doubt uncertain whether we be worthy of love or of hatred [n: (Eccle. IX)], suddenly this fawn rushes swift and flies down from on high, and the subtle eye of the roe searches our Jerusalem with lamps [0309C] [n: (Soph. I)]. He searches, I say, the hidden things of a mind darkened by certain ignorances; bringing in light, he scatters the heavy darkness of her that is whitewashed; and seeing clearly whatever unseemly thing is pressed down by our blindness, he accounts the justices which we think ours as the cloth of a menstruous woman [n: (Isa. LXIV)]. He strikes therefore, and striking that which was thought worthy of reward — what is it but that this fawn is suddenly presented to us, who was as it were absent and set far off while he spared, while he deferred the reward falsely hoped for? Who sometimes, pitying our error, draws back the avenging sword into the scabbard, and godly admonishes us to lay hold of saving discipline [n: (Psal. II)], that we, foolish and seduced, may not look for a reward in place of punishment, but, according to Jeremias, may search out our ways [0309D] with an anxious inquiry [n: (Thren. III)]. But because searching is torpid in the dark and more effectual with lamps, he himself is at hand as a roe, enlightening wonderfully from the everlasting mountains; and he pours that humour of his Spirit upon our inward eyes, that they may see made plain whatever hurtful thing hides itself in our conscience. Which being overturned and wiped away, when we now rejoice in our own cleanness, him by whose gift and grace we work in us so magnificent a work we love to love, and will thenceforth to have him more gracious, to mingle discourse with him, to deserve him propitious, to feel him nearer. Who, feeling us to love and consenting to the vows of lovers, affords us a certain abundance of himself, caresses us with gifts, assails us not with strokes; and, whatever heavy thing out of the past troubles us being put far away, that we may taste how [0310A] sweet he is, he manifests his sweetness to them that desire him. And while he is thought to be as it were at hand before the eyes and now to be felt in the hands, he withdraws himself like a fawn and wills to be sought the more ardently; he absents himself without forsaking, not as inflicting a penalty, but he marks whether an intemperate love hangs itself upon desire of the absent. Who, if he shall have seen us held by a tender love, burning toward the absent, refreshed by nothing present; burning with desire, nothing held more pleasing to us than himself: suffers not that we be tormented longer by his absence. For him whom, not seeing, we believe to have gone away into a far country, suddenly coming he again shows himself near; and those whose desire, now sound, he beholds from on high as a roe, he guards in our humility by his sudden coming, after the manner of a fawn. Therefore perhaps he deserved to be called here not a hart but [0310B] the fawn of harts, who also willed by Isaias to be proclaimed a child and a little man [n: (Isa. IX)]; because he who, high above all gods, containing all things in himself, hides himself, by a humble condescension pours himself into the humble in spirit [n: (Psal. XCV)]. Which, because it is known to none better than to the Virgin (for every unworthy man grieves that he has not had trial enough of this gift), therefore the Virgin rightly and confidently testifies this, and bears witness that he is that one like to a roe or to a fawn. But if any of the hearers desires more curiously to know whither he — who, like to a roe and to a fawn, is said to come leaping and skipping — hastens so, and where at last he has fixed his step, let him hear that he is there where the Virgin said that he stayed his step.
