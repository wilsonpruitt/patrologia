## CHAPTER V.

[0310C] *Lo, he himself standeth behind our wall, looking in through the windows, looking forth through the lattices.* He, more sweetly desired, longer awaited, though he has come, has not yet, as I seem to see, arrived; but drawing near to the Bride, whose love he knew was cherished by no other remedy, he stands behind the wall, willing rather to be known than to be seen. The wall in this place is not undeservedly named the present life *177*, which is built of hours and days, of months and years, as of stones set beside or above one another; within whose straits, while we are for a time confined, we either know not God, or do not yet contemplate his face. Whence to Moses: « Man shall not see me and live [n: (Exod. XXXIII)]. » That is: no man set within the prison of this time, [0310D] within the enclosures of this life (though he be of great merit, of a mind more diligently polished), shall see me in my nature, in the glory of my nature — which glory shall appear beyond this wall to them that go forth to be satisfied. The obscurity of the present life therefore admits not the vision of so great a glory; within its darkness that face permits not itself to be seen: no man bearing flesh wrapped in the prison-house of time sees the brightness of God, freed from temporal mists. Which John beholding says: « No man hath seen God at any time [n: (I Joan. IV)]. » At any time, that is, in time; while, that is to say, the creature is subject to vanity, that is, to transitory time, it sees not God himself in his own form or nature; although sometimes it knows, yea and feels, him to be near, to stir it up to pray, [0311A] to be at hand for prayers to be heard And notably the Virgin says not: *Lo, he himself standeth within the wall,* but *behind our wall.* This wall of ours indeed belongs to time, ever tending toward its setting and oppressive likewise of them that tend with it; it grows dark within with certain darknesses of its own, as being of the evening: no dawn rising, no morning sun receives it. Set within its bounds we see not the Bridegroom's face, the glory of Christ, but beyond it, that is, after we shall have passed its bounds; when, its threshold crossed, we shall know nothing transitory any more, then shall we come to the morning after the evening, to the light after the darkness. Whence David also: « In the morning I will stand before thee and will see [n: (Psal. V)]. » And Job: « After darkness, he says, I hope for light [n: (Job. XVII)]. » Fairly again the Virgin said behind the wall, not mine, but ours. For although [0311B] the Virgin, fuller of an ampler grace, is now free of our guilts, yet this wall of the present life is common to us all; wherein it is not now fault, nor flesh heavy with fault, that withdraws the Virgin (as it seems) from the truth that is to be seen, but that the creature itself is still subject to the aforesaid vanity. We indeed, far beneath the Virgin, weighed down with the burden of faults, darkened with carnal allurements and with thicker darkness: as many motions, as many affections as we bear in us that oppress the inward spirit, so many dividing ramparts do we set between ourselves and God. But the Virgin, who, full of grace, is no longer pressed by the urgency of such things, but is held still under time by the present life alone, unwilling indeed and against her will, asserts that not many walls now, but rather one, stands between; and this one she says is not her own but common [0311C] to all. For she who by the excellence of grace has put many ramparts or walls far from herself has not at once cast off, according to her will and desire, the one that is as much hers as all men's; and she who, living with us, yet consented not to have our faults any longer, felt life itself, as a wall, heavy and set against her. For willingly she wished to see face to face him whom she loved, but that law written with an iron pen held her back from her desire, which, promulgated by God to Moses, and to men through Moses many ages before, then and now and henceforth remains confirmed by an indelible writing: « Man, he says, shall not see me, and live [n: (Exod. XXXIII)]. »

Lo, says the Virgin, I understand that the Bridegroom [0311D] stands behind our wall, who, however near he draw, wills not to show himself clearly to them that live here; and though he be far perchance from any others, between whom it is agreed that many walls are interposed, lo — that is, hard by — he stands to me, who feel that nothing but one is set against me. But he stands that he may attend me with diligent solicitude, and show himself a ready helper to me that desire to go forth; that he may comfort me while I tarry, reach a hand to me as I strive, console me with things present, and promise better things to me as I follow after. Lastly, when Stephen endures the stones and the reproaches of the Jews, grieving not so much for himself as suffering with the malice of the lost [n: (Act. VII)], urgent in prayer he was rapt into a sweet ecstasy, and sees Jesus standing — not granted to the wretched stoners. Jesus indeed was standing, that he might give strength to him that wrestled, hope [0312A] of victory to him that was to die; reaching a hand to him that trusted, that he might loose the wall for him that was to go forth, receive him going forth, and snatch him received from the turbulent conversation of the world, he thenceforth sitting down in the beauty of peace, to sit in an opulent rest. So, while the Virgin is held by the wall of the present life alone, the Bridegroom is said to stand as it were outside, behind that wall, that he may exhort her who is to go forth to stand meanwhile upon her own watch, and that she, standing and constant with manly fortitude, may await him as the keeper of her going forth. But meanwhile, while the Virgin endures delay, set within this wall, desiring indeed to be dissolved but not yet more fully loosed, the Bridegroom is recorded to look in through the windows and to look forth through the lattices; bearing care of the Virgin, he consoles her by the regard of his grace. Lest the house [0312B] be ill-blind and its inward part dark, through the windows and lattices a gracious light is poured within. And windows and lattices seem to me to differ from one another in this, that windows are stretched out lengthwise, while lattices show a round form like an eye. Through the oblong windows therefore the Bridegroom is then seen to look within, when the Bride's sojourning, wherein she is somewhat long detained within the wall, he renders luminous by a long-suffering hope and a constant *178* patience, without the grace of which light nothing is more worthy of hatred, nothing more wearisome. David, oppressed with weariness: « Alas, he says, that my sojourning is prolonged [n: (Psal. CXIX)]. » Filled with joyful light: « Thou art, he says, my patience, O Lord; my hope from my youth [0312C] [n: (Psal. LXX)]. » But through the lattices the Bridegroom looks within, when by his unconfused grace the Virgin more subtly looks about upon her own acts; and finding not among things transitory where she may suffer her foot to rest [n: (Gen. VIII)], she fixes the eyes of her mind upon him whom with a sounder desire she awaits. He looks forth through the lattices, when, putting far from the Bride the affections and motions of the animal soul, he pours into her — or, pouring in, enlightens — spiritual eyes, with which the Virgin, made circumspect, not only cleaves not to things present, regards not greedily the things that shall pass away, but with her pupils made clear sees and loves the more eagerly the things that shall abide without end. Into which eyes the vision of God pours itself, not only cleansing but also preserving cleanness, and through them consequently diffuses itself through the whole building, as it were; whose smooth and round perfection, opened outward with a more august [0312D] bosom and wider within, renders all things uncovered by the light poured in [n: (Ezech. XL et XLI)]. I wonder if the prudent builder of the Church did not look upon this very thing, who that part of her which he understood to be worthier in merits, building it round, willed to name a lattice, wherein he willed the Holy of holies and the altar to be more significantly placed. Which part deserves first to receive the grace of the divine regard, and being first sanctified is not held by the disease of avarice; but with bosom open and filled with the surpassing light of the mysteries, it pours back upon the whole Church everywhere, like an eye, the benefit of the sacrament. Whence there especially tapers or lamps are kindled, which are shown more expressly to signify the grace of the aforesaid light; from which that vision of God is understood to be presented there first, and from them to be spread abroad to the rest, [0313A] as by a scattered light. So he who, standing behind the wall, suffers not himself yet to be seen, loves to behold his beloved through the windows and the lattices; and seeing and not seen, that yet he may not be unknown, he wills his own voice as he speaks to be heard as from an oracle.
