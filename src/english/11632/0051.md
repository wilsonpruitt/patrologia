## CHAPTER VI.

*And my beloved speaketh to me.* To me, I say, who am still held within the wall, even against my will, and, if now not by other things, yet hindered by the stains of the present life; he who, standing behind the wall, awaits me willing and not yet able to go forth, and meanwhile looks within through the windows and the lattices with eyes of mercy, adds also, to the heaping up of the benefit, that he should speak, and with gracious discourse pursue the sweet lines of love. And rightly is speech said to follow sight consequently; [0313B] for her whom we have chanced to see as beloved we straightway wish also to address, and, as the trumpet exhorts them that fight, and the goad or spur the horse let go, so to exhort her by a pleasing word to a greater vigilance of love. The Bridegroom therefore speaks, and rejoices to soothe her with his address; he insinuates his own will to her, and teaches the humble one to obey, to confess herself a handmaid — most of all after hearing Gabriel's salutation — and to hold in memory that saying of Samuel which she knew to have been written long before: « Speak, he says, Lord, for thy servant heareth [n: (I Reg. III)]. » To this handmaid therefore the Lord, nay rather the beloved, speaks, to the Bride that hears; and the Bride, loving and watchful, gives an open ear to him that speaks; he by speaking gives joy and gladness to her hearing [n: (Psal. L)], she is humbled by hearing the Bridegroom's voice the more gratefully. And worthily the more gratefully, because he will not, like an adversary, speak hurtful [0313C] or sad things, but his benevolence will pursue sweet things and useful; because he who loves and is loved rages not, is not angry against his beloved, but reaches out peace to her — though not yet a perfect peace, the wall being as it were interposed. But what peace he reaches out, or what peaceable thing he speaks to her, lest it be long hidden from you, is declared in the following verse.

## CHAPTER VII.

*Arise, make haste, my friend, my dove, my comely one, and come.* Here first it may be objected, why he who a little above warned the maidens not to trouble her, not to raise her up, until she would, as she rested between the left hand and the right, now, as though forgetful of what he had warned, provokes her to rise, and urges also [0313D] that she make haste, as though it were no longer to be deferred. But he who, restraining the daughters of Jerusalem, whose eyes are not opened to this, yet left the Virgin's rising (as you know) to her own will, himself knows far more amply what and when is fitting or expedient for holiness; from whom the Virgin also has it that she prudently spends either leisure or business upon the truth. To whom, when the Bridegroom breathes in, after the quiet of leisure, the will for business; when he stirs up and rouses her charity to the care of her neighbours, he urges her slowness with a sweet goad to a work now necessary, namely that « hearing, she may say: Come [n: (Apoc. VI)], » and, being taught, may teach the truth. What does he say or enjoin to her but, Arise and make haste? That is: as in *179* leisure, so in business obey me speedily. [0314A] Thou, he says, who liest hidden sweetly above the left hand and beneath the right, who within the wall growest glad not vainly at my regard: arise, break off for a little the unfruitful embraces of sweet leisure; go out quickly into the streets and lanes, and admit hither the lame and the weak [n: (Luc. XIV)]. And because thou willest this when I will, and willest not when I will not, I may rightly have called thee my friend, marking thee an enemy to all wills that displease me. With which saying not only the present truth of the Canticles, but also the truthful maxim of the heathen worthily accords: To will the same thing, he says, and not to will the same thing, that at last is firm friendship. And thou art a friend, since thou willest to feel with me in all things, privy to my secrets, and to keep the will of my mind; thou art none the less a dove, since thou aspirest not to the things of the world [0314B] that are present, but for the absence of things eternal groanest inconsolably and sighest. For although thou sometimes willingly exposest thyself to the instructing of thy neighbours, yet thou puttest not after it that desire wherewith thou burnest toward me [n: (Luc. XI)]; and coming to these many things, thou lettest not go that one thing necessary, but only for a little breakest off the leisure of being free and of contemplating. And because thou excellest with a fitting discretion, in both thou drawest to thyself from both praise or merit in all things, and in none of them showest thyself unshapely, but comely — and by the desert of this comeliness mine, that is, not moderately pleasing to me. Thou therefore, *my friend, dove, my comely one; arise, make haste, and come.* Arise from leisure, gird thyself bravely to labour; make haste, obey without delay, lest the neediness of thy neighbours [0314C] find thee too slow; and come — not to go far from me after the footsteps of the flocks, after the flocks of the companions, but labouring with me standing by, without any loss or damage. For lest, when thou hast risen and made haste, thou desist as it were impatient from what thou hast begun, I will be at hand, comforting thee and framing labour in commandment [n: (Psal. XCIII)]; nor will I forsake thee, when thy strength shall have failed, as one set far off: to me, when thou shalt come to me labouring, thou shalt find thyself near [n: (Rom. XIII)]. *Arise,* because it is now the hour for thee to rise from sleep; *make haste,* because God loveth a cheerful giver [n: (II Cor. IX)]; *and come,* because I am with thee, saith the Lord. But that it is the hour, he consequently declares, saying: *Now the winter,* etc.
