## CHAPTER VIII

[0314D]

*For now the winter is past, the rain is over and gone; the flowers have appeared in our land; the time of pruning is come.* While it is winter and the earth is hard-bound with cold frost, while its surface is pressed by the snow poured over it and its face is not truly coloured; while with looser reins the north wind rejoices to rage through the fields of this air, and the south wind appears not, and Favonius defers to come: assuredly the husbandman hastens not to go out to his work and to set forth the plough; he sits at home, is at leisure for himself, rejoices in the fire, and fattens himself with richer feasts. Likewise, while it is rain, the showers flowing down long and unseasonably, and pools or standing waters now occupy the fields lately dry; while from the high mountains [0315A] the torrents cast themselves down with fury, and the rivers exceed their channels and wander far this way and that; while the crops are overwhelmed, houses float, trees are torn up, and the waters of the flood, unchecked, are driven back by no command and by no obstacle: what ploughman would go out to move the oxen to the yoke? What husbandman would set to the vine the hook that shall cut away things hurtful or superfluous? But when a mightier hand harshly rebukes these things and beats them back, and, the air being made clear, a vernal sweetness pours itself upon the lands; when the more grievous importunity of rain and winter is now wiped away, and, the cloud wiped away, their own face has returned to things: then at last the ploughman grows glad, and the mind for working returns; a man goes out to the vineyard, because it is the fitting time of pruning. That is our winter, when we are held by a hard coldness of mind, when [0315B] by ignorance or negligence we are hindered from loving God; when the wicked spirit like the north wind hardens us with a continual blast, and the world set in wickedness conforms us to its own malice. But our rain is when the flux of carnal affections pours itself into our minds, and their nerveless softness diffuses itself everywhere with a billowy onset, and a great inundation is found to exceed measure and to overstep the mark, while it is neither restrained by the channel of discipline nor forestalled by the opposing hand of justice. For then, after the manner of a torrent, passion rages, the filth of vices roars, it drowns and suffocates the mind and renders it ignorant of the commandments; and if any good thing had been sown and conceived in hope or in the blade, the water of the flood snatches it away, the Egyptian river carries it off, a bitter death overwhelms it. Lastly the mind, now lost, stretches itself out [0315C] *180* to the seeking of unlawful things abroad, and draws to its own uses whatever it finds softer and sweeter; it is soothed with the sweetness of foods, with the soft touch of women, and savours nothing but flesh, and whatever thus flatters the wretched flesh. But if the divine grace vouchsafe at some time to have pity on calamities so great, to look with eyes of mercy upon the aforesaid hardness of mind, so that it will that the mind, long pressed by a hurtful frost, be loosed at last by the warmth of the Spirit, and that we be absolved from the desert of death by the benefit of indulgence: then the tenacious and harder winter, oppressive to the wretched until then, its estate being shaken and lost, is now made a thing that passes. It passes, I say, and in its passing greenness and a revived temperateness return; the south wind comes, Favonius breathes, a vernal sweetness follows, and a summer one; there laughs [0315D] the beauty of flowers, promising the fruits that follow; the vine rejoices in the hook that prunes away useless shoots. The same grace blowing, the air is made clear, the great inundation of rains is thinned; the flood is dried up, the river is called back within its bounds; discipline not vainly enjoins frugality upon them that exult and continence upon them that run riot; and, the rheum of vices being dried up, the inward workshop of the mind is graced with virtues. Which being cleansed and made fruitful by an inward gift, not only does the down-flowing rain go away and depart, but a new comeliness of the earth, a certain new face, now comes on; and where the winter has passed and the rain has gone and appears not, there the flower of good works — that is, the hope of a reward to come — now appears. And because the Bridegroom willed to impart such grace to the daughters of Jerusalem, namely that, the rubble [0316A] of a perverse will being torn out, they should now desire to come to an ampler advance, he exhorts the Virgin, rejoicing in leisure, to go to meet them and to succour them, showing at what time and with what preceding fitness this may be worthily and profitably done. Arise, he says, *for now the winter is past* — that hardness, namely, which could for a while hinder the minds of hearers, that they should neither hear the word of God nor, having heard it, love to obey; which cast itself upon them with a heavy hand and a hard command, and made the down-flowing waters, hardened with cold, to stand still; which cast over the earth cold snows as a heavy cloak, and, the burden pressing long, slew the tender sheep. This hardness assuredly was found to have held Peter with a heavy hoof, when he answered that he knew not the man whom he had known [n: (Marc. XIV)]; and a servant or a maid dealt him a buffet of fear, that he might not even [0316B] be able to speak the truth; and lest the disciple should help his master, the slothful man held his hand under his armpit [n: (Prov. XIX)]. You remember, as I think, what the truth of the Gospel affirms: that when Peter was denying and warming himself, it was cold [n: (Joan. XVII)]; and therefore at that time the Virgin is not commanded to rise to speak, but by pitying grace he that denied is first looked upon, that he may weep. For he wept bitterly, feeling himself held by a hard cold [n: (Luc. XXIII)], and that the fire at which he stood was not held fit enough to drive it away; but that same winter having passed by the regard of pitying grace, Mary is sent to Peter to announce the power of him that rose again [n: (Marc. XVI)]. Lastly, do not a great rain, many waters, the malice of the time or of the day, seem to you to have held John [0316C] the son of Zebedee, so that, his spirit as it were suffocated and the rigour of chastity bent, he was hastening to embrace the soft coupling of the flesh, the nuptials of a pleasure that must perish? But most of all a great rain, a great cleanness suffocated him, when Peter, the more confident, thrice denied his master as the passion drew near [n: (Marc. XIV)]; and he none the less, terrified by love of this life at the passion aforesaid, hurried headlong, fled naked, the linen cloth left behind. And truly he is of no slight fault, and swerves and offends not a little, who in the flood of the passions leaves behind the love of heavenly things, cleaner than a linen cloth; wherewith no man can be more honourably, none more usefully clothed, if only Christ, after his own pattern, shall have granted us to be buried in it. « He wrapped him, he says, in a clean [0316D] linen cloth, and laid him in the sepulchre [n: (Matth. XXVII)]. » But love of self and contempt of the linen cloth stripped John as he fled; hatred of the Master, the disease of avarice, the guilt of crime unnerved Judas; all at last the water bore away, the flood injured, guilt drowned — but while the rest escaped by grace, Judas, being lost, emerged not. For he, suffocated by distrust, hanged himself with a noosed throat; John laid hold of the citadel of grace, the summit of seeing, because Christ, suffering on the cross, banished the care of Judas far from his benevolence, but loving John committed the care of him to his mother the Virgin: « Woman, he says, behold thy son [n: (Joan. XIX)]. » After therefore he willed by faith in the rising Christ to remove far off the troubles of winter and of rain, and that a liberating grace should appear more eminently in those whom he had chosen, [0317A] he anxiously invited the Virgin not to be slothful to rise, to make haste, to come, because it is now the time of speaking, when the earth, that is, is not held by the aforesaid rain or winter. Nor only has so great an inclemency gone and departed, but a vernal sweetness, a flowery beauty, a hope of fruits has come on; in our land the flowers of obedience, of patience, of continence now shine forth; yet a little while, and they shall bring forth the desired fruit. It is not therefore to be deferred but that meanwhile the hook and a purging admonition be brought forth, to cut back diligently the springing shoots of negligence [n: (Sap. IV)]; let it scrape away ignorance, instruct faith, prune manners, that with worthy solicitude it may render them readier to bring forth fruit. For when the time of vintage, the day of fruits, shall dawn, there shall be no more pruning; [0317B] both the basket and the mattock shall rest — which, the winter being run out, now more fittingly present themselves, when a new greenness springs. flowers of many kinds show themselves in the earth. *181* *The flowers,* he says, *have appeared in our land, the time of pruning is come.* And he adds: the voice, etc.
