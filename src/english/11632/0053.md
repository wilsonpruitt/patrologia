## CHAPTER IX

*The voice of the turtle is heard in our land, the fig tree hath put forth her green figs: the vineyards in flower have yielded their sweet smell.* The voice of the turtle is not raised, according to musicians, among the high notes, nor is it pleasant to their ears; it sings not over-sweetly, as, according to a certain heathen, the nightingale does; but it dwells among the low notes, mournful with a certain suppressed groan, unwelcome to them that rejoice, [0317C] gracious to them that would mourn. Such a voice that querulous man seems to imitate who harshly complains that his sojourning on earth is prolonged; who, cleaving not to things present and refusing to be consoled in them, groaning for the absence of things to come, ceases not to lament. « Woe is me, he says, that my sojourning is prolonged [n: (Psal. CXIX)]. » And that other: « Weeping she hath wept in the night, and her tears are on her cheeks: there is none to comfort her [n: (Thren. I)]. » If you remember, this voice was heard in the disciples, when — their tender love, their hope provoked by a deferred desire — they dispute among themselves, and sigh, and complain after the manner of a turtle, and among the complainers much doubt and various discourses are exchanged. « What are these discourses, says he, that you hold one with another as you walk, and are sad? *(Luc. [0317D] XXIV.)* » And they: « We hoped, they say, that it was he that should have redeemed Israel [n: (ibid.)]. » When therefore the voice of this turtle is now heard in the land, rightly is the virgin's solicitude summoned to the advancing of the faith, that she may know that her preaching is not found empty nor ineffectual, which is thus forerun by so devout a groan, by so groaning a spirit. Lastly, the turtle loves to dwell in the mountains and in the more secret places; it rejoices not in the assembly of birds, nor in the public place, nor in the throng of the people; holding the form of that spiritual wisdom which neither the wandering multitude of the market-place nor the animal man perceives. Of which Job: « Wisdom, he says, is drawn out of secret places [n: (Job XXVIII)]. » And David: « The uncertain and hidden things of thy wisdom, he says, thou hast made manifest to me [n: (Psal. L)]. » And Paul: « We speak, he says, the wisdom of God [0318A] in a mystery, which is hidden [n: (I Cor. II)]. » Paul, who knew himself a debtor to the wise as also to the foolish, speaking among the perfect draws wisdom out of secret places; and he whom the dullness of the men of the market-place, or a dull ignorance, troubles not, manifests the uncertain and hidden things of wisdom to them that likewise have ears. It is therefore the voice of the turtle which, hiding from the darkened crowds the hidden or uncertain things of wisdom, renders them uncovered to the eyes or ears of very few indeed; which, veiling the secret from the multitude — the secret of Isaias, the prophecy of Elisabeth, the oracle of Zachary — whispers sweetly to those only who, having received the Spirit, hasten to the hill country, whose ears of hearing the crafty asp stops not. Lastly, that woman seemed to utter the voice of the turtle who rejoiced that she had seen the Lord [0318B] when he rose from the dead; and him whom she alone had alone seen, her desire now fulfilled, she announced not to the crowds in the street but to the apostles in secret: « Mary Magdalen came, he says, telling the disciples: I have seen the Lord, and these things he said to me [n: (Joan. XX)]. » But Mark: « Jesus rising, he says, early the first day of the week, appeared first to Mary Magdalen; and she going told them that had been with him [n: (Marc. XVI)]. » Those disciples also tarry in the secret of excellence after the manner of a turtle, who in the absence of Thomas Didymus rejoiced to see the Lord; and when they tell it to him on his return, thinking that he would likewise rejoice at such news, he, distrusting, asserts that he will not believe unless he shall have seen. Nor is it repugnant if you will to declare the same Lord also a turtle, [0318C] who, rising from the dead, willed to appear both to the women and to the disciples; whom Galilee, altogether hidden from the crowds that rejoice in the forum or the street, offered in secret to be seen by none but his own. Whose turtle's voice I deem beyond doubt to be that whereby he made himself known to his own as being at hand, that they might believe; which, removed further from the hardened hearing of the malignant, he brought only to those whom he found held by the begun affection of them that love. « Peace be to you, he says, it is I [n: (Luc. XXIV)]. » Good is that voice of the turtle, a voice from the high mountains, a secret voice, which the roaring crowd, accustomed to the suits and tumults of the forum, does not grasp; which none know but a few, whose ear worthily hears, whose eye is not darkened by obstinacy, whose vigorous understanding [0318D] more anxiously searches out the secrets of wisdom. Of which voice it is written: « His voice shall not be heard in the streets [n: (Isa. XLII)]. » [var: Vulg. Isa. 42:2 *nec audietur vox ejus foris*, "neither shall his voice be heard abroad"; *in plateis* is the wording of Matt. 12:19] Which voice assuredly, after it sounded to the [sic: *torrae*] and was heard by the disciples, the earth, hindered before by a hard cold, opened glad bosoms, growing green with a new temperateness, so that it is now worthy to be called ours — which, cursed in the first man, deserved to be blessed when the second rose again. For her whom of old the Lord strictly forbade to sin, and whom, having transgressed the prohibition, he more harshly cursed in wrath and fury: the same, rising from the dead, he blessed with his own regard, with his own grace, and, renewed by his gifts, called ours by the desert of newness. *182*
