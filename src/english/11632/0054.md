## CHAPTER X.

*The voice of the turtle is heard in our land.* She who once, [0319A] when she sinned, made herself over to another's dominion and usurped for herself a new God and another possessor, after she has been breathed upon by a new spirit and is made free of the wanderings aforesaid, is rightly called ours — the common possession, that is, of them both. For those whose spirit is one, whose flesh is one, must be said to have nothing thenceforth that is not common; but what is the bride's the bridegroom claims to himself fittingly enough, since he wills to hold even what is his own not as proper to himself but to share it with her, that they may rejoice to have all things together in common. « Our Father who is in heaven [n: (Matth. VI)]. » [var: Vulg. *qui es in caelis*, second person, "who art in heaven"] If, I say, I call the Father who is in heaven not properly mine but in common ours, rightly, O bride, do I call the land also not thine but rather ours; because, as by gift [0319B] I gave thee to be made partaker of my things, so by the giver's bounty — mine rather than thine — am I partaker of thine. Otherwise, if the land were held to be thine only and not rather ours, perhaps the voice of the turtle aforesaid would not be heard in it, and the fig tree would not put forth those green figs of hers which she put forth when the turtle was heard; and, these not being put forth, she would not bring on after them the other fruits of the later season. Almost all trees, of whatever differing kind or shape, have this as it were proper to themselves, have this implanted in them by nature: that a tree which is to put forth its fruits in the ripe season rejoices first in a certain passing offspring of blossoms. Whereof it is written: « In the morning let it flourish and pass away [n: (Psal. LXXXIX)]. » But when the tree has been widowed of its passing flower, by nature's working it is made fruitful by the access of fruits that follows; and whatever it grieved for before, [0319C] whatever loss it bore when stripped by the falling of the flower, it makes good with a new joy and exchanges for a better abundance. But the fig tree is found unlike the rest in this, nor is the plenty of fruits in her forerun by the grace of a flower; but while others are in blossom she is said to put forth her first fruits, transitory ones, as others succeed them in the course of time. So indeed the fig tree becomes fruitful by a manner unusual to all, rich with no foregoing flower in a first plenty of fruits and in a second; of which the green figs that come first are not of that grace, not of that savour, which those have that follow — the later they are in season, the better beyond doubt in juice. Yet sometimes among those green figs which are said to forerun the rest it is possible to find some, though very few, [0319D] which both go before and yet yield not place or time to those that are to succeed, but, while the others fall, stand longer and remain with those that are to come. For a green fig is sometimes found to be born between two little branches, and is guarded as it were by the careful standing-about of its neighbours; and, resting in the angle and drawing juice and nourishment to itself from this side and that, not only does it not fall, but is brought to a fuller yield than the rest. For neither are its coevals that fall accounted its equals in grace, nor do the last that come after deserve beyond doubt to be matched with it; but it deserves to be set before both these and those in scent and in savour, fit for the food of nobles, worthy to be set on no table unless a king's. Not unfittingly can the fig tree be named the Jewish people, which, when it heard Christ commend [0320A] heavenly things and teach that earthly things are not to be loved, being steeped in the sacred letters straightway put forth men that would believe, but that would not abide in the word of faith with any steadfastness of works. Who, brought forth in haste and taking on an appearance of godliness, but denying the power of it and lacking the weight of truth, fell before their time at the first blast of the wind, or, while the wind was not yet blowing, dropped off heavy with their own weight. And perhaps such men are not undeservedly called green figs, who by a sense grosser than their body wickedly despise things hidden and subtle; who, according to Paul's [n: (Ephes. IV)] word, having their understanding darkened by grossness, do indeed believe for a time, but in the time of temptation fall away into failure. Gross enough — nay, more than enough — I judge those to have been who are read to have stood by [0320B] Christ as he spoke in the Gospel; and when he discoursed mystically of the eating of his flesh and the drinking of his blood, and affirmed that none had eternal life in themselves save those who should eat, they, from whose sides an over-gross fat seemed to hang, not taking in the mystery, were found to shrink from the mystic word. « This is a hard saying, say they, who can hear it? » [n: (Joan. VI.)] When by the hardness of this saying those rude ones are wretchedly shaken, and, not solid in the truth, endure the blast of the north wind, bearing ill a plain stumbling-block in a somewhat obscure word, they plainly fall, like green figs, before their time. « From this time, he says, many went back; and now they walked not with him [n: (Joan. VI)]. » But when these had withdrawn, do not think that of that same people [0320C] there were thereafter none who would hear Christ. and be present among his hearers; for he sent before him afterwards seventy-two others into every place whither he knew he was himself to come, and by taking these out of that people he did not permit the fig tree to remain wholly barren. Lastly Judas the traitor, who for a while followed the Master among the apostles and at the last pursued him even to betrayal and to an incurable hatred, when he found himself fallen into the deep like a green fig by his own weight, bearing himself back on high far worse, hanged himself with a noose. But lest the Apostle's place should long *183* be held empty, and by one man's fall the due perfection of the consecrated number be defrauded, Matthias, as you have read, was chosen an apostle by the casting of the lot, and so neither [0320D] did the fig tree stand empty nor the number of twelve imperfect. But if this seems to any hearers small and insufficient, if a careful reader thinks that something here holds a scruple in it, let him betake himself to those of that people who afterwards believed at Peter's word, and who, now three thousand and now five thousand, filled up the number of them that fell away. Of whom there is no doubt but that, the later they are in time, the more worthily they ought to be called better than Judas and the apostates aforesaid; and that their evening succession and conversion pleased Christ far more than the morning withdrawal and headlong fall of the others. But those green figs which, when the rest were falling, remained — very few indeed — and, not slipping away with them that slipped, endured even with them that followed after, may rightly be understood of the apostles, who are chosen by Christ among [0321A] the first and perhaps first of all, and are driven from him neither by entreaty, nor by price, nor by the violence of threats. And when the good Master set himself to question them more closely, whether they too would go when the others were going back, Peter, the readier to speak, hid not what he purposed, but before the rest (as their first and chief) answered thus: « Lord, to whom shall we go? Thou hast the words of eternal life: and we have believed and have known that thou art Christ the Son of God [n: (Joan. VI)]. » Peter therefore lies hid between two little branches, that is, between the Law and the Gospel, and, taking nourishment to himself from this side and that, grows strong to persevere bravely; resting on the border of both, he leans sweetly upon either, and, carefully supported by the one, turns not away at all from the other. For he understands [0321B] that the old law prefigures the new by signs and riddles; that the new makes the old understood and does not darken it; that the old does not prejudge the new but runs before it; that the new is not against the old but runs to meet it. The lawgiver and the giver of the Gospel seem to Peter not to be at odds one with the other, since the law foretells Christ, and Christ wills not to destroy the law but to fulfil it; and in him, as in a corner, the old is joined to the new law by a certain fitness, upon which Peter, firmly leaning, is guarded by a double defence. Whereof he himself says: « We have the more firm prophetical word, whereunto you do well to attend, as to a lamp shining in a dark [ *alias* caliginoso] place [n: (II Petr. I)]. » This of the old. What of the new? « Do penance, says he, and be every one of you baptized in the name of Jesus Christ [n: (Act. II)]. » [0321C] To Peter therefore and to his fellow-apostles, who first believed in Christ, and who, when some withdrew, remained with him in his temptations, that same Christ willed to impart so great a ripeness of manners and so great a sweetness of grace that it is certain they go before both their coevals and those that came after in the merit of their living. To this order no order, to an apostle no other man could compare himself, nor — as some judge — could any, earlier or later, set himself before them in life and merits; but they become so sweet in scent, and by the ripeness of their works so ripe, that both present and future men might worthily enough yield to them. And since the fig tree has put forth such as these — not so much to be preferred to the evil as assuredly better than the good, greater after the manner of mountains, grosser in the abundance of their merits — rightly is the Bride [0321D] invited not to put off the visiting of such any longer, but sometimes to set the business of her neighbours before her own special leisure. The Bridegroom wills that the Virgin, absent too long, be not burdensome to the apostles who are as yet maidens, but that, graciously speaking to them, she confer manifest things upon their hidden ones; that she strengthen their faith, instruct their rude understanding, advance their beginning affections and lead them to perfection. He wills, I say, that the Virgin labour with watchful solicitude, that the rude apostles may be able to lay their head each under his own vine and under his own fig tree; and that, while the present iniquity passes by and the day dawns more fully upon them, each may rest meanwhile under the mighty [0322A] hand of Christ, under the shadow of the Holy Spirit. And that the Virgin's care may labour patiently upon those same maidens and may hope for fruit near at hand if she persevere diligently: the flowering vineyards, he says, have already yielded their scent; they that have already flowered in the first season will give autumn grapes. By the vineyards I judge are meant the souls of individual men now converted to the faith, in whom the calling of God who has mercy works the grace of merits, and which, growing green by an infused mildness, now plainly put forth their flower. The flower, not yet rich in fruit, gives meanwhile its scent. And as the flower by its presence renders the vineyard's place pleasant, so the flower's scent makes that same place cleaner and serene, while it suffers neither serpent nor creeping thing to remain in it, nor venom, but by a certain undivided force renders it [0322B] a stranger to all these. A good flower, I would say, and a good scent that, wherewith a recent conversion so adorns the soul that first it drives far off whatever inwardly dishonours her; so that at her first fruits the beholder's eye worthily [sic: *hila rescat*], and venomous envy, not enduring her good scent, takes fright. The wicked will not, or cannot, look with an even mind upon the good, and a juster place cannot be possessed by two masters; but when the fresh flower and the comely scent pour themselves out with a large bounty, guilt flees, the poison is shut out, iniquity hides itself afar off. Lastly, scent and stench are harshly opposed one to the other, and agree not well, nor do they abide in one lodging; but when virtue worthily flowers and the report of it sweetly grows strong, vice is brought to nothing, and whatever is evilly foul vanishes away. When Simon [0322C] Peter, flowering with a summer *184* grace, gave forth his scent far and wide, and with a large munificence published the new rule of having nothing of one's own, some who wished to be fed with that good scent in a clean conscience sold their possessions and laid the prices of them at the apostle's feet [n: (Act. V)]. But Ananias and Sapphira, who laboured with a venomous fraud and a lost mind, who burned with the disease of avarice as with inward fevers, being unwilling to aspire freely to the height of apostolic poverty, were forced at the scent of this word wretchedly to expire. So Peter, so the brethren who had come together, shine forth with a new flower, that, shining before men, they may fill the whole house with the scent; and so great a strictness is found to prevail in the Church that neither [0322D] will nor can Simon Magus endure Peter, nor Nicolas Stephen. Good vineyards indeed, which flowered so at that time; very happy souls, which put forth so prevailing a scent, in whose fellowship the serpent of envy and a poisoning fraud abide not, and the stinking malignity of the lost withstands not the health-bearing scent. « They were not able, he says, to resist the wisdom and the Spirit that spoke [n: (Act. VI)]. » But our vineyards now, since the days are evil, grow dull in no small measure; virtue is darker, love feeble, merits languish the more negligently; within the cloisters of our [0323A] conscience a disease creeps on that thrives to our hurt, and it discolours the flowers above while it plucks us at the tender root. For us therefore the holy Virgin tarries all too long, as I see; for us she sleeps, is at leisure for herself, delights in her own rest; lying upon the Bridegroom's left hand she gazes upon his right, forgetting us perhaps, and rejoices to take care of herself alone. Who shall rouse her? Who shall say to her: « Arise, why sleepest thou? » [n: (Psal. XLIII.)] For because languor and sloth have loosed us from labour and watching, and the enemy, beholding us, has now mocked our torpor and our sabbaths, the Virgin has not revisited us who are full of leaves but thin in flower and scent, and long since, our faults requiring it, she has ceased to revisit us. But if she shall see our flower flower again, and shall perceive the scent, she will not refuse to rise and to spend a more gracious diligence upon us; [0323B] and she who was found mercifully to succour her own maidens will deign to impart a fuller mercy to us who are far more wretched. For she is not wanting to those to whom she shall see that her Bridegroom also is not wanting, to whom she shall know his present grace so to be at hand that they both lack not the scent and will not deny that same scent to their neighbours, but give freely to the rest what was freely given to themselves. For those who, not having virtue, take to themselves the appearance of virtue, who study not to obtain the grace of living well but presume upon it, bestow a scent not their own but rather another's, while they contrive to give to others the form of a virtue presumed rather than received. But those souls that rejoice that they truly have the gift of some virtue, and will not have it hidden under a bushel but appear to their neighbours' eyes, [0323C] if from it they hunt after their own glory as displayers rather than the glory of the beholders, are more truly said not to give their scent but to sell it. Passing over in silence, therefore, those who are convicted of giving another's scent or of selling their own, and making mention of those who are known to give their own scent like flowering vineyards, he admonishes the Virgin to rise to them and to hasten with a watchful care; and lest, being admonished, she delay, he loves to repeat this very thing.
