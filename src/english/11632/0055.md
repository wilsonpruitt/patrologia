## CHAPTER XI.

*Arise,* he says, *my friend, my Bride, and come, my dove, in the clefts of the rock, in the hollows of the dry-stone wall.* [var: Vulg. *speciosa mea*, "my beautiful one", in the slot that here names the Bride] [var: Vulg. *in caverna maceriae*, singular] It suffices not for a lover to admonish once her whom he loves, but he repeats it and presses it home, willing that she be not sluggish to fulfil what he admonishes; and, that he may the more move to willingness her who knows nothing of the body, he studies to soothe her [0323D] with caresses and with caressing names. Her, therefore, whom he named friend, dove, comely one when he said *Arise* the first time, he named none the less friend and dove when he said *arise* a second time — inserting the name of Bride, which I do not recall that he named anywhere above, though he is found to have called her otherwise often enough. Because to the Bride, who loves leisure and to cleave to the Bridegroom's embraces, nothing is harder than to turn the mind's edge back from him to public acts, therefore, when the Bridegroom admonishes her, and repeats it, that she be drawn a little away from what she loves the more, he casts upon her that name by which he knows her to be the more held to obedience. For the Bridegroom and the Bride seem to be bound by a certain sacred or consecrated mystery in such a way, and he to set her a bound which it is not lawful to pass, [0324A] namely that the Bridegroom shall bear the care, shall command as the powerful one, shall write the law; and the bride shall lend her ear, shall [sic: *oltemperet*] to his words, shall love his command and subscribe to it. When therefore the Virgin hears herself called bride the more insistently, remembering not so much the burden as the honour by which she loves to be bound to him that calls her, she understands that she ought not to withstand the Bridegroom even in a little thing, but always to reckon his good pleasure the merit of her own salvation. For he does not desert her when he invites her to give an example abroad to others, when he suggests that she interrupt for an hour her pleasant leisure of contemplating; he does not envy her advancement when he wills her work to be seen by those who stand about, but he wills them to profit who shall have loved to behold her good works. *185* For he wills her holiness to be so set forth to them that behold [0324B] that she may rejoice, being set forth, not to be exposed to winds and whirlwinds; he wills her so to go forth into public and to be shown to the rest, even against her will, that she may nevertheless feel herself fortified by the watchful care of him that commands. This perhaps is what he says: *My dove, in the clefts of the rock, in the hollows of the dry-stone wall.* I will, he says, that the Bride be sometimes shown to the maidens, that they may be able and may rejoice to be strengthened by a needful example; but that she be none the less kept within the clefts of the rock, and that the hawk's onset and hostile ambushes be barred far away. The rock (it is well enough known) is called Christ in figure, which at the touch of Moses, at the striking of the mystic rod, yields a fountain, pours out water, and gives streams with a large bounty: the people, ill parched with thirst, drink, and their beasts. The clefts of which rock are called Christ's [0324C] wounds, his passion, his death; faith in these and love of these bestow a health-bearing defence, and in the mindful diligence of them, or the diligent memory of them, whosoever shall lie hid in hope and in silence shall neither be crushed by things present nor dread the troubles to come. Nor do I think it absurd if the law and grace, mercy and truth, justice and peace, and any like things be named Christ's clefts, which, opened and found out, guard us from the onset of them that work malice: in which, that is, if any man shall have lain hid in a worthy understanding of them, they both carry him up to profit and free him from hurtful failing. But a dry-stone wall is called a plain heap of stones which, stretched out after the manner of a hedge, is not bound with mortar between, but, compacted by a slight work for the keeping [0324D] of vineyards, hedges them in and frees them from the harmful onset of beasts. And this wall may be called that fellowship of angels which was long ago ordained by the Creator's command for the keeping of the elect; which, to stand the more firmly, needs no binding of law nor of Gospel, and for which the squaring of its first condition, confirmed by grace, suffices. Whose hollows and inward little dwellings, as it were, or recesses — which whosoever has worthily entered the vineyards finds standing open — are the care and the love which the angels are seen to show to men, whom, being like doves, they will to keep from malignant onsets. To the Virgin therefore, who is invited to revisit the flowering vineyards, that, putting off her leisure for an hour, she may bestow the form of manners upon them that behold her, he himself who invites promises the clefts of life and [0325A] the hollows of the wall, that the dove, relying on such a defence, may with confidence fulfil what is commanded. Thou, he says, whose devotion of mind burns to be at leisure and to see, arise and hasten, and let no fear hold thee back; because, although thy mind, gaping after secrecy, as it were hates the public, yet there is no cause for thee to fear, whom the rock now guards in its own clefts. A good rock, which is opened by the careful hand of them that dig, which when opened imparts a health-bearing defence to the digger, which the Bride too deserved to open, not with a mattock but with desire, and to find in it not a deceitful confidence but a faithful keeping. She indeed, impatient of degenerate labour, disdained to dig the earth, and, strong in a praiseworthy pride, sought not to be plunged in muddy desires, as did that wicked servant who, having received his Lord's talent and being over-anxious [0325B] about the loss of it, dug the ground and covered it over, and would not return it with usury. « He went his way, he says, and digged in the earth, and hid his lord's money [n: (Matth. XXV)]; » and below: « Being afraid, he says, I went and hid thy talent in the earth: behold, thou hast that which is thine [n: (ibid.)]. » Far happier had this man been if he had not given his pains to this labour, if he had not with soft negligence dug the muddy earth, if he had answered his greedy lord more graciously with a doubled interest, and had so deserved to be hidden in the rock and not to be cast into the exterior darkness: « The unprofitable servant, he says, cast ye out into the exterior darkness [n: (Matth. XXV)]. » Lastly, they who digged the hands and feet of Christ and pierced his side with a lance, given over to nothing but the earth, came not to the solid rock; cleaving to softer things, plunged the more negligently into the deep, not knowing the first [0325C] commandment, they held not the second that is like it. The Virgin therefore, although she knew most perfectly the Word made flesh from the womb, yet knows his excellence no longer according to the flesh; but she is exalted upon the rock, sets her foot upon the solid rock, and, a dove nesting in the clefts of the rock, orders and disposes her young. Set upon the rock, or rather in the clefts of the rock, she avoids the ravening onsets and remains safe from ambushes; standing in the mouth of the cleft she rejoices to behold the flowering vineyards, and, that they may profit the more, she endures to be seen by them. Worthily therefore are the hollows of the dry-stone wall not closed to that same Virgin, but are spread the wider with an open recess to receive her; and they suffer her not, while she provides for the vineyards, to labour under the open sky in heat and rain, [0325D] but the angels standing about omit not to take care of her. Lastly, from of old there have stood open hollows in this wall, from the time when the angels fell thence in a ruinous fall; which hollows the prudent architect willed not to patch up out of those same stones, but willed that in the room of them that fell men should enter in by grace and by merits. And since the broken wall waits to be repaired by men, it loves to be opened to none more than to the Bride; and it puts forth to her all its approaches, all its openings, that the virgin may freely enter in by whichever she shall see will profit her. To her, thus having refuge in the clefts of the rock and in the hollows of the dry-stone wall, and, fortified by this defence, now fearing no onsets of them that work malice, the Bridegroom, adding to speak, [0326A] wills that her face be shown to him — curious perhaps to know whether anything reprehensible can be found in her. *186*
