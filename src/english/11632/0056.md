## CHAPTER XII.

*Show me thy face, let thy voice sound in my ears: for thy voice is sweet, and thy face comely.* After the Virgin, and dove, is said to be in the hollows of the dry-stone wall and in the clefts of the rock — not so much groaning, I judge, as glad with a safe defence — she is admonished to offer her face, nourished by so great benefits, to be seen by the Bridegroom, desired for the merit of its comeliness. For the Bridegroom judges that nothing now remains in her which, when offered, ought to displease the beholder, since with a mind secure her face grows cheerful as at a continual banquet, and her inward purity shines out the more manifestly [0326B] upon the surface of it. Whereat Solomon hints: « A joyful mind, he says, maketh a flourishing age: a sorrowful spirit drieth up the bones [n: (Prov. XVII)]. » When therefore the mind languishes with sadness and the soul sickens, the bones are consequently dried and the face wastes away; conscious of its own deformity it blushes to go out in public, and, though it perhaps desires it, dreads to be seen by him that loves it. But the Virgin — whose foulness, if any there was before; whose mind now rejoices; the beauty of whose conscience now flowers — when she has heard the Bridegroom's voice will not draw over herself the cloak of a degenerate shamefastness, but will show her form, which indeed shines out the more in the face. Adam, conscious of his sin, withdrew straightway into sad darkness, and, deformed with a sad face, came not forth to meet God who called him, knowing that the stains of filth [0326C] which sad guilt had brought upon his face would not please God, who is read to be a lover of beauty. But the Virgin's face, which the Bridegroom so anxiously wills to be shown to him, knowing that she is not foul nor to be reproved for a sordid foulness — her face is her conscience, strong with a certain singular grace, to which no woman can be preferred, nor indeed can any other be made her equal. For she both seeks leisure the more ardently, and, when it is found, knows how to rejoice in it more sweetly than the rest, and bears the business laid upon her the more soundly the more patiently she bears it; she knows not how to halt now on this path and now on that, but so walks that she runs to the commandment, not overstepping the bounds set for her. A conscience of such a kind shows itself in nothing save to the Bridegroom, and to him confidently; at the voice of him that commands it regards nothing as its own; when a difficulty [0326D] is set before it, it holds out no cloak of excuse; but whatever he commands, this conscience lays hold of with outstretched hands. And the Bridegroom expressly demands that this face be shown to him, because he recognizes neither the Virgin nor any other man save by the face; and the purer he shall see it, its soot wiped away, the more he rejoices to have a fuller knowledge of her. Because it is given thee to excel in manifold goods, I will that from the perceiving of these present things thou have such confidence that thou fear not to appear to me face to face, and to exhibit thy conscience conspicuous to my gaze. And when it shall show itself to me the more securely the more purely it does so, consequently thy voice also shall sound [0327A] in my ears; because, thy face being presented, thou dost rightly close thy mouth no longer, but thanksgiving and the voice of praise break forth confidently. For when the face has been seen, so serene by the privilege of grace, the pleasant voice no longer holds itself within the cloisters of silence; but as the face is worthily to be gazed on and comely to the sight, so the voice is rendered sweet and sonorous to the hearing. For she who by the desert of her imperfection still either fears the Bridegroom or offends him, and therefore shows not a secure face to him that asks it, or perhaps refuses it, her voice as yet breaks not forth sufficiently harmonious in his ears; and the averted face interrupts the sound of it, lest it grow sweet. The Bridegroom therefore wills the Virgin's voice to sound in his ears — a voice which an exceeding sweetness is found to commend, and which, sweet and delightful in the concord of a sweeter sound, sounds among the voices of women [0327B] as the swan's voice among the clamour of geese. No woman indeed so puts forth her hands to the commandments, nor consequently is any woman's exultation or confession so acceptable; none's face shines with so great a grace or comeliness, and therefore none's voice resounds with a sweeter melody. *Thy voice,* he says, *is sweet, and thy face comely.* Therefore, he says, my ear wills to hear thy voice with open bosom, because that face is found to soothe this ear with a concordant sound; and of her whose seen face pleases by the merit of so great a beauty, the voice, concordant with the face already seen, heaps up grace. A good order, that first the face of the inward conscience be adorned, and so the voice, worthily sounding, be preached as commendable; because the praise or the teaching of one whose face, for its foulness, does not yet deserve to be shown, is not seen to sound sweetly enough. And perhaps anyone's [0327C] face has in silence a certain grace and is praised, when, namely, he lives justly and neither has he opportunity nor does necessity require that he speak; when he presents himself more secretly to God with a clean face of the heart, and does not display this very thing outwardly by the proclamation of a voice. But a clamorous voice never profits, and sometimes hinders, if a beautiful face has not gone before the voice; because praise in the mouth of a sinner is not, as we read, precious, and the declaring of justice, joined with a hatred of discipline, is none the less hateful [n: (Eccli. XV)]. But the Virgin, who is led to a greater advancement than the rest, whom the Bridegroom and Son willed to be led to perfection, both flowered with a comely face, advancing into a wondrous virtue, and sounded forth in teaching and in example, worthily *187* unto the salvation of them that heard. Whose inward face desires to be shown to us today also, [0327D] while she loves us, while she admonishes us by example, speaks to us, cries out to us; in both she is zealous, takes counsel for us, favours us, and, no longer anxious for herself, trembles for us, fears for us. But if our eye grows dim, and our ear (like the asps') grows deaf, and if her face is hidden from us and her voice grows hoarse, none the less is the Virgin's comeliness believed to have flowered in the Bridegroom's eyes both now and then, and her praising and exulting voice to have sounded sweetly in those same ears. Therefore perhaps he says expressly: *Show me thy face; let thy voice sound in my ears.* Though, he says, thou show not thy face to them that would see it, though thou bestow not the benefit of thy voice on them that stop their ears, to me [0328A] nevertheless thy face also is shown, who cannot but see my own gifts, and thy voice sounds in my ears, to which I judge it worthy to lend them. But that the Virgin's presence might not be idle to them that stood about, but might become fruitful to the maidens that worthily beheld her, the Bridegroom willed that whatever was hurtful be removed from them, that they might deserve to behold her more fruitfully when she revisited them. Therefore he adds: *Catch us,* etc.
