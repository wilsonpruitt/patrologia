## CHAPTER XIII.

*Catch us the little foxes that destroy the vines; for our vineyard hath flourished.* Whom, save his own dogs, does the Bridegroom seem to invite to catch the little foxes, lest those seducing little beasts dare to lurk within the vineyards, which in the dogs' absence they are found to gnaw, to hurt, to destroy — so that it is needful that the dogs [0328B] be found careful to catch them? Those therefore whom the Bridegroom has already set over the keeping of the vines, whom, being set over the churches, he has made skilled in hunting whatever is hurtful, he admonishes to be watchful, and with a keen search to pursue the deceitful little beasts, to press on and not to desist until they catch what they hunt. Of such by Jeremias: « I will send, he says, many hunters, and they shall hunt them [n: (Jer. XVI)]. » And in the psalm: « The tongue of thy dogs [n: (Psal. LXVII)]. » These dogs, to whom the Bridegroom willed to join the care of us, stand by us as we begin, and ward off loss or damage; they press on carefully, that the evils that threaten us may cease at their very rising, before they grow the harder [0328C] upon our negligence or our ignorance. For sometimes our present manner of life burdens us with more secret faults, whose soft seduction thrusts itself in privily after the manner of a fox; and, running craftily through blind windings, it wills to be unknown, and to destroy us, ill provided as we are, the more freely the more hiddenly. And when our elders meet us with their teaching, instruct the ignorant, reprove the wandering with discipline; when they show whence temptation comes and whither it proceeds, what destruction, or what death at last, follows upon which suggestion; when they rebuke fresh diseases with a censorious tongue: then, like provident dogs, they catch the little foxes in us. Whence they also who are bidden to do this the more carefully and do not care, who, slothful to hunt, shape their negligence to sleep, are called by Isaias — as by another dog — « dumb dogs [n: (Isa. LI)], » [0328D] sleeping and loving dreams, set in the place of watchmen. And indeed that same care lies upon us also far more heavily: that we should study to be found watchful in the same catching, and should pursue the foxes within our very selves with a careful search, until we prudently reach their lurking-places by a hunter's scent. And when we have come thither, we ought, according to the poet, to crush the evil seeds of the disease while they are new; and, according to the Prophet [n: (Psal. CXXXVI)], to dash our little ones against the rock; lest, either by ignorance drawing a veil or by negligence drawing us away, we be seduced into a byway of error, and be led into deadly falls and into the snares of temptations. For sometimes a crafty temptation feigns itself either nothing at all or a very little thing, and so flatters a negligent mind [0329A] with a fraudulent enticement that, while it annuls or makes light of what is felt beforehand in secret with concupiscence, it afterwards causes to be despised the actual consent given to it. And when the seduced mind attends not with a sufficiently diligent eye how the canker creeps, what the wickedness conceived intends, the very destruction of the vineyard plainly shows that the fox, little before, which brought itself in secretly, has done no small hurt. Such little foxes therefore, which thrust themselves fraudulently into our minds, we are bidden to watch, to hunt, to catch diligently; that what at first lay hid as in a chamber, decently under a cloak, may break out and come forth, and rejoice at last to be shown shamelessly. « Some, as it is written, are glad when they have done evil, and rejoice in most wicked things [n: (Prov. II)]. » [0329B] But some say (if indeed your diligence has committed to memory what it heard) that this present little book, which Solomon, as was said before, set in order after the manner of a drama, contains in itself the words of no more than four persons, as it were, who speak of nuptial love and of the spiritual chastity of the marriage. For the Bride and the maidens, the Bridegroom and the Bridegroom's companions are brought in as four persons, who for the weaving of the drama are led forth as from the wings into the midst, that the speech may pass from these to those in turn, *188* and that by an alternating speech the epithalamial drama may be satisfied. The Bridegroom therefore, who above spoke now to the Bride, now to the maidens, and with gracious diligence pursued what befits the Bride, seems here to turn his speech now to his companions, [0329C] that he may exhort them, as they attend the nuptials, to the catching of the foxes. And they say that the Bridegroom's companions are the holy angels, who, it is agreed, are certainly present at these nuptials — as you know that Gabriel was sent to the Virgin in the beginning, and that neither he nor the rest are wanting to the ministry of men. In these nuptials indeed no small care presses the Bridegroom, that he provide for the Virgin, and for them that attend him, things that shall profit: that the Virgin first and more excellently may rejoice in the nuptial chamber, and secondly that the maidens lack not spiritual advancement. It was said far above that by the name of maidens are figured the apostles and apostolic men, who are seen more especially to attend the Virgin Mother of the Bridegroom with a handmaid's affection, while they love to venerate her childbearing [0329D] with an assiduous wonder and a solid faith, and to imitate her fruitfulness by bearing sons also, not by coition but by the spirit. These shepherds and teachers the Bridegroom wills not to be burdened beyond their power by the onset of malignant spirits, but to be freed by the kindly affection and patronage of the angels, that the vineyards may not wither in the flower of their first season by the fraud of the foxes, but by the care of the good spirits may grow strong unto the late fruit. The Bridegroom therefore admonishes the good spirits, who in this epithalamium are the companions, to be careful and to strive after the nuptial advancements; to stand nearer to them, and worthily to take care of those whose destruction or hurt the careful Virgin wills not. For the Virgin has much care for the maidens [0330A] or apostles, whose manner of life will profit herself indeed most of all, and others much; from whom the angels' solicitude is bidden diligently to ward off things hurtful — a solicitude that by this zeal or ministry will please both the Bridegroom and the Bride in no small measure. To whom the Bridegroom, speaking, says: *Catch,* etc.
