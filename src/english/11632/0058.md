## CHAPTER XIV.

*Catch us the little foxes that destroy the vines.* You, he says, angels, whom flesh has not made equal to men, nor guilt to the evil spirits, whom pride or disobedience has not cast out from the Creator's face; who abide and remain with me from the beginning unto perpetuity as companions, glorious by a likeness received, not made equal by an eternal greatness: I will that you be diligently present to us, that is, [0330B] to me and to the Bride as we celebrate our nuptials, and that whatever strives to withstand be by you made ineffectual and weak. For since some no longer run with open violence upon a level path, they glide in by blind windings and whisper venomous frauds; and, detracting from the Virgin, they labour to resist her wishes, while what she asserts of Christ they discolour with a dissuading guile. They dissuade, lest either the hidden divinity of Christ be seen by the eye of faith, or, when faith has seen it, lest it be firmly loved in the mind; and they strike a hard buffet of ignorance or of fear into those who will deny, or they graft in the love of visible things into those who will neglect the faith of things invisible. Who are these but wicked demons, learned in malice, full of fraud, who love nothing holy, who seek nothing worthy of praise; who, [0330C] when violence is wanting to them, have the love and the skill of seducing, and, according to a certain heathen, have a thousand arts of hurting and more than that? These, by the cunning of a wondrous art, not by any right of power or of virtue, seduce Judas Iscariot, greedy of money rather than of salvation; and lest he love his master; they dissuade him with manifold guile, and persuade him to become a traitor guilty of hatred. « When the devil, he says, had put it into the heart that Judas Iscariot, the son of Simon, should betray him [n: (Joan. XIII)]. » These shake with the thunder of fear, a little after, unto denying, that Simon Peter who a little before had promised himself a lot for standing fast; and the sudden denier would have perished within the crafty jaws of the beasts, had not the powerful prayer of his pitying Master come to his aid: « Simon, he says, behold Satan hath desired to have you, that he may sift you [0330D] as wheat: but I have prayed for thee, that thy faith fail not [n: (Luc. XXII)]. » Nor only before Christ's resurrection did the cunning of them that work malice cut short the number of the apostles, but after the resurrection also it prepared a snare for the chosen deacons; into which, though it drew not Stephen, full of faith and of spirit, nor the five that remained, yet it brought in the seventh, Nicolas the newcomer, unto a new heresy. Whose fraudulent cunning the Bridegroom rightly designated by the name of foxes — creatures which nature has not armed with much strength, but has fortified with so great a craft, with so great a refuge of divers frauds, [0331A] that they are said in the Gospel to have manifold retreats of burrows among us: « The foxes, he says, have holes [n: (Luc. IX)]. » The demons have hidden retreats and dark lurking-holes; they have runs past tracking and winding footprints, so that no simple man easily sets his foot to track them out, since either he cannot see them, or, if perchance he has seen them, straightway loses them. Sometimes the fox, when the necessity of hunger seems to press her hard, running to the refuge of fraud, feigns to lie dead; and when the birds have come near, hoping to have their food in the dead carcase, *189* she seizes them unawares, rejoicing to win a victory over the living. So that wicked spirit, who is ever found to hunger after our ruin, when with open nets he cannot ensnare [0331B] any of the winged (for, as Holy Scripture recalls [n: (Prov. I)], and the truth of them that have proved it affirms, in vain is the net cast before the eyes of them that have wings), feigns himself dead and of no strength at all against the just; he yields, and as it were now grants them to prevail unto the good things they shall have willed. And they, when they judge that they stand as though they should thenceforth not fall, rejoicing together in a long advancement and now secure by much practice, come sometimes to this carrion of the world, seeking a little too immoderately after the things that pass, believing that nothing hurtful is in them, but rather things that shall profit for necessary uses. But that wicked one, holding it in memory that by the ancient law it is forbidden that carrion be touched [n: (Levit. II)], and seeing the just now held guilty of the written law, if even but a little; judging that the incautious appetites of temporal things are of his own right, wickedly [0331C] reviving on a sudden, lays a strong hand upon the birds that are to perish. So indeed they who thought that they were catching, and turning what they caught to their own uses, are found to be caught, and, being alive, to lie beneath the dead; because, while the living covet a little too incautiously the carrion of faults, that carrion, as it were dead, revives strongly and truly unto the ruin of the living. That Samson, more prudently execrating the fraudulent foxes, laid hold of them alive, and held no fewer than three hundred; and, binding them two by two, with their heads free and their tails bound, and fire set beneath their tails, he wove a mystery worthy of praise [n: (Judic. XV)]. This prudent Samson, who is also said to be strong of arm, who by a prudent fortitude is believed to have carried away the gates of death, is the beloved of this our [0331D] Virgin of whom our discourse is, whom the truthful understanding of faith preaches to be God and man. Who took not one fox, not two only, but three hundred together, because he detected all the malice of all the malignant spirits; who so is man that as God also he regards all things circumspectly, and nothing appears imperfectly to his sight. By the number of a hundred, as you know, any perfection is figured, and the hundredfold perfection is not unfittingly recorded to be tripled: since, for that which the malignant spirits have suggested to us, however slightly, unto affection, and for what by word and what by deed they have drawn to any effect whatever, this sterner judge gathers them all with a hooked hand unto perfection, and, preparing fire for them all, drags them all to a miserable failing. And indeed now, as with head unbound [0332A] and mouth free, the wicked spirits rave; but their hinder parts are bound indissolubly; and when at the end of things the possibility of raging is forbidden them, fire being rendered to their deserts, an unfailing labour of suffering presses them hard. Whereof in the Gospel: « Everlasting fire, he says, which was prepared for the devil and his angels [n: (Matth. XXV)]. » And Paul: « Whose consummation, he says, is unto burning [n: (Hebr. VI)]. » But in the meantime, while that consummation is still deferred, while the last burning is not yet brought upon the foxes bound together, the Bridegroom wills to admonish his companions, that is the good angels, the more diligently, that they be willing to apply diligence to the catching of the evil ones. For because men, assailed by demons and filled with many miseries, do not stand if the ministering spirits resist not the ambushes of the hurtful, [0332B] the good come near and detect the frauds of the evil, and, when they are detected, show them beforehand to men, that they may carefully beware. For to this end are the ministering spirits set over men, to this end appointed to their care and keeping: that what men cannot do, being slower by nature and by negligence, these — whom both nature and merit have made far keener — may foresee, may forestall, may forewarn men and help them to beware; lest at any time the enemies exult and insult and say, "We have prevailed to hurt." Man indeed, the weaker, willing to build or to rebuild the walls of an inward holiness, to restore Jerusalem, to repair the breaches of a city long since overthrown, has many who assail and hinder the unremitting labour of one so pressing on; who, when [0332C] they see, envy, and, to say the least, deride the watchful insistence of the labourer. Who, when they observe that of himself he is not strong enough for the building, and scarcely escapes with unhurt footstep the mockery or the pressure of his enemies, despise the work begun, and assert that it cannot be brought to perfection, but that the foxes will lightly pass over the unfinished work with a derisive leap. Whence in Esdras, Tobias the Ammonite: « If a fox go up, he says, he shall leap over their stone wall [n: (II Esdr. IV)]. » And it is added: « Hear, O our God, for we are made a reproach: cover not their iniquity, and let not their sin be blotted out from before thy face, because they have mocked them that build [n: (ibid.)]. » You see the wicked rivals mocking the labour of them that build, and, while these advance, already rejoicing [0332D] in the future leap of the foxes; to whose mockery, or to whose leap already presumed upon, the weakness of those men makes no full resistance, if the skilful keeping of the angels stands not by them as they labour. Because therefore the diligence of the just labours at restoring the building of the mind, and the mockery or oppression of them that work malice sometimes discolours the work begun, the Bridegroom admonishes his companions the more insistently and urges them on, whose stronger care more fully searches out the frauds of the assailants. You, he says, who can forestall with nimble sight the cunning of the envious spirits, catch us the foxes that think to leap over the wall of them that build; lest the unseemly leap of those foxes dishonour what these men build, or lest any fraud of the enemies *190* discolour the reforming of the city. Who say says Isaias, « to my soul: [0333A] Bow down, that we may pass over [n: (Isa. LI)]. » [var: Vulg. *animae tuae*, "to thy soul" — in Isaias the words are spoken to Jerusalem, not to the speaker] Against which Nahum the prophet also: « He shall not add again, he says, that Belial pass through thee [n: (Nahum. I)]. » Who shall grant that Belial add not again to pass through us, nor that the fox, holding the work of the building in contempt, leap over it — unless the Bridegroom's companions, who are commanded to succour men in this, and to forestall with prudent watchfulness the derisive leaps or assaults? Let them therefore catch us the foxes while they are found to be little and as yet weak, lest they grow great and pass over the wall, which, being low, can easily be leapt; and if they be granted to try its lowness with a free leap, growing worse and worse they will hasten to destroy our vineyards. And since these vineyards flower and hope to be advanced to fruit by the summer's mildness, it is needful that the little foxes be removed further off, [0333B] or that their frauds be so detected by a worthy providence that (even if they will) they may not be able to have power to hurt them that advance.
