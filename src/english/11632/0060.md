## CHAPTER XVI.

*My beloved to me, and I to him, who feedeth among the lilies, [0334C] until the day breathe, and the shadows be bowed down.* What does she mean, that when the beloved here speaks of her beloved, this utterance of hers seems somewhat unfinished; *191* nor is any fuller sense found in it, unless something be understood from without for the finding of it? But what she who speaks would more truly, what more certainly, have understood, cannot easily, as I judge, be found out to the clear — least of all by those who, it is agreed, are hindered by a fog of mind, nor do they deserve that the hidden things of Holy Scripture be opened to them. Since therefore I am slender in understanding and abound not in merits, I so hide from myself, and from no other, the supplement of this word, that I may neither arraign her that speaks nor weaken her word with a wandering sense; so that, if I shall have said anything there, I may rather conjecture than affirm. A little above, the [0334D] Virgin, saying: *My beloved to me,* added: « speaketh, » and « My beloved, she says, speaketh to me; » nor does that construction seem beset by the cloud of a thing understood: which, being furnished with the necessary parts, was found plainly sufficient. But now, saying: *My beloved to me,* she did not add either what he does or that he speaks, so that which of these she would have understood is left doubtful; and the more uncertain that is, so much the more freely is it granted to anyone to find whatever he shall have seen fittingly to suit the present place. In short, unrestrained love, scarcely bearing its own incitements, often finds no words more fully expressive of its own affections; and therefore its utterance is sometimes found to hang in suspense, or, that I may say more, defective through a scanty furniture. For when with its whole desire it stretches itself toward that for which it burns, [0335A] gaping after him alone to hold him, it makes little of all the rest; and if perchance it make a word about him, it heeds not time, mood, or order, and while it desires the whole, it strives not to express the whole in a word. Hence that woman in the Gospel, when what she loved had been taken away, knowing nothing but to weep, holding in her mind him alone whose sepulchre she grieved to see appear empty, seeing someone on a sudden and with a straying eye reckoning him the gardener, not much mindful of grammar, fearing no Priscian: « Lord, she says if thou hast taken him away, tell me; and I will take him away [n: (Joan. XX)]. » She who had said nothing of him before sets him down all too heedlessly, and does not set out by any worthy preceding word to whom she refers him; thinking it enough for the hearer that she herself fully thinks and fully feels, even though her half-full outward utterance does [0335B] not more fully agree with the full sense of the same. Nor is it to be wondered at if hidden love, when for the showing of itself it requires another's help, thinks a half-full utterance sufficient enough for itself; which is for the most part wordy by a sigh, for the most part even by desire alone, and despairs not of being heard, though it speak not with a full noise of words. Our Virgin therefore, diligently weighing the grace of this love, and not repaying with abundant speech the abundance of it which she has received, shuts up within the hidden place of her heart wondrous motions in an unexplainable manner, and, breathing them out but thinly, so applauds him with fewer words that by speaking she both makes this love known to the hearer, and, the furniture of words being cut short, leaves the hearer in suspense. *My beloved,* she says, *to me, and I to him.* He, I say, gives me what is his, and I give him what is [0335C] mine, that both his may be mine and mine his: and thus we may have our things alike, and, as the laws of espousal require, may rejoice that all things are common to us. And if a sense of this kind can be believed to shine out more fittingly in these words, the Bride seems to answer the Bridegroom to that which he had said; who, commanding the foxes to be taken because the vineyard had flowered, called it ours, and did not show by the words that followed whence this commendable community had come to be. For he did not say: My vineyard or thine, but: « our vineyard hath flourished. » Which word the prudent Virgin, marking it diligently with a busy ear, and turning herself again — the Bridegroom as it were withdrawing — to the aforesaid maidens, rejoices to speak of him, and with fewer words to intimate whence this [0335D] community is which the Bridegroom would designate. That it might be called, she says, not his, not mine, but rather « our vineyard, » a mutual bestowal wrought it, whereby the two are alike friends: because he of his own condescension regarded me, a lowly handmaid; my lowliness despised not the grace of him that regarded me; he loved me, perchance not by my merit but by his own good pleasure; my faith, being beloved, understood that he was worthily to be loved for his merits. The Word, disposing to be made flesh, poured himself into my handmaid's womb; taking flesh in me from me, he flooded me with his grace: I offered mine own self, I bestowed my flesh, I bound myself over to obedience, I fixed beforehand no manner, no bound, no order for the good pleasure of him that commanded; with my flesh, which in assuming he so exempted from every fault, he bought or redeemed for himself that vineyard which is said to have flowered; and [0336A] she who, while yet she withered, is bought or redeemed by our flesh, is, now that she is loved in flower, expressly also said to be ours. If the patriarchs of old, who were, it is agreed, of one father one after another — although, as you remember, not all begotten of one mother [n: (Gen. XXIX, XXX)] — say of Joseph, the last but one, « For he is our brother and our flesh [n: (Gen. XXXVII)]; » how much more may the Mother of the Bridegroom, the Bride of the son, say, our flesh? and of the [sic: *vinca*] which is redeemed by this flesh, does the son likewise say « ours? » My beloved, she says, to me: he gave what was his, whose benefit, whose mercy in all things goes before me; and I to him: I offered what was mine by a worthy return, that thenceforth the community both of things and of words might be kindly toward us. He too gives me warnings, or a command, to arise, and I offer him [0336B] a ready little ear for hearing; he exhorts and diligently admonishes that I arise and make haste; I, resigning leisure to him, patiently endure labour. My beloved is to me — what? what but loving or beloved? *192* and I render to him — what? what but the gifts of a like diligence or affection? that the love of him who is first and far the chief may be the first-begun, and mine no repayment that follows save by his own bounty. My beloved gives me a commandment, gives none the less a blessing with the commandment; I to him a nimble running along the way of his commandments with an enlarged heart; he to me help, I to him compliance; he to me good will, I to him obedience; he gives to me what is of grace, what is of love, I to him what is of reverence, what is of honour. To me my beloved bestows a saving gift upon one in need; I [0336C] render to him that requires it a willing affection; and he who, being great God and mighty in power, willed to be born man of me, rejoices to be fed of me not so much between the breasts as among the lilies. He who of old fed the patriarch Jacob from his youth, who much more lately satisfied five thousand men out of five loaves [n: (Matth. XIV)] — whence is he so needy, or so careless of his own power, as to be fed, since to be fed seems a mark not of power but of need? In short, when he had fasted forty days and forty nights, afterwards he was hungry [n: (Matth. IV)]. To whom that wicked devil, setting stones before him that he might tempt him, did not suggest that he should change them by a word into lilies, thinking that he hungered after the fashion of other men, not by a voluntary but by a necessary failing, and that therefore [0336D] loaves rather than lilies suited one that hungered. Thou, he says, whose jaws, as I perceive, are empty with a wearying fast, say that these stones become loaves by an effectual power; because, while hunger fasts with the loaves withdrawn, the appearance of lilies (though perchance to the eyes) is not seasonable to the jaws. Although sometimes the eyes too seem to suffer their own hunger, when namely they itch for seeing and are not strong enough to contain themselves; and when the uncovered appearance of the thing coveted is indulged them, their curiosity is not unfittingly said to be fed. This perhaps is what the Virgin asserts, that the Bridegroom is fed among the lilies, because to his eyes the thing wished for gives its answer not so much by word as by appearance; and the gracious greediness and hotter burning of lovers is fed not by the taste, not by the savour, but by the sight or the scent of lilies. For which cause [0337A] he is expressly recorded to be fed, not with lilies, but among the lilies; among which, dwelling and conversing, he rejoices in their fragrant comeliness; and as the Virgin, languishing with love, wills to be stayed up with apples or with flowers, so does he will to be soothed among the lilies by their pleasing bounty. And what shines white more gladly or breathes more sweetly than, in the Bride the mother, virginal integrity, faith purifying the heart, the fruitfulness of matronly modesty; cleanness, innocence, spiritual love, sweetness of manners, the form of justice, the excellence of a special merit? Among which the Bridegroom walks as on a spacious path, and, seeing and smiling, is fed with a gracious sight; he is delighted with the comeliness and the scent of excellent merits, and, being himself the flower and the lily, rejoices in the likeness he has found. For he confesses himself the flower of the field and the lily of the valleys, and in the Bride [0337B] the Virgin he beholds also the burgeoning grace of the lily; and thus he shows himself fed between the two with a certain joy, while by a subtle running to and fro he now presents himself more fully to the Virgin, now, referring himself to his own, renders himself incomprehensible and absents himself: for neither in the bosom of the father, grudging us. does he continually hide himself, nor in the mother's lap does he pour himself back with a continual good will, as it were; but going forth from him, he now runs out to her with a humble condescension, and withdrawing himself from her, he now runs back to his inaccessible throne. His running out is from the father, says Ambrose; his running back is to the father. This will of the Bridegroom, whereby he is found now absent from the Virgin, now more present; whereby he is discovered to be at hand now above man, now beyond man — [0337C] the Virgin, loving and beloved, finds by experience to abide with him as his own, while the shadows still remain, and one comes not to the grace of the following day.
