## CHAPTER XVIII.

*Return, my beloved: be thou like to a roe and to the fawn of harts, upon the mountains of Bethel.* [var: Vulg. *Bether*; and Vulg. sets the vocative after the imperative] She who in the preceding chapter spoke of the Bridegroom to the maidens, or was perhaps set alone with herself in the secret chamber of her mind, did not pursue any longer the tenor of speaking she had proposed, but makes an apostrophe to him by whom she [0338D] believes herself to be forsaken. Love is found insufficient, and consequently impatient too; it wills not to be without him whom it has once found pleasing, once most sweet; and whatever is offered or bestowed upon her that greedily sighs, apart from him, she despises with a worthy disdain, nor does anything suffice her, until she refer herself to him. Since therefore she is not yet able to enjoy him by beholding his face more fully, she wills often to make an apostrophe to him in speaking; and when she speaks of him she is straightway compelled to be turned back to him with a word of entreaty, that by him whom she desires to see she may by that very desire be worthy at least to be seen. But because, her own mortality standing in the way, she feels him more often to withdraw or to turn aside, she asks him nothing more anxiously [0339A] than to return: that returning, and marking with what love the tender Virgin is held, he may bestow upon her that loves him his grace and a more bountiful abundance of himself. But that he may not set about either to go farther away or to tarry longer, the Virgin thinks it fitting to interpose a merit with her asking, so that, even if perchance he dispose not to hear her prayers the sooner, yet, compelled by his own equity, he may not put after it the merit of her that asks. *Return,* she says, *my beloved.* For if, she says, I were entreating some great thing of one whom I love not, I should not wonder to suffer a repulse and to be defrauded of my desire; but when I pour out prayers, when I spread out suppliant hands to my beloved, whence hast thou this hardness, that what I ask thou bringest not to effect? For who is so severe, so austere, that if he weigh that he is tenderly loved he would not repay like with like by a ready good will; and that, being beloved, he would not bestow upon her that loves him [0339B] the benefit she has asked, so that, being over-hard, he offend not against the rights or the laws of love? True and sincere love turns not its ear away for long; to him that gives what is pleasing and wished for it denies not the like, it deals not out the contrary: for her whom it purely loves it is held and moved with much care; it embraces her that loves, and answers a tender affection with usury. In short, by the testimony of Holy Scripture thou hast said that thou lovest him that loves thee [n: (Prov. VIII)]; nay, some also (as they that have proved it declare) thou hast loved before ever they loved [n: (I Joan. IV)]; and since thou art laudably proclaimed to love first, with no merits going before, who would not judge it hard, if thou spurnest the prayers of them that well deserve? But it is certain that she who loves deserves to be loved, and that she who is worthy to be loved consequently deserves not to be defrauded of a just desire: [0339C] especially when she goes not hunting, after the fashion of certain carnal folk, after the things that are her own, but entreats thee alone, and only for thine own sake, to become present. *Return, my beloved.* Thou, she says, by whose love I am held, in regard of whom all other things are vile to me, by whose absence I am weighed down while things present prick or bite me; thou who, when thou art believed to be at hand, suddenly turnest thyself away, [sic: *in cutiens*] to the rude and inexperienced a burden scarce to be borne: turn thyself, I beseech thee, that thou mayest show thyself well-disposed to her that calls thee back; nay (that I may say more) return, that thou mayest present thyself to her that loves thee with the sweetness she has wished. Render, I say, the more gladly what gladly and more often thou hast already given; render to her that loves thee what she has deserved, thou who lovedst her when she had not yet deserved it: repeat *194* now of debt what before thou didst bestow freely, [0339D] or rather return at last, bestowing what thou hast promised to them that love thee. For thou hast promised that, the course of life being finished, thou shouldest be seen face to face [n: (I Cor. XIII)]; that they who see should be satisfied with fulness, and, being satisfied, should not be held by loathing: that we, glad in thy presence, should not thenceforth be weighed down by absence; that, pressed by no trouble, we should always rejoice with thee in thy glory. To this therefore I will and I ask that thou return, my beloved: that what I have somewhat tasted and seen imperfectly through a glass, I may now, the barrier of the flesh being taken away and mortality utterly banished, hold and see more perfectly with unveiled face. But because a fervent desire is not filled full by the bounty of things present, and love unfilled is not held patient enough to await things absent, the Virgin asks that not only he whom she loves should return, but that returning he should be held like [0340A] to a roe and to the fawn of harts. « Be thou like, she says, to a roe and to the fawn of harts *(supra IV)*. » The roe, as you know from what was said above, has this natural power in her eyes, that she quickly discerns and distinguishes what pasture suits her, and even in her running judges what is useful to her, what fitting to be eaten, and what unfitting and therefore by its own desert to be spat out. But the fawn of harts, whom both a younger age and a stronger nature support, is borne along with a swift running and is on a sudden present where he wills; and he so rejoices that the privilege of speed is his, that among the other wild beasts he seems to have not so much feet as winged feet. When therefore the Virgin asks that the bridegroom be like to animals of this kind, she shows that a confident and secure conscience is not wanting to her — a conscience which [0340B] at the present time neither ignorance nor negligence accuses; and, willing that this be quickly repaid to her, she refuses not to be looked upon beforehand. Thou, she says, whose presence my loving mind covets, at whose return my conscience in no wise trembles: search out and look through with what desire, with what instancy I strive after thee; with what affection, with what love, putting present things after, I await thee alone as him that is to come. Be thou like to a roe, that thou mayest search my inward parts the more diligently, and mayest not be loath to sift or to discern what good, what better thing I put after or put before: and when thou shalt have known for certain what each single virtue in me deserves, let thy justice be held swift as a fawn to render what it has deserved. For receiving the benefit of the reward I am indeed so hasty that the scrutiny that goes before, and a sudden sifting, do not [0340C] affright me; and with so great a burning do I desire the nimble good will of the fawn to come, that I refuse not to feel beforehand the subtle watchfulness of the roe. For I know that the grace of the fawn comes not to me unto a fuller gladness, if the truth of the roe go not before to sift my inward conscience; nor shall the glad coming of the one fulfil my desire, if the true sifting of the other shall not first judge it worthy to be fulfilled. « Judge therefore and discern my cause » not only « from the nation that is not holy [n: (Psal. XLII)], » which is in no wise compared with me, but also from the holy nation, whose holiness, compared with mine, is not equal — which perchance, dreading the scrutiny, wills so to be clothed with the glory that is to come upon it, [0340D] that it be not first compelled to prove the winnowing-fan of sifting. For not knowing whether she be worthy of hatred or of love [n: (Eccle. IX)], she serves in fear, and exults before the Lord with trembling: now lifting herself toward him she grows glad with the hope of the gift she wishes for; now referring herself to herself, she greatly fears a scrutiny as severe as it is true. « Who knoweth, she says, if he will turn and forgive, and leave a blessing behind him? » [n: (Joel. II.)] But the Virgin knows beyond doubt that her beloved is turned, and confidently asks that, being turned, he return also; and she wills that her inward parts be marked by the subtle gaze of him that returns, holding it certain that the steadfast building of her mind is overthrown by no battering-ram whatever. In her therefore in whom the keen eye of the roe shall have found nothing that is not his own, in her consequently shall the good will of the fawn give his own leap; and whose [0341A] conscience the judgment that runs before shall approve, her cleanness shall the swift grace of him that repays reward. For the roe too loves to gaze more subtly and more discerningly, and the fawn wills to be seen glad and well-disposed by the nimbleness of his running; and both rejoice to be borne over the ridges of the mountains, the summits of the virtues — he to be borne after, repaying just things; she, subtly considering all things, to be borne before. *Upon the mountains,* she says, *of Bethel.* As they say who reckon that they have the knowledge of the Hebrew names — which we have either not deserved at all or have obtained but scantily — Beth is *a house*, and el is interpreted *God*; and Bethel is named in the Latin tongue *the house of God*. And the house of God is not unfittingly judged to be the inward conscience of the Virgin, namely a spiritual bride-chamber, which is made ready for God the Son of God, which the presence of the divine majesty chose to inhabit, [0341B] adorning and honouring it with the excellence of a wondrous holiness. Which house or habitation is rightly called mountainous, plentiful not in rough and unequal ridges but in the various merits of the virtues; from whose height the flatness of us that are beneath is far removed, and among men the bridegroom alone is declared worthily to overtop it. He indeed, like to a roe or to the fawn of harts, is borne above; to whose excellence the height of any mountains whatever is not compared; who upon the mountains of the Virgin and the steep places of her notable merits is both the subtle knower of them and the nimble renderer of rewards. And while the wished-for glory of rewards does not yet meet the Virgin — which she knows does not meet her, if the giver's worthy [0341C] consideration run not before — the Virgin wishes and asks that he whom she loves tarry no longer, *195* but, returning the sooner, be made like to a roe and to the fawn of harts, so that upon the mountains of the merits and the virtues of her virginal conscience the inspection may run before, and a friendly repayment follow. For that repayer is the hart of friendship and the colt of graces. Who, although he condescends to hear the Virgin's affectionate prayers, yet does not straightway hearken unto effect to her desire; and seeing the Virgin, he wills not yet to be seen face to face, but after the manner of lovers withdraws himself and is hidden, and wills to be sought — of whom she subjoins: *In the little bed,* etc.
