## CHAPTER XIX.

*[CAP. III.] In my little bed through the nights I sought him whom [0341D] my soul loveth. I sought him, and found him not.* The little bed here, as I judge, may be called a certain straitness of mind, in which the will or the intention of her that loves gathers and binds itself together, when namely she wills with the highest desire to please him whom she loves, to speak with him alone, to cleave to his close embraces. Burning with which purpose, while she wills to leap back from these things of sense, to be turned away from earthly things, and to feel only him that is invisible — as it were ignorant of the flesh, drunken with the love of the Bridegroom whom she foretastes, one spirit with him — she straitens herself as in a short little bed. Against which, her who commits not herself to a like straitness, who with a loosened mind lets in lost affections, the truth-telling speech of Isaiah accuses and reproves — nay rather, through [0342A] Isaiah, the truthful spirit of prophecy. « Thou hast received, he says, an adulterer; thou hast enlarged thy couch [n: (Isa. LVII)]. » The prophetic speech, saying that the shameless woman has received an adulterer, adds to show that she is no friend to a sufficiently narrow solitude, who has not reduced the breadth of her couch to a short little bed, nor forced herself within close bounds, her little bed being shortened. For she enlarged her couch when she poured her lost mind abroad, and received another, or rather an adulterer, when she poured in the pandering of sin; when, dissolved with nerveless softness, her feet parted, she was defiled in an enlarged couch with unlawful lovers. But the Virgin, ignorant of the aforesaid breadth, shutting herself up in a certain straitness of mind, and shutting in with her none other beside him with whom she is one spirit, sought him whom [0342B] she loved in this little bed with herself, hoping him to be at hand — she who admitted no other at all. In short, she had read in the Psalm: « The Lord is nigh unto all that call upon him [n: (Psal. CXLIV)]; » the Virgin therefore seeks the bridegroom, but the night blunts the sight of her that seeks — a night which pours itself with a darkened face over her that wills to find the sought one; and, it being poured over, the light that is sought pours not out its full rays, but, its brightness being called back, hides itself as it were under a cloak. Not one voice, but very many set themselves against the Virgin that seeks, and, that the sought one may not be found, interpose a troublesome wall; and for this cause the virgin rightly makes mention not so much of a night as of nights, by whose opposing she perceives that the choice is as yet denied her. A night is the difficulty of seeking, a night the delay of finding; a night is the trouble of deferring, a night the misery [0342C] of enduring. A night is the unfulfilment of spiritual desire; a night is the flesh that is to die, even though the conversation be now not carnal; a night, in fine, is the burdensome presence of this life, however polished with labours or with the virtues of one that labours. Through these nights the Virgin asserts that she sought the bridegroom, and that, since he met her not in the first as one that would remain, she departed not from her purpose; and that although she sought again, yet she has not yet found him according to her liking — proving by experience that there has been no full agreement of light with these darknesses. *Through the nights,* she says, *I sought him whom my soul loveth: I sought him, and found him not.* Therefore, when the Virgin sought the bridegroom, she was not strong enough to find him, because a somewhat dark night seemed to forestall her that sought — nay, a throng of nights seemed as by agreement [0342D] to come together, striving and straining to hinder the hands and the eyes of her that sought. For although the Virgin bravely restrains herself from other occupations, and, straitened in her little bed, girds herself the more anxiously for seeking, yet she reaches not to that inaccessible light of the Bridegroom, so long as the throng of the aforesaid nights thrusts itself against her endeavour. Whence the same Bridegroom: « You shall seek me, he says, and shall not find me; and where I am, you cannot come [n: (Joan. VII)]. » The Son is indeed in the bosom, in the session, at the right hand of God the Father, of co-equal glory, of one substance, of the same majesty: co-eternal with the Eternal, equal to the Invisible, inhabiting also an inaccessible light, ungranted not only to any of us, but even to his Mother [0343A] while she lived here. For to the throne of glory, in which the Son abides in the Father and the Father in the Son, neither John, nor James, nor in fine the Virgin mother came; and as to them the right hand or the left is not given for sitting together, so neither to the Mother, who by the law of mortals is the handmaid of the Son's humanity. Although therefore the Bridegroom is here sought, whether by their desire or by his Mother's, yet he is not found at the right hand, or in the glory of God the Father; and, he being not taken away but deferred, she seems to have entered upon a counsel, and, either speaking to herself alone or perhaps to the maidens, to have said: *I will arise,* etc. *196*
