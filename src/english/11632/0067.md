## CHAPTER XXV.

*I held him, nor will I let him go, till I bring him [0349D] into my mother's house, and into the chamber of her that bore me.* The Virgin, as was said before, sought the Bridegroom with a renewed purpose, nor found him whom she sought until she had passed quite by the watchmen set between; because so long as any other seems to be interposed between the seeker and the sought, he that is sought does not sufficiently endure to expose himself to the seeker's desire. When therefore she was seen to pass the watchmen with a lynx's eye, she rejoiced to find more clear-sightedly him whom she sought — so far, at least, as it is given to a living man to prevail; nay, above the rest of men she deserved to obtain this grace of prevailing. The Virgin found that he could not be found in purity by a living man, that the invisible could not be seen, the insensible not perceived; and the more subtly she apprehended in contemplation that he [0350A] is incomprehensible, so much the more strongly [sic: *ama*], and she intends not to love anything beside him. Thus him whom she judges to be tracked not by the foot but by affection, once tracked she finds not with the eye but with a keen understanding: she holds him not with bolts, not with glue, not with a chain, but with love; she binds him not with an iron fastening but with a stronger embrace. She holds him, I say, and binds him, and slackens not the hands greedy of holding; she interrupts not her love, not her affection, not the intent zeal of advancing; him long desired she lets into the chamber of her secret heart, rejoices in the presence of him let in, and, once he is present, suffers him to withdraw no further. Some indeed are found to have held him at times, and to have drawn him to their will with certain bonds; but he seems to be admitted within their holds in such wise that, as though [0350B] impatient of delay, he at last demands to be let go. « I see, he says to Moses, that this people is stiff-necked: let me alone, that my fury may be kindled against them, and that I may destroy them [n: (Exod. XXXII)]. » The just Lord, whose eye sees and approves equity, cursing the people's hardness and the perverse depravity of idolatry, purposes to be grievously kindled against that same people, to be indignant with the sinners, and to execute swift vengeance upon them. He wills to unbridle his fury more harshly against the idolaters; but Moses withstands him, and strives to bridle him in his raging [n: (Jud. X)]; he holds him whom he loves and knows to obey now and then the prayers of them that love; he curbs him in his anger, and suffers him not to rage against the guilty according to his purpose: « Either forgive them this trespass, he says, or if thou do not, blot me [0350C] out of thy book which thou hast written [n: (Exod. XXXII)]. » God does not, after our manner, often turn the stylus; he does not overturn the marks once ploughed in, as though led by repentance; him whom, enrolled among the eternal citizens, his grace has already decreed to save, he has not afterwards scraped away, nor blotted out of the book of the living. Moses therefore, asking either that he himself be blotted out of the book which God had already written, or that God himself have mercy on the people and remit the trespass, by that which he knows in no way to befit the divine equity provokes that equity to what he judges more fitly expedient; and he holds it the more firmly and as it were binds it, even against its will, lest, incontinent of the fury conceived, it put forth a hand to vengeance. But that equity, which is thus held back from vengeance, wills, as it seems, to be let go for this cause, that its justice wills nothing [0350D] to be remitted unpunished; and Moses thinks that so great a guilt will be avenged by the just death of them all, if the avenging hand endures not to be forestalled by the softened countenance of mercy. Lest therefore he so rage against them that all be utterly blotted out, they are helped not by their own merits but by Moses's; who wills not to hear the Lord asking to be let go, but, holding him more firmly bound, compels him by his prayers mercifully to obey. Long before, a man is read to wrestle with Jacob, who is found to be called now a man, now an angel, now even the Lord; who, marking that his wrestler could not easily be overcome: « Let me go, he says, for the morning riseth [n: (Gen. XXXII)]. » And he: « I will not let thee go, he says, unless thou bless me [n: (ibid.)]. » God, with whom Jacob strongly wrestles, he wills not to let go unless [0351A] he be redeemed at a price, unless the vanquished bestow upon the victor the grace of a blessing, without which the victor himself rejoices in no victory. « And he blessed him, he says, in the same place [n: (Gen. XXXII)]. » That he may deserve to be let go, he reaches out the gift demanded, and the Lord renders him that lets him go heaped with grace; and so at last, the liberty he had demanded being received, he is let go, and he that lets him go rejoices in the newness of the wished-for gift. But the Virgin rejoices not now in the gifts given but in the giver; she wills no other benefit than to enjoy his more bountiful presence: to hold him whom she loves, not to be drawn away from his embraces, not to be induced at any price to let him go. I will not, she says, that the grace of him whom I hold come to me in such wise that, the gift once received, the giver of the gift now withdraw; nor that the beloved's blessing be so presented to her he loves, [0351B] that he whom I love absent himself when he has blessed. For I love the things that are his only for his own sake, and whatsoever he shall have given me suffices not without *201* himself; nay, if these be denied to me that love, and he give himself to the rest, content with him alone I shall sleep in peace in the selfsame. Nor do I hold, or will I hold, him of whom we speak for myself alone, but I will provide that others too may hold him with me; for I will not, by the disease of avarice, begrudge others their profit, but the more I advance, the more shall I rejoice with the rest as they advance. I will hold therefore him whom I hold, nor will I let him go, until, when I bring [him] into my mother's house, nay, and into her chamber, I let him in; that the good which the daughter has already deserved first and more fully to feel, she that bore her may thereafter rejoice to find for herself. The mother, or she that bore this Virgin, is Judaea, who her whom she had first [0351C] borne in flesh by the pain of one bringing forth; afterwards begot to the worship of the one God by the sacraments of the law. Therefore perhaps she is called mother and she that bore her alike, that by the repeated name a twofold begetting may seem to be signified; to which begetter she that is begotten promises to render a benefit. And she renders it when she lets her beloved into her house or chamber. Judaea's house is faith, whereby she knew the Father and the Son, and the Spirit of both, to be God; by which faith she removed herself from the deserts and the fellowship of unbelievers; lying hid and at rest under the shadow of this covering as one secure, not about to feel the sun's importunate heats, nor the whirlwind, nor the rain. With such a roofing the unbelief of the Gentiles is not defended; it is covered by no shade, no rooftree, no tiles of faith; but is found free in the fields or under the [0351D] open sky like a wild ass; it knows no house, it endures not to be applauded, it is hedged in by no wall or dike. When therefore foul weather comes, when snow, hail, ice, the spirit of storms, when at last some power of darkness comes upon her, it rejoices to snatch her at once, finding her wandering and uncovered; and she, like chaff fixed by no weight, is tossed about. But Judaea, shut in her house, fortified as by the strength of a castle, rejoicing in a lofty rooftree and marked with certain bulwarks, puts far from her the heavy threats of the air by a strong repulse, and in the hiding-place of faith, her head laid down, now makes her conscience serene. For when the winds rush down, the heaven thunders, the air rages — which is wont to rage threateningly and over-stubbornly in our parts — she thinks nothing more serene than to be covered the more constantly by the defence of faith, [0352A] if only the defence rest upon a stable foundation. For the architect, gathering a heap of timbers and stones into a structure, if he be worthily anxious or fearful of loss, digging deep, is busy to find the rock upon which he may be able to establish the building with a firm footing. Otherwise the house itself, raised aloft upon loftier columns and finished with marvellous labour and workmanship in the eyes of the beholders — by however much it be greater and more elaborate, yet because it rests not upon a firmer rock, by so much the more does it lie subject, at the beating of the floods and the driving of the winds, to a heavier ruin. That therefore Judaea's house, that is, her faith, may take a worthy support, the Virgin, providing for her mother, promises to bring in a foundation; she promises, that is, to bring in that rock which is Christ, which by its own firmness suffers not the building set upon it [0352B] to fall. God indeed, as you have read, had been known of old in Judaea [n: (Psal. LXXV)], whose stretched-out shoe Idumaea did not yet know, because in those days he imparted some sort of knowledge of himself to the hearts of believers, but did not in their eyes clothe himself personally in visible flesh. But when the new grace, when the fullness of time was at hand, when the strength of God, condescending to the weak, willed to be made weak, the Virgin, fruitful by the Spirit, bore God incarnate, and brought forth to men, shod, him who is invisible in his godhead. And she, understanding for certain that none would thenceforth come to salvation save those who should believe Christ to be the wisdom and the power of God — Christ, I say, begotten before the day-star out of the womb of God the Father, but in the end [0352C] of times made man out of the womb of his own mother — took the utmost care that this knowledge of the matter should be conferred upon Judaea, and that the building of faith should be made firm upon this as upon a foundation. Holding him therefore, the Virgin promises that she will not slacken her greedy hands until she shall make him known more firmly to her own nation to be held; until she shall present him newly born to the believing shepherds, and afterwards, as he grows, commend him to the slower apostles. For in these the faith of the Jews is instructed by Mary's words and merits; lest the deserted house fall, it is stayed up by her help and her work; she bearing him and setting forth her offspring more truly, Judaea receives and honours Christ, believes the man to be God, and loves and adores him whom she believes. This is perhaps what this Virgin's promise signifies, when mention is welcomely made [0352D] not only of the house but also of the chamber: namely, that Judaea in the apostles will rejoice both to believe in Christ and to love him, and by the Virgin's intervention will obtain this twofold benefit. The house, as being wider, receives without distinction the many that flow in; but the remote chamber more secretly admits only them that love; and therefore the house denotes the faith which we see to be common to very many, but the chamber the love which you know to be proper to the elect. It is not enough therefore for the Virgin that she so preaches Christ to Judaea that he be believed, but she takes the utmost care that he be likewise loved by her, while she declares him both God born of God before the ages without a mother, and newly made truly man of herself without a father. To which believing and loving she leads her by her own warnings as well as merits, and brings Christ [0353A] into her house and chamber; nor does she cease from her zeal until she see her own poor desire heard, and rejoice that her beloved is found in the secret place of them that love. When he is found she envies not, she pines not as a rival, but breathes again from her *202* labour and recovers from her sickness; while, burning with charity, she grows glad at the good things of them that advance, and in that reclining, as it were, rests with a glad conscience — to whose rest the Bridegroom, favouring it, says: *I adjure you,* etc.
