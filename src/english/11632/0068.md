## CHAPTER XXVI.

*I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem, by the roes and the harts of the fields, that you stir not up, nor make the beloved to awake, till she please.* The Bridegroom warns the apostles, and those who are seen to have believed alike out of Judaea, and by the Virgin's word and care to have received Christ in the secret place of faith; [0353B] he warns them, I say, not to make light of the grace once received, and not, in contempt of things present, to reach out to their former things, or perhaps to things worse than the former. For if their faith, if their love be more grievously shaken, they will break in upon her sleep, striking harshly upon the Virgin's ear; and from the joy of that rest which the Virgin willed to obtain from their conduct she will be compelled unwilling to awake to troubles. The Bridegroom warns them therefore, lest among the apostles the treachery of Judas thunder harshly; lest the doctrine of Nicolas, discordant from the rest, sound ill; lest, at the hearing of the mystical discourse, many of the disciples be struck with scandal, and, going back, walk no more with him, their purpose harshly broken. And not only these apostates, or any of the like who truly cleaved not to Christ, but the fear [0353C] of the elect also wounded the Virgin's ears not slightly; Peter's denial, John's stripping, the flight of them all made a grievous din, and compelled the resting Virgin to be roused to her watches. The Bridegroom therefore, tenderly solicitous for the Virgin whom he loved, warns those whom he perceived to be troubled by fear of their persecutors, that they faint not from the form and doctrine which the Virgin had given them, or, if they faint, may perhaps the sooner come to themselves. Daughters, he says, of Jerusalem, it is not fitting that you should will what you do not yet find her to will who forewarns you; nor is it free to you to will anything hurtful or unseemly, until she also will it, who, it is agreed, [0354A] never wills at all, the apostles indeed, grace forsaking them, willed against the prohibition, when they were not able to resist to the utmost the fault that met them; but she willed nothing of the sort, who was worthily able to withstand the faults that met her, and for this cause she was found to be roused from a pleasing rest not by her own din but by her own people's. Yet afterwards, rightly mindful of him that adjures them, and visited more fully by the presence of the strengthening Spirit, they are made comely by a secret chamber, as it were, of conduct and of merits, into which they rejoice that the Bridegroom is brought by the Virgin's help and work. And when he is brought in, the Virgin herself also grows glad not slightly, and rests in the sleep she had chosen before, in their conduct; from which the Bridegroom's diligence forbids her to be roused, lest she be afflicted by a punishment whose fault she is not held to share. Above, therefore, when with these same words [0354B] the Bridegroom willed to adjure the daughters of Jerusalem, that they should not presume to rouse the Bride resting amid the Bridegroom's embraces, the Virgin is shown to rejoice in her own advances, or over her own, which she is proclaimed to have gained for herself by grace and merits. But here, when the same adjuration is found to be repeated, the Virgin is found to rejoice over the merits of others, whose solicitude is addressed by the Bridegroom's diligence, that they repel not the grace which is found so welcome to the Virgin's desire. There the Virgin is delighted with a welcome rest amid the Bridegroom's embraces; here she rejoices that the Bridegroom rests in her neighbours' chamber; there she falls asleep in her own advances, here in her neighbours'; and in either sleep, holding the Bridegroom, she longs for his courts. But because there the same verse is in some fashion expounded, I have not thought it necessary that [0354C] the same exposition be repeated here; lest, if what was said before be said again in the present place, a superfluous repetition breed weariness in the hearer. As therefore the second book of this work took its end there, where the Virgin leaned herself upon the Bridegroom's left hand for the sake of rest, so here also I deem it worthy that a close or term be set to the present book, where the Bride rejoices that the Bridegroom is brought into her mother's house or chamber; that where it is granted the Bride to be lulled asleep by delicate advances, there our stylus may rejoice to be repaired by silence as by sleep.
