## CHAPTER THE FIRST.

[0353D]

*Who is she that goeth up by the desert, as a little rod of smoke of spices, of [sic: *myrrhoe*], and of frankincense, and of all the powder of the perfumer?* Thus far in this drama the beloved and the Virgin have spoken to one another and of one another, and have pursued in alternating speech what belonged to love and to grace; nor do any others seem to be brought in to speak, *203* although the maidens and the companions alike have been brought in to hear. But now the Bridegroom, by a more ample benefit, gives to the companions, to whom he had given place and grace to hear, room [0354D] to speak, that the marriage-song may be made more pleasant to them, when they too shall alike have given their fellow's token in it. The companions, therefore — that is, the angels who are said to stand by the Bridegroom at such a wedding, who, though they minister to men, are not for that kept from his face — marking with what affection the Bridegroom is held toward the virgin, by what grace, by what merit the virgin is advanced above all women, are fixed fast, are astonished, and are not worthily able to wonder, nor do they find with what praises they can fitly venerate her. An affectionate devotion indeed admonishes them that they say something; [0355A] the consideration of a magnitude to be wondered at calls them back that they say nothing; for whatever they say seems to themselves little and insufficient, and yet they think themselves worthy to be reproved if they say nothing at all. A marvellous abundance of matter for speech makes them poor indeed, and the poverty by no means satisfies their great desire; yet they speak, that they may awaken [sic: *susciten*] in us the affection of hearing, and give us hearers some sort of confidence of speaking. For none of us would attempt to speak the praises of so great a Virgin, if the excellence of the angels, as not sufficiently conscious of itself, utterly refused them; but by the pattern and solicitude of those who are ministering spirits we too are admonished to venerate the Virgin with such praises as we can. For they too show themselves to do this very thing, so far as possibility avails them, not so far as the Virgin's dignity requires; [0355B] they who do not yet sufficiently apprehend how great the Virgin's merits are, and therefore intend not to express them to the utmost in words or in praises, but wonder, and, wondering, ask who she is whom they so mark as wonderful. Who, they say, is this? We turn over, they say, the roll of women present or foregoing from of old, from the time when Eve, who was made first, gave her consent to the enemy; to many of whom the gifts of divers graces have been granted; the beauty of the inner mind, by the hands of grace as well as of industry, and cultivated — none of these can we find like to her, but she is found to outgo them all by an ineffable grace. Who then is this, to whom alone a special excellence is given, that no woman before her, none after her, is her equal; that she seems now [0355C] not even to need our patronage, nay, to surpass angels as well as men, not by nature but by grace? Who is this that walks not with bended knee after the manner of a delicate woman, but with a strong and unwearied step goes manfully forward; who takes an arduous way untried by feminine paces, and, laying hold of the ladder which Jacob scarcely deserved to see, climbs bravely through the desert? The desert, as I see, is a way and a manner of life set apart, which the multitude knows not and no common hoof treads; which is not touched by mire, not besprinkled with the dust of the streets; which fault does not defile, nor any superfluity of chaff cover. Jacob, when a stone set beneath him was seen to hold up his head, lulled in a mystical sleep, scarcely deserved to see [0355D] such a ladder [n: (Gen., XXVIII)]; which, as it appeared to him, being forbidden to them that live after the manner of men, is left to angels alone, ascending and descending. For the angels descend, meeting the Virgin's advances with a favouring joy. And they ascend, running before [sic: *praeccurrentes*] with festal praises her that advances: hastening to meet the Lord who leans upon the ladder, and asking who is this that hastens to outgo in merits the angels who are her betters in nature. Who is this, they say, to whom a virginal fruitfulness, or a fruitful virginity, has been granted, unapproached in all ages past by angels as by men; who, living in a body wrapped about with the necessities of death, runs through a life liable to vices in an unpolluted course? [0356A] For she goeth up as a little rod of smoke, which, though it rise subtle and slender from below, lifting itself into the thin airs is itself made thin; so advancing on high that it commits itself to the secret of the clouds, and, as it were emptying itself, admits not the eyes of the beholders. The Virgin assuredly, though begotten of earthly parents, yet, the earthly dregs being scraped away, made thin by the zeal of discipline, has gone forward to so great a height of merits above men that she is rightly declared to have gone beyond the capacity of them that wonder at her. Advancing thus, she gives far and wide a most sweet odour, the teaching of justice, a more gracious rule of discipline; she bestows her suffrages, she rejoices to show benevolence, which things to them that worthily consider seem to smell like spices. Therefore perhaps the smoke, to whose little rod the advancing Virgin is compared [sic: *eomparatur*], [0356B] is recorded to come forth not from the pit of the abyss but from spices; for whose compounding a multiplicity of kinds has been gathered, and they have been compounded of myrrh and of frankincense and of all the powder of the perfumer, Myrrh gives a marvellous remedy to the bodies of the dead, and by a certain natural force removes corruption from flesh that is flowing and soft: frankincense burnt up with fire is offered to God in sacrifices, and by its smoky odour the devotion of them that sacrifice is commended. Myrrh therefore, as it seems, is like to that virtue whereby the Virgin worthily subjects her body to servitude, that by fastings, watchings, cleannesses vicious corruption may be put far off, and, the laws of nature being restrained, nothing in it may rot away soft or flowing. But by frankincense the Virgin's humble devotion, or devout humility, is figured; which, when in the censer of the mind it is burnt up *204* by the fire [0356C] of the holy Spirit, melted into love, is directed like smoke to things above; nay, after the manner of a little rod, drawing itself into a narrow compass, hastens the more intently to things eternal. But by the powder of the perfumer, of which a whole heap, as it were, is added on top, the whole company of the remaining virtues is expressed; which that perfumer, by whom the medicinal health of all was found out, exquisitely heaping together and prudently pounding, reduces into pigments. Of all which, agreeing to a saving medicine, heaped together and bruised in the Virgin's heart as in a little mortar of [sic: *discipliuae*], the smoke is directed in the perfumer's sight, the odour breathes forth — to the worthy wondering at which the companions' consideration scarcely aspires. Who then is this, with whom a multiplicity of ears of corn, as it were, has gathered itself into [0356D] a handful, nay, with whom the whole company of kinds has forced itself together in her little mortar, whose merits are bruised like powder by a most subtle examination, and are borne before the perfumer like smoke, nay, like a little rod of smoke, in a marvellous ascent? From these, borne and offered, that same perfumer turns not away, but in her that offers them, as upon a little bed, reclines himself with a welcome benevolence; and he encloses that same little bed with a rampart of strength, whereby he shuts out from it, and far off, whatsoever is unseemly, whatsoever is hurtful. Of which the companions subjoin: *Behold the little bed,* etc.
