## CHAPTER II.

*Behold the little bed of Solomon: threescore valiant ones compass it about, [0357A] of the most valiant of Israel.* Him whom I had called the perfumer they seem to name Solomon; who, being the author and lover of peace, willed to prepare for himself a little bed in which, hidden from the tumult of men, he might lay down his head for rest's sake, and truly show himself peaceable not in name only but in fact. And his little bed, I judge, may be said to be the Virgin herself, who for love's sake is found so given over to Solomon's service that she suffers no stranger to take rest in her, and admits not the crafty enemy, even for a moment, within the containing of her narrow mind; for which cause she too deserved to be called not a bed but a little bed, that by this diminutive a certain narrowness of hers might seem to be designated — she who, not being stretched out to take another likewise, strives to please the bridegroom alone, and is content with him alone [0357B] whom she lawfully loves. To whom that prophecy of Isaias worthily agrees, and prophecies uttered at divers times agree together, showing to every one that loves that a certain narrowness of mind befits him — that a lover embraces not many, serves not two masters. « The bed is straitened, he says, so that one must fall out: and the short covering cannot cover both [n: (Isa. XXVIII.)] » The bed of the mind is assuredly straitened in commendable wise when it purposes to lie open and to please none but the lawful one; when it has not settled in itself a dwelling, a place, a space for another; when the other is compelled to fall, or perhaps, terrified, comes not near from afar. Lastly, the covering of virginal modesty, which dissoluteness has not woven, which superfluity does not warp, draws itself close about the lawful one; the other is by no means covered by it, [0357C] nor does the Bridegroom accept a portion in common with an adulterer, nor does he suffer him to make his dwelling with him under the same covering. Whence her who straitened not the little bed nor shortened the covering, but, an adulterer being invited, wickedly widened all — her the bridegroom himself is found by the same Isaias more grievously to accuse, and to refuse the unworthy fellowship both of the adulteress and of the adulterer. « Thou hast enlarged thy bed, he says, thou hast received an adulterer [n: (Isai. LVII.)] » But the Virgin, her bed being straitened, admitted the stain of no adulterer; her covering being shortened, she suffered no crafty one to be covered; but she removed from the narrow rest of her mind whatsoever is contrary to her, and, keeping faith with the bridegroom, devoted herself wholly and singularly to him. Her therefore, who by zeal with [0357D] grace both willed and was able to show herself a little bed, so that enemies and rivals are of necessity compelled to shrink away, Solomon, loving peace and yet having care of battles, fortifies with an armed soldiery, and by a strong defence makes secure. Who assuredly, girt about not with walls, not with towers, not with material bulwarks, but with certain valiant ones, threescore in mystical number, abides undaunted under the provident hand of the aforesaid Solomon, while the keepers help her and the enemies bring in nothing of hurtful injury. But the keepers, who deserve to be called nothing but valiant, are the spiritual virtues by which it is agreed the Virgin is strengthened; which stand by her not on one side only without the other; themselves prudently [0358A] disposed, they stand round about her in an ordered circuit. Whose perfection is figured in the number sixty, which is completed of six and ten multiplied one by the other; of which the six denotes the perfection of works or labours, and the ten the perfect consequence of rewards. For because God the creator of all fulfilled his works in six days, who, as you know it is written, rested on the seventh day from the works which he had wrought [n: (Gen. II)], rightly is the perfection of working designated by the six, in the fuller obtaining of which one labours through the whole space of days. There are those who hold that the six is not to be judged perfect for this reason, that God willed his works to be completed in it, but that the primordial works were once made in six days for this reason, *205* that God knew this to be the first perfect number, [0358B] so that from the perfection of the number God might commend his works, and, they being completed in a perfect number, might show them to be no less perfect. And by the ten the perfection of the reward is rightly said to be designated, because the householder bids that very sum be given to them that labour in the vineyard; and whosoever has been faithfully held by the labour enjoined, with a view to obedience, is content with this number added at the end of time. The six therefore is taken ten times, or the ten six times, and they make sixty, when the Virgin is strongly girt both with the labour of the day's burden and with the sure hope of the reward to come; when she rejoices to give her pains to perfect works in the present, and altogether fears nothing, doubts nothing concerning what follows. But why either the six or the ten ought, according to the arithmeticians, to be judged perfect, and by what perfection also the sixty [0358C] is found to be commended — because I have undertaken to set that forth more fully in the Response on Obedience, I have willed here for brevity's sake to pass it by in silence. For those seem not yet more fully to obtain the six who, working great things, cannot fulfil greater; who, if perhaps in avoiding wicked works they are circumspect providently enough, are yet in word less perfect than they ought to be. « He that sinneth not, he says, in word, the same is a perfect man [n: (Jacob. III)]. » [var: Vulg. James 3:2 *si quis in verbo non offendit*, "if any man offend not in word"] Some also, though they keep their ways that they offend not with a slippery tongue, and already hold the six, yet do not yet draw near sufficiently to the ten; because, though they hope that they will receive a reward for their labour, they do not yet fully know whether they be worthy of hatred or of love. [0358D] The merits and advances of all these, defective, so to speak (for none of the like has deserved in this life to obtain complete ones), the holy Virgin is found to pass by, nay, far to surpass, by grace as by merits; and, still dwelling with us, she is so compassed by the mystical sixty that the observance of works and labours renders her perfect, and a wholly sure expectation of things to come makes her secure. And beautifully do they that compass her about deserve to be called valiant ones of the most valiant of Israel, because among all the virtues whose merits seek to contemplate God, those are agreed to be valiant and to be commended for especial valour by which the Virgin is compassed, and, being compassed, loves to be preserved.

## CHAPTER III.

[0359A]

*All holding swords, and most expert in war.* With how great valour, with how great care these compass the Virgin about, the armour shows as well as the mystical number; lest, if they come or come together to defend her unarmed, the more numerous they are, the more blameworthy they be, and by a just judgment be made void. Each one therefore holds and draws a twice-sharpened sword, so that if perhaps some robber shall have sought to cross the appointed bound, his presumption, at once beaten back, may utterly vanish, or rather, at the sight of the sword, he may leap far back in terror. This sword is not of iron, wherewith wicked criminals are sensibly struck, but it is the sword of the spirit, which the Apostle declares to be the word of God; for the word of God is proclaimed living and effectual and more piercing than any sword, [0359B] whereby the recesses of the soul and the inward conscience are pierced [n: (Heb. IV)]. The sword is the faithful testimony, the lucid precept, the book of the law, which the priest Joiada willed to give into the hand of Joas the king [n: (II Paral. XXIII)], that he might show to all that the knowledge of the divine law befits a king; nay, that a king ought not so much to know the law as to hold it in his hand, that is, in work. They gave, he says, into the king's hand the law to be held, and made him king. Therefore the threescore are recorded not to have but to hold swords, because it is not they that have who forthwith also hold, but they that hold are not denied to have; and it profits little, or nothing, to have a sword in the sheath, if the hand — that is, the discipline of works — draw it not forth. All the valiant ones therefore who are said to compass the Virgin about hold swords, obey the word, are exercised [0359C] in worthy works, and by so great a defence render the Virgin strong and secure, so that, the enemies being beaten back afar, she suffers neither injury nor loss. And they that so defend her are assuredly most expert in war, whose learning is not new by advance rather than by time; because to know, and not yet to do, the things that are right, is a bare doctrine, rude and, as of a new time, imperfect. For understanding gives advance not to them that have understanding but to them that do it — advance not only of knowledge but of conscience; and as conscience without knowledge is not sufficiently instructed, so knowledge without conscience is found rude and more imperfect. They therefore ought to be called not only expert but most expert, who obey the word for the waging of wars, [0359D] and lend their hand to the sword; whose solicitude so goes forth to repel the enemies' assaults that it is a terror to all, while the hand withdraws not from the sword. Concerning which sword it is subjoined: *Every man's,* etc.
