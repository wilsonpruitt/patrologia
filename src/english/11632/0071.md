## CHAPTER IV.

*Every man's sword upon his thigh, because of fears in the night.* *206* The companions diligently consider and more attentively wonder in what manner or order those threescore work the protection of the Virgin; and how provident, how sagacious they are for protecting her, and how they are held effectual not by valour only but by prudence of purpose. For each one, not laying down the sword once received, but setting upon his thigh the sword he is said to hold in his hand, is so held solicitous to war down from without whatever things may hurt, that he be not [0360A] negligent of himself, but that a diligent care of himself hold him. The thigh signifies the flesh, which is propagated from the thigh; and the flesh is weak, and is proved to fail naturally; and therefore by the thigh, or the flesh, is signified the weakness or defect which our firmness or advance incurs, as it were, from nature. For a man's virtue, to however great a magnitude it be raised aloft, has not of itself that it should be strengthened in its purpose; but if, grace forsaking it, it be relaxed a little from diligence, it is not held with a steady step, as though its wings were lost. Virtue therefore is seen to set its sword upon its thigh when, by the lucid word of the Lord, which is said to be more piercing than any sword, it worthily restrains and chastises its own weakness, and by the rigour of the word makes firm what it naturally searches out to be unavailing. It holds the sword upon the thigh when it worthily watches and strains over the guard [0360B] of itself, apprehending itself not only weak, but naturally weak; and for this cause, the more solicitous, it so guards itself against defect and ruin that it presses down the very causes of defect, as it were the thigh, with the discipline of the [sic: *verb*]. Thus assuredly, watching and straining to remove whatever things are hurtful, the Virgin's virtue rejoices not to fear from fears in the night; and all that the crafty enemy or a dark temptation strives to contrive, her learned and terrible circumspection renders utterly idle. Marking which, the angels, who are called the friends and companions of the Bridegroom, as was said before, wonder ineffably at this little bed of Solomon and at the guard of it; and they go on to commend it with yet other words, esteeming as little enough whatever they utter in the Virgin's praise.
