## CHAPTER V.

[0360C]

*King Solomon made him a ferculum of the wood of [sic: *Libam*], the pillars thereof he made of silver, the reclining-place of gold: the ascent of purple, the midst he strewed with charity, for the daughters of Jerusalem.* That which they had called a little bed, they call also a *ferculum*, whereon many *fercula* are borne to Solomon, and to him that feasts is set the food which perisheth not, but which endureth. The *ferculum*, then, is the Virgin, through whom the souls of the saints are found to be commended to God, and by her prayers and merits to be raised to the Bridegroom's banquet; whom for this very end the Bridegroom willed to be prepared for himself, that through her they might deserve those things which of themselves they could not be presented. For although the souls of the saints are marked with divers virtues, and, after the manner of a banquet, [0360D] are seasoned with a manifold savour of graces, yet they lie far from Solomon's banquet as though utterly forsaken, if they be not helped by the Virgin's intervention as by a table of offering. Therefore the Bride herself is rightly called the mediatrix of us all, herself the mother is not unfitly called the empress; because, entreating the bridegroom, commanding the son, she turns his fury into favour and his wrath into most sweet love, and by the little meal of her prayer converts us, bitter, into sweetness. They therefore who before were hateful with a wild bitterness, being taken up upon the *ferculum*, are made gracious by the merit of her that takes them up; nor is there any that deserves to please at Solomon's banquet, save him whom Solomon rejoices to have offered to him by the Virgin as from a table of propitiation. For of so great merit is the aforesaid *ferculum* or table, that whosoever have had its propitiation more diligently bestowed upon them [0361A] present themselves confidently at Solomon's banquet, while they that are strangers to the table absent themselves consequently from the banquet also. For it is made of the wood of Libanus. Libanus is a mountain which a conspicuous height lifts up, which its surface adorns, which the beauty of groves clothes; whose trees a tall greatness rears up, a wondrous appearance graces, an unrottable strength makes firm. And Libanus is interpreted *a whitening,* signifying Christ, who is the brightness of eternal light, and is prepared as the mountain of the house of the Lord: whose good tree is proved to bring forth no fruit but good; whose trees, planted beside the waters of Siloe, dry not up, are not stripped of their leaves in autumn, wither not in winter, but grow young again with an untiring length and an unrottable strength. Of this wood the companions assert the *ferculum* to be framed, so that [0361B] the work may rightly be seen to be commended, as it were, from its material; because the Virgin's sanctity is compacted out of Christ's virtues, and, unto the profit of us all, is brought into a table and a *ferculum*. And because mighty Solomon so compacted her of strong and unrottable material, she felt no worm, she admitted no rot, but drove it away; she was not hurt by the hammer, by the age of time not broken asunder, but the wholeness of her strength remained, and remained still, uncorrupted. But that this same table might not swerve from Solomon's presence, that it might never nor anywhere lean aside on any part, the same Solomon willed it to rest upon a noble stay, setting under it not unstable tripods, but pillars *207* immovable, of silver. For an eminent height presented the virginal table to Solomon, [0361C] and the silver and unbending straightness of the pillars held it unmoved, whose lucid manners, whose ringing repute, whose temperance of mouth, whose force of speech Solomon's benevolence accepted and made firm. Silver, making itself conspicuous by splendour, and giving a ring at a fitting touch, is wrought into pillars, and that which is laid upon it stands defended; since the Virgin, remaining a Virgin, brought forth the Word of the Father by the power of the Most High, and leaned firmly not upon her own strength but upon his. In her also a reclining-place of gold is said to be found, because nowhere did our Solomon seem to find a rest more welcome to him, where he might will or be able to lay down his head, and to bow the excellency of the Godhead to the taking of flesh. And this is not unfitly said to be, not of lead, but [0361D] rather of gold, because among all metals nothing is held better than gold; which is also dearer in price, and heavier in weight, and stronger under the hammer, so that of it the reclining-place is deservedly said to have been made. By the gold I judge the Virgin's love to be figured, which it is certain holds the first place among her other virtues; because, though faith and hope and very many others are worthy to be commended, yet none of them can deservedly be compared to love. Which love is so weighed down, balanced by its own weight, that it slips not away to the right or the left side by a swaying mobility, runs not hither and thither, wavers not, breaks not out to the loving of any other, embraces the bridegroom alone, and lets no third break off the embrace of the two. In fine, she desires to cleave to the bridegroom with a desire so strong, with a firmness so solid, that [0362A] she is not broken by adversities, nor does she give way under the hammer of tribulation; and when Peter cracked, and John sprang back, and all fled, not bearing the raging hammer, the Virgin alone stood fast, nor ceased to love the bridegroom dying under the eyes of the bride. Such a reclining-place, then, did Solomon frame for himself in the Virgin, which a love heavy and solid and to be compared with none commended, whereon the framer himself laid down his head away from the trouble of men; and, as one about to refresh them, invited us also to the same feast. But that same reclining-place is not easily found by such as we are, whereto there is no coming save with labour, save with very great effort: whereto (as is known to him that has tried it) he attains not more fully, save he that, being about to ascend, girds himself with a worthy strength. For there are steps or stairs by which the ascent is made to [0362B] the reclining-place, whereon no mean labour was bestowed by the Virgin; and because, rightly considering, she knew them to be not dark with soot but purple, by them she advanced herself, with an honourable likeness, even to the top. In the times of the ancients purple belonged to kings alone, which by its form or colour showed forth the royal dignity, so that when the common people observed that none was clothed in purple save the prince, they might be admonished to reverence him that was marked with royal signs. Because, therefore, to the reclining-place, wherein a welcome rest is granted to Solomon, one comes not by a presumptuous leap, but by an ascent of purple; the most prudent Virgin, when she desired to come to so great a merit, clothed herself in royal purple that she might be shown a queen. And she showed herself a queen by fitting tokens, [0362C] when she eagerly took upon her a discipline of a new kind, when she first dared to enter, consecrated by the chaste purpose of her mind, the path of virginal continence untried through all the ages behind her. She showed herself a queen when she put on over her the purple, that is, virtue, whereby she brought upon her most tender body a hard servitude; when she gave her blood, not poured out by the hand of the executioner, but consecrating to God alone the vessel of her virginal body, unpierced; she was zealous to distinguish herself a queen not with womanly but with purple garments, when by loftiness of mind she chose not to feel the enticements of the body, to have dominion over her own flesh — which (as I judge) is sufficiently known to none who, a runaway from the school, has shown himself untried in this exercise. The Virgin therefore, not confounded with a womanish softness, [0362D] but red and steeped in the martyrdom of a royal passion, came and attained to this, that she should be made the reclining-place of Solomon; and by an ascent that was none other than purple she advanced to so excellent a merit, that Solomon, reclining upon her, might take his food with an untiring hunger. But the midst of this *ferculum* Solomon strewed not with tapestries out of Egypt, nor with the passing newness of flowers, but by a counsel far better, with charity; that there might nowhere at all appear in it anything which the eye of him that reclined, being greedy of comeliness, would abhor. For to all the virtues whereby the Virgin is rendered chief and marked out, agreeing with the aforesaid *ferculum* by excellent and worthy merits, there is of necessity superadded the ornament of charity woven in, without which all the rest become as useless refuse. For nothing [0363A] sufficient can be laid in the aforesaid *ferculum* whereon it is not needful that a diffused charity be laid above: nay, all the things set round about are not worthily bound together, if their midst be not filled up with the cement of charity. In the midst of all things, therefore, charity is of necessity placed, that thence a kind of nourishment may be diffused to all that are set around; that all things rightly done may be held by a binding of this sort, lest they leap asunder one from another, and that, if perchance anything have settled amiss, it may have a sufficient covering. « For charity is also the bond of perfection [n: (Col. IV)]; » and it « covereth a multitude of sins [n: (I Petr. IV)]. » And this indeed I judge to have been said not so much for the Virgin's sake as for the daughters of Jerusalem, to whom it had been granted to be present *208* while the bridegroom and the bride discoursed together; lest, when they heard [0363B] pillars of silver, a reclining-place of gold, an ascent of purple proclaimed, they should not presume to think that they could reach to those things: and should seem to be driven back far off from that *ferculum*, by no likeness, not even a slight one, to be held near it. But when they hear that in the midst of those things charity is found, by whose welcome veil the manifoldness of sins is covered, they do not doubt that they too may belong to that *ferculum*, if they love, and choose to cleave more lovingly to him that loves. For although in that *ferculum* many things are recorded to be set which are not sufficiently to be matched with such as we are, but more especially with the Virgin, yet that charity is declared to be set in the midst of them is without doubt not held alien from the daughters of Jerusalem; but it profits and heals their mediocrity not mediocrely, [0363C] to whom in this both an easiness of drawing near and a not ineffectual hope of going forward is afforded. For such a *ferculum* did Solomon, the bridegroom, the bride, Christ the Virgin, will to set forth for himself, one that should deserve to be pre-eminent above all by the excellence of her virtues; and yet should not seem to forbid the daughters of Jerusalem from following after her, who, though not altogether in all things, yet at least for love's sake would cleave to her footsteps. But that they may cleave and may see in whom they ought to rejoice, the companions or angels who speak seem to admonish them: and they turn toward them, perhaps not so much their countenance as their speech, that, being thereby forewarned, they may hasten anxiously to see Solomon.
