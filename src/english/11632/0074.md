## CHAPTER VII.

*[CAP. IV.] How fair art thou, my friend, how fair art thou, thine eyes are of doves, without that which lieth hid within.* Because these words, as you may remember, are set down further back. and in the second book of this work are in some fashion expounded; they are not to be expounded again in this place [0366B] or at this time, but the solicitude of the readers is to be sent back to the aforesaid book. Yet in these words, as I see, somewhat is found changed, and this praise of the virgin is here forestalled by another particle; because there it was said: *Behold thou art fair, behold thou art fair;* but here; *How fair art thou, how fair.* There *ecce* is said, namely the showing of one that confirms; but here *quam* is set, namely the heightening of one that wonders there is understood only a testification that shows forth beauty, here a wonder that expresses much beauty. *How fair art thou,* he says, *my friend, how fair.* How greatly, he says, art thou fair, with how great a beauty art thou adorned, thou who art my *210* friend, and art proved the sharer of my secret; who can utter it with a voluble tongue, with a passing sound, or what hearer can take it in even if it be [0366C] uttered? For the more tenacious confirming of this there avails that which is here subjoined, which had not yet been said, and therefore was not expounded above; wherein it is intimated both that the Virgin's beauty is great, and that in that same beauty there is no crafty foulness: *Without that,* he says, *which lieth hid within.* There are some who have at hand an outward beauty, a fair surface, and a sheep-like one, but within the ravening of a wolf, a serpentine guile; and what lies open without is made comely by an overlaid hypocrisy, but what lies hid within is dark with a malicious foulness. There are others who, though they be not whitened without with a fraudulent hypocrisy, though a venomous assiduity infect them not within yet, while they are held in the body, are so [0366D] penally weighed down by a certain law of sin that sometimes, will they nill they, they slip into a fault, even a slight one; and because not whatsoever of evil our thought conceives can straightway appear to others, since even from us there most often lies hid that which in us deserves by its own desert to displease; when the Bridegroom says the Virgin to be without that which lieth hid within, he proves her free not only from evident faults, but from hidden faults also. Who is he among the saints that should glory that he is without that which lieth hid, and not rather, not understanding his own offences, pray to be cleansed from his hidden ones? who is he, I say, that, not anxious about hidden things, would serve the Lord in fear, fearing because not knowing whether he be worthy of hatred [0367A] or of love? [n: (Eccle. IX.)] The Virgin therefore, who was by no means conscious to herself of offences, and yet had not been ignorant of her own hidden things, the Bridegroom rightly avers to be fair once and again — in whom, as without, so also within, there was nothing unseemly. Whose beauty he pursues part by part, subjoining *Thy hairs,* etc.
