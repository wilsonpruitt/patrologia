## CHAPTER VIII.

*Thy hairs are as a flock of goats, which are come up from mount Galaath. Thy teeth as a flock of the shorn which are come up from the washing.* [var: Vulg. Cant. 4:1–2 has the plural *greges* in both clauses; the singular is the Vulgate's own reading at the parallel Cant. 6:4–5] By the hairs I judge that the senses of the body may be figured, which, coming forth from within, seek to be spread abroad without, and, liberty being received, are found to wander more widely than is just, unless perchance they be called back by censure and by the governance of rule. Whence Job also: [0367B] « I made a covenant with my [sic: *occulis*] [n: (Job XXXI)]; » And David: « Turn away, he says, mine eyes that they may not see vanity [n: (Psal. CXVIII)]. » And so of the rest. But that the hairs may worthily persevere unto the adorning of the head, they are fixed by the root in that same head and cleave to it; otherwise, when they are plucked up from the root, the welcome head of hair no longer remains, and, the hair being struck off, baldness dishonours the head. So also the senses of the body, which are all placed in the head, obtain their powers in it and work chiefly in it; if, the head being troubled, they be driven back as from their own seat, not only the head itself but the remaining members also are widowed of the greatest honour. But if, the head being sound, they have stood solid and tenacious as in a root, and have been effectual enough to fulfil their natural office, [0367C] then, after the manner of hairs sometimes straying too far, they are loosened by a wandering licence, if they be not restrained by a hand laid upon them and by a law set over them. The Virgin's hairs, therefore, a certain force of nature, as it were, renders well rooted, and the regular fitness of a binding renders them chastened without; when with a subtle providence she renders her senses solid in affection, and with a mighty censure restrains them from a dissolute effect; when in her heart she sets a measure to sensual affections, and upon them as they come forth without straightway lays the fillets of discipline. Her hairs also the Virgin worthily brings under censure, when she holds the senses of the inward mind more diligently under measure; when she suffers not those senses, by which she ought to perceive the things that are spiritual and vital, to consent by a wandering licence to things transitory and deadly. Senses of this kind, [0367D] whether bodily or none the less spiritual, the Bridegroom compares — that we, little ones, we who are still animal in our senses, may the more easily grasp what is said — to goats, which are the beasts of country folk, which are fed upon crags and on the very tops of the mountains above. And rightly did the Bridegroom will to compare the Virgin's senses to goats, because the Virgin, despising the lower things, loves to aspire to the highest alone; and though with a swifter leap she apprehends, in contemplating and in loving, things inaccessible to us, yet after a rustic manner she regards herself as lowly and base-born. « He hath regarded, she says, the humility of his handmaid [n: (Luc. I)]. » And expressly he compared the Virgin's hairs not to goats but to a flock of goats, wherein he invited the diligent hearer to understand the gathering together of the senses; because no wandering running to and fro, [0368A] no stealthy loosening drove them apart one from another, but a chaste confederation so brought them into a flock, under the shepherd's care and staff, that no wild beast would dare to break in upon any one of them, but, with hunger wearying it, would stand stupefied with jaws open and untried. But that goats of this kind may be yet more commended, they are declared not to come down but to come up from mount Galaath; whereas rather, as from the plain to the heights of *211* the mountain an ascent is made, so from the mountain to the level places there is, by the proper signification of the words, a descent. « Coming down, he says, from the mountain, Jesus stood in a plain place [n: (Luc. VI)]. » [var: Vulg. Luc. 6:17 *descendens cum illis*, "coming down with them"; the received text names no mountain in this clause] And of Moses: « Moses went up into the mountain, and was there forty days [n: (Exod. XXIV)]. » And below, the Lord to Moses: « Go down, he says, thy people hath sinned [n: (Exod. XXXII)]. » Since therefore of a mountain [0368B] not an ascent but a descent may more rightly be spoken, what does it mean that the aforesaid goats are recorded not to come down but to come up from mount Galaath — unless that great advances of the hairs, that is, of the senses, are indicated, to which that literal context, being referred, to find a worthy fitness, which does not seem literally to fit material goats? For it was not the holy Spirit's care to dispute concerning the ascent of goats, but to demonstrate the Virgin's advances by likenesses of this kind to us that are weaker; who takes pains to set out from mountain to mountain, that is, into virtue from virtue, for the grace of a spiritual pasture, and to obtain not the ridges of the mountains but the heights of advances. Whence also this mountain, of which the discourse is, is named Galaath, which the judgment of the Hebrews declares to be interpreted *a heap of testimony*; [0368C] because when Laban, the fury of his threats and of his stubborn anger being appeased, was disposing to enter a covenant of peace with his kinsman Jacob, they raised a heap of stones, the stones being gathered by an equal hand, which they left to the generations that should come after as a testimony of the peace entered upon. But no one's excellence seems to bear testimony to Christ more than the Virgin's love, which is found by its own merit to be pre-eminent above the rest; which is heaped up of living and chosen stones, and is raised for increase, and gives an indelible monument as well to those that are present as to those that are to come. The Virgin therefore sets out and comes up from mount Galaath, when she apprehends the summit of sanctity by virtues heaped together; and from it she does not, as one coming down, [0368D] hunt after her own glory, but with a fitting advance bears witness to Christ's mighty mercy alone. « He that is mighty, she says, hath done great things to me [n: (Luc. I)]. » Wherefore the Bridegroom also praises the Virgin's teeth consequently, that not only in the hairs may her comeliness evidently appear, but that in the teeth also no mean usefulness may be shown, if only what is said be attended to not literally but spiritually. *Thy teeth,* he says, *are as a flock of the shorn which are come up from the washing.* The Virgin's teeth root out with a strong bite the pestilence of heresies, lest the seeds of falsehood should mightily gainsay the truth; and they chew the hardest saying concerning the incarnate Word with fitting assertions, that little ones may now the more easily give themselves over to this belief. Which teeth are not unfitly compared to a flock of sheep [0369A] — not of those which are weighed down by the hard trouble of fleece and of sweat, but of those which, their fleeces being shorn, show that they have now nothing superfluous, and which reprehend by the washing of confession whatsoever they may seem to have had. The Virgin therefore, who burns with the lawful love of him that loves her, is rightly said, as a Bride, to come up from the washing as one shorn; because she was zealous to be the more cleanly freed of the superfluous burdens of faults, the more she willed to be found worthy of belief for the commending of the Bridegroom. Concerning which it is subjoined: *All with twins,* etc.

## CHAPTER IX

*All with twin young, and there is not [sic: sternis] among them.* All, that is to say, both the goats that came up from mount Galaath and hung with undaunted step from its highest top, and the sheep that washed themselves, their fleeces being shorn, [0369B] and, coming forth from the washing, lacked the hard weight of fleece and of sweat. All, I say, are so fruitful by an equal grace, so pleasant by the large benefit of fecundity, that among them not one is condemned of barrenness, and each rejoices with an ampler gift and at once with a twin birth. A thing worthy of wonder, that in the flock neither goat nor sheep was found barren, that none of the fruitful ones was content with a single birth; that the Virgin's virtues, which we have taken to be figured by hairs or by teeth, are both made fruitful by love, and are not held content with a single love. For they love God, they love man also, and they despise neither God for man's sake nor man for God's sake, but according to the new law and the old they venerate the first commandment and the second [0369C] like unto it; while in the same Christ they embrace with twin arms the twin substance of the giant. For in the one Christ, whom the Virgin loves to have as bridegroom and as son, she sees two substances cohere personally one to the other, whereof the nature is indeed diverse and unlike (being, as it is, of God and of man), which nevertheless constitute in their joining one Christ, one person. To love him more tenderly and to be gladdened with a tender love, this is to be not barren, and to be made fruitful with twin young; because the holy Spirit has not much care concerning goats or sheep, but rather concerning virtues and the commendable offspring of virtues.
