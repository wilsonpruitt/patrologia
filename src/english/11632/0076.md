## CHAPTER X.

*Thy lips are as a scarlet fillet; and thy speech sweet.* The Virgin's lips, being closed, keep the knowledge of the law, [0369D] and what they have known to be kept they heap together in the secret chamber of the heart; whose closure deserves not to be accused of an unfruitful silence, since a wise man holds his peace until a time, and the heart of him that is silent meditates prudence. In fine: « Mary kept, he says, all these things, pondering them in her heart [n: (Luc. II)]. » And when she is shaken by the hand of one that knocks, that the things gathered and kept may be brought forth, and the bolt or bar is at last struck off from her closed lips, she, obedient and now receiving the time of speaking, knocks in her turn, and brings forth nothing but discipline to them that have ears of hearing. For the obtaining whereof the prophet wills to have a door, namely that he may be able to restrain his lips by a fitting law; lest either he see, and repent, that he is pressed down by an immoderate silence, or, by a fault no less, [0370A] pass over into excess by a light much-speaking. « Set, he says, O Lord, a door of enclosure about my lips [n: (Psal. CXL)]. » O Lord, he says, give a fitting closure to my lips, give none the less an opening when the time shall be, that upon the hinge of discretion they may so turn as a door, that they be neither closed for keeping silence, nor loosed for speaking save at a fitting time. « Woe is me, saith Isaias, because I have held my peace [n: (Isa. VI)]. » And another on the contrary: « A wordy man, he says, shall not be justified [n: (Job. II)]. » [var: Vulg. Job 11:2 prints the clause as a question, *aut vir verbosus iustificabitur?*, with no negative particle in it] For the avoiding of the danger on either side the Virgin's lips found an efficacy, and by a worthy guard forestalled the faults both of silence and of much-speaking; which, lest they should speak superfluous things, pressed themselves together with a laudable closure, and at an opportune time uttered [0370B] what had seemed honourable, what useful. Wherefore they are not undeservedly compared to a scarlet fillet, whereby the hairs of the head are called back lest they flow out licentiously; because a girl's hair, lest it stray by its native lightness, is bound and conquered, and is pressed down by the power of rule. For to this end the Virgin prepares for herself a fillet clean and fine, that when with the process of time she shall have felt her youthful hair grow, she may set the one upon the other with a fitting governance, lest the hair display itself either at an unlawful time or in a disordered fashion. Further, when the prudent Virgin shall have seen the time draw near wherein it is fitting that her hair appear more freely and more openly, with a diligent hand she looses the fine fillet, and for a time frees and absolves her hair from its rule, as it were. And the hair now seems the more welcome to the eyes of beholders, the longer it is held before under the fillet's [0370C] guard; and, lying hid under a seal, it is composed and cherished, and earns to obtain a welcome beauty, which shines out the more honourably when, the fillet being laid aside, it is now permitted to go forth to the sight of men, if only it never cease to remember the fillet laid aside. So assuredly, when the discretion of him that speaks shall have been instructed under silence, and polished by a long stretch of time with lips knit together, then at last, they being loosed, the utterance leaves its native workshop, and, going forth into the ears of men, resounds a fitting discipline. Whence it is well added: *And thy speech sweet.* For when the lips, after the manner of a fillet, shall have worthily pressed together the inward affections, and shall have prudently formed with the hammer and anvil of silence the things that are to be brought forth, then their loosing, like the laying aside of the fillet, is not reprehended, grows not cheap, but [0370D] the timely utterance pleases, and the speech grows sweet to them that hear. Otherwise both the word is darkened to no purpose and the speech grows bitter, which is not formed under the lips nor grows together in silence: but for that which is to be brought forth the lips do not first hold the office of a fillet; but whatsoever shall have come into the mouth they bring forth unskilfully with open mouth. But what is it that the Virgin's lips are matched with a fillet that is none other than scarlet, unless because in this also they are preferred to ours, which are far unlike them — for our lips, though they show themselves to be closed and opened at divers times, yet to them that worthily look upon them represent not scarlet but perhaps rather mire? The scarlet, bearing the appearance of fire, rightly signifies love, which, being fiery, or rather being fire, purifies gold as in a furnace; which renders the just, more precious [0371A] than gold, fervent and burning; which burns yet consumes not, but refines and makes them that love clean. To this scarlet, or to this fire, the Virgin's lips are likened, which as well by silence as by speech serve a tender love; which, knowing no mire, grow not pale like ashes, but with a scarlet redness modestly glow ruddy and take fire. Closed indeed and given to silence, they are at leisure for God, they meditate on the ineffable, they cleave to the invisible, they rejoice in him, they suspend themselves with fervent desire toward things inward and eternal, and hold with a greedy embrace him whom they apprehend to be incomprehensible. « How great, they say, is the multitude of thy sweetness, O Lord, which thou hast hidden for them that fear thee [n: (Psal. XXX)]. » But being loosed, and yet not dissolute, they stretch themselves out in outward things, they set about speaking, in speaking they condescend to their neighbours, [0371B] and what they had tasted beforehand within they now bestow without upon the needy, and in this none the less show themselves to burn or to be red with scarlet. « For the sake of my brethren, saith he, and of my neighbours, I spoke peace of thee [n: (Psal. CXXI)]. » Because therefore the Virgin's lips are graced with the likeness of a fillet, and are irreprehensibly commended by a red scarlet or a fiery love, then, as her silence and her meditation *213* know no shadow of bitterness, so her speech shines again with the grace of sweetness.
