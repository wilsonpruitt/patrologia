## CHAPTER XI.

*As a fragment of a pomegranate, so are thy cheeks, without that which lieth hid within.* Because so great is the beauty of this Virgin whom we have in hand, that our dullness cannot [0371C] easily grasp it clearly, her praiser, choosing to have pity on our infirmity, sets forth divers likenesses, that by them we may in some fashion behold her. Of which, although none can be esteemed sufficient, yet some seems in part fitting, so far only as material things can be fitted to spiritual, mean things be likened to beautiful, transitory things to abiding. Hence it is that the Virgin's cheeks, which bloom with a shamefast redness, are likened to a pomegranate, which shines with a ruddy rind, which, when it is broken, shows within a gathered multitude of grains, wherein there shines also no small beauty of whiteness. In the cheeks of a young girl, as on the eminence of the face, the excellence of beauty sets its throne for itself; and sitting there [0371D] it obtains a royal princedom, one that leans upon grace, not strengthened by privilege. Purposing therefore to commend the Virgin's beauty, the Bridegroom loves to compare her cheeks to a pomegranate, nay, to a fragment of one; that the double colour of one fragment, red without, snowy within, may speak the Virgin's praises, may sound with an equal proclamation, and that neither may gainsay the other. The red colour, bearing the form of blood, is a type of the passion which the holy Virgin sustains out of desire for her Solomon; who, whatsoever the world sounds forth by promises or thunders by terrors, spurns with a strong mind, drives off with an even foot, and distinguishes not the greatest things from the least. To shearings and pressures she opposes her rind as a shield, strongly embracing that commendable ordinance of Solomon, whereby he promises him blessed [sic: *lore*] who [0372A] now dashes the little ones against the rock, and that everyone must suffer who disdains not to live godly. And so the Virgin, patient at pressures and breakings, sickens not, nay (if she be not pressed, if she be not broken) she blushes; but broken and bruised, she grows not pale with a wan failing, but is made conspicuous by an inward comeliness, and grows white with a cheerful conscience. In fine, the pomegranate not yet broken gives a redness pleasing to the sight, but broken it gives a savour agreeable to the taste; not yet broken it gives to open nostrils an odour of wondrous sweetness, but broken it lessens it not, but reaches out one far ampler. So too the Virgin, not pressed down, not shaken by hammer or by torturer, shines with a Punic colour, is red with desire or with love; whose conversation is ruddy to the eyes of beholders, flourishing with a ripe deed, with a modest look, with a shamefast face. But if [0372B] a heavy hand press her, break her, and reduce her as it were to a morsel, and if a sword or an unsweet tribulation pass through not so much her body as her soul, no blackness is foul within, but another comeliness appears, of no less grace — namely an inward purity, and the cheerful security of a cleaner conscience. Whosoever is wise, worthily considering such a fragment, takes its sweetness not with the tooth but rather with the mind, and understands it to be gracious to the eyes not only by whiteness or by redness, but fruitful by sweetness and savour to the inward palate and to a sound taste. Whose odour also, or repute, then chiefly grows fragrant, when not only her works but her conscience also, being sifted, grows clear; when her patience is beaten with pressures and breakings, her mind is shaken, and in her there is nothing but a gathering of virtues and the whiteness or cleanness [0372C] of them displayed. This beauty of the Virgin, which can in some fashion be known both by herself and by another — if indeed any man can be found to know it — the Bridegroom praises under the name of cheeks, which display themselves in the face, and represent, after the manner of the aforesaid fragment, a twin comeliness of redness and of whiteness. But that which for the present he gives not to the Virgin, but sets forth, he expounds not by praises or comparisons of this kind: rather he wills that chief thing to lie hid from us who are too weak to take it in, that he may show it to be far more excellently pre-eminent than all the things here received. *Without that,* he says, *which lieth hid within.* From that, he says, which lies open, O Virgin, thou must be proclaimed as one to be praised — from the gifts of grace which can in some fashion be compared [0372D] with temporal things. *Without that,* that is, that being left unspoken which as yet is not seen but hidden, which, being utterly incomparable, is held far from the eyes under a seal. From which a chief praise would worthily be given thee, if it either could be said, or, being said, could be taken in by men; but what the eye seeth not, the ear beareth not, what ascendeth not into the heart of man, the present speech purposes not to expound. Sparing therefore those whom I shall bring into the auditory of this drama, I will bring myself back to the setting forth of those things which shall be able to be taken in: and, that being left unspoken which the understanding of mortals is not yet able to take in, speaking after the manner of men I will discourse of thy praises and of thy advances. *214*

## CHAPTER XII.

*Thy neck is as the tower of David, which is built [0373A] with bulwarks.* Because in the cheeks, compared to a pomegranate or to a fragment of one, the Virgin's beauty has been commended, in the neck her strength is consequently commended, which bends not itself to the weak and needy and transitory things of the world, and turns not aside its own uprightness from its purpose by a nerveless softness. She indeed sustains the presence of this life, the pack of the flesh, a heavy load, as a sweet thing, by a strong patience, by the constancy of patience; and whatsoever the world strikes in — the world which buffets the necks of all that are present — shakes her not from her standing, nor scandalizes her after the manner of the weak. For she is filled with so great love, with so great fervour of spirit, that she weighs burdens at a cheap rate, as one accustomed to such things; and after the manner of a heifer that has been taught to love the yoke or the threshing, she esteems the practice of bearing heavy things a fruit, not a hurt. Wherefore [0373B] her neck is declared like to a tower, which, fixed by its own weight, is not moved by the driving of winds or the dashing of floods; which is of so great strength that it yields not its place to the power of wicked spirits, and confers an unassailable defence upon the whole city. Whence also it is called David's, which is interpreted *strong of hand,* signifying Christ, whose hand, unassailable and unconquered, the good feel ready for help, the evil for vengeance. A tower of this kind is raised in Jerusalem to this end, that with a circumspect solicitude it may be able to foresee enemies from afar; and, if perchance they presume to draw near, that it may defend itself from their inroads or assaults, and by its own strength make light of whatsoever they bring against it. Nor does its defence profit itself alone, but the whole city also, to which it gives both a necessary [0373C] strength and a not unseemly ornament; to which the weaker city runs back at once if the enemy assail, and it, being mightier, gives confidence to her that runs back, and succours her. So too the Virgin, after the manner of David's tower, descries the enemy from afar, nay looks down upon him, and cares not; at whose look he shudders and flees back, and endures not in his purpose of confidence; and whatsoever of threats or of frauds he shall have presumed to contrive is dashed out, and the malice of him that presumes is dashed back upon himself: so great a horror does the face of her that beholds strike in, so mightily does it beat back the enemy. For the tower is built with its own bulwarks. Bulwarks cleaving to a tower seem to jut out from without, to fortify the tower strongly, to hang heavily over the enemies; from which poles, darts, stones and the rest of such things [0373D] are thrown, whereby, the tower being defended, the enemies are mightily shaken. These, as I judge, are the precepts or admonitions of Holy Scripture, the utterances of the prophets, the spiritual knowledge of letters; which it is agreed cleave with a steadfast memory to the Virgin's strong mind, and, by a manifold advance of works, keep off further whatsoever things are hurtful. For the subtlety of Holy Scripture and a spiritual understanding, a tenacious memory of the commandments, a manifest literal knowledge, render the Virgin's conscience — their own jutting-forth — unshaken, and the pressing of the enemies beaten back by ready hurlings of works. With bulwarks of this kind David's tower defends itself, and stretches itself out, as with certain arms, to beat back the wicked: because the unbending strength of the Virgin (which was said to be figured by the neck) rejoices to be strengthened by the patronage of Holy Scripture set beside it. [0374A] For there she receives that whereby she may be held stronger and more secure, whereby she may repress enemies and faithfully protect friends; which seems to cleave to her the more firmly for her own defence, but for ours to hang from her by a benefit, if not an equal one, yet not a contemptible one. Whence it is subjoined: *A thousand,* etc.
