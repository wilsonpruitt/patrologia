## CHAPTER XIV.

*Thy two breasts are like two fawns, twins of a roe, that feed among the lilies, until the day breathe, and the shadows be bowed down.* Far above, the Virgin commended the Bridegroom's breasts, and affirmed them better than wine, fragrant with the *216* best ointments; which the Bridegroom perchance remembering, and although a delay as it were of time has come between, allowed not her breasts likewise to pass by without praises. After the neck, therefore, which he compared to a most fortified tower, passing to the breasts, he fitted to them also a wondrous likeness, bringing forward not something of the same kind according to the letter, but fawns that feed [0376B] rather in the time of shadows than of day. The breasts signify, as I seem to see, that fruitfulness of the Virgin whereby she cherishes and advances our littleness who are infirm, whereby she gives us suck and weans us not, until she make us come to advancement, until the day arrive, and the night at length, the shadows growing thin, come to its failing. But it moves me that, bringing forward the Virgin's breasts, he says *two*, whereas according to the letter any woman's breasts are not but two: and therefore, if he had said only "breasts", keeping silence as to *two,* yet no one rightly wise would understand any but two. But in this perchance he willed to signify that the fruitfulness of which I spoke is twofold, because a single one would not pour out for us a bounty full enough; whereas the double suffices to nourish us by a joined benefit, and no sound man seeks to find a third, nor anything beyond it. For the [0376C] Virgin has a certain affection of a more bountiful benevolence toward those whom she perceives to need a stronger patronage, whom, redeemed by her son, she considers to will and to strive, and yet, weighed down by their own burden, not to be permitted to accomplish. The Virgin loves therefore to milk out a tender breast for such, and by benevolent affection to advance the weak to whatsoever is valiant: the holy Mother holding it certain that it pleases not the redeeming Son that those should perish whom he willed not to hold guilty of the original debt. But since that breast and affection of benevolence would be held insufficient if to the kindly affection there were not joined a more powerful effect, the Virgin has and obtains from the Son efficacy or effect, that she may effectually lead the weak on to such advancement as she wills. For on whom she has mercy with affectionate solicitude, him she succours and heals with efficacious power; [0376D] and with one breast as it were she bends herself to have compassion on the wretched, and with the other she now proffers to the needy the necessary help. Out of these two breasts, then — namely affection and effect — the Virgin pours into us nourishing milk, and with solicitous hand hides us from the whirlwind and from the rain; and whatsoever the lion thunders she drives back and beats back with more powerful threats, and heals with healing milk whatsoever the venomous asp pours over us. Whence also the breasts are not unfittingly compared to fawns, which, spurning what is hurtful, freely betake themselves to what is useful; which from afar see and discern what is fitting with a keen eye, and pass over whatever is harmful by a leap or by efficacious power. Which assuredly they are agreed to have derived from their mother the roe, on whom mighty nature is believed to have conferred that gift, [0377A] that she may see more sharply and discern more discreetly whatsoever shall profit, and more solicitously spurn and more swiftly pass by what shall hurt. So the affection and effect of the Virgin obtain this virtue from their mother grace, that they may discreetly see through whom they are solicitously to advance to salvation; but may pass by those whom they have perceived not to pertain to grace, and may abhor the filthy, and permit the reprobate to be the more filthy. And that the fawns are asserted to be not unlike but rather twins, in that, begotten of one mother, they are alike nourished with one milk, seems to agree with the aforesaid affection and effect, which one mother, grace, willed to bring forth by a grateful benefit. Which assuredly are associated by a certain brotherhood, by a conjoining covenant, while what the first undertakes the second follows up with completing work; nor does the former aim at anything [0377B] but what the latter is able to consummate, nor does the second accomplish anything but what it sees the first more diligently to aim at. They feed among the lilies so long as they are delighted with the virtues of them that advance, who prosper by the whiteness of their manners and the odour of good testimony; who both within grow more wholesomely enkindled, not with blushing but with love, and without grow white, as with leaves, by whitened works. And these indeed cease not to prosper by the ample benefit of the virgin, and the Virgin ceases not likewise to rejoice at their advances: until, their spirits being polished to a nicety, a sweeter day shall breathe, and the darkness of the shadows, impatient of this day, shall breathe its last. For when the morning light that knows no evening draws near, the night of present ignorance and of all trouble withdraws; and the gathered lilies are offered with dancing to the highest King, in whose [0377C] sanctuary they enjoy a sevenfold and unfading comeliness. Where there is no hunger, where now the fawns feed not, where the Virgin's breasts are poured into none that advance, where the Virgin now brings up none, instructs no one, has mercy on no one, because there infancy, there ignorance, there also misery is not had. Here therefore the Virgin feeds, where she is declared still to feed us, where, the shadows still standing, the face of the day is not seen, where, the day being now bright, a man is not taught by his neighbour, where, according to the prophecy [n: (Jer. XXXI)], God shall be known from the lesser even to the greatest. But the bride's breasts being praised, the bridegroom, as it were withdrawing himself from her and giving himself back to his companions, speaks to them, but of her, because for one that loves tenderly the solicitude concerning her is so vehement [0377D] that in this song he knows not how to speak save to her or of her. *217*

## CHAPTER XV.

*I will go to the mountain of myrrh, and to the hill of frankincense.* It suffices not a lover to go to the mountain which he loves, but he chooses also to anticipate his journey with a word; and while he defers rather than omits to revisit it, with a word running before he promises to go afterward. If, he says, I have withdrawn myself a little from the mountain's presence, yet I have not drawn back from it my memory or my tender love: nor have I contracted for myself contempt of it, or anger, or hatred, but have thus the more strongly drawn to me its love and desire toward me. Now the mountain, is the loftiness of this our virgin, which a fixed and immovable firmness renders commendable, [0378A] against which, although the blasts of the winds, in an array as it were drawn up, strike threats from this side and that, yet they cast not the mountain down from grace, nor from its abiding state. For against this virgin the enemies frequently hurl arrows and darts, and, their malice going forward, add the assaults of battering-rams and heavy engines; all which the virgin's mountainous loftiness and unmoved firmness cast far back, and, their efforts being made void, hurl the enemies, more harshly beaten back, to the depths. For whether the adjured hand of the mighty one be reckoned present or absent, yet so great is the love toward the virgin, so great is the bridegroom's care concerning the virgin, that she, after the manner of a mountain, is secure by his patronage, and whatsoever the raging fury of the winds shall bring in is made void. For when the bridegroom seems to withdraw from her, she permits not the withdrawal; sometimes, absenting himself, he promises to return the [0378B] sooner; and by that counsel which he himself knows he declares that he goes and returns, according as he knows it to be much expedient for himself and most of all for the virgin. Withdrawing as it were from the virgin, yet he escapes not the meshes of love; absented a little, he goes thither again; and neither the approach nor the withdrawal takes service under torpor but under love, that they may be more closely bound and cleave to one another with a stronger thong. To this end the bridegroom withdraws himself, that he may not now grow cheap by the giving of abundance of himself; to this end he goes to the virgin, that love may the more tenderly break out afresh, that he who loves and wills to be more loved by the virgin may by that furnace make her to be the more vehemently inflamed. He goes therefore to the virgin, that her love may burn again more ardently, and that, she loving more, he himself may be the more gladdened; that when he shall have felt her the more scorched with heat or with love, he may love her as she advances and cherish her with a more grateful embrace.
