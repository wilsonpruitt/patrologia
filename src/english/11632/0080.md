## CHAPTER XVI.

[0378C]

Whence another translation has: *I will go for myself to the mountain of myrrh.* I will go to the Virgin, and lest I seem to go in vain, I will go for myself — seeking indeed to take counsel for her, seeking to satisfy her, but for myself also: assuredly I shall go thither not empty, not superfluous, but I shall both bestow upon her and shall bring much to myself when I return. I will go to the virgin, whose height myrrh now marvellously claims, whose unbroken flesh no native corruption disfigures: whose integrity a wondrous chastisement renders excellent above all women, and whose excellence it renders solid and not swelling. Great is this mountain, which is agreed to be so devoted to chastised acts that it may be called, not soft and flowing, but specially a mountain of myrrh: in that no [0378D] enticing softness enervated the virgin, but an untouched rigour and the myrrh of continence preserved her. Whom also the bridegroom not unfittingly names a hill of frankincense, in that she scatters a good report both to those present and to those to come: and, being kindled by coals and by the violence of heat or of love, she is lifted up into a heap and gives to God a sacrifice of odour. Whose devotion, whose fervent affection is sweeter than all frankincense, offering itself to the Creator, bearing itself above the creature; she is a mountain that knows not how to bring forth a laughing-stock, but a hill that chooses to think nothing but humble things of itself. When she restrains and extinguishes and suffers not the allurements of the flesh so much as to mutter, when she strives to forestall with most approved myrrh the affections that breed worms, when she applies a yoke to her own wills and lays down a law, when she restrains and composes with salt whatsoever the flesh savours, [0379A] then assuredly she is a mountain, to which so incomparable a height is at hand, great virtue, great constancy, great valour of wisdom. But when she looses herself from transitory pursuits and works, and turns over in mind how sweet to the inward throat are the things of heaven, when with fiery love she resolves herself into liquid frankincense, and, forgetful of earthly things, wraps herself wholly in the contemplation of things above, then assuredly she is made a hill, while the loftier she is in merits and the more devout, so much the humbler in heart and, as it were, not far removed from the rest. And when the angel found her alone in her chamber, given over to meditation, and the Lord himself who had sent him had already come before him, the messenger of the childbearing to come; she, hanging upon the word of the Angel — nay, upon the word of the Father — with most devout love, and condescending to us that are lower and not yet capable of things great enough: [0379B] « Behold, she says, the handmaid of the Lord [n: (Luc. I)]. » When by a new grace the virgin's mind surpassed our level ground, and he who came between as a messenger *218* saw himself forestalled, with how great humility did the most devout Virgin press herself down, who, being already called the mother of God, granted to herself no name but that of a little handmaid? A strong fire being applied, the frankincense melts into a cloud of smoke, the smoke is lifted on high, the sweetness of the odour now grows fragrant: far and wide the world is seen to be taken up with the excellence of this mountain, and yet the humble Virgin still constrains herself to be called a hill. To this mountain by merits, this hill by humility, the bridegroom pledges himself to go, and, present sooner than said, he now answers the Virgin as she speaks; or rather he himself comes before her as she is about to speak, and commends her, that she may kindle herself the more closely into love of him that commends her.

## CHAPTER XVII.

[0379C]

*Thou art all fair, my friend, and there is not a spot in thee.* From the time that the Angel announces to the Virgin the word enjoined him and her salutation, and the holy Spirit comes upon her and, overshadowing her, gives virtue: in her the iniquitous law obtains not its privilege, nor does any defilement of sin, recent or ancient, remain. Now one woman is foul, another all foul, another fair, and another all fair. Foul indeed is she whom the ancient wrath, the law of nature, darkens and bows down, which contracts guilt for itself from the beginning and from the vice of birth, whose difficulty is so overmastering, so inextricable, that neither virtue nor faculty suffices to be utterly without it. But she that is all foul subjects her corrupted will to the depraved law, [0379D] and by a pestilent consent fashions an execrable unity, while what the oppressive law suggests, or rather thrusts in, she follows up with her will and obeys with completing action. On the other side, she is fair who understands the written law, the sequence of the commandments, to be good, and, the spirit now granting it, loves that it has been given, and is advanced by voluntary instinct to fulfil it more diligently, but does not yet deserve to obtain the power to prevail. To will indeed lies close at hand, but she experiences a contradictory struggle, in that the door of the difficulty is not yet opened to her will; while the one knocks strongly, strives obstinately and presses on, the other beats back more strongly, struggles more stubbornly and resists. The one, tending her face with the hand of diligence, wills to strive that, every spot being wiped away, she may be let in to what she desires; the other busies herself to resist [0380A] and struggle against this purpose, and sometimes, being the more powerful, rejoices to be sent forward to victory. But she is all fair whom the vicious law, the reproach of nature, the disease of wrath, no longer press down, no longer oppress, are not able to hinder, but that the will is provoked even by desire to repel faults, and a worthy possibility supports it as it goes before. She is all fair whose keenness of mind is vigorous, whose spirit sickens not, whose flesh is quiet and grows not insolent against the spirit; whose spirit, enduring no rebellion from what is subject to it, obeys God alone and no longer halts in the precept. She is all fair who by a rare kind of example has now come to this height, upon whom so great a grace of the overshadowing spirit has come, whose every spot the powerful hand of mercy has so wiped away that not only [0380B] guilt but even the anxious fear of guilt does not remain. That any one like ourselves should be able to obtain this, while this time rolls on and he groans under it and complains that he remains — that I may confess the truth to you, I take not to myself any certain knowledge, and therefore I presume not rashly to affirm what I know not. For although the light of the new grace is poured into our eyes, at whose rising the darkness poured over us by our parents' fault has perished, yet there is a certain spot as it were imprinted upon our face, under whose urging, while we live here, we are guilty at least in some small measure. Of which it is written: « No man is clean from filth [n: (Job XV)].--There is not a man that sinneth not [n: (II Par. VI)]. » [var: Vulg. has no clause in this form; nearest are Job 14:4 *Quis potest facere mundum de immundo conceptum semine?* and Job 15:14 *Quid est homo ut immaculatus sit* — the chapter Migne's own note cites] A spot of this kind the severe law seems to have imprinted upon us wretches, and with this as with a branding-iron to have stained our beauty, so that although we strive to polish it away with the hand [0380C] of solicitude, yet we cannot wholly free it from this injurious right. Now a spot is a more tenacious mole, an unseemly brand, a discordant foulness, wherewith the beauty lying next and bordering upon it grieves to be infected, or, that I may say more, to be affected; to the wiping away of which, although the hand applies much care, yet it removes not what has now grown in and turned into nature. But in this virgin, if there was in her aught of natural vice, the spiritual grace that comes upon her cleanses and unspots it; and what in us perseveres more harshly by a wretched and tenacious law, in her the hand of grace washes away with efficacious medicine. And because the general iniquity of us all darkens her not, but in the bridegroom's sight she now flashes with a special excellence of merits, rightly does the bridegroom [0380D] love her the more tenderly and invite her, that, coming to him, she may commit herself to him more devoutly and more attentively.
