## CHAPTER XVIII.

*Come from Libanus, my Bride, come from Libanus, come.* Her that is all fair, so that she is not held even by a light spot — lest perchance she seem to have given her labour to beauty without fruit — the bridegroom admonishes to come: namely from great beauty to *219* greater, that with a learned foot she may pass from that which has been given to the giver. For it is not granted to any one to remain in the things he has received, and to cleave to them with all his desire and with grasping hands, but rather, not out of contempt or hatred but out of advancement and counsel, to turn back the eyes of the mind from these to him who bestowed them, and to hold more dearly not the things themselves but himself now, while the things are held for his sake. Libanus [0381A] seems to me to designate that whiteness to which the bridegroom willed to assign the virgin — nay rather, which he willed rather to assign to the virgin — the whiteness, namely, of continence and of virginal purity, which the son conferred upon his bride and mother by the gift of his own goodness. For the bridegroom willed his own mother to dwell in whiteness, that her excellence might deserve to be commended as it were from the place; that the mountain Libanus itself, that is, the whiteness of virginal excellence, might so consecrate her that was fruitful, that her fruitfulness might become spiritual. Although, if it may be said — nay perhaps because it may — not so much the place seems to consecrate the virgin as the Virgin the place, nor is the virgin held commendable from the mountain, but the mountain from the virgin; nor has this whiteness so much exalted the virgin as the virgin has exalted this whiteness and rendered it altogether more grateful in the eyes of them that worthily consider. For even before the virgin, as mount Libanus, [0381B] so also virginity can be found, but it is said to be marked with little honour or with none; nor was any one found to be commended for virginal comeliness, or by whose merit virginity itself might rejoice to be made illustrious. To our time indeed and to this our virgin we know this to have been reserved, that, being exalted by this mountain, she should rather render the mountain itself exalted, and in a wondrous manner should herself, being the whiter, whiten Libanus, and by her own merit adorn the virginal comeliness. Great therefore is the Virgin, and incomparably to be proclaimed, to be compared with no women before her, with none that come after, who brought merit to the virginal whiteness that had before no merit or honour, and conferred on it the gift of a fuller grace. That therefore which for many ages back was among women unseemly and burdensome, from that time and thenceforward is rendered pleasing [0381C] and beautifying; and that which the daughter of Jephte, slighting it, as it were devotes and bewails, the rod of Jesse, prudently choosing it, lifts up more sacredly and honours. But because the Virgin has nothing of grace, nothing of merit, that is not received, from him who gave it she hears and receives this precept: that from so great a whiteness, in the gift whereof she first rejoices, she should refer herself to him by whose authorship virginity itself is made fruitful. *Come,* he says, *from Libanus, my bride.* Thou, he says, that hast whitened mount Libanus with a new gift, that hast exalted virginal whiteness by the excellence of thy merits, come — yet not laying aside that virginal comeliness, but, not content with it, preferring the bridegroom's grace before it. Come, not that thou now desist from the purpose of virginity, but that thou be joined more nearly to me the giver and [0381D] stand by me; that thou who rejoicest above the solid ground in virginal whiteness mayest exult the more readily in the privilege of a bride by a nuptial covenant. Therefore it is said: *Come from Libanus, bride.* But when it is repeated: *Come from Libanus,* and *bride,* is not added, he willed, so far as I judge, to admonish twice concerning that to which he perceives the virgin to cleave the more tenaciously the longer she has done so; whence, when, being thus admonished, she turns herself away, not out of hatred but at the dictate of counsel, she turns herself with an easier passage to the things that are offered. But when he repeats a third time *Come,* and does not add, *from Libanus, Bride,* he exhorts her not only to be removed from the things she now holds by advancing merits, or to be presently advanced to the espousals that are set before her, but that whatsoever she shall have gained, and this too, as though she had sweated in a twofold contest, she should do it all for love of the reward, all in hope of the crown. Come therefore, he says; turn thyself away from the things [0382A] thou now holdest, for the sake of advancing; come a second time, turn thyself more familiarly to the espousal embraces. Come a third time, that thou mayest rejoice not only in virginal honour and in the espousal chamber, but that on the royal seat thou mayest, a mother with her son, be crowned in glory. Whence it is subjoined: *Thou shalt be crowned,* etc.
