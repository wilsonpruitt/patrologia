## CHAPTER XIX.

*Thou shalt be crowned from the head of Amana, from the top of Sanir, and Hermon, from the couches of lions, from the mountains of leopards.* To this end is the Virgin, the bridegroom's mother, so solicitously admonished to come: that she may be worthily honoured for her merits with royal insignia, that a crown be given her not compacted of gold or of silver, of electrum, or of stones, but wrought by a certain new kind out of lofty mountains. Whose most excellent smith sets mount Amana first, [0382B] sets mount Sanir and Hermon above it and joins them to it: the couches of lions, the ridges of leopards, the prudent craftsman puts between; and thus with wondrous prudence he forges and composes a marvellous work. But what is it, that out of so many mountains everywhere in the earth he chose three, which, naming them expressly, he so brought into this marvellous work as to order Amana first, Sanir second, Hermon third, and to associate in this work those that are divided from one another by place? It does not readily occur what mystery is figured in this deed, unless perchance the interpretation of the names themselves come to our aid, which, being more diligently discussed, makes clear the darkened understanding and mystically brings the Virgin's crown to perfection. Amana is interpreted *a watching tooth,* designating the wicked *220* spirit, which is agreed to envy all upright [0382C] minds, which, ever envying, loves and desires to bite us: and by a pestilent bite to drive us on to death or to vices; and sometimes it rejoices to prevail in such business. For unto so great a crime it is found not to be torpid with sluggish sleep, but to remain constant and solicitous with watchful malice; which, although it shows not itself shameless to them that watch and stand upon their guard, yet, watching, bites and infests them that are given over to sleep and less provident of themselves. From which Sanir also is not discordant, of which mention is made next, whose interpretation evidently expresses the same watchfulness; and by this name too the same wicked spirit seems to be designated, to which it is agreed that no malice is assigned but such as is ever watchful. For Sanir is interpreted [0382D] *a night bird,* or *a stench.* A bird, using the oarage of its feathers, sustains itself by the vehicle of its wings; when it is believed to be far off, it presents itself by swift flight; and if perchance the light of day is believed not to favour its purpose, the bird of spiritual malice becomes a night bird for the seducing of the wretched. It becomes a night bird when temptation fraudulently attacks the unwary and secure man, when, being perchance detected, it thrusts itself in secretly under another appearance, when it mocks and derides him that is carried off by languor and drowsiness, when the sluggard is led like an ox to the victim — nay, is seduced — and sees it not. Nor is Sanir undeservedly said to be also a stench, because temptation, although it shuns to be foreseen, yet wills to be present perceptibly; and though, like a snake creeping in silence, it strives not to be heard, yet, bringing in by its bite a poisonous stench, it wills to be felt. Temptation [0383A] unforeseen is not perceived, as though by an open nostril, when the tempted man, his mind being weighed down, experiences the heavy pack of guilt: and whithersoever he turns himself in the little bed of darkness under the coverlet, he perceives the fruit of the stench, uncertain perhaps of the root. The root full of vice is the suggestion that secretly oppresses, but the fetid fruit is the consent or the fulfilment that follows; whose stench, when it infects the nostrils and affects the inward smelling, renders the whole man enervated with poisoned inward parts. To which Hermon is rightly subjoined, which is interpreted *execration,* or *his anathema.* For from that time and thenceforward, not only does that lost man work nothing of good, but with a fuller malice he execrates the good that he perceives with his ear, because through the chinks of the nostrils there flows in so great a gathering or bilge of vices that the discipline of the word can nowhere enter [0383B] into the heart itself. But the word being shut out, that wicked one hardens the wretched heart yet more, and, it being hardened, he closes and stops the ear also after the manner of an asp, so that not only does he no longer believe right hearing, nor love to obey, but he chooses thereafter not even to hear what is set before him as right. Thus assuredly he execrates whatsoever rectitude of manners suggests, so that a dullness wickedly let in closes every approach to him, rejoicing to present open nostrils to the stenches of vices alone, and to fill up with unclean floods the pool of his inward recesses. Because therefore no admonition, no Holy Scripture is admitted to the hardened mind, it accounts in the place of an anathema whatsoever true religion or worship holds; and, loving to be detained by the softness of mire alone and by unclean dregs, it thinks nothing more execrable than to be removed [0383C] from such things. Whence it is written: « But wisdom is an execration to the sinner [n: (Eccli. I)], » who, advancing to the worse, wills to be hurtful not only to himself but to others also; and, being powerful in malice, rejoices to be a horror to his inferiors; from whom fierceness goes forth, like a lion lying in wait from his couch, and wills that all the rest should sink under a servile fear of him. For the cruelty of some is not easily held back within the narrows of the mind, but rages and boils, that it may be stretched out to the harming of others; and when the furnace or the oven has grown white-hot with the heat of malice, a lion-like fury seeks to rage abroad against all that are subject to it. Which, if perchance it consider that the force of its power is not enough for it, seeks a new fowling, namely the guile of falsehood; and now, furious, like a roaring lion he seeks [0383D] whom he may devour, now, spotted with manifold guile, he shows himself a leopard. For that wicked spirit is manifoldly warlike, using now power to subdue the wretched, now guile; now agreeing with them that will and them that will not, now rebellious, hurting with a thousand arts, and, after the leopard's fashion, a turn-skin. And notably the bridegroom assigned lions to couches and leopards to mountains, wherein he designated both the fraudulent strength of malignant spirits and their robust fraudulence; of whom sometimes a savage strength goes forth from the couch or from ambush, sometimes from the height of power a no less deadly multitude of frauds. Whether therefore the deceitful and crafty stretch themselves out more evidently to the exercising of strength, or the robust and lofty now descend to the weaving of the meshes of frauds, [0384A] they bring in a hurtful war both upon the unwary and upon the weak; and unless the Virgin come and in mercy succour, they bear back a triumphal standard. But the holy Virgin hastens to meet such wicked enemies, bearing it ill that any of the elect should turn aside from her son's purpose; and having mercy on those whom the enemy's malice has almost destroyed; she intercedes with her son and reconciles to him those whom he has redeemed. And her son being appeased, she turns herself confidently to assail the enemies, breaks their forces, strips bare their frauds and overturns them; and that mount Amana swells, that Sanir or Hermon lifts itself up with uplifted top, the Virgin strongly treads down and softens like the mire of the streets. Worthily therefore, when full victory is carried off over them, her hand is adorned with laurel, the Virgin's head is crowned with gold; and she is said to be crowned *221* from those same mountains, [0384B] since it is now fully granted her to glory in the victory over them.
