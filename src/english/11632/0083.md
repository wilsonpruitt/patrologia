## CHAPTER XX.

*From the head of Amana, from the top of Sanir and Hermon,* etc. But I deem it not unfitting if the aforesaid mountains be beautified by a favourable interpretation, and if by them not malignant demons but men converted to the faith be figured, in whose faith the Virgin rejoicing, and solicitous for their increase, cherishes them by her benevolence and advances them by her intervention. Amana, as has been said, is interpreted *a watching tooth.* And that preacher is not unfittingly named a watching tooth, to whom, as to a watchman, the keeping of us that are infirm is committed; to whom, while there is offered a linen sheet full of living creatures, or rather of men living after an animal manner [n: (Judae 19)], it is commanded to slay and to eat, not really, [0384C] but in a spiritual mystery. « Slay, he says, and eat [n: (Act. X)]. » [var: Vulg. Acts 10:13 *occide et manduca* — Migne prints *macta*, a sacrificial verb, for *occide*] For a shepherd takes not up his watches worthily over his flock who consumes not its hardness by the admonition of doctrine; to whose watchful eye, when anything worthy of reproof sets itself over against him, applies not his tooth the more bitingly to cut it away the sooner. Against which it is written of others: « They shall eat the sins of my people [n: (Ose. IV)]. » But after the mind of Amana, the Bridegroom willed to name mount Sanir, whose interpretation seems to sound *a night bird,* because, while we grow torpid with the long custom of vices, the ever-solicitous shepherd becomes without doubt a night bird. For when a blind softness invites us to sin, nay to persist in it, the good shepherd, [0384D] who sleeps not over our keeping nor slumbers, after the manner of a night bird seems to arrive importunate and sudden, and to arraign more harshly, with the sword of the word, manners that are ill secure. Lastly Judas Machabeus, when he perceived that the nights were now pressed with silence, and that the opportunity of the time agreed with his great daring, coming upon the unwary enemies valiantly wrought great slaughter, and with watchful war triumphed over them that rested in secure torpor. For that warlike man judged that it mattered very little whether he dared to hurl himself upon the enemy in the presence of night or of day, provided only that the sluggish and cowardly incircumspection of the enemy yielded to the more cautious man, and that either by guileful valour or by warlike guile the victory were given to the warrior Moreover among the ancients it was published in a famous saying, and, lest oblivion should blot it out, it was committed to letters [0385A] among the heathen, that in war the excellence of valour alone be not sought, but a fitting guile also, that the malignity of the enemy may be craftily crushed. Guile, he says, or valour — who would ask, in an enemy? [n: (Virgil. II Aeneid.)] A prudent prince therefore, when he wills to subject the enemy's malice to servitude, as necessity requires, applies his hand now to guile, now to valour; so that if the enemy resist guile, he may fall pressed by the hammer of valour, and if the sun has not scorched him by day, he may not escape the fraudulence of the night. In that fashion a good teacher, coming upon us as we grow torpid, like a bird, taking counsel for our salvation, importunate to our will, unpleasant to our manners, closes with us that are heavy with sleep and shakes off from us an execrable torpor; and now that we are watchful, he provokes us to fly, as an eagle its young. But with what confidence or efficacy [0385B] can he undertake or fulfil this, save because he first loves to hold also the things which he preaches are to be held, and embraces with no desire the darkness of the present night, but, flying across like Sanir, like Hermon, execrates the things which he beholds below? For he invites us to doctrine as effectually as confidently who commends by his manners the discipline which he preaches by word, who neglects not himself while setting medicine before others, but execrates and shuns as bilge-water whatsoever savours of the world. « The things that were gains to me, he says, these I count as dung [n: (Philip. 3)]. » But Hermon is said to be interpreted not only *execration,* but also *anathema*; and as to what is added, *his,* that is, the anathema of an anathema, so that a teacher is then deservedly called *his anathema,* that is, the separation of separation, when he himself also is separated with all diligence [0385C] from that from which he teaches men to be separated. « I consented not, says Paul, to flesh and blood [n: (Galat. I)]. Paul therefore is his anathema, that is, the anathema of an anathema, the separation of separation: separating from himself what he strives to separate from others, or separating from the rest what he first took pains to separate from himself. And a wondrous diadem of this Virgin is marvellously woven, in which a watching tooth, a night bird, the anathema of an anathema is set; although, if any one will more diligently search out the sequence of the words, the Virgin is not expressly said to be crowned from these mountains. For it is not said: Thou shalt be crowned from Amana, from Sanir, from Hermon, but *Thou shalt be crowned from the head of Amana, from the top of Sanir and Hermon.* The head, as is known to all, has the primacy over the remaining members and over the whole body, [0385D] but the top is called the summit of the head itself; and the head is not contained in the top, but the top in the head, since the head is not held to be a part of the top, but the top the upper part of the head. By the head therefore is not unfittingly figured the order of prelates, whom the prophet names chief men, heads of the peoples; but by the top the apostolic choir seems to be designated, which in the order of prelates is agreed to hold the primacy by a certain excellence. Or perchance the apostolic order itself may be named the head; *222* but the top is Peter, who is agreed to be proclaimed first and chief, who, being appointed key-bearer of heaven as among the apostles so also above the apostles, John so remaining, followed Christ in a special manner. These apostles too are figuratively the couches of lions in which kings lay themselves down; and the Lofty [0386A] princes of the regions, their fierceness being laid aside wherewith they are wont to terrify all their inferiors, bow themselves to these men and refuse not to lie beneath their merits. For when an emperor, laying aside the diadem wherewith he shines, forgets his sceptre, indulges not his royal dignity, and, humbling himself, obeys Peter and acquiesces in Paul's admonitions: what is he but a lion stretched out resting in the apostolic chamber? These same are none the less mountains Lofty in merits, solid in holiness, who are pre-eminent not by earthly height but by spiritual dignity, to whom he lifts his eyes whosoever desires to be adorned with a luminous face, who understands himself to be a leopard, varied and spotted in his skin. Who under the burden of manifold vices, not secure in his own virtue. and not trusting in victory, about to fight in the valley or in the field against the spiritual things of wickedness: passing over to [0386B] their high places, he seeks that help and doctrine may come to him, and, lest he perish, he lays hold of discipline by their learned mastership. « I have lifted up, he says, my eyes to the mountains, from whence help shall come to me [n: (Psal. CXX)]. » Thus the apostles are rightly declared to be both the couches of lions and the mountains of leopards, whom both kings and governors, and they that are held by the various multitude of vices, hear; and, having heard them, they obey by the grace of the divine gift; and, their beastly ferocity being laid aside, they follow an ordered rule of living. But when the Bridegroom orders and disposes those ordainers themselves as living stones, they being duly disposed, he encompasses the queen Virgin and crowns her; and to her honour he renders them worthier in merits, that in the choir of just men they may by a special excellence become nearer to the Bride herself. And [0386C] because he is found not to bestow this more fully at once, he bestows on her whom he loves a promissory hope, that, the promise being made, the Virgin may now be glad in the presence of hope, and, what is more truly the case, may await with sure confidence what is to come. *Thou shalt be crowned,* he says. But by what merit the Virgin is worthy to obtain the presence of hope, the Bridegroom seems to call to mind by his own grace, that it may be made known to all that a perfect reward is not conferred on any one whose merit is not found worthily to go before it.
