## CHAPTER XXI.

*Thou hast wounded my heart, my sister, my spouse, thou hast wounded my heart with one of thine eyes, and with one hair of thy neck.* Enemies are wont to be frighted off by enemies through the fear of wounds, and whoever perchance hath inflicted a wound is wont to earn the greater hatred [0386D]; and as much as he rejoiceth who hath understood himself to prevail unto hurting and unto the inflicting of wounds, so much, nay more, doth he grieve who hath been compelled to endure it. But here the Virgin woundeth, and the wounded one repayeth not hatred; she as it were prevaileth, he stretcheth not out his hands unto injuries; she, mistress of the victory, groweth merry of face and clappeth her hand, he is as it were pricked, and being pricked complaineth not, but applaudeth. For the hand taught unto battle, and the arrow of one inflicting a strong wound, is not anger, is not loss, is not an injury to him that beareth it; but the wounds of one so loving are judged more welcome and better than the kisses of a deceitful man and the frauds of one wickedly kissing [n: (Prov. XXVII)]. For the Bridegroom loveth to be wounded strongly, yet by love, to be transfixed not by a sword, but by the stronger affection of them that love him; out of the depth of which wound floweth [0387A] grace, love breatheth more sweetly, and with not blood but sweetness poured forth he loveth the more livingly, and expireth not. And rightly doth he declare that not his head, not his foot, not anything of the like is wounded — a thing which, being sometimes wounded, we see a man yet reserved unto life — but he nameth the heart itself, which, wounded even but a little, admitteth not the physician's hand, but wanting alike both space and remedy, straightway sendeth forth the vital breath. When therefore he asserteth, as it were, that his heart is made weak by the Bride's wound, and by the repeated word would have the certainty of that same wound confirmed, he intimateth that such a wound is to be understood as not pestilent, not mortal, because the heart also is not corporeal nor liable to death, but vital. But because the heart of the body is the seat of the soul, the place of life, the dwelling of hatred or of love, in naming the heart he would have understood the secret [0387B] place of a more gracious affection, wherein he receiveth and accepteth her who hath deserved to come thither specially, and by a singular merit to enter the sanctuary of grace. And this entrance he expressed by the name of a wound — not bloody, violent not by fury but by love, not by anger but by good will — because thither pierceth not the man drowsy with a degenerate sloth, but he that is clean of heart, keen of mind, violent with an unremitted constancy. Further, he twisteth no effectual violence against this sanctuary who with a foot *223* less upright turneth himself back to the love of things temporal; but rather he who with a prevailing effort studieth to forget all things that pass away, and cleaving to God alone, loveth to obtain the eternal alone. Whence also in this place he nameth her at once spouse and sister, that by these names he may signify the firmer affection of a common good will; because both to a spouse [0387C] that which is the spouse's is not denied by the right of matrimony, and the divine law requireth that a sister be held partner and sharer with her brother of the paternal inheritance. Now the Virgin is Christ's sister, and Christ equally the Virgin's brother, whose kindred upon earth is one, and in heaven one Father is said to be theirs; who, though he grant not one portion for all — not according to sex, but according to merits — yet distributing justly, as he disinheriteth not the son, so neither the daughter. Of which Father the Virgin, as she is daughter, so also heir, is not undeservedly called Christ's sister and coheir; whose more gracious affection, a love both sisterly and spousal, woundeth her brother and spouse, not with the point of a dart, but of a spiritual eye.

## CHAPTER XXII.

[0387D] *With one of thine eyes.* By this saying he intimateth that the Virgin is luminous with two eyes, but with one of them more amply and more sweetly gracious; and approving the one of them, he evidently in nowise reprehendeth the other, and not accusing the other, he esteemeth that one the more excellently. The one is perchance that eye to which it is given to behold things visible — the world indeed, and all that is agreed to be contained within the world's compass — which marketh and understandeth that those things which the order of things or nature unfoldeth together, or one after another, are to pass away by a movable change or by a changeable motion. Nay, it considereth that whatsoever is founded hasteneth naturally unto some passing away or other, and that, as in bodies, so also in spirits, nothing standeth firm to itself, save that the mighty author of things and lord of the creature hath already established certain angels [0388A] by the benefit of his own will, not of nature. But the first and chief is that spiritual eye which, removing itself far from the beholding of the temporal handiwork, and transcending the angels and utterly every creature, lifteth itself up to see the face or the nature of the Creator. Which eye, because knowing not the world it laudably fainteth unto the salvation of God, and is not troubled about many things, but coveteth that one thing necessary, is rightly called one; that is, not manifold by the love of manifold things, but loving God alone uniquely, and, beholding him alone, uniform. That eye which poureth not itself forth abroad unto the living of many things, but with a gathered gaze poureth itself into Christ's secret chamber, woundeth his heart, while it hideth itself there, sharper than a sword, where Christ hideth no eye save the one and simple. Not only [0388B] in the eye, but also in the hair, doth the Bridegroom assert himself to be wounded; who, repeating the word, plainly showeth that the wound itself is repeated, because neither was it enough for the Virgin to have driven in one dart, as it were, nor again enough for the Bridegroom to have told of one, the second being passed over in silence. The Bridegroom therefore is wounded in the Virgin's eye; he is wounded also, as he asserteth, in her hair, in that the multiplicity of hairs, loving comeliness and patient of discipline, is not snatched away by the wind, is not scattered through the empty things of a hurtful liberty, but is gathered in season, and is as it were brought back to the rule of unity itself. No woman, as I judge, hath been found in all the ages past who, her hairs being cut away and one left, was singularly content therewith, nor who could so worthily be commended for the merit of one hair, save when a plurality bound up is restrained into one by the mastery of a rule. So the Virgin also, rejoicing [0388C] to be adorned with many hairs, that is, with virtues, lest that very multiplicity be driven to be fanned by the breeze of vanity, gathereth them all together and bindeth them by a certain unity, and by a wondrous mastery bringeth them under one charity. For whatsoever is acquired by a multitude or abundance of merits, if it be not bound with the bond of love, lacketh the glory of rewards; nor doth worthy praise approve whatsoever is found manifold in merits, if it be not united after the manner of hairs by the fillet of charity. Well did Paul esteem of it [n: (I Cor. XIII)], as was fitting, who, wishing to commend it, named it the bond of perfection; because although an innumerable gathering of the remaining virtues be made, not being bound with this bond it remaineth utterly imperfect. But being bound, it fortifieth the Virgin's faith and adorneth it, [0388D] and like a hair it rather adorneth the neck than loadeth it; and with such an ornament, with such a work doth faith glory — glorying worthily indeed, when through love it worketh that which the divine law commandeth. For us whom a heavy and, as it were, bodily mass removeth and disjoineth from the head, faith, like a neck, knitteth on to Christ and joineth us; without which it is impossible that even the virgin herself should please God, and by the intervention whereof Elisabeth would declare her blessed. « And blessed, saith she, art thou that hast believed, because those things shall be accomplished that were spoken to thee by the Lord [n: (Luc. I)]. » And the Apostle: « That Christ, he saith, may dwell by faith in your hearts [n: (Ephes. III)]. » In the hair of this neck, which is united by the aforesaid bond of love, and by whose not burden but honour the Virgin's faith is distinguished, the Bridegroom with a glad dancing maketh manifest his heart [0389A] wounded, intimating that the Virgin's robust love powerfully affecteth his tender affection and troubleth it not.
