## CHAPTER XXIII.

*How beautiful are thy breasts, my sister, my spouse: thy paps more beautiful than wine, and the odour of thine ointments above all spices.* He calleth breasts of the Virgin those wherewith she instructeth little ones that they may grow great, and cherisheth the somewhat grown, that in the process of time they may come to their youth; which also he declareth beautiful, that they grow not vile to the Jews that glory in the letter of the law, but may shine forth the more graciously both to them and to the rest of the hearers. And these, whether they be first named breasts, or afterward paps, being compared with the wine of the law, are declared [0389B] to be of a greater beauty; whilst the austerity of that wine maketh the Jews as it were drunken and senseless, but the sweetness of these maketh us pleasing to God and of one accord. But because you know that the Virgin's paps were set down not far above, and there expounded, according as God granted, I forbear to make a longer delay in expounding them anew, referring the diligent hearer to the things already said. Yet because here beauty is declared to be not in the paps only, but in the breasts also, whereof there, or anywhere above, no mention is made, he seemeth more subtly to intimate somewhat perhaps new, whereto I have not applied hand or study by expounding it more diligently. The breasts, as is said, send not forth milk to little ones, but rather promise themselves about to send it forth when the time shall be, lying hid meanwhile under the shadow of an unripe season, not yet with a fitting [0389C] fruit, but rather swelling with the hope of what is to come. For so long as the Virgin marketh us untaught, hardened, and — our faults so requiring — not yet born unto the living life, she keepeth within the breasts the benefit of doctrine, and bringeth it not forth, presuming not to nourish those whom a fitting birth leadeth not out unto life. For she knoweth that it belongeth to Christ to give life to us that were long since dead, to make us new out of the darkness of the womb unto the grace of the light; but to those already born it is granted to her to impart the benefit itself, and to pour out the paps more largely upon them as now capable. If, saith she, thou wouldest give thine elect to be born unto that grace of light which I greatly desire, that, thou giving life, they might be fed with any benefit whatsoever, my breasts would come to the abundance of paps, and, living in the spirit, would the more effectually nourish them with a worthy milk. [0389D] To them indeed would I little by little unfold somewhat of thy conception or thy birth, and would reveal the secret mystery to a hearing ear; and that which with an unbelieving mind neither the Gentiles nor the Jews receive, John the son of Zebedee, sucking my paps, would drink in. Such breasts and paps doth the Bridegroom praise in the Virgin mother; and that he may close their praises with a due and equally mystical end, he setteth himself to commend not the nourishment of milk, not its rivulets or streams, but the greatest odour and the ointments preferred before all spices. *And the odour,* saith he, *of thine ointments above all spices.* Out of the aforesaid paps, wherewith the Virgin, marvellous in her childbearing, is distinguished, there is found to flow not a woman's milk, but a plenty of ointments; concerning which, if the whole past succession of the perfumers be convened, all their confection [0390A] is forestalled, not in time, but by a wondrous odour. For whatsoever the subtlety of the ancients brought forth from the beginning, whatsoever the curiosity of the heathen apprehended concerning the world so wondrously founded; whatsoever the sages, or, after Pythagoras, the philosophers, compounded by a seducing subtlety, wherewith, as with a corrupting spice, the masters infected their disciples — all this doth the preaching and the doctrine of the Virgin far surpass, wherein truth hath the sovereignty and the discipline of living prevaileth. Whatsoever also the patriarchs and prophets, relying on the gift of a foreseeing understanding, wove in obscurities of words or in mysterial sacraments; whatsoever the fathers of old perceived beforehand with a prophetic nostril, who agreed to announce with a watchful heralding the coming of the true light — the Virgin, the queen of virgins, hath surpassed by a special [0390B] advance, who brought forth in a virginal womb the Man that is the salvation of men. Whose childbearing and conception, a novelty compounded, a new confection, was first in the virginal bridal chamber an effectual health, a sound re-reading; and afterwards it so cometh forth to us by the sure mastery of the Virgin, that the very things she bestoweth transcend whatsoever went before by way of promise. For none of them that went before so expressed the truth to come as did the holy Virgin, to whom the divine goodness granted this: that beyond what could be uttered by man or by angel she herself should know the mystery of the incarnate Word more expressly and more sensibly. Whose knowing, and the sweet remembrance of that knowing, is so wondrous a confection and so great an abolishing of the ancient hurt, that the Virgin, anointed therewith as with spices or ointments, knoweth no more the wound of the fault which the malice of Eve or [0390C] of the serpent brought in. Which ointments of the Virgin, wherewith she deserved to be medicinally anointed, being shut up under a certain seal cannot be fully set forth by our discourses, because the light of the ointments so deigned to arise in the Virgin that, such as it is, it cannot so much as be perceived by another man. But the odour, or the report, of the ointments breatheth upon us sweetly and healthfully, by the sweetness whereof our languishing spirit doth now breathe again; and at that little which the Virgin spake out of her sure fulness, the odour being as it were drawn in at the nostrils, our spirit hath now revived. Rightly therefore doth he set not the ointments *225* of the Virgin — which she setteth not before us that are far inferior — but rather, what is less, the odour of the ointments before all spices; because with the benefit which was given to the apostles [0390D] through the Virgin to be smelled, no knowledge of them of old, no spices can be compared. Not without cause, when Christ was returned to the Father whence he had come, and, the apostles being left behind, the Spirit was not yet granted in tongues of fire, do the apostles judge it worthy to persevere with one accord in prayer with Mary the mother of Jesus, that what they had drawn from her they might affirm both the more constantly and thereby the more surely. For upon whom the sun shone again with a more liberal plenty of understanding, upon them there so flowed in from the Virgin herself a honey-flowing odour that, if it be compared with any sense or hearing of them of old, it deserveth beyond doubt to be borne above it in sweet delight. Whereof it is added: *A honeycomb,* etc.
