## CHAPTER XXIV.

*Thy lips, O Spouse, are a dropping honeycomb, honey and milk are under [0391A] thy tongue; and the odour of thy garments as the odour of frankincense.* Well ought the Virgin's lips to be named a honeycomb, because the Virgin herself is also said to be figured under the name of the bee, which by a natural privilege is fed upon flowers, liveth upon dew, and, knowing not the marriage bed, marvellously gathereth up her offspring with her mouth; she is made fruitful with a brood without groaning or grief, and, chaste in a virginal bosom, becometh a mother not by wantonness but by love. Strong in art, skilful in wit, she groweth not slothful in her care, and with study wondrous enough maketh her prudence known, while, framing for herself a honeycomb, she setteth cells distinct in order, and within their narrow spaces layeth up honey with a busy mouth. To this bee the Virgin is not unfittingly reckoned like, who delighteth to be fed spiritually upon the flowering comeliness of virtues, who liveth in the spirit, by grace divine from above as by dew, and, knowing not man, conceiveth [0391B] and beareth a son without travail. Who, heaping up spiritual riches with a busy study, and conferring and keeping in her heart words big with meaning, filleth the secret place of her conscience with a sweetness so pleasant that inwardly she may be called honeyed, after the manner of a honeycomb. But this sweetness, which she herself tasteth more perfectly and more fully, she straiteneth not by the disease of envy, not by the key of avarice; but what the divine Spirit willed to instil into her from above of dew and of sweetness, she, loosing her lips, suffereth to drop down upon us that stand by. For the Virgin, marking what is fitting for us to hear first and what afterward, doth not put forth at once all the things she judgeth worthy to be covered; nor, like a vessel turned upside down, doth she straightway pour out whatsoever she containeth, but bedeweth us, that are less fit for all things, with a thin dropping. Not so did the Old [0391C] Testament, the figured speech of the prophets, the enigmas of the ancients, the obscure surface of the Scriptures; not so, I say did they deserve to be or even to be called a dropping honeycomb, which, though they were a honeycomb, were kept shut up under a seal. For we may call the obscurity of them of old a honeycomb, which held honey within, but knew not how to drop it, because it in no wise brought the mystic senses, the inward sweetnesses, and its profits home to our darkened understandings. That honeycomb therefore, then pressed down by a certain weight of its own, lay long unpressed by the hand of them that built; nor did there come one who, taking pity on it, might lift it up, and, being lifted, might with a welcome hand strain it into the mouth of the hungry. But the Virgin, filled not so much with honey as with [0391D] the sweetness of mind, rich not in money but in spiritual documents, poured not out, as from an open vessel, in a sudden rush, that which she had laid up in the comb, sweeter than honey and more delightsome than all wine; but, tempering the opening of her lips by the door of circumspection, she chose rather to bring forth so much as the very capacity of her hearers was able to bear. Less therefore did her lips drop while Christ yet lived as man among men, but more amply when, death being triumphed over, he now lived again in a greater glory; because both a blindness poured over them then pressed hard upon the stubborn Jews, and now a greater knowledge is poured into the apostles by the gift of the Spirit. The Virgin's lips therefore are dropping like a honeycomb, which are not barred with a bolt, nor is a nail driven into them; but, one part of the mouth being closed, the other part is open, and rendereth uncovered to the hearers such things as suit [0392A] the time. For she doth not, like a wordy woman, straightway babble whatsoever hath come into her mouth, but moderateth the hidden things of secrets with a discreet silence, judging it worthy that certain things be more warily held back under the veil of the tongue, which, when the time presseth, she then committeth to her lips to be dropped forth. Therefore perhaps is she declared to have honey and milk under her tongue, in whose inner chamber a twofold knowledge is contained: the one which is seen to be expedient for little ones and sucklings; the other to suit them that are now advanced and grown greater. For little ones are of necessity nourished with milk, but the somewhat grown are brought forward to the eating of honey; those protesting Christ to be a man truly born of the Virgin, these venerating the same as God born of God before the ages. Milk coming forth from flesh [0392B] may be called Christ's humanity; honey coming from flower or from dew is his true divinity; the knowledge whereof the Virgin keepeth in secret under her tongue, nor doth she scatter it abroad everywhere, or at once, with an indiscreet speech. But whereas Christ, God and man, therefore *226* showeth himself first as man, that, the visible being known, the heart of the knower may ascend to the invisible, what meaneth it that he saith there is not milk and honey, but honey and milk under the Virgin's tongue — she who teacheth him to be first man and afterward God? To none is it doubtful that in Christ the Godhead goeth first in dignity, and therefore he judgeth it worthy that in speech his humanity should not go before; but that, if a speech be framed of both alike, whether open or covered with mystery, that which excelleth [0392C] in dignity be set first in the order of speaking also. Or rather, wishing to show forth the Virgin's twofold knowledge, which she willed to hide the longer in secret under her tongue, he signally expresseth which of them was not only the worthier but also the earlier, mention being made mystically of honey first and of milk after. For the angel first announced that God was present to the Virgin, and afterward declared that, he being present, she should conceive a son, that in him whom the Virgin knew to be present she might now more firmly trust, and, having already received the greater, might not mistrust concerning the lesser gift. « Hail, saith he, the angel, full of grace, the Lord is with thee [n: (Luc. I)]. » And he addeth of the other: « Behold thou shalt conceive and bring forth a son [n: (ibid.)]. » The Lord teacheth the Virgin by the angel that he is present, and poureth in a knowledge growing sweet [0392D] more pleasantly than honey; and, the knowledge or the honeyed savour which he sendeth first being tasted, he promiseth by the same messenger the conception, which is as it were milk. For she, believing enough, could not be forewarned of the conception, if she rejoiced not to be more surely taught of the divine presence, unless she knew and felt that the Holy Spirit was coming upon her from above, by whose shadow whatsoever she is said to bring forth is made holy. But this knowledge of God's presence and of the Son's conception lieth long under her tongue in the Virgin's own understanding; of which benefit, nevertheless, her virginal discretion doth not wholly deprive us, nor doth she straightway render the stored fountain open, but draweth it forth and deriveth it drop by drop. Whose so great discretion, whose discreet working, do as it were clothe her and commend her, if yet there be any who now duly weigh her merits; [0393A] who may foresee how she chooseth not so much as to mutter to the obscure and the negligent, but loveth to pour forth by dropping to the diligent, with hands not prodigal. By garments, as is known, our nakedness is covered; by the glory of garments a virginal nobility is distinguished; nor perchance doth she please the Bridegroom save such as is so clothed with fine linen and purple that not only she fear not the cold, but appear also comely in her adorning, being found decked with double garments. Although in the present place he hath not described the quality of the garments, nor expressed how to define the quantity of their very number, reserving this for that woman whom — as hath elsewhere been said — who shall find? whose price is from afar, and cometh hither from the uttermost bounds. Which woman may doubtless be called any soul that, careful for her own, is found so given to business [0393B] that, guarding beforehand against heat or cold and a sudden onset, putting out her hand to strong things, she laboureth as though she sat at the loom. But here there is in hand not any soul whatsoever, fearful with a general want, but a certain soul of special merit or excellence; whose garments do not now cover a shame, do not now shut out the cold, but show her festal in the Bridegroom's sight by their ornaments. But because how solemn, how festal she appeared in the Bridegroom's eyes the oppressing blindness of our mind suffereth us not to see, here he hath not expressed to us the number of the garments, nor their comeliness, but, lest he be altogether silent, he maketh a passage to the odour. For in that which the Virgin either inwardly feeleth of Christ within herself, or hath now consented to drop forth — how busy she hath been, rejoicing like a bee in her honey, [0393C] or how studious of a discreet governance, like a dropping honeycomb — all this pleaseth him the more, to whose eyes alone the whole lieth open, while to us the whole is brought near as the odour of frankincense with coals. For when the virginal frankincense plungeth itself into the divine fire, it inclineth wholly, in a manner unspeakable, unto a salutary consuming: yet by the command of the stronger it is not damnably consumed, but by the merit of love and of comeliness is delightfully taken up. But how much it burneth, or how much it pleaseth by that burning, we perceive not, but scarce draw in even a thin odour, as men set far off; because, the frankincense being melted, the smoke stretcheth to the Bridegroom's breadth, while the odour or report extendeth itself by breadth unto us. Those virtues therefore wherewith the noble Virgin is honoured, and by whose noble honour she is [0393D] presented to the divine sight, are certain garments, not needful for the covering of nakedness, but suitable for a comeliness to be put forward more confidently. Which comeliness is put forward and offered to the divine sight after the manner of frankincense, conspicuous not in gold, not in garments, but in manners that shall please; whose welcome sweetness so seemeth to please the Lord that it toucheth us also with its odour, while his good will hath mercy upon us. For she loveth to wander abroad to nothing else, but rather to be straitened within these two by a rampart of fortitude: to wit, that she may show herself to her bridegroom more fully commendable in comeliness, and, dropping more thinly, may fill us with her odour. Whence it is added. *A garden,* etc.
