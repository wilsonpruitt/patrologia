## CHAPTER XXV.

*A garden enclosed, my sister, my spouse, a garden enclosed, [0394A] a fountain sealed up.* Because in the first garden the serpent supplanted the first woman, God the Son of God then planted for himself another garden *227*, when he filled the blessed Virgin, beyond the manner allotted to other women, with his grace, with a great kind not of herbs but of virtues. For in that garden the woman's simplicity was circumvented, the man tasted the forbidden thing, and, the taste being had, nakedness was found; death came upon them that were to live for ever, by the desert of the transgression, and the curse of mortality came upon them and upon their posterity alike. But this garden, containing not shrubs, herbs, flowers, woods of thick foliage, transitory fruits and odours, hath certain other things worthy either to be compared with such, or, that I may speak more truly, to be set above all transitory things. For it hath that which a sound and wise taste [0394B] judgeth fruitful, that which the smell by a right judgment judgeth precious, that which the inward sight, its darkness being wiped away, judgeth gracious, that which by a wondrous fulness is so plentiful as to wipe away all want. For to this garden the tree of life is set in with fixed roots, whereof he that shall have eaten (as they that have made trial have found out) is not hurt by a grievous death, but liveth a life happier than can be told, and, having been able to obtain it, hath not ceased to live further. It hath also a marvellous frankincense, or a commendable spice, or an ointment, which is found not in Arabia but in a land denied to them that die; whose odour he that shall have drawn in shall not know the serpent's stenches, nor, drowsiness prevailing, shall he feel sudden bitings. It hath none the less that welcome flower of the rose or of the violet, or even of the lily, or if there be any in flowers accounted more welcome to the sight, [0394C] which he that shall have duly marked, and shall have bent himself upon it with a keen-sighted eye, shall pass over whatever sad thing the night murmureth with a joy not transitory but tenacious. For there is in the garden a tree of life, an ointment poured out, a comely flower, the very Bridegroom of the Virgin, fair in form above the sons of men, at whose presence no base want, no needy baseness besetteth him, but in him dwelleth bodily the fulness of the Godhead [n: (Coloss. II)]. For it is certain that God is in Christ, and Christ in the Virgin, not shadowily but truly; and therefore that this very garden is full of sufficient goods, which he that planted it willed with a prudent counsel so to be enclosed, that he who seeth and envieth might grieve to be shut out. And in very truth, as it is not at all barred to the faithful and to the friend, [0394D] so, being walled about with strength, it is closed to the enemy: it letteth in no Jew, no Gentile, none empty of faith, and whom it marketh as worthy of hatred it sendeth far off with a strong repulse. In it therefore, not as in the ancient garden, doth the serpent craftily creep; he poisoneth not its fruit, he defileth not its odour, he plucketh not its little flowers; a depraved softness hungereth not, hunger proceedeth not into effect, the woman obeyeth not the serpent, nor the man the woman. And yet no weariness of a desert or of a solitude hurteth it, no grief goadeth it, while its own author walketh therein festally: at whose glad countenance the things in the garden bear fruit, the kinds of things flourish, and the manifoldness of kinds smileth upon him that is fair in form above men. For while he walketh in whose countenance is comeliness, and grace is poured abroad in whose lips, the Virgin's garden answereth with a glad [0395A] spirit, with a face unconfounded: pleasing him that walketh there, all baseness being so shut out that within nothing remaineth but beauty, poured round about with a strong defence. But what meaneth it that to say "a garden enclosed" once to the bridegroom is not enough, but he doubleth this very thing with the same names repeated — unless it be, as I remember to have been said already, that this is the force of love's intemperance, that by a speech frequently repeated it may express the greatness of a more gracious affection? Or perhaps he willed for this cause to call the garden twice enclosed, that he might show a twofold evil so shut out by the same virgin, that neither doth the spirit stand by the tender flesh with a nerveless softness, nor doth the flesh stubbornly resist the stronger spirit. For we, although with the mind we serve the law of God by grace, are nevertheless in the flesh, through the law engrafted in us, somewhat [0395B] guilty; and when we seem to ourselves circumspect with a watching spirit, we are found, the flesh being loosed in sleep, not slightly imperfect. Or therefore did this garden deserve to be called twice enclosed, because the Virgin is so found to embrace a twofold study that in neither doth she show herself at all reprehensible, but with a circumspect mind defendeth herself from the onset of enemies of every sort. For so, when necessity required, she betook herself to active business, that, hands being given to this, she might not withdraw them from the spiritual; and again she so fixed a spiritual eye upon contemplation that she shrank not from the active, which God cursed unto punishment by the desert of sin. Into an excess of contemplating she hung herself up by a honeyed sweetness; to the gathering in of her neighbour she stooped down with a milky affection; on either side, or rather round about, so fortified that she gave place to none, though sought after by many [0395C] that assailed her. But because a garden hath need of watering lest it wither, and that it may rather abound with fitting plantings and grow into a wood, this garden is said not so much to be watered by a fountain as, by a more express significance, to be called a fountain. Now a fountain is named from pouring forth, as some assert, in that it is seen to pour forth living water out of hidden places, and, bubbling up with a perennial vein, to live by an abundance not outward but inward — not by torrents, like a lake, not by rains of water, like a cistern [n: (Prov. V)]. A fountain therefore ought this Virgin also fittingly enough to be called, whose understanding withereth not, whose sense hath no parching heat, but by the grace of an inward [sic: *muueris*] she is taught unspeakably and groweth rich, and with a wondrous perpetuity, beyond what can be told, groweth glad. Who indeed [0395D] hideth long, as under a seal, the mystery revealed to her, and revealeth not her living abundance to them that are less capable: the manner of her fruitfulness, the form of her nuptials, the excellence of her celibacy she holdeth shut up *228* in her knowledge, like a fountain sealed. For he that assigned to her such a knowledge, so singular a conscience, left her not as a vessel without a lid, but sealed her, lest they that hear and receive not, being unworthy, be troubled with a scandal, and hasten rather to stones than venerate the mystery. And so the nuptial bed of the virgin, the inner recesses of secrets, the fountain of leaping water, the wondrous knowledge of things divine, the very author of the gift shutteth with a bar, sealeth to himself with the key of David, and unsealeth it not to the blaspheming, to the darkened, to him that worthily lacketh the price. Without standeth the crowd of the people, the undiscerning multitude maketh its noise, [0396A] and, the seal not being unloosed, it perceiveth not so secret a fountain: the subtlety of the philosophers, the princes of the world are fasting; barking, they receive not; importunate, they profit nothing. Much plenty indeed, much subtlety is in the fountain; the wave leapeth, the vein coming forth from the inner parts maketh its leaping perpetual; but the key set upon it seemeth so to cover it from all that stand without, that to the unbelieving it deigneth to open nothing at all, but to the faithful somewhat little. Yet that very thing which is little so aboundeth for us, in respect of the abundance that remaineth, so surpasseth our littleness, that none but a gathered mind, none but an undivided love can take it in — because a paradise of this sort admitteth not the envious serpent. Whence it is added: *Thy emissions,* etc.
