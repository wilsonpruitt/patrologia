## CHAPTER XXVI.

[0396B] *Thy emissions are a paradise of pomegranates with the fruits of apples.* The things which this enclosed garden, or this fountain fortified with a seal, sendeth forth — which letteth in none, or very few, to the secrets of its fulness — are as it were a certain paradise or delight of pomegranates, within of a snowy colour, but without of a red. For if a garden be planted with a prudent hand or counsel, and moistened at fitting seasons with the watering of living water, what shall be said to be more fitly brought forth than that which either hunger seeketh for the eating, or which sickness judgeth healthful and effectual for the healing? The emissions therefore of the virgin, who is figured under the name of a garden or of a fountain, he that commendeth them beareth witness to be like a paradise: nay, speaking more expressly, he saith they are a paradise of pomegranates with the fruits of apples, and [0396C] the rest that follow, which it is certain are medicinal. Of old there was a paradise that persuaded to a serpentine depravity, that conceived a new fraud, that slew holiness, wherein the strong man yielded to the soft counsel of the woman, and the forbidden taste of the apple brought in to them that made trial of it the fruit of death. But this paradise, which is sent forth out of the garden or the fountain of the virgin, maketh recompense more mightily for the things that were in the other more curiously lost, consisting of pomegranates, which within are of a snowy and without of a red colour, and of other apples, which are of a most healthful savour to them that eat. The red colour in the pomegranates, which presenteth itself outwardly, representeth the love of suffering and the bearing of it, which he may behold in the Virgin's life and manners who by her mastery [0396D] willeth to be taught to suffer in like sort. The whiteness within is the flower of conscience, a mind secure, which, when the world rageth without or a passion about to rage threateneth, embraceth purity and forsaketh not virginal cleanness; it is not troubled by injuries, and being beaten with vexations it faileth not. But the fruits of the other apples which are sent forth together with the pomegranates are her good works, which are not only set forth as welcome to the sight, but are also not slightly fruitful to them that taste, that is, to them that imitate, and precious not by a seducing colour but by a most profitable savour. But because to an eye that gazeth more curiously one or another kind of thing is not welcome enough, he addeth, for the heaping up of praises, many sorts of spices, that emissions so manifold may make us greatly to marvel, and to venerate her as worthy of many praises in fitting ways.
