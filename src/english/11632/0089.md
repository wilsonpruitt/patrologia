## CHAPTER XXVII.

[0397A]

*Cypress with spikenard, spikenard and saffron, sweet cane and cinnamon, with all the trees of Libanus, myrrh and aloes with all the chief ointments.* Cypresses are slender shrubs which are stretched up to a great height, whose grains are boiled down in oil by the skilled care of physicians; by which manner of boiling a commendable ointment is made, whereby a desirable soothing is rendered to the pains that are anointed therewith. So also the Virgin's virtues, exceeding subtle and straight, are carried up into the height of dignity, not by branches but by merits, whose fruitful acts are boiled in a spiritual oil, when in holy love they are stripped of a hurtful or carnal humour. But spikenard, which is a very small tree, is joined to cypresses of this sort, when the excellence of virtues is pressed down [0397B] by regard of humility; and by how much the more the Virgin is exalted among women with singular titles, by so much the more doth she appear in her own eyes humble and despised. Well therefore are *cypress with spikenard* set first in this catalogue of medicinal things *229*, which after the manner of a paradise are sent forth out of the Virgin's garden; because humility, being joined to them, so commendeth the excellence of virtues that so great a confection safely extendeth itself to our knowledge. Again, *spikenard and saffron.* Out of spikenard alone, as they say, without another spice or mixture, there is made a laudable ointment and a confection that shall profit; and Mary Magdalene willed to anoint the Lord's feet with this ointment, as hath been made sufficiently known by the witness of the gospel lesson. « She took, he saith, a pound of ointment of pistic spikenard, of great price, and Jesus anointed the feet [n: (Joan. XII)]. » [var: Vulg. Jn 12:3 *Maria ergo accepit libram… et unxit pedes Jesu*, the genitive — "anointed the feet **of Jesus**." Migne's nominative makes Jesus the agent of the anointing; the genitive stands correctly five lines later, at 0397C.] Spikenard is the contrition of the heart [0397C] which the humble and devout offer, who by a broken spirit are advanced to so great a grace that they anoint the feet of Jesus, and by a fuller progress come to the head itself, and fill the nostrils of them that stand by, or rather the whole house, with the odour. « The house, saith he, was filled with the odour of the ointment [n: (ibid.)]. » And above in this epithalamium: « My spikenard, saith she, gave forth the odour thereof [n: (Cant. I)]. » To this spikenard, both as tree and as ointment, saffron sitteth by and standeth by, which putteth to flight present fevers and meeteth and withstandeth those that are coming; which upon its surface displayeth a golden grace or colour, signifying charity, which possesseth a higher throne. Which charity doubtless loveth to be at hand to a devout humility, and so to be present to a contrite spirit as an undivided companion, [0397D] that whom she hath known to be pricked and anointed with a more devout affection, him she maketh to love the more, and not to be sundered from him whom he hath loved. For if any feverish bitterness of sadness or of sloth run in between, charity, being near and not enduring that loss be brought in, straightway meeteth it; which, weakening the hurtful things, admonisheth the devout spirit to stand in what it hath begun, lest the malevolent enemy bear off a triumph from one deceived. *Sweet cane,* saith he, *and cinnamon.* Sweet cane, which is of a somewhat red colour, having a hard bark, signifieth the strength of a constancy that will not yield to assailing vexations, which loveth to show so continual a fellowship to the aforesaid virtues that for their sake it refuseth not the loss of blood, nor the torment of martyrdom. Which constancy doubtless seemeth necessarily to be joined to the aforesaid spices, lest weariness hinder [0398A] that which devotion loveth from being consummated; lest, when a violent suddenness breaketh not the good purpose, a longer and outstretched hand wear it away step by step. And because, according to the commandment, the wise man ever remembereth his last ends [n: (Eccles. VII)], straightway cinnamon is at hand, which in colour is made like to ashes; admonishing that in all things whereunto by a fitting advance the wise man deserveth to go forward, he never cease to call to mind who, and whence, and whither he cometh. « Earth thou art, saith he, and unto earth shalt thou go [n: (Gen. III)]. » [var: Vulg. Gen. 3:19 *pulvis es, et in pulverem reverteris*, "dust thou art, and into dust thou shalt return."] And Job: « I do, saith he, penance in dust and ashes [n: (Job. XLII)]. » Lastly, when Abraham hung himself up to speak with God with a prosperous affection and effect, yet in that excellent height he marked himself to be ashes, showing that he so rejoiced in the benefit offered him that, duly remembering what was his own, he seemed as it were [0398B] to dare nothing out of his own. « I will speak, saith he, to my Lord, whereas I am dust and ashes [n: (Gen. XVIII)]. » These are the aromatic shrubs that avail unto a healthful medicine, more fit for the cultivating of manners, suited to a truthful discipline, together with very many others which in aspect and in effect are diverse one from another; and Libanus bringeth them all forth together unto healing. For Christ, whom very many say to be figured under the name of Libanus, willeth the series of his virtues to be derived to us through the Virgin; and that which in many ages past the prophetic promise foretold, the virginal emission hath brought to effect in our hands. *Myrrh,* saith he, *and aloes with all the chief ointments.* » The foregoing virtues being described under an enigma or figure, whereby the love of the inward mind is spiritually instructed in the things to come, [0398C] he seemeth to pass over to the kinds of other graces, which it is certain suit bodies by a medicinal mystery. For that the flesh, upon which a natural law is laid from its conceiving, so that, living more or less, it be necessarily mortal, when it hath shaken off its morning flower by an evening setting, may yet not flow away by rottenness nor by a serpentine worm — myrrh mightily commandeth, and is seen to prevail by its command; and in the presence of this the flesh feareth not to lie subject to natural things. For myrrh is an ointment wherewith dead flesh rejoiceth to be anointed; anointed wherewith, its wholeness marvelleth that it is not dissolved by the law engrafted in it — which flesh, deprived of living vigour, knowing itself now weak, now unarmed, yet by the wondrous safeguard of myrrh knoweth not the worm of the rottenness of old ingrown in it. This same thing doth aloes also work by the power [0398D] of its own strength, whence also with merit enough it is coupled to myrrh in this epithalamium, that of things diverse, whereof the working or the effect is one, a certain companionable friendship or affection may become known. But this hath aloes by a certain right and special privilege, which myrrh hath not prevailed to obtain by the general fellowship: that besides being an unction preservative of dead bodies, it be also a potion purgative of living bowels. Myrrh therefore is the assiduity of fasting, the harshness of abstaining, the restraint of continence, the exercise of labour, the frequency of meditating, the study of learning — which things at the first taste indeed bring forth a wondrous bitterness, but, use working more strongly, are expulsive of carnal rottenness. But [0399A] because this effect prevaileth to the utmost only in dead flesh, it is needful that the sweetness and affection of living be taken away; and that, to the end the affliction sought from without may profit more sufficiently, the present life be taken out of the midst for us who are flesh. For we, though sometimes we be afflicted outwardly by some grievous action, yet live ill, the root being still venomous and the passion still alive; and it is expedient that a prudent physician, the vein being felt and knowledge gained *230* of the inward motions, should make the ill-living humours to be voided by a purgative potion. But this potion is aloes, or rather it is the inmost love of virtue, which begetteth a good hatred of guilt and of a destroying servitude; which, spitting far out from the borders of the heart the base dregs of Pharaoh, rendereth the heart, well purged, unto Christ as the porch of Solomon. Which sort [0399B] of dregs and of the uncleanness of an oppressing lees, and which potion of love and efficacy of an expelling virtue, a certain heathen, not unskilled nor ignorant of the like medicine, well showed — not to the wandering and negligent, but to them that sat in the school of discipline. *They hate,* saith he, *to sin, the good, for the love of virtue.*
Good is love, and none the less good is the virtue of love, which with a perfect hatred condemneth the bilge of a baser uncleanness; and with the besom of a strict judgment it scrapeth away whatsoever it findeth miry, and for the bridegroom at his coming spreadeth a pavement of welcome marble. But after the inward parts are thoroughly cleansed by the love of virtue, as by the efficacy of a potion, from thenceforth and thereafter myrrh and aloes work alike, and will and working pass over for us into an ointment which, against everlasting [0399C] rottenness, giveth a sufficient defence to carnal men, who are such not by vice but by nature [n: (Rom. VIII; Ephes. II)]. For neither would the will without work, languishing in idleness, attain to the obtaining of merit, nor would work without will find glory with God; but the affection rejoiceth to shine forth laudably outwardly through the effect, and the effect to bear evident witness of the affection. Whereas therefore the will, or the love of virtue, is seen to hold the former place, and it is doubtful to none that work followeth the will that goeth before, what meaneth it that, speaking in this epithalamium, he set not aloes first in his speech — which, though not in the ointment, yet cometh first in the potion? For he saith not, aloes and myrrh, but *myrrh and aloes with all the chief ointments,* because [0399D] the labour of work, which we said to be figured by myrrh, as it were leadeth and leadeth forth the love of virtue (which was said to work in the stead of a potion), and presenteth it in public; for not labour through love, but love through labour showeth itself as it were visible; and therefore myrrh is named first, which willeth to be seen first as guide and forerunner, by whose gift love deserveth to be shown forth knowable. « By their fruits, saith he, you shall know them [n: (Matth. VII)]. » Rightly doth Nicodemus in the Gospel, bearing a mixture of myrrh and aloes, a hundred pounds, and compounding this ointment for himself in altogether the same order, will to anoint the flesh of the dead Christ with this mixture, showing that it should not pass to rottenness by the law of mortals. And here doubtless myrrh [0400A] is named first, and aloes afterward, for this reason, that the will is now shown forth by a visible work; and in the eyes of them that rightly believe they came together into one mixture, by which coming together they compounded an ointment profitable for our pains. « For he was offered because it was his own will [n: (Isa. LIII)]; » and « Christ, saith he, died for us [n: (Rom. V)]. » But that Nicodemus is read to have brought a hundred pounds of this mixture intimateth that Christ bestowed his benefit upon us in weight and number; and that what had before been prophesied in transitory words and significations was at last consummated in a certain weight and a perfect number. « It is consummated [n: (Joan. XIX)], » saith he. I am deceived if the Virgin compounded not a mixture of this sort, if by this confection she put not worms and rottenness [0400B] far off; if in the compounding she passed over the weight, or the number, or the very order, and did not rather send forth hither a paradise of an ointment-bearing fulness. And lest perchance this one thing should seem to any insufficient, because a manifold necessity is for the most part not supplied by one benefit, he addeth, *with all the chief ointments,* that the Virgin's opulent emission may be signified, which together with myrrh and aloes giveth us the ointments of the remaining graces. But the first and chief are the ointments of the twin charity, which this enclosed garden bringeth forth out of the storehouse of holiness; and to worthy hearers the Virgin droppeth them down with salutary admonitions and with doctrine, heaping doctrine upon example, upon manners, upon discipline. And because she deserved above to be called not only a garden enclosed but also a fountain sealed, [0400C] those things being now unfolded which can be more especially assigned to the garden, the Bridegroom deferreth not to pass over consequently in his speech to those things which seem more probably to suit the sealed fountain also.
