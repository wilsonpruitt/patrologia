## CHAPTER XXVIII.

*A fountain of gardens, a well of living waters, which run with a strong stream from Libanus.* Even as thy emissions are pomegranates, or the rest that follow, which are brought forth out of the garden of delight, fitting either for eating or for healing, so thy emission is not of one garden, but rather a fountain of gardens, whereby the fruit-bearing hearts of very many love to be watered. For whosoever willeth to become a garden, and to set himself before a barren field, who willeth to bring forth a flowering comeliness and a useful fruit, ought the more studiously to betake himself to [0400D] this conspicuous and unmuddied fountain, or not to bring back the reproach of a repulse where grace is perchance offered; for to this end the Virgin's benevolence (which it is agreed that much wisdom bringeth back), willed to offer herself to us that are the weaker as a fountain, lest upon them whom eagerness seemeth to persuade to the hope of drinking, *231* the difficulty of drawing should heap a harder repulse. For we, to whom the sweetness of perceiving, once found, is pleasing, but whom too great a labour of seeking shutteth out as it were from the entrance — her fullness, like unto a fountain, doth not exasperate but rather tempereth itself, and her breadth receiveth them that are already eager but as yet without strength. But because to the greater sort, who burn to be more fully heard, the level shallowness of a fountain seemeth to be the sooner drained, to these same [0401A] the Virgin's wisdom withdraweth itself into a deep mystery, and with labour leadeth them into her fullness. But they, the more they consider that the thing they desire is removed, and hope that in a well a larger abundance of waters is contained, being kindled with desire, gird themselves with a strong urgency, knowing that they reach not deep things save by a bucket. Not content therefore with that which seemeth as it were open and common to the more part, they let down a bucket into the depth of the well by a long rope; and they persist in the work, broken off by no shakings, until they draw back the full bucket by an unbroken rope. Tasting the draught of it, they are astonished at so great a sweetness, and by their own powers they are not strong enough to take it in; and full of its abundance, at the last they do [0401B] not wipe away their dry thirst, but after the manner or with the love of them that have the dropsy, they plunge themselves into the well itself. But when, according to a certain heathen, *Waters take a taint unless they be moved,*
in the bottom of the well they find waters not standing, not corruptible by a degenerate lying-still, not corruptible by the fault of standing, but in a wondrous manner; and finding, they draw living waters, showing forth as much marvellous sweetness as fullness. To these nothing dead, nothing hurtful cometh near; no serpent hisseth in, none woundeth with venomous breath; no branch fallen from above troubleth them, no inflowing mire cometh in; their keeper the Bridegroom granteth not that any turbid thing be within. He who is Libanus, ever white with snows not [0401C] melted — nay rather is the whiteness of the highest light and the mirror of the divine power — poureth forth waters out of himself, and a spiritual affluence of waters, and by a hidden channel filleth the virginal fountain or well. Whose deep pools, though we draw not with a larger abundance, we who come with a narrow vessel indeed and with a desire that cannot contain; yet whosoever is able to weigh with what richness Libanus poureth forth waters, knoweth with what a strong stream their abundance poureth itself into this well. Come together to this fountain of living waters, ye that are set afar off, and loose you from her fetters, ye whom the wisdom of the world holdeth: pour out the philosophic swelling with repeated vomits; drunken with the wine of dragons, repair the ruin of your study by the living draught of water. A good garden, which hath [0401D] obtained the name of a fountain or of a well, marked with many a kind of fruits and of medicinal spices; rich in the watering of a water that breedeth not worms with a stinking stillness, but floweth with a strong stream from Libanus and leapeth up with a living force unto life. Of which garden the bridegroom is held with so great solicitude, with so great care, heaping up its advancements, so perfectly removing the things that would hurt, that he hath not only sealed it up enclosed, lest it should lie open to a thief or a violent robber, but hath also protected it from above, lest it should be touched even for a little by a wicked wind.

## CHAPTER XXIX.

*Arise, O north wind, and come, O south wind: blow through my garden, and its spices shall flow.* [var: Vulg. Cant. 4:16 *et fluant aromata illius*, "and let its spices flow" — a jussive subjunctive where Migne prints the future indicative *fluent*] A fair order. First indeed [0402A] he addresseth the north wind and removeth him far off, lest he come hastily before and seize beforehand upon the region: lest he spread himself freely through the fields of the broad air, and pour upon a garden rich in fruits heavy blasts or hail. Thou, saith he, who hast set thee a seat as it were royal toward Arcturus, purposing to bear rule and to subject the world to serve thee, arise, and let this presumption hold thee secure no longer — thee whom I will to depart afar off, and not to rage above the beauty of the garden. Thou didst think to be like unto the Most High, thou that wast called Lucifer only, whereas to the sun, who alone is, there is no giving of light by another; and it is agreed that thy wickedness is gainsaid by the very name thou bearest, for he that hath a like can no longer be called the Most High. *Arise* therefore, and now restrain the swollen bellows of this sitting; driven from thy swollen seat, [0402B] vanish into thin airs; as a thinner dust, scatter thee among thy like; or as an emptier smoke, plunge thee into the eyes of the proud. Among our serious things thy mockeries please not, the jest of them that make sport pleaseth not; in the garden of holiness, lost to thee, there is no place for thee, who didst corrupt Eve, laughing and prevailing in her in paradise: the virgin, or the virgin's garden, shutteth the door against thee, now derided. Arise therefore, thou whom the reckoning woundeth, whom the swelling emptieth the nearer, while according to a lying hope it rageth not within the bounds of this garden: the garden being strongly enclosed, care not now to go about it in vain; give place to a better, whom it is expedient should enter as he cometh. *And come,* saith he, *thou south wind.* That other being removed further off, whose presence, whose neighbourhood is rightly grievous, come [0402C] thou south wind, come most healthful and sweet to the garden, whom the meridian pole and a warmer sun send hither, whose sending admitteth not the cold north wind. Thou therefore who rejoicest not in his hurtful company, nor sufferest him to rejoice in the princedom in the garden of holiness — because, he being set afar off, the Virgin is not yet content with such a benefit, present thee to her with a calm *232* countenance, to supply what she requireth. Out of that hidden seat wherein is nothing of darkness, nothing of foulness, rich in pure light, breathing more sweetly, come, O south wind: breathe and blow, nay rather *blow through*, that is, accomplish it with a gentle breath, that this garden may be adorned and may rejoice in a fixed adorning. It seemeth to me that the south wind holdeth the type of the holy Spirit, whose sweet breath the north wind cannot bear, and when he perceiveth him coming even by a slight scent, [0402D] he shuddereth and hasteneth to give up the primacy he had presumed. For the powerful kindness of the one is so set against the malice of the other, that by its presence it destroyeth whatsoever the other wickedly worketh; and them whom the other holdeth pressed with frost and fixed, and bindeth hard into error, this one looseth, and to them that are loosed granteth to run after the manner of torrents into love. This one visiteth the enclosed garden with a fuller coming, and with a gentler breeze cherisheth and advanceth its divers fruits, that of them may be made spices fitting for medicinal disciplines, which may flow, nay rather flow in, not to the bodies of them that need healing, but to their inward loves. And rightly doth the bridegroom either command or exhort the south wind to come, because as the Father, so also the Son testifieth [0403A] that he sendeth the Spirit, which spirit, although he is believed to be present where he will and when he will, and to profit, yet is sent by him with whom he hath naturally one being. « When the Paraclete shall come, saith he, whom I will send you [n: (Joan. XV)]. » The son therefore sendeth the spirit to the garden or virginal bridal chamber, that he coming may breathe and breathe upon it, and may soothe it with a spiritual breath; and when with a sweet breathing he shall have poured into it the marvellous persuasion of virtues, the garden yielding its spices may flow, and as it were overflow to us for a remedy.
