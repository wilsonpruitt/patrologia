## CHAPTER XXX.

*Arise, O north wind, and come, O south wind.* Or, if it please any better, in the words aforesaid the bridegroom himself doth not wholly remove the north wind, but granteth a free choice to him that willeth to arise; because, though he be full of malice, yet he prevaileth not to come unto us unless he be sent, nor to rise up to a wicked work [0403B] unless leave be given and he be permitted. The bridegroom therefore, seeing the garden strong enough with an incomparable enclosing; and that it feareth not horrible threats, the brandished sword, cruel death; that it cannot fail through the troubles of assaults or of onsets, but be proved, and by the merit of the proving be more worthily and more gloriously crowned: turneth himself to him who ever rejoiceth to become a persecutor, and, knowing no end of the matter, dareth to put forth his hands against whatsoever men are just. *Arise,* saith he, *O north wind,* break forth out of the lurking-place of depravity, put forth all thy powers and the malice of thy perverse will: thunder, lighten, make an uproar; bring in whirlwinds, hails and storms, heap up whatsoever thou canst, add new ones to the old. But think not that with unbridled malice thou shalt prevail against the virgin: the south wind cometh, giving the virgin [0403C] to bear evenly whatsoever thou bringest in; blowing through the garden within and making it drunken with a marvellous soothing, that either she may not feel it at all, or at the least may not fail in the torment. Thou north wind, arise and rise up, and sound violently without; thou south wind, breathe within, grant that what he shall have sounded be borne patiently; thou malignant one, vex the house, that the next wall may seem to burn; [0404A] thou benign one, strengthen the bridal chamber, that, it being well secured, no burning be feared. Did not the north wind seem to you to rage the more harshly, when it drove the Jews, near of kin to her, to be mad against Mary, so that by argument they should deny the virgin's integrity of childbearing, and by the law of us all should affirm that her Son, and God's Son, was begotten? Doth he not thunder harshly, when the Jews persecute the same son, when men of another nation are joined to them as partners of their malice, when the kinsmen's stubborn fury and unlovely clamour urgeth on the strangers, and both crucify the guiltless one, those by their voices, these by their nails? It is not easy to say what a buffet, nay rather what a heavy hammer, so great a force of persecutors struck upon the mother; and yet it shook not out of her garden the comeliness of her faith and of her patience, because the south wind [0404B] reigning within enclosed it about with a rampart of fortitude, and shut out from her faith and patience all oppressing force. Thus with divers affection and contrary effect are the south wind and the north wind joined in strife; thus with divers and adverse front are they dashed together after the manner of them that wage war; and against a strong foundation the heavy assaults of the flood are hurled, whence both by a victorious constancy and by the building unhurt they are beaten back. By which conflict, by which pressure the Virgin is not dried up as kindling-wood, but as a fruitful olive is medicinally made fat; out of which sweateth an oil that is more precious than any ointment, and worthy spices flow, comparable neither to gold nor to silver. Not therefore to them alone is the contention of the north wind and the south wind profitable, whose contending purpose abideth so diverse, but the profit conferred reacheth even unto us — a stretching out of the hand, rejoicing to render back [0404C] what it hath taken, not to shut all things up in silence. But since, after the praises of the Virgin which he hath vouchsafed to recount so many, the bridegroom hath willed in this present place to be silent now, I have thought it worthy that our pen also should for a little lay a finger upon its mouth, and that the fourth book of this work should now set upon itself a fitting end.

## CHAPTER THE FIRST.

[0403D]

*[CAP. V.] Let my beloved come into his garden, that he may eat the fruit of his apples.* The Virgin, seeing and approving that she hath the likeness of a garden, that she is planted with virtues and floureth with meet merits, and that with a marvellous advancement she burneth for nothing but the bridegroom's face, can no longer keep her desire under silence. For the more carefully he fortifieth her that he may defend her, the more diligently he raiseth up and commendeth her when fortified and defended: so much the more doth she love, so much the more doth she will to see her beloved's face, thinking it but little if she have somewhat, so long as she grieveth that she hath not the rest. Let him, saith she, who with a hand of power, with a word not weak, willed to root his garden, and to water it, when rooted, with living waters; who willed to enclose it and to shut out from it all things that would hurt, that they might not prevail against it, nay that they might not have any right at all in it: let him, I say, come, and let him vouchsafe to look more nearly upon that which is his own, and by a kindly looking to increase and to [0404D] guard his grace. For that the north wind is bidden to arise or is granted to rage, that the south wind on the other side is charged to come with soothing; it shall come to pass, and the garden shall feel it done, and it shall be profitable to her that feeleth it, if the beloved come to show his presence to her that loveth. He being present, the malevolent north wind shall gnash his teeth, and, hurtful though he be, shall not hurt; the south wind shall appear both sweeter and more effectual: the one, by tribulating, shall work patience; patience, trial; trial, a hope that is not confounded; the other shall work the charity which showeth itself poured abroad in our hearts. And what beloved would longer deny his presence or his coming to a garden which he planted, which he watered, to which he even gave the increase: from whose enclosure the besom of judgment cast far off whatsoever was hurtful, and which, shut in with a strong defence, he made specially his own? And whom would it not move, that he is the beloved whom she that loveth entreateth to come, and that it is nothing but his own to which he is invited as Lord, seeing that it becometh a possessor to come to his possessions even unbidden, [0405A] and that love itself is a cry, so that he cannot but call one so beloved? Let the beloved therefore come into the garden — not another's, not into another's right, but into his own — and let him show himself lovely and serene to his own; and holding as welcome whatsoever he shall see in it that is useful and honest, let him pluck it with his hand, nay let him even eat it, unless perchance he shall find the season unripe. My apples, as I judge, show themselves fit for the gathering — nay rather his apples, which put forth no ripeness save by his gift; which do not, as the apples of some men do, please when seen and straightway vanish when touched, but ripen with a savoury odour and a solid taste. Let him coming eat not only the outward face of these apples, comely to the eyes, but rather their ripeness, fruitful to an eager taste — that is, let him be pleased to take them to himself, let him take them out of the midst, [0405B] and let him rejoice to lay them up in his own storehouse. For though it be needful to abide here for the gaining of merit, yet I count it far better to rejoice there in the reward: and therefore I would be, not so much in the field of labour, as in the barn of rest, and would have my husbandman fed and refreshed with his own harvest. The bridegroom heareth the words of the bride, nay rather her desire, and answereth; and he doth not promise, according to the Virgin's prayers, that he will come, or even that he will eat; but, what is far more, he showeth sooner than the saying that he hath already come, and that he hath not with a deaf ear put off the asked-for coming even for a little.
