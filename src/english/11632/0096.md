## CHAPTER VII.

[0415D]

*Open to me, my sister, my friend, my dove, my undefiled, for my head is full of dew, and my locks of the drops of the nights.* Thou, saith he, that hast barred the chamber of thy mind against the malevolent robber, that hast made firm the virginal enclosure, that hast made firm the watchful guard against all things whatsoever: to me, who am not envious of thee but desirous of thee and of thine advancement and of thy crown, unbar the doors, lay aside now for a little while thy leisure or thy silence. Rising, awake; hear more healthfully the voice of him that knocketh; shake the lock from the doorpost — to me indeed nothing at all ought to be shut; and that which thou shuttest worthily against the enemy, against the world, against any stranger whatsoever, try thou more surely that it is worthy of merit or of praise to open to me alone. When I suffer thee to be at leisure *240* and [0416A] to give thyself more quietly to sleep, to take care of thyself, to give thy hands to leisure or to be silent — I confess it — the bridal chamber ought to be shut, the door barred, the virginal secret not to be unbarred by vice nor by outward business. For with the door shut that Father of spirits willeth to be prayed to, willeth his face to be sought, willeth the kiss of him that seeketh to be made serene: he willeth so fruitful a leisure not to be troubled by the crowds of businesses, but to be prospered by a gentler breeze and by a most effectual course. But when I stand by and knock more diligently at the fastening, when my voice soundeth and asketh that an opening be given it, it is not of counsel that thou lie hid longer under the shadow of silence, but that thou stand to the commandment and show thyself subject to him. Open therefore to me, the sweetness of sleep being interrupted a little, not cast away, that what thou hast received in the chamber thou mayest preach upon the housetops; and, as it were forgetful of thyself, that thou be mindful for me of them that [0416B] stand by me, and be not grieved to speak for the sake of thy brethren and thy neighbours the peace thou hast foretasted. For neither oughtest thou to indulge thy own desires only, nor to milk out the sweetness of the heavenly milk unto thine own lips alone, but also, when the time requireth, to provide for the profit of others, lest thou seem to envy them that cannot what thou hast been able to draw sooner or more abundantly. In short, there is no cause wherefore a sister ought to shut against a brother, who as heirs, yea rather as co-heirs, war together for the most high Father; whom kinship of flesh, whom the most powerful force of nature joineth, whom one love, one concord maketh of one mind by a just right. As therefore thou shuttest, and rightly shuttest, against the enemy with the bar of anxiety, so, as a friend, draw back the bar, shake off the lock for thy brother and thy friend alike — thou that art made a friend by the diligence of thy mind, by the bond of charity, [0416C] a faithful keeper of secrets, privy to my truth. And because thou dost not, like the peacock, rejoice or swell with a blind beauty of eyes, but growest gentle in the spirit of innocence by my irradiating grace, thou that art sister and friend art rightly called my dove also, cheerful in the hope of things to come, groaning for those same things because they are not yet fully present. In these and in other such things, well enough furnished about by my gift, thou art worthily called an undefiled Virgin and perfect; perfect, I say, not that nothing at all is wanting to thy present life, but because thou art so wholly beautiful that now there is no spot in thee. Thou therefore, that art marked with names of this sort — nay rather with the very substance of the names — that findest me so favourable in the bounty of these gifts: hear the voice of him that knocketh, open the shut doors; and lest thou think the instancy of him that knocketh idle, behold the causes. For my head [0416D] is full of dew: and my locks of the drops of the nights. The head of Christ, Paul bearing witness, is God. But the dew, which the icy night poureth over the earth, and which the sun dissolveth as a mist when with the morning radiance it poureth itself into things, is the cold of unbelievers, and the subtle malice of the perfidious whereby the nations are held fast, and the iniquity of the Jews is chilled [n: (I Cor. II)]. Who, while they understand not, confess not, that Christ the man is God, the Son of God, what do they but fear not to burden and to offend his head, and to fill it with so great burdens and vexations that he can as it were not bear so full a labour? Whence God by Isaias: « *I am full, saith he [n: (Isa. I)]*. And again: *I have laboured with bearing (ibid)*. » With so great a dew, saith he, with so nightly a cold do you burden me, [0417A] that I seem to myself stricken and full with the weariness of your depravity; and I, who, knowing not to be troubled, do tranquilly judge the world that groweth worse, do labour hard in bearing the loads of you that are unjust. The Jewish dew indeed, and the cold malice of him that believeth not, speaking in the Gospel uttereth the words of one that oppresseth more harshly; who, seeing Christ to be that man, understandeth not nor loveth aught else, offending herein most of all, that he truly proclaimeth himself God. *For a good,* say they, *work we stone thee not, but for blasphemy; and because that thou, being a man, makest thyself God [n: (Joan. X)]*. And above: *Therefore did the Jews the more seek to kill him, because he said God was his Father, making himself equal to God [n: (Joan. V)]*. You see the head of Christ pressed down with dew cold enough, and Christ worthily complaining of a malice [0417B] more wicked, whose not only head but locks also are weighed down with the like cold, while unbelievers persecute them with hatred and with reproaches. Locks is the name given to the balls or heaps of hairs which, cleaving to the head by the root, are outwardly leagued together by a certain covenant of nearness; which, while each keepeth its due place or course, do themselves afford to the head both covering and honour alike. By whom, as I see, the saints are not unfitly figured, who are called together unto the faith out of divers places or nations; whom the firmness of the same faith maketh to cleave the more tenaciously to the head, and whom a brotherly nearness maketh outwardly to cohere one to another after the manner of bees. Such do I judge them to have been in the primitive Church who are read to have come together to Christ with a devout spirit; who either, few in number, followed him before his passion when he was about to suffer, [0417C] or, when he was raised up, obtained more largely the faith of the resurrection. Of whom Mark: *He called,* saith he, *unto himself whom he would himself, and he made that there should be twelve with him [n: (Marc. III)]*. And Luke in the Acts of the apostles: *There was,* saith he, *a company of men together, about an hundred and twenty [n: (Act. I)]*, *and they were all filled with the Holy Ghost, and began to speak with divers tongues, according as the Holy Ghost gave them to speak [n: (Act. II)]*. And below: *They were baptized,* saith he, *and there were added in that day about three thousand souls [n: (ibid.)]*. And a little after: *The number of the men was made five thousand [n: (Act. IV)]*. And far below, Paul preaching: *they were baptized,* saith he, *in the name of the Lord Jesus; and all the men were about twelve [n: (Act. XIX)]*. These are the locks of the Bridegroom, for whom he hath no mean [0417D] care, *241* and whose advancement loving, he hardly beareth their oppression: for the most part the nights grow heavy with drops that water, or rather that rush in, which, the sun being put far off, grow dull with colder darknesses. But as every just man is by merit called a day of life, so the ungodly man and the sinner, living in ruin, is called night; upon whom, because the light hath not risen and the sun of justice hath not shone, he hath drawn over himself a horrid cold and the darknesses of ignorance. Which sort of night, and the dark conversation of sinners, hateth justice, condemneth the innocence of the better sort; and forbiddeth her to go forth with a clean step as under the naked air, while with more frequent vexations, as with rainy drops, it disquieteth her. Doth not that night seem to you to have stood forth the more importunate, which rejoiced to have oppressed the apostolic choir the more harshly; which, although [0418A] it plucked not away utterly the whole lock from the head to which it had once cleaved, yet, shaking out one hair, brought it into the pit of perdition? For when the princes of the Jews would take and kill Christ, with many wiles and with many contrivings they laboured to seduce and to lead on Judas Iscariot, whom they had perceived beforehand to labour the more maliciously with avarice, that he should sell for a vile price his own Salvation. But all the rest were indeed grievously shaken by the wind of fear, yet were not shaken off from the base set under them nor from the root implanted in them; whom the hand of the spouse, calling them back, willed to restrain under a rule, that thenceforth they might bear with a more even mind both the threats of princes and the drops of the nights. Whence, when after the resurrection the princes of the priests thundered with threats and prisons, with words and stripes, that by fear they might call back the disciples [0418B] from their purpose of preaching, they: *If it be just,* say they, *to hear you rather than God, judge ye; for we cannot but speak the things which we have seen and heard [n: (Act. IV)]*. And below: *We ought to obey God rather than men [n: (Act. V)]*. Locks of this sort, whom charity knitteth, whom a fellowly love coupleth, whom an inward faith and a spiritual devotion maketh to cleave to God — these the nights assail from without, and the darksome hands of unbelievers, burdensome with colder drops and with more frequent malices. For as, the prophet bearing witness, a cistern maketh water cold [n: (Jer. VI)], so hath she made her malice cold. That therefore the locks of the faithful may be fortified against the enemies and against the enemies' burdens, and may not be wickedly oppressed either by ignorance or by voluntary consent, the spouse admonisheth the Virgin that [0418C] she open to him the gate of secrets, and, expounding what she knoweth, instruct the minds of her neighbours. For although the unction of the spirit is read to teach the just concerning all things [n: (I Joan. II)], yet not in vain is the Virgin bidden to apply diligence, because it hindereth not, nay rather it profiteth unto an effectual increase of spiritual grace, if by planting and by watering a fitting foundation be laid beforehand. But because it is sweeter to be at leisure and to see how sweet is the leisure and the pleasantness of contemplating, than to labour with the word and with the anxiety of preaching: the Virgin, choosing to be shut in and diligent not to be torn from her purpose, answereth as though she grieved to be driven forth to businesses.
