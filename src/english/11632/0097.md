## CHAPTER VIII.

[0418D] *I have put off my garment, how shall I put it on? I have washed my feet, how shall I defile them?* Adam was made naked, set under the naked air, wrapped neither in the enclosure of a chamber nor in swaddling clothes, that he might look up to heaven with a countenance hindered by no covering, that unto the seeing of God he might pass with an unhindered step. But because, being uxorious, he condescended to the will of the seduced woman, and, the Creator being offended, stretched himself out to that which was forbidden: guilty of death, he entangled himself and his own in troubles and in labours, and, his nakedness being lost, clothed them with a wretched mortality. Whence also after his disobedience he was clothed with skins, that, being destitute of a simple life and of a naked justice, he might know the loss of a miserable punishment to hang over him, the form whereof a garment made of dead animals representeth. To a punishment of this sort there seemeth, [0419A] as I see, to pertain that anxious labour whereby we desire to be taught or to teach; and, until we see that which is perfect with the eye of a sound mind more fully unveiled, in the sweat of our countenance we eat our own bread. From which heat or sweat we take breath a little, when, the burden of cares being removed, we breathe again unto things invisible; and we are stripped by a sweet leisure of the ragged garment of labour, when with the door of the mind shut we behold the sweetness of an inward quiet. Which sweetness, because the spouse now foretasteth it with a greedy taste, she consenteth not to open the sooner to the spouse that disquieteth her, but, fleeing, rejoiceth to behold the naked truth, her linen cloth left behind, fearing to be twisted back from so happy a leisure unto business. After that, saith she, the anxiety being laid aside wherein I sweated the more laboriously, I have stripped myself of the entangled garment of outward things by the beholding of peace, [0419B] how shall I break off the pleasantness of the sweetness I have found, and send myself back to outward cares, which I love not? I refuse not the commandment, I abjure not obedience, but I fear to be subjected again to a burden that will weigh me down: inward things being tasted, I care not now to revisit outward things; and yet against him that commandeth I stop not mine ear after the manner of the asp. I had indeed laid down the spoils of an old heaviness, and had found for myself the joys of a pure nakedness; I rejoiced that I was no longer where *242* I had been, nay rather that I was no longer what I had been; and by an advance pleasing enough I seemed, naked, to be present among the naked angels. Forgetful of things visible, I beheld things invisible the more gratefully; I knew not vexations, I was not pressed by narrow bars; nothing withstood me in this breadth of mind; what I love, what I desire, stood by me the more largely. Whence if mine own will [0419C] hath drawn me back, I am worthy to be accused; if necessity hath pressed my will, I seem to be weighed down; and thus a kind of peril as it were hangeth over on either side, since it is hard to do anything unwillingly or unjustly. Being free therefore from a ragged anxiety, how shall I be entangled again? Being stripped of temporal things, with what patience shall I be clothed anew? How shall I carry back my washed feet to the mire of the streets, that I may defile them with the vexations either of faults or of cares? For although in speaking to my neighbours and in anxiously bringing forth the things I now know I have not moved my foot from the just path, as very many are wont to do, yet I seem to myself to have incurred certain defilements, feeling that the vexations of cares have run against my heart. For whatsoever meeteth and withstandeth, even for [0419D] a little, one that contemplateth the divine cleanness, or standeth not by that same contemplator with a favouring compliance — if it be compared by a fitting consideration to that cleanness itself, then, although holy in its own kind, it shall yet rightly be judged defiled. How therefore, having tasted that sweetness, shall I bear this bitterness again, and twist back the steps of so great advances unto the defilements of failings? By what fruit of another shall I put myself under a burden of my own, when I desire henceforth to approach not the contest but the crown? But while I hesitate, while I doubt, as it were not knowing how to find a way whereby I may now be willing or able to return to that which I had broken off; while, rejoicing in silence, I defer to return to the accustomed [0420A] burden — behold, I feel the hand of my beloved put in through the hole.

## CHAPTER IX.

*My beloved put his hand through the hole, and my belly trembled at his touch.* He suffereth me not, admonished by his voice, to stick fast any longer; but the beloved, boring through the fastening, putteth his hand through the slender hole; to a shut mind he openeth a subtle understanding after the likeness of a hole, and, it being opened, he inserteth what he knew profitable for me unto advancement. The hand of the spouse is not unfitly named that grace of his which openeth the understanding, and, it being opened, worketh effectually; which poureth in and inspireth the more subtly to the opened understanding by what manner or order a man tendeth and aspireth the more perfectly to the pleasing of the spouse. By the help therefore and the working of this hand a subtler [0420B] understanding perceiveth that without the good pleasure of the spouse there is no advancement of them that are at leisure, none of them that labour; and that leisure is to be pursued when his good will inviteth us to it, and that he who, being bidden, shall have willed to desist from this escapeth not his wrath and judgment. For a man may both displease, being stubborn in leisure, and, being humble, obtain merit in business; and neither state is found profitable enough, if he be not, as one obedient, prevented by the good will of Christ. This being subtly understood, she striveth no more to resist violently, but that which in her had as it were hardened is rendered soft, like a belly; and her heart, softened by the gracious hand, is shaken strongly and trembleth; and, fearing lest she offend, she now desireth nothing but the good pleasure of the spouse. Good is the hand of grace, which mightily boreth through the things that are somewhat dark, giving light [0420C] to the understanding that it may the more clear-sightedly see the things that shall profit: at whose [sic: *sublilem*] touch, if aught had been hard, it groweth soft; and, being made soft, the trembling groweth strong unto obedience. Who will grant that he put his hand to us through the straits of the hole, and, the understanding being opened, suffer not anything to grow hard, but, when he hath given a subtle intelligence for the seeing, may consequently pour in a nimble diligence for the fulfilling? Happy therefore is the Virgin who is touched by the sent hand of him that worketh, whose soft heart trembleth at the touch or the efficacy of him that commandeth; who, the command being heard, withdraweth not into the lodging of negligence, is not darkened in understanding, halteth not in affection, but obeyeth. Whence she addeth: *I arose,* etc.

## CHAPTER X.

[0420D] *I arose to open to my beloved.* She who a little above shuddereth to take up again the garment she had laid down, and as it were knoweth not how she should defile her washed feet anew, now perceiving more fully in what and to whom she ought to obey, riseth and hasteneth to open to him whom she loveth. She was held indeed with herself in a pleasing seat or chamber, and rejoiced as in an inner room in a sweet leisure: rejoicing in a soft reclining, with her head laid back she rested, and knew not the wakeful tumults and whatsoever maketh noise without. Out of a rest of this sort, leisured, or out of a peaceful sitting, the Virgin, rising, is shaken out and is girded for the contest; and what the spouse's uttered voice obtained not with her, his hand put in [0421A] prevaileth to do with a fuller effect. For the voice that is heard teacheth to do, but giveth not also to do; and the ear which is rendered ready for hearing is not straightway rendered ready for effect also; but that which *243* the voice of him that commandeth speaketh out to us for the knowing, the touch of him that worketh followeth up with grace for the fulfilling. Whence Isaias: *Lord,* saith he, *who hath believed our hearing [n: (Isa. LXIII)]*? [var: Vulg. Isa 53:1 *Quis credidit auditui nostro?* — without *Domine*; the vocative belongs to the New Testament citations of the verse, Rom 10:16 and John 12:38.] The words of prophecy sound everywhere in the ears of all, but, the hand of the spouse being withdrawn, they give not faith to Isaias; because the word, whereby the ears of unbelievers are struck from without, becometh like a tinkling cymbal [n: (I Cor. XIII)], if their minds be not touched by grace. After the voice therefore whereby the spouse admonished the Virgin to open, because she deferred to obey the admonitions of him that admonished, he set himself by an operative grace to soften her heart, and by the touch of his hand to make her ready [0421B] to fulfil what he had commanded. Whence elsewhere also Solomon is found to commend the same Lord, showing that what he speaketh out with his mouth he consummateth with his hands, giving to understand that it is not sufficient enough that he be prompt to speak, unless with a more gracious good will he have set his hands also to the fulfilling. *With thy mouth,* saith he, *thou hast spoken: and with thy hands thou hast performed [n: (III Reg. VIII)]*. With his mouth therefore the Bridegroom admonished the Bride unto labour, but by the touch of his sent hand he rendered her the prompter: her that rested in sleep or in leisure he rendered watchful with his mouth, that she might hear, but nimble with his hand, that rising she might open the more watchfully. *I arose,* saith she, *to open to my beloved.* She who, lying, could not, or perhaps would not, open, being made to tremble by the touch of the hand riseth and hasteneth to obey; she riseth, I say, lest by deferring she seem to hinder him that knocketh, accounting profitable to herself [0421C] whatsoever at last she perceiveth to please him. Her silence therefore being broken off, she riseth and prepareth herself for doctrine, that she may set a moral discipline within the hearts of her hearers; she rendereth her hands active, that she may give to the rest a form of living, and, as well by word as by work, may open to the spouse a path for his coming. For then indeed is the way effectually opened to Christ, when in the teacher's word truth, and in his act severity, is found; when preaching or doctrine so instructeth the minds of the hearers that the discipline of the virtue shown beforehand edifieth them the more. Which the Virgin showing addeth: *My hands,* etc.
