## CHAPTER XV.

[0428C] *I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem, if you find my beloved, that you announce to him that I languish with love.* The Virgin, after she had been prevented, as hath been said above, by the admonitions of her Beloved, and, he turning aside, had been found by the watchmen; these also withdrawing themselves, willeth to return to the daughters of Jerusalem — that is, to the apostles — and to accost these also concerning him whom she loveth with so great a desire. You, saith she, who after the likeness of daughters burn the more vehemently with the tenderness of love, who prepare the inward chamber of the mind for the invisible Bridegroom; you I admonish — nay, lest the admonition be held insufficient, you I adjure, willing that none pass over what is so firmly adjured. If therefore by any manner or order it shall have been granted you to find the invisible, to touch [0428D] the unapproachable: announce ye, preach ye, that I am burdened with a tender love, that by your intervention I may deserve to be commended to him whom I love. If a purified delay hath held you in suspense, if it hath led you to the Bridegroom with a prompt desire, with an eye made clean: tell him of my heats, of my untiring love, of my mighty languor; ask him that he attend, calling to mind a wounded charity. But if the day most desired shall have led any of you out of the body, if you shall have come thither first, the linen cloth of the flesh being cast off, with a face utterly unveiled — because both the last for the most part rejoice to be made first, and they who seemed the first complain to be made the slower — if divine grace shall have given you an unhindered way, and you shall have found him first, whom we seek together, being sought for: I will that, when you have found him, you announce with what heat I am held, with what love, with what failing [0429A] I languish, with what desire or with what languor I faint away. If you, I say, the king's table shall have received; if the light that is found shall have poured itself over you before, while I remain in darkness, being still with Joseph kept in the prison: remember me who am held, and let a fresh prosperity not lead you into forgetfulness, but ask the mighty Lord that he lead me out also the sooner. And, that he who is mighty may be moved with a swifter mercy, say ye that I languish, and that my languor is intolerably weighed down; and, lest he laugh at my pains and at a heavier impatience, announce ye that this languor is begotten not of hatred of him but of love. It is an immoderate languor, which warmeth mine own soul with a more vehement fever, nay rather, being stronger than fire, melteth it like wax; which soul, being melted with heat, groweth utterly torpid toward the transitory things of the world, [0429B] *248* and, now knowing not to speak save of the Bridegroom, groweth the more languidly dumb toward all else. Which the daughters of Jerusalem weighing, they answer: *What manner of one is,* etc.

## CHAPTER XVI.

*What manner of one is thy beloved of the beloved, O thou most beautiful among women: what manner of one is thy beloved, that thou hast so adjured us?* [var: Vulg. Cant 5:9 repeats *ex dilecto* in the second question — *qualis est dilectus tuus **ex dilecto**, quia sic adiurasti nos?* Philip expounds the omission at 0430B–C and reports that most of the codices he could find carry the phrase twice.] The daughters of Jerusalem, hearing themselves the more diligently adjured by the Virgin that, if they find the Beloved, they should announce to him that she is sick even unto languor, seem to be the more inflamed unto the finding of him, and set about to inquire the more studiously what manner of one he is who is sought. Thou, say they, who hast already known the beloved by a wondrous privilege of love, out of whom by a new fashion thou hast known thyself Bride, mother, Virgin: hereby thou already seemest to us worthily most beautiful among all [0429C] women, thou who art not held by the blindness of us all, nor by a lot equal with ours. Because in no ages past hath the divine bounty granted this, and no woman hath so come near to the pleasing of him of whom the speech is: we know that none shall be better able to instruct our ignorance, and to impart to us some knowledge, such as it may be, of that same one who is sought. For this cause perchance hath his providence not yet utterly sent him forth from the present world, and hath not yet admitted thy desire into full rest, but hath left thee among us, though against thy will, that thou mayest teach who he is that revisiteth thee the more frequently. For by how much the oftener and the more lovingly thou beholdest him, though through a glass, by so much the more dost thou deserve to obtain a fuller knowledge of him; and [0429D] by his visiting and his knocking thou art beyond doubt admonished to open for this end, that thou be not slothful to pour back upon the lesser ones the knowledge thou hast received. Say therefore what manner of one is he whom thou wilt have sought by us also, unto whom thou allegest us as messengers, if he shall have given himself to be found? Say what manner of one he is, lest in seeking we be rashly seduced, and, darkened with error, be brought in upon one instead of another. For because thou hast adjured us so firmly, so lovingly, we will not to despise but rather to attend to what thou hast asked; and we truly account the word of the oath as a precept: only say what manner of one is that beloved of thine of the beloved. We know indeed that thou lovest the Father, whose Son thou so affectest, and that thou failest not to join the begetter to the begotten in loving; but because none hath seen the Father save the Son, and none save through the Son, whereas to thee the same Son [0430A] hath not at all begrudged himself: tell us, if thou hast aught, of the quality of that same Son, of whose quantity we either hear or understand nothing, or but little. And because we too rejoice in no mean measure to love him whom thou lovest, we inquire for the grace of love not once only but again, that when thou hearest the inquiry of this attentive one repeated, thou mayest prudently understand with what affection it is asked by them that love. Let therefore the inquiry pointedly repeated move thee, that thou defer not to answer to the things inquired by them that love; and say what manner of one that beloved of thine ought to be reckoned, how God man may be firmly believed and loved. He indeed is God of God, light of light, and of flesh flesh, man of a virgin: and we will to be taught by thee what we owe, and to whom, since to each substance that which is its own [0430B] and proper ought to be rendered. For which cause also the aforesaid inquiry is fittingly repeated, wherein a twofold love of a twofold substance is unfolded; the knowledge whereof, because it is folded up in a certain volume as it were, a diligent inquiry is instantly applied to the loosing of its seal. She asketh therefore once, and asketh again, that it be more manifestly declared to her in what manner that giant of a twin substance willeth to be loved; what he willeth to be thought of himself according as he is called *beloved* *of the beloved,* and what according as he is called *beloved* and *of the beloved* is not added. For the first inquiry, when it had said *Beloved,* straightway setteth under it *of the beloved;* the second, repeating *beloved,* addeth not *of the beloved* according to certain codices: giving thereby to understand that God is none the less worthy to be loved [0430C] out of the Father by loving; but that man, although he be loved, is yet not worthy to be embraced equally out of man. For the deity of the Son is loved equally in the Father also; but his humanity, not knowing a father, is not so loved in his mother also — which that twofold inquiry seemeth subtly to admonish, whereof the first set down *of the beloved,* and the second willed to keep silence. But if any shall say that the second also set it down and kept it not silent, I will not to contend overmuch, nor stubbornly to strive against it, because I have found many codices to support this testimony. For, although not all the codices I could find, yet very many of them, as in the first inquiry, so in the second also have *beloved of the beloved;* nor have I sufficiently ascertained whether *of the beloved* was repeated by the author, or rather written by a fault [0430D] or an error. But *beloved of the beloved* may be said, man of man, because the Virgin both loveth the son in that he is man, and loveth that of which he is; for she loveth her own flesh, *for no man hated his own flesh [n: (Ephes. V)]*. We indeed love him because, being made man, he is partaker of our nature, and our creator took upon him the lowly things of all creation; but the Virgin loveth him not only as made man, but as made man out of *249* herself, and out of that whereof he was made man she rather loveth herself for his sake. Whose becoming man, although it deserve worthily to be loved by angel or by man, yet it more befitteth him to be loved by her in whose womb, being the Virgin's, he becometh man; and what the obedience of all creation of necessity rendereth to him made man, the virgin, richer in grace, doth by a certain voluntary force of nature. Which [0431A] the daughters of Jerusalem seem to intimate in their inquiring, when they say not: What manner of one is *our* beloved, but: *What manner of one is thy beloved,* giving thereby to understand that that beloved is rightly assigned to the Virgin as it were specially, whose love nature together with grace mightily worketh. Of which beloved being inquired of, the Virgin deferreth not to answer, nor seemeth to envy the knowledge of him to them that seek it: understanding that, though she desire to be dissolved, she must yet for this end remain, that she may study to teach upon the housetops what she hath drawn in the chamber.
