## CHAPTER XVIII.

*His head is the finest gold, his locks as the raised boughs of palm trees, black as a raven.* This beloved of the Virgin, [0432D] even as he is not of one begetting, so is he believed not to be of one substance or nature; because both from the Father alone he is believed to have that which he is as God, and from the mother alone that which he is as man: and thus, being of a twofold substance or nature, Christ abideth alike God and man. In which juncture, wherein a twofold nature have to come together into one person, there is one thing principal and another secondary to be found; and that indeed may by the privilege of dignity be named the head, but this other, whatsoever it be, may — if nevertheless it so seem — be fitted to it by some likeness. The head of Christ therefore is that principal nature in him which, begotten of the Father, is believed coequal to him that begetteth; according to which, as he is not greater than the Father, [0433A] so neither is he less than him; but greater, as than every man, so truly than himself also. Now this head is not unfitly compared to gold — to gold, I say, the finest, which is approved above the rest, which by the worth of its own price holdeth the primacy over all metals, and which, though it lie perchance in the mire, is not disfigured by any miry nature. For the Godhead of Christ, which he is known to have coeternal and common with the Father, taking our flesh, kept itself immune from carnal faults: and being mercifully bowed down by a deep counsel toward our mire, as it were, it lay hid under the shadow of the flesh, defiled by none of its filth. By this mystery therefore, wherein God bowed himself down, remaining in his own nature, and by the voluntary right of his power was wondrously made a creature — these natures running together, and neither complaining [0433B] of loss — Christ, God and man, was given to us out of such a juncture. In which juncture God, or the Godhead, is borne above after the manner of a head, and man, or the manhood, is brought in beneath after the manner of the feet or of the body. And the head, like gold, is more precious than all bronze, forasmuch as the creator is believed to be preeminent over all creatures. But the feet, or the body, are a vessel of clay or of earth, which, taken out of a hurt matter, is rendered alien from all vices; over which, as a head, he standeth eminent who taketh it up, who advanceth what he hath taken, enlighteneth what he hath advanced, and consumeth it not. Such a head in Christ usurpeth not to itself the primacy undeservedly — a head which foulness maketh not bald, which no unseemly baldness disfigureth; but, all foulness being put far off, being fortified with an inborn beauty, it is adorned for the showing of itself with befitting [0433C] locks. Now his locks are beyond doubt the virtues of the heavens, the worthier creatures, the preeminent choir of angels, who, while others flowed away and fell irrevocably into hell, cleaving more firmly to the head, remain fixed for ever. Who by the privilege of excellence and by the merit of the dignity they have obtained are like not to any trees soever, but to the raised boughs of palms, which warriors carry in their hand when their enemies are overcome, when on a festal day, all resplendent, they desire to show themselves conquerors. And those raised boughs of the palms are said to be the little branches which are advanced on high above the rest by a tall uprightness, which, when being advanced they have stood more firmly with their top on high, remain in all seasons alike unbending both in stiffness and in comeliness. So the virtues of the heavens are raised on high [0433D] above the rest, when they rejoice to enjoy him by whom, while the others fell, they were saved; to behold whose face they hasten ever to lift themselves up, and persisting in desire, they turn not aside through any languishing weariness. Whence as men triumphing they are glad to bear the raised bough of the palm, when, now secure concerning themselves, they glory in the titles of victory, when, fear being put far away, they keep now a festal day, carrying in their hand the branch that showeth forth the gift they have obtained. They indeed hold themselves forth to them that seek to see God, and they show not the bare head of Christ everywhere to any that will: and the splendour of the gold admitteth not the eyes of them that strain toward it, but that it may in some measure be borne, the hair goeth before them to meet them. [0434A] Which hair assuredly taketh not away the presence of that same head, but promiseth it, and by a fitting progress admitteth us afterward unto it; wherein, although it hangeth first and nearer by a certain glue of love, yet to us that follow far off it condescendeth by a certain likeness. For cleaving to the head the beauty of the locks shineth out, but condescending to us their foulness appeareth: the locks are white as a lily when they present themselves to the golden head, but when they are at hand to us, they represent the blackness of a raven. Considering the head of Christ, who with God the Father hath naturally one being, they rejoice to be like to the tall and glowing raised boughs of the palms. But considering whatsoever they understand to have been made, compared with the white maker, they see it to be as it were black. The angels therefore, lifted up [0434B] by the strivings of contemplation toward their maker, see that the Creator far excelleth all creatures; and that he indeed is white, or is somewhat that may be reckoned brighter still, while these grow as it were torpid and are made like unto blackening ravens. For by how much the more they hang upon loving him *251* with a greater desire, by so much the more do the angels perceive not only men, but themselves also, to be black; and to whom the excellence of the head shineth out brighter than gold, to those same their own humble consideration groweth black. So assuredly the locks of Christ are like, as hath already been said, to the raised boughs of palms, when they attentively consider the height of his Godhead; and yet they are not unfitly said to grow black after the manner of a raven, when by their own cry they confess themselves far unlike to him. For when they strongly and singularly love his eternal beauty, [0434C] they cry out with a humble voice that they are black and abject: considering that that which hath its beginning from another, and lacketh not an end save through another, is not compared to him who through himself abideth without beginning and without end. But this consideration, whereby the angels extol the head of Christ and humble themselves in the contest, derogateth not, detracteth not from their merits, nor from their crown; but they the more confidently seek out or obtain the branch of victory, by how much the more evidently they cry aloud that they are black and humble, like a raven.
