## CHAPTER XIX.

*His eyes as doves upon the brooks of waters, which have been made washed, and sit beside the fullest streams.* [var: Vulg. *super rivulos aquarum, quae lacte sunt lotae* — "upon the little brooks of waters, which are washed with milk." Philip's exposition requires the milk, and glosses it four times below.] The Bridegroom hath eyes also, wherewith he goeth about and surveyeth all things, and with a mighty compassing advanceth [0434D] and illumineth what he hath seen; which eyes he loveth to open for the bestowing of his gifts upon us, and, lest he condemn, he hasteneth to cover us because of our deserts. Whence also these eyes are said to have not the eagle's severity, but with a more gracious affection they hold the dove's simplicity, because assuredly the eagle seeth far more severely and more keenly for the prey, but the dove more innocently, signifying a mild and simple discipline. And since eyes of this sort are likened to doves rather than to eagles, I deem it not unfitting if they be called grace and truth, because in grace and truth God made all things, and saw all that he had made to be very good, and vouchsafeth to angels as well as to men that are good a reward or a crown. For he who gave being to the angels, who [0435A] had not been, willed, when some of them fell, not to be wanting to the others that remained; and those whom he saw with grace, lest they should fall by a proud wickedness, he rendered fixed and stable by truth, so that now they could not fall. But that angel whom God looked not upon with the eye of grace turned himself aside from the uprightness wherein he had been made; and not being prevented by grace, he received no stable footing in truth, and being turned away from either eye, the wretch irreparably deceived himself. Happy therefore is that man whom the divine goodness beholdeth with the eye of grace, when, guilt requiring it, he is already held beneath the tree, when, his wife's false counsel being preferred to the rule of truth, he feareth the voice he hath heard, because of the fresh ignominy of his nakedness. For him whom, darkened beneath the fig tree, he mercifully enlightened with the look of grace, he consequently [0435B] recalled by an order gracious enough to the light of truth: and him that was worn down with labours, that he might render a requital for the wickedness he had presumed, grace instructed, that it might render him fixed unto truth. But that he might confer this benefit upon the whole human race, and, its origin being hurt, might set mercy above judgment, when the world was now pressing on toward evening there arose a light of sudden newness, whereby the Word was made flesh, full of grace and full of truth. Whose brighter rising, when it poured itself into us that were dim of sight, poured over us these two luminaries; and being made man he vouchsafed to see us with a twofold eye, that is, to join to grace, which seeth beforehand, truth as an undivided companion. For those whom he saw with grace, that he might endow us undeserving with deserts, he saw also with truth, that he might now heap rewards upon the deserving; who assuredly giveth the first deserts to none but the undeserving, [0435C] and again crowneth none with the rewards of deserts but the deserving Therefore he saw us with grace, when he called us undeserving, and them that were called he provoked unto justice and to the deserts of justice; he saw us also with truth, when, as we yielded our assent, he bestowed himself as a helper, who before had bestowed himself only freely as a promiser. Grace indeed was the calling and the promise of him that strengtheneth, but truth was the exhibiting or the benefit of him that helpeth: nor is it profitable enough if grace promise us any good thing with a liberal hand, if truth its companion answer not to that promise. Rightly therefore is the Bridegroom said to have eyes, wherewith he innocently and simply beholdeth all the elect; whereof the one going before envieth not, but freely seeth the undeserving, [0435D] and the other followeth them as now deserving, and dischargeth the office of truth. And since they look not upon us too severely, but regard us with a harmless simplicity, and meditate no prey, but stretch themselves out unto the salvation of us all, rightly doth the holy Virgin say that they are like doves, which their own nature maketh to be simple and harmless. Which doves haunt the brooks of waters and the fullest streams, that they may foresee the ravening birds which wickedly set themselves against the simpler sort — as they say who search more curiously into the natures of birds, and prove by certain conjectures that the matter standeth so. But that the doves are said to be washed not with waters but rather with milk intimateth that they are far removed from material doves; and because neither use nor reason admitteth *252* that doves be washed so, [0436A] this saying plainly sendeth us back to a spiritual understanding. The eyes therefore of the Bridegroom, as hath already been said, being like unto doves, are not only over the waters but over the little brooks of waters, when his grace and truth teach us the content of the Scriptures, showing whence, and by what way, and whither the doctrine of the prophets floweth as it passeth by. For they, drawing in joy out of the fountains of the Saviour, bring down even unto us the manifold promises of the Creator, which cease not to come upon us and to pass us by with a promising murmur, until the thirst of them that wait be refreshed with a fulfilling gift. Lastly, to this end are the Bridegroom's eyes said to be over the flowing abundance of the waters, and they set us in that same place, and grace and truth behold us so set, that they may teach us that nothing that is begotten [0436B] standeth fast, after the manner of a little brook, but by a natural flowing and by its own motion hasteneth to destruction. For over this — that the fashion of this world passeth away, and is rolled onward in the course of things, and that the inconstancy of times and days suffereth nothing to stand — there is need of a cleaner and simpler understanding, that in these transitory things the profit may be firmer and more solid. Therefore perchance the doves to which the aforesaid eyes are compared are reported to be washed not with waters but rather with milk, because to milk a snowy whiteness is naturally assigned, wherein the clean simplicity of the dovelike grace is designated. Which grace assuredly so seeth us, and so also giveth us to see, that it both harm us not and will have us not to harm another: and in our sight it willeth the mirror of the Scriptures to shine, that we may be able to foresee the hurtful assaults of others. Which [0436C] cleanness of seeing the prophet Habacuc commendeth in the Lord himself: *Thy eyes are clean,* saith he, *that thou mayest not behold evil, and thou canst not look upon iniquity [n: (Habac. I)]*. Good eyes indeed, which are known to shine again with so great a cleanness that they mercifully choose not to see our malice, which cannot turn back their gaze upon our iniquity, but rejoice to advance their glance toward our profit. Whence also they are consequently said to sit beside the fullest streams, forasmuch as they enlighten us and make us to sit beside those same streams, when they teach us to approach the Scriptures of the Gospel, which flow with a fuller doctrine, and to embrace them with a more gracious affection. For after the manner of waters the prophetic Scriptures make us fruitful, but the Scriptures of the Gospel [0436D] flow far more plentifully and abound, because in the former the spiritual understandings are wrapped up in figures and dark sayings, but in the latter they shine fully and are unfolded in plainer words. The bright fountain of good poured itself into us of old with an abiding little brook, but afterward more largely overflowed us with the great rush of a river, because both by the old Scriptures it gave us a profitable watering to drink, and afterward by the Scriptures of the Gospel it more fully satisfied our thirst. Who would not commend these eyes of the Bridegroom, like unto doves, which mingle for us the brooks and the fullest streams of the Scriptures, whose sight, knowing not our evils, advanceth and maketh fruitful our good things, and with a prosperous profit cleanseth us as with a milky whiteness? For that the eyes, or the doves to which the eyes are likened, are recorded either to sit beside the waters or to use a milky [0437A] washing, ought to be reckoned not frivolous but profitable, not superfluous but rather mystical, wherein the Bridegroom loveth to work our salvation by grace and truth.
