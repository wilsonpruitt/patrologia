## CHAPTER XX.

*His cheeks are as beds of spices, set by the perfumers.* After the praises of the eyes, the Virgin maketh a passage to the cheeks also, which she judgeth in no wise alien from the merit of praise; wherein, as was said long since [n: (supra l. I, c. 2)], the beauty of the face shineth out, and the face is worthy to be praised for the merit of its fairness. The Virgin therefore praiseth the Bridegroom's face, that is, the beauty of the Godhead, which flowereth with a certain laughter and gloweth with the flower of honourableness, which appeareth cheerful and delightsome to them that behold it dutifully, and is found [0437B] profitable and fruitful by them that worship it devoutly. He indeed is *in form fair above the sons of men [n: (Psal. XLIV)]*, whose comeliness is inestimable and, beyond what can be told, fruitful; to whom, although she judged that nothing at all was properly to be compared, yet the Virgin compared him to beds of spices, by a certain licence of speech. Now beds are little gardens, rich not in gold, not in silver, nor indeed in things of this sort, but in aromatic trees and in manifold spices, out of which by the skilled hand of the perfumer a medicinal ointment is made, whereby wounds are healed and a soothing is given to pains. So also is the Bridegroom's, that is, his divine beauty, wherein is nothing defiled, no unseemly foulness: adorned indeed with a flower, not with a transitory colour, not too fraudulently sought out by art, but [0437C] with an inborn splendour and with a form of worthy virtues. In which beauty eternity knoweth no beginning, immortality no end; an unwearied light shineth, change is fixed, peace resteth: power hath power, wisdom is wise, the presence of charity burneth, and there flourisheth a certain commonwealth, leagued together not of citizens but of virtues. Whose league is found not weak, not unprofitable, but whosoever loveth it findeth it mighty and profitable, while its aromatic ointment, while its confection or its fatness, is made unto us that are sick a mighty and sufficient medicine. For no *253* bed is set by a hand so prudent as to grow rich with spices so great, with a medicine so mighty; no perfumer hath adorned his little garden with plantings so effectual, nor pounded such [0437D] herbs or spices in his little mortar. Or certainly the cheeks of the bridegroom are his two substances or natures, for the Creator willed to be united personally to the creature; and a twofold substance was found to come together into one and the same bridegroom, and by a wondrous coming together to adorn as it were one face. Whose coming together made one God and man, long before divided, and, loosing the wall set between, joined heavenly things to earthly; condescending to men, it brought them an example and a doctrine, poured in the gift of grace, and afforded a salutary medicine. Whence also these cheeks are compared to beds, that is, to little gardens, out of which herbs and spices are fitly brought forth for aromatic uses, which assuredly the perfumers till with a carefulness that shall profit — perfumers whom, being taught in the healing art, the [0438A] care of the languishing that are to be made whole holdeth fast. For when God suffered the world to perish by sin through many thousands of years, and neither man nor angel prevailed to root it out, at length the perfumers prudently chose certain beds for themselves, out of which they gathered medicinal spices and the spices of salvation. For they joined together alike the divine nature and the human, and by a deep counsel laid upon them as it were a certain traffic of healing; and when these were joined into one person, as cheeks into one face, they compounded a spice, and refreshed the languishing world with the grace of health. Nor do the perfumers seem to me to be any other than the Father and the Son and their Spirit, who are said to take part in the aforesaid traffic by a common counsel; of whom, although none but the Son is rightly to be believed to be incarnate, yet the Father together with the Spirit are said to work [0438B] the incarnation. The cheeks therefore of the bridegroom, that is, the two natures which wondrously came together into one person, as beds set by the perfumers, brought forth aromatic spices, whose confection and the showing forth of a strange art conferred upon us the wished-for benefit of a salutary medicine. Worthily therefore, and for the cheeks' sake, doth the bridegroom deserve to be loved, in whose union the fruitful height of the mystery is contained, which the same perfumer turneth into an ointment for us that are sick, but for them that make progress, the Father together with the Spirit working with him, into a pigment. Whence it is subjoined: *The lips,* etc.

## CHAPTER XXI.

*His lips are lilies dropping the first myrrh.* [0438C]Whosoever is a teacher and lacketh not understanding knoweth, as I judge, that sometimes in the Scriptures the efficient is put for the effect; and because we come not to the effect of speaking save by the mouth and the lips, we find the mouth and the lips for the most part put for speech. By the lips therefore of the Bridegroom the Virgin willed to figure his words, which also she set herself, by a new manner of speech, to liken to lilies, because lilies, when looked upon, more simply signify innocence by their whiteness, and when bruised, more prudently confer a more liberal medicine. For the words of the Bridegroom know no guile, no deceit, and assuredly no falsehood, but being more diligently discussed they bring forth a firm truth; being more graciously understood they teach benevolence, innocence, chastity; being more perfectly loved they confer true holiness upon them that love. Which words, or [0438D] lips, are therefore said to drop the first or the chief myrrh, that is, to preach a doctrine effectual and necessary to all, because, although the wise men of the world willed both by writings and by words to condemn vices and to commend virtues, yet, *as sounding brass or a tinkling cymbal [n: (I Cor. XIII)]*, they lacked spiritual efficacy. But the lips of the Bridegroom, bringing forth both the Old and the New Testament, confer in either a salutary lesson upon their hearers; because, although that which went before is perchance said to be in somewhat imperfect, since it found not yet the time of grace nor fitting hearers, this latter, which came upon it in the accepted time, brought a supplement, when the sun looked back upon the darkness and light came to them that sat in the shadow. And assuredly to the lips, whereof the one is necessarily [0439A] added upon the other that we may speak, the two Testaments agree, by whose admonitions we are rightly instructed; whereof the Old seemeth of itself to lie beneath as weak and insufficient, and the New, coming upon it, to fill up its insufficiency. In which Testaments, since they were brought forth by the two lips of the bridegroom and framed with a prudent craft unto the doctrine and salvation of us all, the same bridegroom alike provoketh his hearers to a twofold profit — such of them, at least, as by his gift have obtained a spiritual understanding. For he inviteth them both to love God and to have care of their neighbours, that, dispensing temporal things, they be not unmindful of eternal ones that every just man so put his hand to a temporal study or business that at fitting seasons he may the more eagerly apply himself to spiritual leisure. Because therefore the bridegroom's [0439B] lessons instruct us in these two pursuits, rightly are his lips two, and none the less are the Testaments two; that things which no discord soever of matters or of acts gnaweth at should in these also, by a like form, not disagree in the very number. For which cause also the lilies come in by grace of a seemly likeness, which in form or in colour exceed not the aforesaid pair, in the midst whereof somewhat *254* a little red is found, which is compassed about with leaves bent back and gleaming with whiteness. That middle redness indicateth a laborious action, which, while it is lived in this life, bringeth in much suffering; nor is any man so happy as to be able utterly to avoid the presence, the burning, some coming even to the shedding of their own [0439C] blood. But the whiteness in the leaves prefigureth that blessedness wherein the Bridegroom configureth our bodies to his own body, when, whiteness being drawn over — which befalleth us by merits together with grace — hiding, nay swallowing up what was mortal, he girdeth us about with immortal glory. So assuredly the lilies, agreeing with the lips in a twofold form or colour, signify present things in the short and red part, and the glory to come in the whiteness; whose kind droppeth the first myrrh, that is, a right profitable doctrine, at that time when the understanding of it wavereth not among us that are capable thereof.
