## CHAPTER XXII.

*His hands are turned, golden, full of hyacinths.* The hands of the Bridegroom, as I judge, the Virgin named his works, which both when he was not yet born and when he was already born of the Virgin [0439D] he wrought; wherein so incomparable a praise of him that wrought them is held, that what he willeth is proved by an easy efficacy to be also what he can do. For which cause she saith also that these same hands are turned, intimating that nothing standeth in the way of the will of him that worketh, because a carpenter is found to do nothing more easily than with the lathe, and what hath been wrought with the lathe is found wholly round. In the lathe therefore the ease of him that worketh is understood, but the roundness holdeth the praise of him that consummateth, because he bringeth to effect whatsoever he willeth as soon as he willeth it, and what he willingly effecteth he also mightily perfecteth, knowing not how to slip back into defect. Which perfection assuredly, of an easy and rolling turning, is not of an angel, not of a man, not [0440A] of any simple creature whatsoever, but is of him alone who alone willed to be born of the Virgin, man and God; who was able to do whatsoever he willed, not simply as man, but as God also. To whom it rightly agreeth that the Virgin called these same hands golden, who in the gold, as it seemeth, figured the divine power, because as gold is said to prevail over the other metals, so in the Bridegroom's works the divine power is preeminent. For, to be silent of other things, that the Creator was made a creature by his own work, God willed to use his own power, not nature; and that, doing wonderful things in man, he endured grievous ones also — in the bringing of them forward it was not, as it seemed, a defect of strength, but a power. *Power,* saith he, *I have to lay down my soul [n: (Joan. X)]*. And the prophet: *He was offered,* saith he, *because [0440B] he himself willed it [n: (Isa. LIII)]*. In all things therefore which God willed to work in man, it is believed that neither lead nor tin, as it hath seemed to some, holdeth the primacy, but to them that rightly behold gold is conspicuous, gold shineth; and though it be not comprehended by the darkness, yet in the darkness the light shineth again. For the divine power, brighter and more precious than all gold, not appearing to blind men, flourisheth as it were more secretly in a treasury; and that which hideth itself from the eyes of the unbelieving, the cloud of the flesh being set between, spreadeth itself abroad (though unknown) into the branches of its works. But they who deserve worthily to understand that same power are not held by the love of visible works alone, but are rather kindled by the hope of invisible goods, and in them are held suspended by love and desire. Therefore the Virgin not only called the turned hands golden, [0440C] but with an equal testimony declared them full of hyacinths also, signifying in the hyacinths, which are stones like to the air in colour, the supernal goods which are commanded to be sought after out of love. Those very works indeed which God made man was seen to accomplish are known to abound in heavenly goods for them that behold worthily, while by a manifold doctrine they lead us in to a true understanding of them, and by sure promises bring us on to hope for them. For the dispensation of the incarnate Word containeth nothing else than hyacinths, to the buying of which it inviteth all that are nimble and girded, because the temporal working of Christ, which tendeth to nothing but heavenly goods, purposeth to lead us prompt and watchful to purchase the same by the merit of faith. Whose [0440D] praise is subjoined: *His belly,* etc.

## CHAPTER XXIII.

*His belly is of ivory, distinguished with sapphires.* Because the belly is held to be the softer and tenderer part in the body, by it the Bridegroom's flesh or manhood is rightly said to be signified, because, whereas he was God long before, he at length willed to be made weak for us, and to be made like to other men in nature, not in the vice of nature. But because *the Word made flesh [n: (Joan. I)]* was utterly ignorant of the faults of the flesh, the Virgin markedly called his belly not fleshly, because the softness of our custom or nature had not hurt the manner and the order of that begetting, such as she had known it. For that vice [0441A] which cleaved forthwith to the first men when they sinned hath by their desert so hurt the begetting of us all, that no man who begetteth can utterly *255* free himself from it, but maketh the guilt to pass over, as it were naturally, into the very child begotten. Which vice, which passing over, which now as it were a second nature, compelling both begetters and begotten to complain of the hurt, weakeneth the belly of all, that is, the flesh so begotten, and unnerveth and softeneth it, whose vicious softness no man's labour or industry taketh away from him. Which law, harshly implanted. The Virgin alone in conceiving is believed not to have felt, forasmuch as it is certain that she, knowing not a man, conceived of the Holy Spirit; and the guilt which after the manner of us all went not before her that bare came not to the Son, begotten without a father, of a mother. Whose conception brought [0441B] to his flesh not only no loss, but, the Spirit working, brought it no small strengthening; because as our begetting struck into us a nature worthy of wrath, so his flesh, conceived of the Spirit, is made stronger and more solid by nature. For from this, that both man and guilt were wanting to the conception against custom, and that moreover the Holy Spirit gave it a purer efficacy, he himself who was conceived — flesh indeed in substance, not carnal in vice — obtained a new strength of a certain natural eminence. Endowed with which nature he was utterly ignorant of the enkindling passions of the body, which generally oppress the rest of men living in this flesh; but those which are penal not by guilt but by the desert of guilt, he made subject to himself by a strength as of ivory. For unto hunger, thirst, sleep, [0441C] weariness, and such things as Scripture hath recorded him to have borne, he inclined not by any defect of strength, by the law necessary to us all, but, having by a certain inborn force powerfully surpassed the law implanted in us, he suffered none of these things save because, and when, and as much as he willed. Necessity therefore being a delay generally compulsive among us, he used his own will, powerfully effective; and he who, conceived man of the Spirit, could have not suffered human troubles nor been hurt, yet willed to bear hurts, that he might be able — which was that whereunto he had come — to be believed a man by men. For to this end had he come, that, the substance of flesh being assumed, he might truly be believed man, and by suffering willingly might heal the necessary sufferings of them that believe; that *the ivory belly,* [0441D] that is, the hidden and inborn strength of the manhood shown forth beforehand, might be turned into a remedy whereby our sickness, who truly languish, might be healed. Such a belly is not only clothed and girded with an ivory strength, but with *sapphires,* precious stones, is also *distinguished,* because the true flesh, which a singular conception strengthened with a wondrous fortitude, the Godhead joined to it beautified with an excellence of miracles. The sapphire stone is like the sky, when it is serene, when with a face as it were wiped clean it is seen alien from dark clouds; by which stone those works of the Bridegroom are rightly figured which, far above man, are specially assigned to the Godhead. For it is granted to man that he is flesh and yet [0442A] knoweth not the disordered motions of the flesh, that against soft heats he is found harder and groweth cold, bearing the part of ivory, since, if it be laid under a linen cloth, that same cloth is not burned though a coal be laid upon it. It is granted also to man that all the troubles which we are penally constrained to endure, he was able to keep off from his own body as well as his mind, as by a natural privilege, and none the less to apply the same to himself when he willed and as he willed, and, when applied, to restrain them, lest they avail more or less than the dispensation requireth. But it is granted to God that amid these things he changed water into wine by a wondrous mystery, that with scanty bread he satisfied many thousands of men, that he comforted all the sick with a wished-for remedy, and that at the last he raised the dead by the sole command of his voice. Therefore the ivory belly, [0442B] that is, the robust series of human works, is distinguished with sapphires, that is, with the beauty of divine ones, when amid those things which as man he was able, as he willed, strongly to endure, the heavenly excellence rejoiceth now and again to shine out in miracles. For he loveth neither always to work human things nor continually divine ones, but that the series of his working should be adorned by grace of distinction; and thus he so commendeth himself a man by an ivory strength, greater than Jonas or Solomon, that even in the man he showeth himself, to them that rightly believe, the beauty of the sapphire, that is, God.
