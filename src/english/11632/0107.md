## CHAPTER XXIV.

*His legs are pillars of marble, which are founded upon bases of gold. The legs* of the Bridegroom she nameth the ways or journeys whereby he came visibly unto us, when [0442C] he found us weak and torpid and knowing not how to travel, when, invisible in himself and by nature far unlike to man, condescending to men, he was made a creature, and appeared, and became known. Whose one leg was whatsoever belongeth to the incarnation and to a conversation that should pass away in time, and the other leg was that which pertaineth to his death and to his burial; so that his whole action and his whole passion were as it were two legs, whereby there came to us a salvation that should abide, while they passed away. Which are therefore not unfitly compared to pillars of marble, because these his journeys are proved straight and strong, since in willing what he was able there was a just straightness, and in fulfilling what he willed an equal and sufficient strength. And because it is doubtful to none that [0442D] the whole heap of works is confounded if *256* that which seemeth well heaped up be not founded upon a solid base, *the pillars of marble founded upon bases of gold* are recorded, which being set beneath them, those pillars are not denied to be stable and commendable. For whatsoever God, made man, willed to do or to suffer, he is believed to have done or borne unconstrained; but he was seen to lean upon that twin charity which, more precious than gold, assigneth all things set upon it unto firmness. For he loved God, whose commandment he willed to obey, and he loved men, to whom he knew his obedience would be profitable; in the one of which he understood the first and greatest commandment to be held, and in the other he neglected not the second and like unto it. The Son loved [0443A] the Father, because the Father also loved the Son; he loved men, whom together with the Father he alike disposed to save; whose twofold love he held as certain causes lying beforehand, whereby he endured to be made man, and in man to do and to suffer so many things.

## CHAPTER XXV.

*His fairness is as of Libanus, chosen as the cedars.* Because the Virgin above commended the bridegroom as it were by parts, and from the head even to the legs set forth his praises one by one, now with a shortened speech she comprehendeth as it were the whole alike, and highly esteemeth his fairness by comparison with Libanus. Among the other mountains by which the land of the Jews is said to be occupied — whose level plain complaineth that it is straitened by the frequency of the mountains — as they say [0443B] who know it, Libanus shineth out higher, more fruitful in trees, and groweth white with continual snows. Which is well said to hold the type of the bridegroom, forasmuch as the excellence of this mountain is compared to none of the saints; who, made fruitful with the plantings of virtues and with the density of works, knoweth not to be burned with vices, being whitened by the native frost of the flesh. Whom assuredly God the Father chose, unto the salvation of us all, that through him every just man might be advanced from virtue to virtue, and that by his examples, manners and doctrine the workhouse of the Church might be built, as with beams that cannot rot. The cedar, of a nature that cannot rot, knowing no sudden failures, by its odour more largely scattered driveth off the assaults of serpents; which cedar Solomon with prudent counsel willed to employ, when he was zealous to set up [0443C] either a house for himself or the temple promised to the Lord. So also the Virgin's bridegroom is of a nature that cannot rot, knowing not to be hurt by guilt from the beginning of his begetting; whose odour of works, whose repute of virtues, whose praise of doctrine drive all hurtful things far from the thresholds of discipline. For which cause he is chosen that he may be placed in the building, that through him the house of God may be strengthened with a worthy strength, that no man may work anything at all worthy of praise which is not commended by his benevolence, as by the strong presence of cedars. For whatsoever is found to be raised up by the zeal of them that build cannot stand if it be not fortified with his cedarn strength; and the incautious workman, who measureth not himself with a worthy judgment, findeth whatsoever he doth without cedars to be liable to ruin. But he who chooseth the bridegroom for the safeguard of all his work findeth no ruin, [0443D] suffereth no loss, because his benevolence both giveth to them that build a welcome patience in their labours, and conferreth upon the building the far more welcome strength of cedars. Whereof also it is added: *His throat,* etc.

## CHAPTER XXVI.

*His throat is most sweet, and he is all desirable.* Because taste is contained in the throat, and holdeth the nearer place, wherein also it judgeth of all savoury things and of savour, by the name of the throat I reckon that taste is figured, by that manner of speech whereby the container is wont to be named for the thing contained. The taste therefore of the bridegroom is called not so much sweeter as most sweet, lest, if aught were said too little, somewhat should be reckoned wanting to him; because than that which proceedeth out of him, if it be tasted with a sound throat, no delightfulness is sweeter, no sweetness more delightful. For whether [0444A] a man attend distinctly to that which he is as God, or to that which he is as man, and stretch himself with a devout zeal to see what is proper to each, he seeth nothing subtler than this distinction, nothing more wondrous than this union, and tasteth nothing sweeter than a vision so subtle, so conspicuous. For he seeth that the Godhead, taking man, was in no wise consumed; nor did the manhood fail, but it profited, when it was wondrously taken up into God, where there is no confusion, no conversion of the one nature into the other, but a personal union, in the mystery of a virginal begetting. For the contemplating whereof, if a heavy rheum straiten not the eye, if the mind with open throat taste the delightfulness of this secret, what can be found sweeter than this taste, what happier than this throat, to which it is now granted to experience that which the angels desire? [0444B] But if the angels desire God, whom they contemplate unceasingly, and venerate him made man with an unfillable wonder, worthily do men also both desire and marvel at the same whole, and with their whole desire embrace him who is wholly desirable. For he is God, and there is none other beside him; he is man, clothed with the form of man for his own sake: worthy to be loved, not only because he is God, not only *257* because he is man, but because by a salutary mystery one and the same is alike God and man. He is man, I say, not such as any other man soever, but one through whom every man may be able to be made safe; that as a man was the fall or ruin of men, so, nay far more, this man may be the medicine of men. Who therefore would not desire such a one wholly, would not [0444C] wholly love him, would not wholly admire him? who would not rejoice to embrace him with his whole desire, as God and as neighbour? who would not seek such a one more zealously, would not hold him more eagerly if it fell to him to find him, and would not be zealous to instruct others with fitting admonitions to seek him? Therefore the prudent Virgin, who in love knoweth no rival, nor feareth a concubine, everywhere preacheth him whom she loveth, that he may be loved; and him whom she deserved to see such as he is, she willeth not to grudge to the rest, but rejoiceth with a solicitous affection to provide for them that stand by her. For which cause she thus concludeth: *Such is,* etc.

## CHAPTER XXVII.

*Such is my beloved, and he is my friend, O daughters of Jerusalem.* Such, I say, is he by whose tender love I am held fast, and (as I seem to see) I am not [0444D] deceitfully beguiled; nor hath an intemperate love, as sometimes it useth, defrauded me, because a transitory speech hath not yet worthily praised him. Although many things have been said whereby it is clear that he is proclaimed commendable, yet must this proclamation still be judged insufficient, because such as I rejoice to perceive him to be, I am not yet able utterly to pour out in transitory words. And to this end have I set about to follow out his praises, so manifold — although the things which ought to have been said are not yet fittingly enough expressed — that he whom I love may in some measure become known to you, and that your mind also may grow warm toward the love of him. For he cannot be loved if he have not been understood, nor doth he seem to be understood if he have not also been loved; for this very cause I desire that he be understood and be loved of all, whom [0445A] I have loved, and whom I have understood to become a friend also to them that truly love. You therefore, O daughters of Jerusalem, labour with all your strength to love him and to be loved by him, because nothing is holier than to love one so benevolent, so mighty, and nothing more blessed than to be loved by him who knoweth not how not to love him that loveth. *I,* saith he, *love them that love me [n: (Prov. VIII)]*. The praises of the Bridegroom being unfolded in so diverse a kind of figures, the Virgin is silent for a little while, and awaiteth [0446A] the answers of the daughters, considering whether perchance by this hearing they have received a fuller grace, whereby they may be more vehemently carried away into the love of him whom she preacheth. But before their answer be brought forth into the midst, I have thought it fitting that our pen be a little withdrawn from its toil; and that, where the Virgin was silent, the pen also choose to be silent, that this fifth book may at last rejoice to obtain its end.
