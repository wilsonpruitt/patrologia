## CHAPTER THE FIRST.

[0445A]

[0445B] *Whither is thy beloved gone, O thou most beautiful among women, whither is thy beloved turned aside, and we will seek him with thee.* The praise of the Bridegroom being heard, the daughters of Jerusalem are stirred up, and are provoked with a greater desire into the love of him; rejoicing that the Virgin hath spoken so many things beforehand concerning him, and hath followed out that quality of his after which she had inquired above. For above they had said: *What manner of one is the beloved of the beloved,* and now, rejoicing, they rejoice in him understood by mystical similitudes; but because he suffereth not himself as yet to be seen clearly even by them that understand and after a sort love him, they ask whither he is gone, that he may be sought by them more certainly. To whom, when the Virgin above [n: (l. IV, c. 10-12)] made haste to open the bolt of her door, hoping to find him nearer, whose voice knocking she had heard in the doors: *He,* saith she, *had turned aside and was gone by,* [0445C] and offered not himself to be seen of the Virgin — perchance that, the sight being withdrawn, he might render her more solicitous to seek. Since therefore the Virgin is found to have commended the Bridegroom in manifold wise, and it is certain that such a one, as hath been said already before, turned aside, the daughters of Jerusalem ask, and will be taught more diligently, to whom, as it were to a place, he that went away and turned aside willed to show himself. Such a one, say they, whose praises so manifold thou hast woven, unto whom thou hast so solicitously admonished us that thy love, nay thy languishing, should be told — how shall we either seek him, or, being sought, be able to find him, since we know not the place whereunto, when he escapeth us, he chooseth to come? Say therefore whether he hastened to the left, or rather to the right; say, if thou knowest, unto what desert, or unto whose, [0445D] he turned aside, that we, seeking, *258* be not carried away by a wandering error, and that we may follow after him, unto whom he came, by the imitation of desert. For when, thou teaching us, the place or the desert of the place shall become known, our mind shall the more burn to hasten thither; and we shall be carried away to seek the Bridegroom with a desire like unto thine, if not equal; of whose finding we shall not have the fruition unless thy benevolence accompany us. For we know that whosoever shall have sought him, thee being neglected, such an inquiry shall be proved utterly imperfect; nor willeth the Bridegroom to be found by us, or by the rest that seek, if he see not them that seek to be prevented by the fellowship of thy favour. With thee therefore, that is, thy patronage favouring us, we will seek him whom without thee, without thy benevolence, we are not able to find; by thy benefit our devotion shall follow after him: [0446A] being taught beforehand in what place, or in what desert of place, he tarrieth. [0446B] And in very truth none other shall be able to teach us better whither the Bridegroom is gone, or where he hath chosen to show himself present, than thou, who by the gift of grace hast entered into his sanctuary, and hast better known the secrets of spiritual understanding. To these things the Virgin answereth: *The beloved,* etc.

## CHAPTER II.

*[CAP. VI.] My beloved is gone down into his garden, to the bed of spice, that he may feed in the gardens, and gather lilies.* You desire to know whither he goeth or turneth aside whom I love, since he inclineth not himself as yet utterly unto your desire, since he setteth not before you, as it were hungering, a full refreshment; in what places meanwhile he layeth himself open more fully and more abundantly. He hath indeed deferred your hope, and hath not [0446C] cast away your desire, choosing to go down into a garden which by the gift of grace he hath made his own, that he may vouchsafe a prosperous sight unto the bed of spice, and, the lilies being found, may rejoice in the ripe reaping of them. Who, when he heareth not the apostles more fully according to their pleasure, and applaudeth not their prayers with a fulfilling effect, cometh down perchance unto the deacons as unto a pleasing garden, wherein above the rest he findeth Stephen worthy to be reaped. For he, as a bed diligently tilled, humble in the contest, bruised like spice with a manifold passion, both representeth lilies by the whiteness of an innocent soul, and, the sickle coming, showeth himself meet for the reaping. For attending what the Saviour in his pity set about for them that were to be saved, what breasts, what death at last [0446D] he endured, he glows, nay he burns, to imitate that which the other showed beforehand, accounting it too grievous if he be not the first to repay the debt that is due. Therefore in the enduring of martyrdom, fearing to be prevented by another, impatient of delay, he is zealous to be found the readier, esteeming that he hath somewhat the more glory in the martyrdom itself if, the time being shortened, he shall be able to obtain it first. Unto such a one and unto them that are like him the beloved loveth to turn aside, when he willeth to carry them over from the threshing-floor into the barn; who, withdrawing himself as it were from them whom he willeth to tarry longer in this life, hasteneth to be presented unto the untimely and unto them that shall depart hence more quickly. Of whom she addeth: *I to the beloved,* etc.

## CHAPTER III.

*I to my beloved, and my beloved to me, who feedeth among the lilies.* I know, saith she, that my beloved [0447A] layeth himself open as it were more nearly and more manifestly unto those whom, white as lilies, he now disposeth to gather unto the harvest; whom, threshed on the floor and stripped of the ragged covering of the chaff, he willeth to carry over into the barns and the full rest of souls. Therefore do I strive with all my strength to please him, to show myself a garden, myself a bed, myself a shining lily, that when he shall have come and found me flowering with the tillage that is due, taking me out of the midst, he may make me cleave unto him for ever. I, I say, prepare for my beloved a welcome pasture, that he may feed and suffer no hurt of bitterness; for him, fed and refreshed, I prepare a mansion in myself, that, being held by the mansion, he may thenceforth make no mention of flight. And the beloved, as I perceive, prepareth himself for me, that he may love and gather me, and draw me unto himself: [0447B] being about to give nothing better unto her that is gathered and drawn than himself, that I may sleep and rest in the selfsame, my cares being driven far away. He, I say, lifteth me up, though yet set upon the earth, into the love of heavenly things, and, lest I cleave long unto the earth, whetteth as it were the reaping-hook; he rejoiceth to be fed with my lilies, not only while they are yet fixed by the root in the earth, but if, already reaped in ripe season, they be set upon the table. I therefore am zealous to prepare myself for him unto this, and I will purchase by deserts the joy that I hope for; he rejoiceth to render me the like, nay to add over and above, whose benevolence delighteth in conferring benefits. *259*
