## CHAPTER IV.

*Thou art fair, my friend, sweet and beautiful as Jerusalem, terrible as an army set in array.* The Bridegroom, attending [0447C] that the Virgin is ready in his praises, that she willeth to be dissolved more quickly and rather to be always with him, although he hasteneth not yet to fulfil her desire wholly, yet inclineth himself with a benevolent affection to console her. Thou, saith he, who burnest with so great a desire to see me, and art fed with my praise, while thou art not yet able to obtain that which thou hopest: by this affection or zeal thou formest for thyself a wondrous fairness, and conformest thyself by a fair likeness unto him whom thou lovest. Being therefore like unto the fair one, thou canst fitly enough be called fair, who by the zeal of likening rejoicest and art busy so to be devoted, that, solicitously spitting out all the dregs of the enemy, thou mayest as a friend rejoice in those things which are wholesome, which are delightful, of thy friend. In which affection and profiting thou knowest not to envy the rest, [0447D] but by form and by doctrine thou lovest to advance all unto better things: rival of none, detracting from none, grievous to none, but solicitous for all, drawing all, and sweet unto all. For nothing is sweeter than, in loving God, so to embrace one's neighbour that he who loveth rightly should choose not to be turned aside from either alike; so to love God and to embrace him with the tenderer arms, that he in no wise envy, nay that he even rejoice that he is loved by any neighbours whatsoever. But the Virgin, rightly loving the Bridegroom, knoweth not to envy — than which not even the Sicilian tyrants, according to a certain heathen, found a greater torment: she feareth no rival, she is not racked by the suspicion of a concubine, nay she gathereth together all those women whom she beholdeth to love him whom she loveth. She flourisheth assuredly with a spirit so sweet, [0448A] so peaceable, that she rejoiceth that the Bridegroom is loved of all, and grieveth when he is not loved; nor doth she judge her spirit to be burdened in any other way whatsoever than when he whom she so greatly desireth to be loved is not loved. And so, seeing that he is loved by the daughters of Jerusalem, she, knowing nothing of spite, rather chooseth to rejoice, and with a glad spirit is zealous to work whatsoever she is able, that they which behold may rejoice to imitate the beauty of her works. She is sweet, when she willeth that the daughters of Jerusalem should please together with the Bridegroom; beautiful, when she is zealous to show forth in words and in works the form of pleasing; sweet indeed, when she willeth that the same glory which is hers should be present unto them, save perchance that she willeth herself to be the only mother of an only Son. Although she envieth not unto them the dignity of mothers, knowing that whoso shall have worthily done the will of the Bridegroom, or of the Son, he shall deserve to be called his brother, [0448B] and sister, and mother, even as the same is found to testify in the Gospel [n: (Matth. XII)]. But because she among mothers hath deserved to obtain an excellent primacy, who by a singular grace begat God incarnate of herself: although unto others no small loftiness of deserts is granted, hers above the rest is the beauty, the sweetness, the fairness that is commended. She is fair, while she burneth toward him who *in form is beautiful above the sons of men [n: (Psal. XLIV)]*, in whose manners sweetness, in whose works beauty shineth again; and, to be compared to none of men, she is likened unto that Jerusalem which, set in the heavens, contemplateth God unceasingly. For that holy multitude of angels, consonant with itself, which an indissoluble strength fortifieth after the manner of a city, is conspicuous with so great beauty that in it nothing seemeth worthy to be reproved, [0448C] burning with a wondrous zeal that man also may deserve to come unto this very thing. Whereunto, because the Virgin is believed to have come by the grace of the Bridegroom, and, set in the flesh, to have found a desert above the flesh, she is rightly proclaimed comely, like Jerusalem, whose beauty the enemy beholding marvelleth with terror. For seeing her to be fortified with so many and so great virtues, and that, as in an army of a camp set in array, no entrance is anywhere found, he is astonished that the firmness of angels is strong in a human being, and that in one Virgin there is the order of an army, the force of a camp. For in the angels so great an order, so great a firmness of array is strong, that the enemy leapeth back at the meeting of the first face, and, shaken with a mighty terror, presumeth to pursue nothing against them, to dare nothing, whom, relying upon a singular order, he seeth to prevail by a strong prudence. [0448D] Whom when Jacob had once seen, he said: *These are the camps of God [n: (Gen. XXXII)]*. And Eliseus to his servant: *Fear not,* saith he, *for there are more with us than with them [n: (IV Reg. VI)]*. Eliseus feareth not, and the assault of the enemy shaketh him not, whom the array of angels set in order beateth far back, whose stronger order admitteth not the onsets of the enemies that run against it, and permitteth not the threatening sword to enter the compass of the camps. With a like terror the enemy fleeth back from the Virgin when he hath assailed her, marvelling in her both at the order of virtues and at an ordered fortitude: and considering that a human being holdeth the likeness of the heavenly camps, he faileth at the first encounter, and, leaping back further off, sufficeth not to shudder. And rightly [0449A] is the holy Virgin compared both to camps and to angels, forasmuch as in both the excellence of chastity is commended; because the flesh, or the coupling of the flesh, never hurteth the angels, and chastity gave its name unto the *castra*, the camps that are removed from the household embrace. As an army of camps, therefore, is the Virgin found terrible, who, begetting God in the flesh, experienceth not the coupling of the flesh; whom when the enemy heareth proclaimed chaste in conceiving, he is terrified and fleeth back, nor perceiveth he such a mystery with his understanding. *260*

## CHAPTER V.

*Turn away thy eyes from me, for they have made me fly away.* Although by my judgment thou art proclaimed fair, sweet and beautiful, yet I will that thou search not out things higher than thyself and unsearchable, nor think, while thou livest here, to obtain more fully those things which I will to show unto no mortals [0449B] save after this life. Those eyes therefore wherewith thou hangest thyself toward things invisible, call them back as it were, restrain them as it were, while thou now perceivest that thou canst not reach thither; and while thou stretchest thyself with hope and desire unto the things that are before, know that thou dost not yet fully or effectually apprehend them. Thus assuredly shalt thou seem to remove thine eyes from me, when thou shalt be constrained humbly to turn them back upon thyself; and when, living still mortally, thou shalt turn thee unto thine own ineffectualness, thou shalt turn thyself away, as it were from my excellence, suffering not a repulse but a delay. In fine, by Moses, a most truthful seer, I spake long time above, that no man living hath attained unto the sight of me, but that whosoever is mortally wrapped about with the girdle of the flesh is darkened and groweth dim, until he shall have been loosed. *Not me,* I say, *shall [0449C] man see, and live [n: (Exod. XXXIII)]*. So long as man is bound by the stains of the present life, he is darkened as it were by a certain cloud, though not damnably, yet penally; nor shall he be able to behold my Godhead as it is, if he cease not to live and to be confined by the fellowship of the flesh. The more attentively therefore thou fixest thine eyes upon the light inaccessible, the more dost thou perceive it to be ungranted unto thee; and when thou strivest to see, and yet art constrained to call back thine eyes, those same eyes being beaten back, I seem unto thee to fly away. For because they stretch themselves with all their efforts to see, and yet do not as yet fully apprehend that which they aim at, I fly away from them, while they find themselves dim and weak, or even when, being clear-sighted, they find me to be incomprehensible. That which I solicitously say unto thee concerning the turning away [0449D] of these is not a repulse, not a command, but I foretell it, that thou mayest know that the sight of me can be given more fully unto no man, unless he first rejoice to be stripped of the burden of the flesh.

## CHAPTER VI.

*Thy hair is as a flock of goats, which have appeared from Galaad. Thy teeth as a flock of sheep, which came up from the washing. All with twins, and there is none barren among them. As the bark of a pomegranate, so are thy cheeks, beside thy hidden things.* Lest it should as it were trouble the Virgin that she is not yet able to behold the face of the Bridegroom, that he swiftly flieth away when he is truly believed to be already held, the Bridegroom hasteneth to soothe her with repeated praises, that she may bear the delay more patiently, her ears being soothed. Which praises, because they are set down above [n: (l. IV, c. 8)] in wellnigh the same words, [0450A] and, when we came thither, were expounded as God gave; I forbear at present to repeat the exposition, lest I should seem to apply delay unto the reading and weariness unto the reader. Yet when the Bridegroom repeateth those praises, when he bringeth them so often into the midst, he doth not act idly, nor speaketh he negligently; but it is proper to a lover (if indeed he burn vehemently) to make mention of that which he loveth, nay and to repeat it diligently.
