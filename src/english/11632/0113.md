## CHAPTER XI.

*I went down into the garden of nuts, to see the fruits of the valleys, and to look if the vineyard had flourished, and the pomegranates had budded.* Whence this cometh to the Virgin, that she should be likened, as hath been said before, unto the moon and the sun, that, set before the rest, she should be adorned with so great an excellence of virtues, is not hidden, since the Bridegroom confesseth that he goeth down unto her, [0456B] showing that out of his own presence this is mercifully given unto her. In fine, he showed himself to confer a benefit upon the children of Israel in Egypt, when at last he came down from the highest throne of heaven unto them that were oppressed with clay and brick, when he visited with the regard of his mercy them that were afflicted with misery, and, having visited them, delivered them from a hard servitude with a most mighty hand: *Seeing I have seen,* saith he, *the affliction of my people, and I am come down to deliver them [n: (Exod. III)]*. [var: Vulg. Exod. 3:7–8 gives *Vidi* and *descendi ut liberarem eum*, "I have seen… I am come down that I might deliver him"; the doubled verb and the plural object printed here are the wording of Acts 7:34, under a note naming Exodus] In the Gospel also he is reported to come down not in vain [n: (Luc. VI)], but that he might console the multitudes set in a plain place; whom, when he understood them to be [sic: *invados*] and poor in spiritual things, and yet by the counsel that he knew carried them not up unto the mountains, he despised not to visit them where they stood in the plain, and, out of the fulness of his treasure of blessings, [0456C] neglected not to bring forth gifts. But if his going down into Egypt, or into the plain, is found to have been fruitful, much more rightly shall his going down unto the Bride not be esteemed idle — who is like neither unto darksome Egypt nor unto a turbulent plain, but unto a garden of delight rich in fruits and in nuts. The garden of nuts is the Virgin, into which the Bridegroom saith that he goeth down, forasmuch as no difficulty in any wise, no importunity gainsayeth him as he goeth down; unto whom, although the heaven of heaven and the habitation of angels be pleasing, yet neither the nuts of the garden, nor the flowers of the vineyard, nor the buds of the pomegranates displease. In a nut the outer rind is bitter, and that not in a middling degree; beneath the rind a shell hard yet brittle lieth hid; but the kernel shut up beneath the shell groweth the more sweetly pleasant unto him that eateth. And so the Virgin is a garden of nuts, [0456D] when she is troubled with the bitterness of sufferings and of temptations, when the malice of them that persecute her wickedly withstandeth her repose, when whatsoever this world either flattereth or thundereth groweth utterly bitter unto her, when all that shineth out unto them that savour ill is filthy in her sight. She is a garden, when, as a harder shell, she is fortified with a wondrous strength, when she is found unfailing in watchings, in fastings, in prayers, when she is in no wise softened by the reproaches of the Jews, by the swords of the Gentiles, when in every strait, with an unwearied rigour, she is found to stand strongly. Who, although she be found to remain fixed and stable unto all things, yet desireth to be broken and dissolved, and to appear more manifestly unto Christ, knowing that he is delighted not with the shell, not with the rind, [0457A] not with any bitterness whatsoever, but that, the kernel being offered, the sweeter it is the more festively he feasteth thereon. But the hidden kernel is the inward sweetness of the loving soul, which lieth hid from the eyes of the persecutor that are seduced by malice, which, the shell being broken, is not taken away, but is borne as it were in the hands of her that offereth it, and is presented unto the Bridegroom, and unto him that presideth at the same table or banquet. Into this garden of nuts the loving Bridegroom then goeth down, when he stretcheth himself near and propitious unto his beloved that laboureth, when he is solicitous to advance her from virtue unto virtue [n: (Psal. LXXXIII)], and willeth with a gracious regard to see the fruits of the valleys in her. For what humble virtues busy themselves to work in the Virgin, what out of their own labour or desert they love to await with a sure faith, the Bridegroom seeth, and approveth, *265* and maketh all things to prosper, and with a prosperous favour, [0457B] as with a west wind blowing, to ripen. For the fruits of the valleys are the labours of humble virtues, they are solicitous endeavours, hands the readier unto works: they are fruitful acts, the more welcome in a well-planted garden, which, although they be not yet fully ripe for reaping or for storing, yet please the sight, and shall pass the sooner unto the eating. But the fruits of the valleys being seen, the bridegroom loveth nevertheless to see through whether the vineyard, worthily flowering, hasteneth to answer the husbandman; whether the ordered series of virtues is found to shine again with a fragrant beauty; whether with a flowery odour to forbid all hurtful venom from her borders. For lest a venomous spite should hurt the tender Virgin, a seemly fame fortified her, a flowery beauty exalted her; who, when with a new kind she brought forth unto us the swelling of the womb, yet far off brought forth the stain of unlawful suspicion and the [0457C] serpent's venom. Whose conscience not only did the presence of faults not confound, but neither did a sinister rumour suffuse her face with a blush; with so great a flower did the vineyard, with so great an odour did the flower flourish, that her integrity lacketh both fault and blush. In a garden of this kind the bridegroom seeth also that pomegranates are not lacking, but that in the hidden place of the will the mind is ready unto patience: that she willeth to endure not only death but even the threatening sword, and that, if not yet in effect, yet already in affection, the shedding of blood is ruddy beneath. The pomegranates indeed do not yet present themselves more manifestly, but unto him that beholdeth more diligently they show themselves as it were in the bud: the passion is not yet red, because the executioner draweth not the sword, but the will, more firmly rooted, already putteth forth a bud upon [0457D] the shoot. To look upon this the bridegroom sent no messenger before him, but with a lowly benevolence he himself comforteth the Virgin and visiteth her again: with a gracious descent he cherisheth the garden, he rejoiceth to ripen the fruits of the valleys, he giveth the vineyard to flower, and the pomegranates to bud.

## CHAPTER XII.

*I knew not: my soul was troubled because of the chariots of Aminadab.* [var: Vulg. *anima mea conturbavit me*, "my soul troubled me" — the active, with the soul as the agent of the trouble; Migne prints the passive, which Philip glosses both ways at 0458A–B] The Virgin, seeing that the bridegroom hath wondrously come down into her garden, and by a wondrous descent hath brought a grace equally wondrous, cannot sufficiently utter that which she spiritually beholdeth in herself; and, rejoicing in her present knowledge, she confesseth her former ignorance. *I knew not,* saith she, before the Angel announced me full [0458A] of grace, and the Holy Spirit overshadowed me with a cloud bright enough, before the Son of God made me fruitful with his kiss, and a new fruitfulness honoured a virginal conscience: I knew not that so much of grace, so much of gift, was imparted unto me, that a lowly handmaid could experience things so unexperienced by the rest. Before he enclosed his garden — yet not with a hedge, not with a wall, nor with a rampart — before he sealed the fountain — yet not with an iron bar, not with a bolt, not with metal — before he came down into the same garden, and looked upon it with the large gift of his goodness, I knew not that he was so mighty, so kindly, so rich in so great bounty. When therefore he came down unto me, and showed himself unto me after a sort, I was troubled, I was afraid, I was astonished, marvelling at so great a benefit: my soul laboured toward its salvation [0458B] with a prosperous fainting, and, fainting with a healthful labour — or rather profiting — it troubled me. For [sic: *Videos*] that the bridegroom had come down not poor, not needy, not as any one of the people, but had arrived upon horses or in chariots after the manner of the mighty, I had no more spirit in me, considering that he both worketh salvation mightily, and that in that very might religion is worthily preserved: *Thou that ridest,* saith he, *upon thy horses, and thy chariots are salvation [n: (Habac. III)]*. You know that the men of Azotus, who held the ark of the Lord some days after they had taken it, being constrained by the wounding of their hinder parts, at last restored it free; which, set upon a new cart, was carried back into the house of Aminadab, where both a more reverent worship and a more fitting religion was rendered unto it. Aminadab is interpreted [0458C] *spontaneous*, signifying him who of his own accord willed to come from the bosom of the Father, and to unite unto himself in the womb of the Virgin the nature of man; who presideth over the angelic powers after the manner of a king or of a charioteer, and with an agile concord all serve him as chariots. Which kind of empire or of obedience when the Virgin more diligently beholdeth, she confesseth that she, aforetime unknowing of them, is after a manner troubled at this knowledge: recalling that at the marriages of them of old festal assemblies of men were wont to be employed, and that angelic chariots and invisible timbrel-players were not seen. Who therefore perchance hold in this epithalamium the name or the appearance of chariots, because this solemnity of the nuptials runneth together with and succoureth a fourfold world: and those four living creatures which are described in the prophecy of John or of Ezechiel [0458D] are subscribed unto the same business as heralds or as yoke-fellows. Because of the chariots of Aminadab therefore the Virgin, before unknowing, is troubled, when, being brought into his sanctuary, she marvelleth at the thousands of thousands that minister unto him, when, as one delivered from the borders of the enemies or from the men of Azotus, both the mighty excellence of a king and the religion of a priest receiveth her.
