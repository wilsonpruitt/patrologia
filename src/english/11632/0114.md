## CHAPTER XIII.

*Return, return, Sunamite: return, return, that we may behold thee.* [var: Vulg. *Sulamitis*, the Sulamitess; the name Migne prints is the one whose etymology — captive, despised, scarlet — the whole chapter expounds] Lest the Virgin should seem to return into the house of Aminadab by her own merits (for she herself is the ark of God, which for some time is held captive by the law of us all), Aminadab sheweth that it is not the efficacy [0459A] of her that returneth, but rather the compassion and the mighty benevolence of him that recalleth her. The bridegroom therefore recalleth her who is called Sunamite, that being loosed from her bonds she may return with unhindered steps, lest she tarry among the men of Azotus and the allurements of carnal senses, but be carried back of her own accord, upon the wain of a foursquare newness, into the house of him that loveth her. Sunamite is interpreted *a captive* or *despised,* or *scarlet*. But the Virgin, like the rest, was by nature a daughter of wrath, from whose stains her manner of life could not wholly free her; wherein, although there was present in her a virtue expulsive of damnable faults, yet she is still weighed down by the bond, and by the laws of nature is a captive. And since she was by nature conceived in sins, her manner of life was infected with faults, even light ones; by the desert of that infection she is [0459B] despised in the purer eyes of God, but her humility was at length regarded through grace. For he regarded the humility of his handmaid, and recalled her once and again, and by a twofold recalling delivered her both from captivity and from contempt; that her whom the law of wrath, the law inborn, had held guilty, the divine law might afterward keep wholly free from fault. Therefore the bridegroom also addeth to recall the same once and again, and with a repeated word more diligently to invite her to return; and her whom the first recalling led forth from the harder sentence, he maketh firm the second time by a more bountiful estate or grace. For having first said, *Return, return, Sunamite,* he added, *return, return that we may behold thee.* Whom I would have return, saith he, that she be no longer held a captive and despised; I would have her return, that she may be held more pleasing to me also, that she may love him that recalleth her, and may burn, [0459C] not with a burning, but with love, and be worthy to be named scarlet by desert, not by colour. Scarlet holdeth the appearance of fire, signifying love; for he that loveth burneth. But because that fire, or the love of the Virgin, is of necessity doubled, while in the bridegroom, as I recall was said already, she embraceth God and man: her whom the bridegroom enricheth beyond the rest with so great a gift, that the scarlet may be twice dyed, he twice inviteth to this twofold love. Her therefore whom the first doubling recalleth captive and despised, the second far more firmly maketh loving and beloved; and both he himself and the companions whom he willeth to be present and privy to this mystery love to see and to behold her, such and so scarlet. Whom the angels, marking her both free from fault against the wonted [0459D] right of nature, and scarlet with love, above the efficacy of any visible creature, are not able enough to wonder at so great an excellency of the thing; and rejoicing with the Bridegroom, they love to enquire concerning the Bride.

## CHAPTER XIV.

*[CAP. VII.] What wilt thou see, say they, in the Sunamite, but the choirs of camps?* Thou invitest, say they, the Virgin, and so often and so often wilt thou have her return, and with a marvellous advancement [0460A] wilt have her wholly turned to thy good pleasure, that fault may avail to obtain nothing in her of its own right, and that by the privilege of love she may be worthy to please us that behold her. In this Sunamite therefore, who so often returneth, what wilt thou see, and of her, when she shall have been presented to thy sight, what wilt thou judge — in whom, as we consider, there meeteth us so great a perfection of manners that not men, but rather the camps of angels, accord with her? For the divine reading is found to name the angels camps, in that the violence of enemies cannot pierce their squadrons, and the presiding emperor giveth them so great confidence that every assailant, beaten far back, taketh flight. Of whom Jacob: *These are the camps of God [n: (Gen. XXXII)]*. Who also may not unfitly be called choirs, in that they are both concordant in affections and tuneful with unwearied [0460B] praises, whose melody soundeth so great in the ears of him that presideth that neither the harmony of the heavens, nor of the stars, nor even of the planets can compare itself thereto. And by what virtues the angels consider themselves to flourish, by those same they both see the Virgin to be pre-eminent and envy it not; and since they recognize in her the angelic life with a marvellous newness, they wonder, and now demand of the bridegroom whether he know aught further. What wilt thou see, say they, in this Sunamite whom thou recallest, but a marvellous concord of praise and of exquisite fortitude, but praiseworthy choirs and the ancient armies of the angels, but concordant virtues and a strength unfailing as of camps! These things assuredly we already see in the virgin whom thou lovest so greatly, and if aught yet lieth hid, we would see it with a desire pleasing enough; [0460C] and both these things and the rest which we rejoice to behold in her, we would have recounted by thyself, who art known to know them better. To these the Bridegroom: *How fair,* etc. *267*

## CHAPTER XV

*How beautiful are thy steps in shoes, prince's daughter.* The Bridegroom deferreth not to answer benignly to the things enquired, but in answering he loveth to turn back his speech to the virgin; not deeming that he in any wise displeaseth the aforesaid companions, if to them that enquire he chooseth to shew himself affable to her. To them therefore that ask he cutteth not off hope by silence, but turneth himself to her to whom he is not weary to speak the more often; and when he undertaketh to accost her with new praises, he willeth the bridesmen standing by to hear whatsoever he speaketh. But now [0460D] he beginneth to set forth his praises from the steps, who above [n: (l. IV, c. 7 et 22)], when he praised her, chose to begin from the eyes; perchance that, if the repetition of praises admonish the drowsy to weary, another order and a varied colouring may render them watchful and attentive. Finally above [n: (l. IV, c. 23)], when he many times sought to praise the virgin as it were part by part, if I recall well, he passed not beyond her breasts or paps, but beginning from the upper parts, his praise so turneth about them [0461A] that from the breasts downward he speaketh not of the remaining members. But the Virgin herself in the preceding book [n: (l. V a c. 18 ad 27)], praising him as it were particularly or member by member, descended in order from the head to the legs and their bases; to whom the Bridegroom, rendering the like, ordered herein the series of her praises, but for variety's sake changed that very order. The steps of the virgin I deem to name her acts, which he willed to be hidden from us that are far more remote; through which he willed her to come into our knowledge, whose sanctity would lie hid from us if she chose a slothful or a continual sitting. Which steps assuredly no halting limp disfigured, no girlish levity accused of being wandering and unstable; no dry disease twisted them back to the weak and needy elements of this world, nor did any wicked [0461B] exactor wring from them anything by way of tribute. Of so fair a soundness, of so strong a beauty were they, and in this beauty they stood fast with so great a constancy, that wonder rather than narration befitteth the praiser, since not only the full expression, but even a foretaste, may suffice the hearer. And that it may be plain that nothing necessary is excluded from those same steps, walking through the unevennesses of this life they are not bare; but lest either the dryness of the sun or the harshness of winter bring in aught of grievousness, a certain mortification of pleasure shoeth them as it were. If therefore aught threateneth, whether delightful or troublesome, which would overturn in them anything useful or honest, a certain patient insensibility putteth itself forward to repel it, [0461C] which permitteth nothing pestilent to come to the tender foot. In shoes of this kind the beauty of the steps lieth hid, which, not open enough to us, is better known to the bridegroom, who loveth to be at hand for that same virgin in all things, and maketh her to be like himself as a daughter. For this cause perchance in the present place — that nowhere above did he name her daughter, that after his own fashion he shod her more especially, and made her in the shoes to accord with him more expressly and more like, while, set in the flesh, he granted her not to feel the laws of carnal men. Who is rightly said to hold the princedom of the prerogative of that gift, who, when he was made flesh, would not be bound to carnal things, who willed to keep the manhood he assumed unharmed by vices, and to conform the chosen virgin to himself as a daughter. Taking away therefore from the midst the privilege [0461D] of stubborn wrath, he is rightly called by Isaias' testimony the prince of peace [n: (Isa. IX)], whose peace whosoever is granted truly to experience in himself (for many say peace, peace, in whom peace cannot be found); whosoever, I say, is found partaker of this peace by the gift of grace, is rightly marked with the filial name because of the likeness.
