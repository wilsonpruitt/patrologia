## CHAPTER XVI.

*The joint of thy thighs is like necklaces, which are wrought by the hand of a craftsman.* With a fair manner of speech he hath commended the integrity of the virgin, while he compared the joint of the thighs to wrought necklaces, because in the thighs is most often figured carnal begetting, [0462A] but in the joint of the thighs the denial of begetting and barrenness. Finally the sinful soul which embittereth God with carnal allurements, as the prophet [n: (Isa. LI)] recordeth, spreadeth her feet apart to all that pass by, and not content with the lawful, being loosed from law taketh other men — nay, adulterers; she beareth not the straitness of the marriage-bed, but, her couch widened, is prostituted to panderings. But the Virgin, who in conceiving feeleth no man at all, who by an ancient privilege of nature consenteth not in body any more than in mind, her thighs being coupled with an indissoluble joint, holdeth from thenceforth and thereafter the firm covenant of perfect chastity. For such as she is read to have been at the conception, the Angel preaching it [n: (Luc. I)], such by a pious faith not straying from the truth is she believed to have continued; and neither was the body divided from the mind, nor afterward [0462B] the mind from the body, but undivided by a continual consent and by unharmed affections. Which joint ought rightly to be compared to necklaces, which are known to be wrought by a learned hand out of gold and stones, which not only by their own worthy *268* price bring the comeliness of an ornament, but to fortify the virgin bosom clasp together the upper edges of the garment. So the joint of the thighs, more precious than gold or stone, keeping flesh and spirit, and busy for the acquiring of merit, fortifieth indeed the virgin, that no seducing corrupter be admitted, and admonisheth her also to love him by whose benefit she is thus adorned. For from fortifying or from admonishing, as some say, are necklaces named, in that whatsoever things are fortified are by such a gift admonished, that by such a guard they may drive far off [0462C] the envious corrupter, and with a mutual affection benignly love the giver. For the learned craftsman with his own hand wrought these necklaces, which, denied to many, he denied not to the Virgin Mother; which Paul with a wondrous desire asketh to be given him — I know not, while he lived here, whether perchance he deserved so to be blessed.

## CHAPTER XVII.

*Thy navel is a turned bowl, never wanting cups.* The navel coupleth and fasteneth together the parts of the belly set round about it, and lest by their native softness they flow down or perchance flow away, calleth them back and restraineth them; wherein the woman also is said to have the seed-bed of begetting, which nevertheless among men is chiefly contained in the loins. The navel therefore of the virgin is her stable doctrine, which, when the apostles were flowing away with a fleshly fear [0462D] and a womanish softness; standeth firm by a [sic: *virita*] faith, and floweth not away with the like levity, but by a certain firmness of her own calleth back those that are light and wandering more than is just. For the men, seeing that they had too lightly fallen away from the purpose of faith, and that she, though shaken with exceeding grief, had yet stood the more firmly — when the resurrection accomplished was shewn by sundry visions, and the Virgin was held suspended in the zeal of contemplation and in devout prayers — being called back to her, humbly blush for the things past; and thenceforth cleave to her hearing more believingly and more constantly. For than a turned bowl her turning is fuller and more perfect, to whom the begetting of the rising Christ had been more fully made known; and all things which are read of him in Scripture through the long series of times, [0463A] these she drew more abundantly, and poured back to her hearers with measure. Whose turned bowl, or ready perfection of discoursing, drieth not with want, is not straitened by narrowness of understanding, but filled with cups and inebriated with the wine of wisdom, both sufficeth for all peoples far off and, being poured out, never faileth.

## CHAPTER XVIII.

*Thy belly is like a heap of wheat, set about with lilies.* The belly is humanity, weakness, or temporal manner of life, which needeth the fostering of raiment and of bodily nourishment, whose softness, as it were, so demandeth the care of natural fostering that in it there may be heaped up spiritual advancement and eminence. For although the Virgin hath not her own flesh in hatred, but preserveth it, yet in the care of it she enervateth not the powers of the spirit, but while she of necessity cherisheth [0463B] it, she reserveth herself for hard labours, in which she heapeth up a store of merits after the manner of wheat. Which store, while it lifteth itself on high as by a certain point, yet raiseth not itself with the windy multitude of chaff; and is held gracious to the husbandman for the making of bread, and no less fruitful for the casting of seed. For not only is the Virgin's manner of life proved to please the Bridegroom, but in these also that stand by it worketh as it were a seminal force, while it instructeth them and provoketh them to all better things, and admonisheth them, not by word but by example, to grow and to stretch forth unto the things that are before. Whose merits, so great, lest robbers presume to carry them off violently, lest thieves plunder them, lest even mice seek fraudulently to drag them away, are set about indeed not with stakes, [0463C] not with stones, not with mortar, but with lilies, that is, with the allurement of cleanness and of inviolable grace.

## CHAPTER XIX.

*Thy two breasts are like two twin fawns of a roe: thy neck like a tower of ivory.* The force of love maketh these same words said above to be repeated, but they are not to be expounded again, for the sake of a briefer delay; yet that which can be reckoned changed and as it were different may be enquired into — what it meaneth, what ought therein to be figured. For above it was said [n: (l. IV, c. 12)]: *Thy neck is like the tower of David;* but here, *like a tower of ivory.* In the tower is figured a certain fortitude of the virgin, in the ivory the chaste and sincere beauty of that same fortitude; because her whom the tower setteth against the enemies fore-armed with virtues, the coldness inborn [0463D] in ivory sheweth to be polished from fiery passions. A tower therefore is the Virgin, when she bravely *269* defendeth herself from enemies, but a tower of ivory when she sheweth herself free from the heat of the flesh; a tower, I say, when the onset of the malignant shuddereth at the mass of her virtues, of ivory when with a cold rigour she whiteneth as with the frost of winter.

## CHAPTER XX.

*Thine eyes are like the fishpools in Hesebon, which are in the gate of the daughter of the multitude.* Of the eyes also I deem enough was said in the second book [n: (sup. l. IV, c. 7, et alibi)], nor do I think it necessary that what was said be repeated but because a new comparison seemeth here to be added, that very newness demandeth to be here expounded. Hesebon is a city which a multitude of peoples inhabiteth [0464A] the fishpools are fountains, or lakes and a fitting fulness of waters, which, set in the gate of the city — the truth of the matter being so — refresh the dwellers of that city with a manifold bounty. The eyes therefore of the Bride, not infected with serpentine dregs, but, as the second book explaineth, conformed to the manners of doves, are for this cause perchance in the present place like unto fishpools, because they steep the washed, or wash the steeped, with saving disciplines. For those whom the Virgin vouchsafeth to behold with gracious eyes, on whom she worthily chooseth to have mercy by a regard of benevolence, calling them back from wandering in the open field, she leadeth us to a profitable gate, through which she bringeth us in to the citizens and the household and the throng of them that worthily deserve. Hesebon is interpreted *the girdle of sorrow.* By her regard indeed the Virgin restraineth us from an outward licence, and when we have entered the straits of the gate a necessary sorrow [0464B] girdeth us; and she no longer suffereth us to be glad from all the things that clamour without, but willeth us to be afflicted with misery and worthy of mercy. And when the Virgin in pity beholdeth us such, she guardeth us as citizens and household of the daughter of the multitude; which daughter, that is, the city, admitteth indeed many comers unto herself, but, by a law strange enough, admitteth none save the mournful and the wretched. This refuge they have, this guard they use for the present, that the Virgin cherisheth them with the fitting benefit of her eyes, [sic: *qnod*] she visiteth the mourning with a diligent solicitude, so kindly refreshing them for the present that she promiseth far better in what followeth. I have experienced what I say. When, by the divine judgment urging it, the sons of my mother fought against me, when, while strangers spared me, they whom I believed to be mine own bowels wounded me, [0464C] the ash of this Hesebon, mine own sadness, would have compelled me to perish, had not the Virgin vouchsafed with outstretched hand the sooner to succour me. She stood by me, and cast not away the care of me any further, but interposed herself in the midst of the tongues of the malignant; and marking that they raged not with zeal of justice but with a hostile faction, with the strong hand of mercy she stopped the mouth of them that spoke things so unjust.
