## CHAPTER XXI.

*Thy nose is like the tower of Libanus, which looketh toward Damascus.* Pestilent men, who are the champions and athletes of the devil, like unto him by whom they are sent, stir up quarrels without discretion; and blinded by malice they know not the reason of judgment, and if perchance they have known it, rancour [0464D] and envy corrupt their knowledge. But the Virgin, seeing that some have need of a greater patronage, knoweth not how to fail those whom she hath known to suffer wrong, shewing to them that rightly consider that she hath a strong nose, not flattened after the manner of an ape, not unnerved with softness, not shorn away. For although as it were in silence she sometimes permitteth her own to be afflicted, perchance so far as it is expedient that they be amended by tribulations, yet when the malignity of the envious groweth up more violently and assaileth, the discretion of this strong one, as the tower of Libanus, standeth forth and fighteth back. And so much the more doth she prevail and more effectually withstand the malignant, as she is solidly confirmed in Christ, who is the mount of Libanus, upon whom set and built up she looketh and gazeth toward Damascus, that with a solicitous gaze she may call it back and turn it from its purpose. Damascus is interpreted *thirsting for blood.* [0465A] But the city of the lost ever thirsteth for our blood; yet lest it drink it all, the Virgin forbiddeth by the regard of her eyes, which both confer a saving defence upon us that piously ask, and mightily bring loss upon her that evilly thirsteth. For her discretion knoweth when and how she may console those that suffer unjust things, and with what penalty she may strike back, or with what patience await, them that inflict them; she knoweth whom, humble and abject, she may regard with a pitying countenance, and whom on the contrary, Damascenes, she may despise, their malice so deserving. In all which things she attendeth not to her own good pleasure alone, but greatly esteemeth the will of the Bridegroom, whom she desireth to please; loving whose friends, she groweth glad at their advancement, and over whose enemies, as with a perfect hatred, she pineth away. To whom is well subjoined: *Thy head,* etc. *270*

## CHAPTER XXII.

[0465B]

*Thy head is as Carmel, and the hairs of thy head as the purple of the king joined to channels.* [var: Vulg. *vincta canalibus*, "bound in channels"; Philip's own gloss keeps the Vulgate's word, saying that the purple is *alligari* — bound — in the channels] The head of the Virgin may not unfitly be called her mind or intention, to which the remaining works, as subject members, seem so to be assigned that they deserve neither to be directed unto [sic: *proficiendnm*] nor to be raised up unto pleasing, if they be not governed by the intention going before as by a head. Which intention, because iniquity presseth it not down, weakness softeneth it not, but it raiseth itself to the things above with a sound desire of pleasing, hath deserved to obtain the eminence and the name of a mountain, whose interpretation is found not to lack the fruit of a mystery. Carmel is interpreted *the knowledge of circumcision,* or, *knowing circumcision.* Circumcision, as the Jews have known, is a cutting of the flesh, cutting away the foreskin, that is, carnal desire [0465C] in a figure; which, given by Moses, the Jews held literally, but they spiritually who studied to lay aside what the flesh suggesteth. The Virgin therefore, whose intention is known to stand above desire, is rightly called Carmel, not knowing how to lie under carnal things — she who, spiritually circumcised in heart and ears and all her senses, in bearing a son pleased, undivided, not a man but God alone. That new mother indeed saw in herself the wondrous excellency of the mountain, when, coupling herself to the Bridegroom, she circumcised the desires of carnal men; when the Spirit so overshadoweth her, a spiritual circumcision so cleanseth her, that she who beareth transmitteth not to the born the hereditary fault of the flesh. Whose hairs of the head, that is, her holy and most subtle thoughts, [0465D] by which there cometh to the head no mean comeliness or ornament, are likened to the purple of the king joined to channels, while by faith and love of the passion of Christ they are empurpled as with a certain blood. Among those who are said to be skilled in this craft, the purple is wont to be bound in little bundles in narrow channels, and upon it so bound water is poured, in due manner and measure, by which pouring the dye may be serviceable for empurpling the wool. Or, according to some, at Tyre and Sidon, where this craft is chiefly practised, fine and white wool is straitened in channels, which by the blood of the shellfish poured over it is changed into a purple colour, and thenceforth is called purple — nay, is called the purple. Which is therefore said to be the purple of the king, because from of old it was wont to be as it were proper to kings that they should be clothed [0466A] with a purple garment by special privilege, and that by this sign, marked off from the rest, they should without doubt be shewn to be kings. Whence in the Gospel [n: (Matth. XXVII; Joan. XIX)], because Christ had as it were presumed to attest himself a king, the persecuting Jews were seen to be intolerably indignant; and before they slew him whom they would not have reign over them, they in mockery clothed him with a [sic: *pnrpuream*] chlamys. But the hairs of the head are laid in narrow channels, when the Virgin's thoughts are put under spiritual exercises, when the faith of Christ's passion prepareth for itself a cleaner place, when the rain descending upon the fleece turneth the whole of it into love. The blood of the sea-fish is the very love of the Redeemer, who was made man in the world by the precept of the eternal begetter; upon the Virgin, whom he marketh to be shut in the frying-pan of discipline, [0466B] he poureth over and poureth in the love of his own blood by the grace of a medicine. Whereby she is said to become purple — nay, the purple — and thenceforth is exhibited to the highest king so properly, so specially, that she is fitted no longer to human but to divine use only; and is proved to know and to love not a man that is man, but God and man. Which God-man also is not unfitly called the head of the Virgin, who by a princely excellency is proclaimed the mountain of the house of the Lord above the top of the mountains, to whom perchance the name of no other but of Carmel is assigned, because as knowingly as mightily he taketh away the foreskin from the true Israel. But the hairs, which are known to cleave to the head by the root, and yet to stray further abroad unless discipline study to restrain them, are all the acts of the Virgin which she hath more firmly established from the root in Christ, and hath called back from [0466C] excessive going forth by a judicial strictness. Which she willed to be brought into channels, that is, into low and humble straits, and the joy of the passion to be drawn over them as a purple dye — whose whole manner of life knoweth not to be highminded, but to fear, and to shine forth with a reddening martyrdom as a living victim.

## CHAPTER XXIII.

*How fair art thou, and how comely, most dear one, in delights? Thy stature is likened to a palm tree, and thy breasts to clusters of grapes.* Because the praises begun from the steps have come in a straight series to the head or the hairs, and have found nothing in all the aforesaid that is not praiseworthy, he comprehendeth the beauty of the Virgin in a kind of little summary, calling her most dear, in whom he findeth [0466D] nothing reprehensible. How fair art thou, saith he, with a will *271* that knoweth not torpor, but is lively; how comely, pursuing a just willing with an effectual hand; and that the praises may be strengthened by an end more worthily heaped up, most dear amid so many delights to me who am delicate. In one such, so praiseworthy, I complain of no loss, because thy stature is like a commendable palm tree, which is narrower in its lowest parts, spacious in the upper: thou rejoicest in a good beginning, fruitful with a better end. Finally, as the palm is set before the girded warrior, so assuredly it is delivered to the ungirded victor also; and that which had led on to labour and roused the mind of the fighter is raised up unto praise when it is borne in the hand of the triumpher. Thy stature therefore is found like unto this palm, when persevering labour is marked with such titles of praise, when, beginning from [0467A] the lowest, thou art enlarged with a fruitful advance, when, the world's champion being conquered, thou rejoicest in a reward now certain. Thy stature is said not undeservedly to be likened to a palm, which is known rather to stand in an upright purpose than to be bowed; and because her standing is granted to go forward unbending unto perfection, she deserveth to obtain the palm, that is, a worthy title of victory. And thy breasts, wherewith thou givest suck, or feedest abundantly them that stand about — that is, the form of thy life whereby thou instructest the ignorant both morally and spiritually — is like, not to useless herbs, not to barren trees, but to fertile clusters, joyous with [sic: *recundito*] wine. For they that attentively behold thee perfect both in moral and in spiritual things, and so carried up from the bosom of the earth to the lofty likeness of a palm, wonder, are amazed, and are not held within their own [0467B] bounds, while among all women they behold none like unto thee. Pressing therefore thy breasts with certain hands of consideration, and foreseeing more fully that the perfection of both lives floweth from them, they seem to have pressed out for themselves as it were certain clusters, and to have brought forth an inebriating wine of amazement and wonder.
