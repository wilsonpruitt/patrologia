## CHAPTER XXIV.

*I said: I will go up into the palm tree, and will take hold of its fruits; and thy breasts shall be as the clusters of the vine; and the odour of thy mouth as the odour of apples.* Whence so great a grace beyond the rest is conferred upon the Virgin, that her breasts are held like to clusters and her stature like to a palm, the Bridegroom evidently intimateth in the following verse, shewing what he had foretold, and that he hath consequently fulfilled what was foretold. *I said,* saith he: [0467C] *I will go up, and thy breasts shall be clusters.* His saying is the purpose which from eternity he willed to be ordained, which, remaining fixed with himself, he willed for a very long while to be hidden; but at length, when the happy fulness of times shone forth, there was given to this Virgin an evident certainty of this saying. For he who had long been hidden with himself or in the Father, an Angel indeed being sent, yet himself going before the Angel, was presented to the Virgin; and he willed that wonderful saying to be exhibited as it were visibly, while, invisible by nature, he granted himself by grace to be seen. *I said,* saith he, and what I said I will no longer keep silent, but *I will go up into the palm tree,* that is, I will appear visible: I will go up into the Virgin, through her I will shew myself to the rest, through the flesh assumed of her I will triumph over the prince of the world. For the depth [0467D] of my divinity was inaccessible to men, by love of whom being led I will go up on high, that when from the deep and hidden invisible I shall have been visibly exalted, I may draw all things to me, not defrauded of the efficacy of drawing. The Virgin therefore is a palm, lofty by the privilege of her merits, into whom the Bridegroom willed to go up as into a sycamore tree, whose ascent, that is, the assumption of flesh, is reckoned by every faithful one a foolish fig, but to him that worthily looketh up and humbly receiveth it, worketh a saving fruit. Which fruit the Bridegroom himself thankfully taketh hold of, when he findeth in it nothing at all to displease him, when he accepteth, when he approveth, when he rejoiceth that it accordeth with his good pleasure, that the fruitfulness of the palm is granted to bring forth the salvation of the faithful. He taketh hold, I say, when a keeper [0468A] most faithful draweth near to it, when he fore-armeth it and taketh heed that he leave nothing of it to be exposed to the birds, when he cherisheth the palm itself and advanceth it into so great a power that whatsoever befitteth the world unto salvation hangeth from it. And there hangeth from it the flesh of Christ, the passion of the flesh, the wood of the cross: the abolishing of faults, the flight of night, the return of the true light, the reconciliation of the world, the constancy of virtues, the confidence of rewards — all which things he thankfully taketh hold of, who is the author and disposer of them. Which being taken hold of, the breasts of the Bride are likened to the clusters of the vine, because by her benefits the minds of them that worthily consider are made drunken; and her odours, or the odour of her mouth, is made as of apples, because her fame and her teaching hath a good savour in the nostrils of her hearers. To which is subjoined: *The throat,* etc.

## CHAPTER XXV.

[0468B]

*Thy throat like the best wine.* If the things that are borne to them without by a faithful messenger are as clusters, as apples, what and how great things *272* may be believed to be those which are felt by the Virgin herself, which she, pouring out with a bountiful hand, offereth timely to the throats of the hungry — who but a blind man would deem that she is fasting from things so great and such? She indeed above the rest rejoiceth to feel things so many, so excellent; she chiefly draweth in what sweetness is had in the Bridegroom, and how great; and what others scarce foretaste, whom so great an excellency of merits adorneth not, she with an eager draught feeleth more fully and tasteth. Whose taste the Bridegroom figured by the name of the throat, and willing to commend it, compared it to the best wine, because to whomsoever the taste or the throat of the Virgin becometh known, he seemeth [0468C] to himself to have drunk, in that he groweth unspeakably glad. For to whom the Virgin, not envious, maketh her taste known, his heart she gladdeneth as though it had drunk wine; and so much is she granted to know and to feel in the secret of her heart that he to whom it shall be given to consent is not able not to rejoice. And well did the Bridegroom proclaim that wine to be the best, whereof whosoever hath drunk hath deemed none better: the vineyard of Naboth gave not the like to drink, nor perchance the vineyard of Sorec; nor did Noe drink better of the first vineyard that he planted. But because that taste, or the throat of the Virgin, is more fully made known to none, nor doth any other rejoice more over what he hath received, than the Bridegroom, who needeth not to be taught by a transitory word (for he seeth and approveth whatsoever useful thing is thought); the Virgin, knowing that wine [0468D] to please more worthily the herald of it, catching the word as it were from his mouth, hasteneth to answer.

## CHAPTER XXVI.

*Worthy for my Beloved to drink, and for his lips and his teeth to ruminate upon.* He commendeth, saith she, my throat — nay, that which now groweth sweet in the throat, which whosoever is dull and stupid in palate approveth not enough; but he himself, by whose gift so great a grace shineth clear to me, drinketh with a full draught, and his bowl drieth not with want. I indeed have experienced the wondrous sweetness of this wine, and, the Bridegroom proclaiming it to be the best, am not uncertain of it; whereof, lest by keeping silence I be reproved, I have confidently spoken, and what he rightly began I have followed up with a worthy heralding. He, a discerning judge, saith that wine to be the best; and relying on that saying [0469A] I consequently assert it to be worthy — worthy, I say, that it be offered him to drink in a pleasing cup, to whom assuredly whatsoever shall be unworthy is not owed. But all the best things ought to be given to none more worthily than to him who ought to be deemed the best of all, who, as he in no wise willeth to be given drink in a dreggy cup, so requireth of me that unmixed wine, and that only, be presented to him. My throat therefore, which he named the best wine — that is, the affection of my heart, which hath tasted of him things so great, so unspeakable — I judge it worthy that I diligently present it to his sight, and fill up his thirst, offering a fitting draught. For he thirsteth, and thirsteth for the good of us all, who nevertheless admitteth not the gall of dragons and the wine of asps [n: (Deut. XXXII)]; he drinketh not the vinegar of the Jews, nor the bitterness offered him, [0469B] but refuseth it; and he putteth far off the wicked, whom in the Gospel he thus accuseth: *Go,* saith he, *you cursed; for I thirsted, and you gave me not to drink [n: (Matth. XXV)]*. [var: Vulg. Matt. 25:41 has *Discedite a me*, "Depart from me," where Migne prints *Ite*, "Go"; and Vulg. 25:42 has *potum*, "drink," where Migne prints *bibere*, "to drink." Migne also joins the two verses and inserts *enim*] And so I judge it worthy that I offer the best wine to him that thirsteth, which may please not only at the first taste but no less at the repeating; that drinking it eagerly, and holding what he hath drunk pleasing, he may suffer no weariness, but rather love it ruminated. The lips and the teeth, wherewith clean animals are known to ruminate, I deem to figure a re-handling, and in the re-handling a strictness, wherewith that benevolent and most keen judge, to see whatsoever he loveth in the Bride, handleth it again and strictly examineth it unto pleasing. He drinketh indeed when he thankfully embraceth her understanding or her affection; he ruminateth when he handleth again and strictly examineth [0469C] what is loved; and the more he perceiveth that nothing unfitting appeareth before, nothing after, so much the more he loveth to rejoice in things exquisite and well-pleasing. And as the law willed that the animal which ruminateth not be proclaimed unclean, so that understanding or affection is not clean enough which endureth not to be handled again; but whatsoever lieth hid in the Virgin feareth not a repeated judgment, and the subtle ruminator findeth nothing in her that he should cut away. Therefore she addeth: *I,* etc.

## CHAPTER XXVII.

*I to my beloved, and his turning is toward me.* I, I say, rejoice to be found serving and obeying my Beloved, to cleave to him more gratefully, to experience his pleasing sweetness; [0469D] whatsoever I feel in myself feareth not to be laid open to him more openly, my conscience shrinketh not from being met by his scrutiny. I indeed, not distended with womanish levity through the vain and various windings of the world, am intent with a ripe gravity upon him whom alone I love; from him I am turned away neither in act nor in affection, not even for a little, but am turned to his face with a devout desire. And my beloved himself slumbereth not while he keepeth me *273*, nor doth he snore, but is at hand; when I shall have cried out, he sheweth himself benevolent to her that crieth; he turneth not away his face from my prayers, nor even from my affections, but turneth himself to my desire with a cheerful countenance. And since between us there is proclaimed not an aversion but a mutual conversion, it pleaseth that my speech also be turned again unto him, that my face, which in seeking out beholdeth his face [0470A] sought out, may address that very face familiarly as it were mouth to mouth.
