## CHAPTER XXX.

[0472D]

*There will I give thee my breasts.* When I shall have seen, saith she, the vineyard marked with new flowers, when I shall have seen the following fruits brought forth by the foregoing comeliness of the flowers, when I shall have seen any oppressed man afflicted more than is just, and coloured in our sight with a Punic beauty: *There,* that is, then — at an opportune hour, to wit, at a fitting time — I will pour in my breasts, I will give my help to him that is in need. And well doth the Virgin promise that she will give, not a breast, but breasts, because very many will, whose action answereth not their will; for many seem to have compassion when they mark us wretched, but labouring under want or avarice they stretch not forth the hand of help. Of such men a certain heathen: All men, saith he, pardon, no man succoureth. But the Virgin both pitieth our [0473A] miseries with an affection of benevolence, and with a most powerful hand worketh whatsoever she shall have seen necessary for us; and lest under a hard burden faith had languished and hope should waver, she is quickly at hand, so to refresh us with both breasts. But because her benignity hath mercy on none of us save for the sake of the son, *275* whom she beholdeth most merciful toward us, lest she should seem to refer the cause of the benefit to us rather than to him — whom assuredly she loveth not for our sake, but us for his — therefore she said not: *I will give* them *my breasts,* but, *I will give thee.* Whatsoever, saith she, I shall have bestowed on them who so need to be helped, I will attribute the whole to thee, whose good pleasure I see to work in these things; nor will I have this favourably assigned either to them or to myself, but rather that both they and I myself more justly glory in thee alone. Whatsoever [0473B] therefore I be found mercifully to impart to thine — whom I shall see to flourish as vineyards, whom to bring forth good works, whom finally, suffering unjust things, I shall have weighed to be like unto pomegranates, sighing only unto us, awaiting our patronage — whatsoever help, I say, I shall confer upon such, I will give to thee, because through thee and for thee, and referring the whole to thee, I will help them.

## CHAPTER XXXI

*The mandrakes have given a smell in our gates.* [var: Vulg. divides otherwise — *Mandragorae dederunt odorem. In portis nostris omnia poma…* — attaching the gates to the apples, not to the mandrakes; Migne's division gives the gates to the mandrakes, which is what this whole chapter expounds] Because the Virgin before named the villages and the vineyards and the pomegranates, in which she figured the Churches of the Gentiles and their various advancements, making a passage to the Jews, under a riddle nevertheless and a figure, she foretelleth that their conversion shall one day be. Whose conversion [0473C] a prophetic nostril, most sagacious in perceiving, smelleth out, and she rejoiceth with a most certain hope of their converting, knowing and foreknowing the conversion of the people of the Jews, which, though it must be deferred, shall yet come at the end of the ages. Signifying which beforehand: *A smell,* saith she, *in our gates the mandrakes* now *have given;* our nostrils, not blunted with ignorance, have perceived beforehand that, after the fulness of the Gentiles shall be granted to enter in, then the remnant of the Jews, then all Israel shall be converted. Mandrakes, as they say, are herbs suited to the uses of medicine, bearing before them a human likeness, save for the head; by which the Jews, prudent and erring, are figured fitly enough, who are formed to the divine worship by a written law, as being men. But because they know not Christ the head of the faithful, nor [0473D] adore him, being truncated and disfigured they discolour all the remaining body; and they who by the knowledge of the Scriptures and by the legal commandments are conformed to the faithful are deformed by the absence of the head and by an unbelieving ignorance. Who also confer no mean medicine upon the sick, when they read the law to us, when they bring forth a discipline of living, when they keep for us the codices, as a furnace of moral things, as a workshop of spiritual, and bring to us for teaching those whom they hold as severe judges. And these indeed bring forth no fuller fruit as yet in the houses, as yet in the bridechamber, but in the gates, that is, in spiritual senses, they give a pleasing smell to the virgin, because what the divine goodness disposeth to impart to them in the last days is already granted to the virgin, smelling it from afar, to perceive beforehand; for she perceiveth that after the fulness [0474A] of the Gentiles shall have entered in, Judea, which now, wandering, is gone forth from the borders of the Church, which, labouring with famine, receiveth not even the crumbs from the table of the masters, shall then be converted to the faith, and shall feed on the bread of the children.

## CHAPTER XXXII.

*All the apples, new and old, my Beloved, I have kept for thee.* That the Gentiles, called together to the faith by the gift of a new grace, bring forth worthy fruits and works agreeable to holiness — this is my desire, this assuredly my zeal, my care, who diligently care for whatsoever I shall have known will please thee. Likewise that the Jews, who now stray abroad as with bonds broken, are called back in the last days to the ancient binding of faith — my solicitude no less desireth this, demandeth this, which affectionately grieveth with them that go astray, and pardoneth them. And so, my Beloved, [0474B] I keep for thee apples new and old, when I more studiously heap up the merits of both peoples, when I permit not the good beginnings of the Gentile and of the Jew to perish, but study to make firm the purpose of the one and to repair the hurt of the other. Which merits, or fruits, or works the Virgin perchance willed for this cause to name apples, because she rejoiceth to present gifts of this kind to the bridegroom for his eating: that he may both be taken with their delightful appearance, and, lest he start back as one defrauded, be held by the useful and commendable substance. She keepeth the apples of the Gentiles, when she cherisheth and embraceth their newness, when she rejoiceth that they are grafted in and that a richer fatness of the olive is given to them so grafted, when she presenteth herself powerful and benevolent to them as they labour, and with a pious devotion presenteth their fruitful labours to the son. But she is seen no less to preserve the apples [0474C] of the Jews, when, at length pitying them, she willeth them not to err any longer, when she busieth herself to restore the household of her own race to their ancient state, and, giving her hand, turneth the people long an enemy of the faith into a friend. She keepeth new things and old, when she so helpeth the Gentiles to be now converted to the newness of faith that she willeth them not to be wholly turned away from the older Jews; when she loveth the Jews so to return to a faith now lost, or rather *276* interrupted, that yet she grudgeth not that the Gentiles were grafted in before. These things I have kept, saith she, not seeking, to wit, a nearer glory for myself, but all of them utterly for thee, that is, unto thine honour; because, when the Virgin, solicitous, rejoiceth concerning them or for them, she glorieth not in herself but in the Bridegroom, as was said a little before.
