## CHAPTER XL.

*Under the apple tree I raised thee up: there thy mother was corrupted, there she that bore thee was violated.* Thou, saith he, who so leanest upon thy beloved with a delicate tenderness, who, hunger being driven far off, now reclinest at the banquet upon my breast, [0479D] to whom thou owest whatsoever thou hast — who raised thee up to this desert, save he who raised thee when thou wast languishing and lying under the tree? That first woman, who, ill containing herself, coveted the forbidden apple, and by an unlawful eating ensnared a husband ill uxorious, was straightway under the tree given over to languor, or rather to death; and the same death was left to them that came after as it were by hereditary right. Under this tree therefore, which is made comely by a mystical fruit, all posterity lieth and is laden with the laws of death, while in the fault which the tree brought in every creature languisheth and groaneth which a like begetting bringeth forth from those parents. Under the same tree the Virgin was held by a like bond, when the divine goodness (a thing that was covered with itself through many ages gone by) [0480A] looked more kindly, and chose her before all women, that through her he might redeem them whom hereditary death compelled to be damned. From the tree therefore to the tree he carried over the chosen Virgin, when, leading her out of fault, he raised her to the height of a greater grace; when, being free from the blemish of the fatherly and hereditary hurt, he translated her to the faith and the love of his passion to come. For the Virgin, receiving God out of herself, flesh being taken, to be born as man, acknowledged consequently that the same should die according to the flesh; in which death, because she understood the life and salvation of us all, she loved, if not the death, yet assuredly the gift of grace which it brought. When therefore she was snatched from under the apple tree which brought in death, she was none the less carried over under the apple tree which brought the fruit of life; [0480B] under whose shadow she was borne up to so great a summit that in grace and in desert she was made perfect before all women. But that which is said, *There thy mother was corrupted,* if it be referred to the first tree which brought in death, human nature is not unfitly called the Virgin's mother, which under that tree bore so great a hurt and loss that, being corrupted, it corrupted, and turned vice into nature for us. But if under the tree of the cross the bride's mother is believed to have been violated, Judaea is rightly understood to be called her mother, who, where the Bridegroom raised the Virgin to a singular grace by the desert of faith, there the mother, demanding that the Bridegroom be slain, violated her heart by obstinacy. Under the wood of the cross therefore both the bride's faith is strong and Judaea's chastity is violated, who, denying the true Bridegroom, is obstinately joined [0480C] to a corrupter; and him to whom she ought to have cleaved familiarly and been fastened, him, with a corrupted mind, with repeated voices she demandeth to be crucified. Where the Virgin, being raised up, heareth the bridegroom consequently saying: *Put me,* etc.

## CHAPTER XLI.

*Put me as a seal upon thy heart, as a seal upon thy arm; for love is strong as death, jealousy hard as hell.* Because thy mother, by a ruinous error, cast me forth out of her heart, she also put far from the chamber of that same heart the salvation that was nigh; but thou, my Beloved, who in that same place art raised up by an incomparable grace, love him that raiseth thee, that loving thou mayest be the more diligently loved. But because in loving [0480D] thou appearest vehement enough, and not weak, I see that this must be added: what thou doest, do it more perseveringly, and confirm it; and thy heart, wherein my love seemeth in a large fulness *280* to be contained, close thou with a seal set upon it, which by no craft whatsoever can be done away. Now this seal, which I admonish to be set upon thy heart as it were a cover, lieth open to no craft, to no hurt; because that which a subtler art doth not newly devise, nor a more learned hand fasten together, craft doth not unbar, violence doth not break. Me therefore, whom it is plain that no contrivance at all can hurt or corrupt — me, I say, myself set thou upon thy heart as a seal; and it sealing thee, no entrance nor going forth that bringeth loss shall lie open, and, that lying not open, love shall abide constant and unfailing. The same [0481A] seal also I will that thou set upon thine arm, namely that thou set not thyself to work anything save out of me, save through me; and that, when thou shalt have rightly sealed whatsoever thou doest with such a defence, thou mayest know that thine arm lieth open to no detriment at all. And for this cause I admonish that what thou doest, what thou lovest, be sealed by the setting of me upon it, because thy heart or thine arm, relying upon such a seal, shall not be hurt, but shall prevail against all things with an incomparable strength, and, being to be compared with death itself, shall not fear whatsoever cometh upon it. For as death standeth by all men indifferently, strong and undaunted, and no power or industry whatsoever withstandeth it as it standeth by, so love, if it be truly fortified beforehand with the aforesaid seal, is found strongly to prevail against all hurtful things. Again, as hell, when it [0481B] rejoiceth to rage more hardly against the wretched, and neither labour nor the groaning of the wretched can soften its hard cruelty, so the jealousy wherewith the virgin, loving, burneth to imitate God willeth not to be called back from her purpose, neither by prayer, nor by price, nor by softness. It is expedient therefore that this love, this jealousy, be held in thee strong and unbending, that the memory of me be not taken away from heart and from arm, from love and from work; because whosoever lacketh that seal is easily violated, and a vessel lacking a cover is judged unclean by the testimony of the law [n: (Num. XIX)]. But the vessel that burneth with the fire of love, and is covered with the hand of my solicitude — the heart, that is, of him that strongly loveth, which is guarded by me set upon it as a seal — is a commendable vessel, knowing not to be emptied, copious; the heart is [sic: *incibile*] [ *f.* lucibile], the heart is like unto a lamp, [0481C] not shadowy. Whence it is subjoined: *The lamps thereof,* etc.

## CHAPTER XLII.

*The lamps thereof are lamps of fire and of flames; many waters shall not be able to quench charity, neither shall the floods overwhelm it.* [var: Vulg. Cant. 8:7 *aquae multae non **potuerunt** extinguere caritatem*, a perfect — "many waters have not been able to quench"; Migne prints the future *poterunt*, and *neque* for the Vulgate's *nec*.] Not thou alone, saith he, O Virgin, who art commended with a worthy title of praise, in that thou art chief both in the grace of deserts and in the excellence of love, but whosoever loveth is called a lamp of fire and of flames, withstanding indeed a fall, yet not fleeing from pressures. For the lamp which stretcheth itself with open mouth to obtain love, and among the ill-hearted rich holdeth itself forth outwardly contemptible, is shown to them that worthily consider, and is understood to be [0481D] a lamp of fire, hiding its fire from them that are ill-darkened and unworthy. For it burneth and delightfully kindleth with an inward heat, and toward those things that are without it seemeth not to glow, but groweth cold, while it now careth not for whatsoever is of this life, as for a barren frost, and by an inward desire conformeth itself to the burning seraphim. Nevertheless, that it may be plain that the same lamp is not uselessly kindled, nor that a neighbour complain that nothing useful is bestowed upon him out of it, that which is first a lamp of fire is consequently said to be also a lamp of flames, that to them who diligently weigh it it may appear to be wanting not only to itself, but the neighbour also. For he that loveth, and is strongly burned up with the fire of love, is not torpid with languor, nor with leisure, but worketh the more watchfully; and with eyes not blear [0482A] he poureth out a welcome light with a bountiful gift, and offereth himself as a pattern to them that work, as a guide to them that walk. Him, burning and shining, the multitude of waters quencheth not; the lover, or his strength, yieldeth not to threats, not to blandishments; nor at last shall floods, nor the inundation of greater pressures, nor famine, nor the sword overwhelm the exuberance of the fire or of the flames. For charity is strong with so great violence or vigour that it cannot be overwhelmed by a small onset of tribulations nor by a greater, so that for love of the spiritual reward it beareth the multitude of waters, and the little drops of rainy dew, and the greater assaults of floods. To this charity therefore nothing perfunctory is rightly compared; nay rather, charity is worthily set before whatsoever can be had here: and whosoever hath not a sound head and the truth of judgment taken from him [0482B] chooseth her and loveth her above all that is offered here as transitory. Whence he addeth: *If a man shall give,* etc.
