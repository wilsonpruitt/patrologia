## §1 · ⭐⭐ THE THREE FINDINGS THE MERGE SHOULD CARRY

### 1.1 · ⭐⭐⭐ Gen. 3:19 at 0398A — **brief §6's second attested reading turns up in 11632, guilleted, under a correct siglum**

> « **Terra es**, inquit, **et in terram ibis** `[n: (Gen. III)]`. » (0398A)

The Vulgate reads *pulvis es, et in pulverem reverteris*. Brief §6 lists this
exact divergence as one of two "already attested in his other commentary,"
confirmed across several agents' ranges **on 11638** — evidence about Philip's
copy of the Bible rather than a compositor's slip.

⭐ **This is, so far as this range can tell, its first attestation inside 11632
itself**, and it arrives in the strongest available shape: **inside guillemets,
with a correct `[n: (Gen. III)]`, in a chain of three proof-texts Philip has
assembled deliberately** — *Terra es et in terram ibis* (Gen. 3:19), *Ago
poenitentiam in favilla et cinere* (Job 42:6), *cum sim pulvis et cinis*
(Gen. 18:27) — all three adduced to gloss the *cinnamomum* of Cant. 4:14, "quod
colore, cineri similatur."

⚑ **And there is a detail that makes it sharper, not weaker.** The chain's theme
is **ash and dust**, and the other two members print *cinis* and *pulvis*
verbatim — so the Vulgate's own *pulvis es, et in pulverem reverteris* would
have suited Philip's argument **better** than what Migne prints. **He is not
adapting the verse to his context; the *terra* reading is simply what stood in
front of him.** That is precisely the inference brief §6 draws from a copy-text
reading, and this instance supplies it without needing an etymological
exposition to carry it (as *Bethel* did at 0338D).

`[var:]` fired at the quotation, naming the witness and giving the Vulgate's
whole clause; it does not speculate about origin (Pattern 14). **The merge should
count this as the §6 class's first cross-work attestation** — the class was
established on 11638 and is now confirmed in the work the brief governs.

### 1.2 · ⭐⭐ John 19:39 at 0399D — a near-verbatim quotation Migne marks with **nothing at all**, and the dropped word is the one Philip's next sentence argues from

Migne prints, as running text in Philip's own paragraph, with **no guillemets,
no italics and no `[n:]`**:

> *Recte Nicodemus in Evangelio ferens misturam myrrhae et aloes **libras
> centum**, eodem prorsus ordine sibi conficiens hoc unguentum…* (0399D)

Vulg. John 19:39: *venit… ferens mixturam myrrhae et aloes, **quasi** libras
centum.* Every printed word is the Vulgate's except that **the Vulgate's *quasi*
is gone** — "about a hundred pounds" has become "a hundred pounds."

⭐⭐ **And that is exactly the word the exposition cannot spare.** Twelve lines
later Philip writes:

> *Quod autem misturae hujus **libras centum** Nicodemus legitur attulisse,
> innuit Christum **in pondere et numero** suum nobis beneficium contulisse; et
> quod ante fuerat… prophetatum, tandem esse **certo pondere, perfecto numero
> consummatum**.* (0400A)

The whole argument is that the hundred is **exact** — a *certain* weight, a
*perfect* number, and that exactness is what makes it a figure of the
*Consummatum est* he quotes two sentences later. With the Vulgate's *quasi* the
number is approximate and the inference does not run.

**Ruling applied: logged in full, `[var:]` NOT fired** (brief §8a — where Migne
marks the words as neither quotation nor lemma we cannot establish the
quotation's boundary, and a marker would assert a boundary the plate does not
print). The English renders what is printed: "bearing a mixture of myrrh and
aloes, a hundred pounds."

⚑ **This is a stronger member of the §8a class than Lam. 4:20 or 2 Paral. 23:11
in one respect and weaker in another, and the merge should note both.** Weaker:
it carries no *inquit*, so Migne does not even present it as speech. Stronger:
**the divergence is a DELETION, and the deleted word is a hedge.** The three
earlier members of the class all substitute one reading for another, which a
reader could in principle notice. Nothing on this page tells a reader that a
qualifier has gone missing — and the argument built on its absence is four
sentences long. ⛔ **This is also the single most repairable place in the range:
one word restores the Vulgate and silently unbuilds Philip's paragraph.**

### 1.3 · ⭐⭐ John 12:3 at 0397B — Migne prints « unxit pedes **Jesus** » for the Vulgate's *Jesu*, and the correct genitive stands five lines later

> « Sumpsit, inquit, libram unguenti nardi pystici pretiosi, et unxit pedes
> **Jesus** `[n: (Joan. XII)]`. » (0397B)

Vulg. John 12:3: *Maria ergo accepit libram unguenti nardi pistici pretiosi, et
unxit pedes **Jesu**, et extersit pedes eius capillis suis.*

*Jesu* is the genitive — "anointed **the feet of Jesus**." Migne's *Jesus* is
nominative, and as printed it makes Jesus the **agent** of the anointing: "and
Jesus anointed the feet." **That is Pattern 7a's fifth mechanism — reference or
agency mis-bound — and it is the mechanism no negation-aware check can see.**

⭐ **The control is internal and needs no appeal outside the work.** Migne prints
the correct genitive at **0397C, five lines later, in Philip's own gloss**: *ut
ungant pedes **Jesu**, et ad caput ipsum profectu veniant ampliore.* And Philip's
own sentence immediately **before** the quotation names the true agent — *et
**Maria Magdalena** pedes Domini ungere voluit hoc unguento*. So within one
column the plate has the right form on either side of the wrong one, and the
prose on both sides contradicts what the quotation as printed asserts.

**Rendered as printed** ("and Jesus anointed the feet"), which is meant to read
wrong, with a `[var:]` naming the Vulgate's genitive and pointing at 0397C.
**Not a `[sic:]`:** *Jesus* is a real word and Pattern 12's "real word merely
wrong" clause keeps it on the English side — the same call cruces-0060 made for
*Incipit*/*accipit* at 0345B and cruces-0042 for *Adjuvo*/*Adjuro* at 0299D.
This is now the third instance of that shape in the work, and **all three are
single letters.**

⚠ **Also divergent in the same span, and deliberately kept out of the marker so
it cannot elide the finding: *Sumpsit* for the Vulgate's *accepit*.** Synonymous,
and the subject *Maria ergo* falls away where Philip's frame plus the Pattern 5
*inquit* takes over — below Pattern 14's threshold. Logged here, unmarked.
The `[var:]` names both readings but is worded so the *Jesus*/*Jesu* point is
the one it asserts.

## §3 · TYPOGRAPHY

### 3a · ⚠ THE GUILLEMET ASYMMETRY — an unopened » at 0398B, and it closes a lemma

0089 prints **10 « against 11 »**. The extra closing mark is here:

> *Myrrha,* inquit, *et aloe cum omnibus primis unguentis.* **»** (0398B)

The lemma is set in **italics** with the ordinary Pattern 5 *inquit* break, and
then a **closing guillemet with no opening one** follows the full stop. The
nearest preceding « is at 0398B (*Loquar, inquit, ad Dominum meum…*) and it is
**properly closed** eight lines earlier, so this is a stray », not a run-on.

**Reproduced exactly as printed** (Pattern 5 corollary, brief §7a: "guillemet
asymmetries are Migne's and are mirrored, never balanced"). Nothing supplied,
nothing deleted; the English carries 10 « and 11 » and the verifier's parity
check passes on the pair, not on either side alone.

⚑ **The interesting part for the merge is WHERE it falls.** It closes a **lemma**
— italic, a re-quotation of Cant. 4:14 introducing the myrrh-and-aloes section —
in a chunk where every other run is a guilleted proof-text. So the stray mark is
a compositor's reflex reaching for the mark the *neighbouring* class takes, on
the one line where the italic class was in use. That is weak evidence *for* brief
§7's functional division rather than against it: the two marks are distinct
enough in practice that the error is visible as an error.

### 3b · ⭐⭐ Horace at 0399B, adduced as an authority and printed in ITALICS — the collision §7 has not yet had to adjudicate

> *Oderunt,* inquit, *peccare boni virtutis amore.* (0399B)

This is **Horace, *Epist.* I.xvi.52**, verbatim, and Philip adduces it with an
explicit authority formula: *bene quidam **ethnicus** non expers vel inscius
similis medicinae, **demonstravit** non vagis et negligentibus, sed in auditorio
residentibus disciplinae.* By the function brief §7 assigns to « » — "a quotation
adduced as an authority" — this is the guillemet case exactly, and it is the same
authority-formula shape as the Delphic oracle and the Virgil at 0251D–0253A,
which **are** guilleted.

**Migne prints it in italics.**

⭐ **But this is not a fourth counter-instance, and the merge should not file it
as one.** The three known counter-instances — Ovid at 0259, Sallust at 0314A,
Ambrose at 0337B — are all printed **bare**, with no mark at all. This one is
**marked**, and marked with the mark §7 assigns to *"classical verse quoted as
verse rather than adduced as proof (Horace in the prologue)."* So the two
functional rules genuinely collide here — classical **verse**, adduced as
**proof** — and **the plate resolves the collision in favour of the verse rule.**

⚑ **That is a positive finding about the typography rather than another hole in
it**, and it is the same author (Horace) the prologue's italic instance uses,
which makes it as near a controlled comparison as this work offers.
**Offered to the merge as a refinement of §7, not a contradiction of it: where a
run is BOTH classical verse AND an adduced authority, 11632 prints italics.** One
instance is one instance; a second would settle it.

⚠ **The Pattern 5 break is awkward in English and was kept anyway.** Migne breaks
the italics after the single word *Oderunt* — before the subject *boni* has
arrived — so the English reads "*They hate,* saith he, *to sin, the good, for the
love of virtue.*" The subject lands where Latin puts it. Same call cruces-0066
made for *Cum pertransissem,* inquit, *eos* at 0348C.

**non** sit lege mortalium ad putredinem transitura… **non** confecit… **non**
vermes, **non** putredinem longe fecit… **non** magis paradisum… **ne** forte
cuilibet… **non** suppletur*).

**No negation was dropped and none was supplied.** ⚠ **One place was caught by
this pass and would otherwise have shipped wrong: 0396A, *signaculo **non**
soluto, **non** fontem percipit tam secretum*.** The draft had rendered the
ablative absolute affirmatively ("the seal being unloosed"), which reverses the
sentence — the crowd stands outside *because* the seal is **not** loosed. Fixed
to "the seal not being unloosed". Recorded per brief §9 as a caught instance of
mechanism 2, in the exact shape (a negated ablative absolute governing a negated
main verb) that the count-based check is documented to miss.

## §5 · CONVENTIONS SET OR HELD IN THIS RANGE

1. ⭐⭐ **`emissio` = "emission", NOT Douay's "plants" — set here, and the whole of
   *Caput* XXVI depends on it.** Cant. 4:13's *Emissiones tuae* opens the chapter,
   and Philip's entire gloss is the **verb**: *Quae conclusus iste hortus, vel
   munitus signaculo fons **emittit*** · *Emissiones itaque virginis* · *quae de
   horto vel fonte virginis est **emissa*** · *ut signetur **emissio** Virginis
   opulenta* · *paradisum unguentariae plenitudinis huc **emisit*** (0400B) ·
   *virginalis duxit **emissio*** (0398B) · *quae instar paradisi de horto Virginis
   sunt **emissae*** (0397B) — **eight occurrences of the root across three
   chunks, and every one of them is the argument.** Douay's "thy plants" is
   botanical and severs the chain: "the garden **sends forth**… thy **plants**"
   explains nothing. This is the Song section's case 2 exactly (*stylus* /
   *epistylium*, *murenulae* / *ferculum*): where the gloss IS the etymology, an
   English word sharing no root makes the gloss explain nothing. ⚠ **The lemma
   recurs — Cant. 4:13 is re-quoted at 0396B and the noun again at 0400B, and the
   fountain half of the same verse-pair is promised for the NEXT chapter at 0400C
   (*ad ea consequenter non differt Sponsus loquendo pertransire, quae signato
   quoque fonti videntur probabilius convenire*). The 0090+ range inherits this
   word.** ⛔ **This is the one cross-chunk decision in my range the merge must not
   undo.**
2. ⭐ **The beauty-words kept strictly distinct, extending brief §5's colour
   triple.** *formosus* = **comely** (0394C *flos formosus*, per brief §5's
   *formosa*) · *speciosus* = **fair** (0394C *forma prae filiis hominum
   speciosus*; 0394D *forma prae hominibus specioso*) · *pulcher* = **beautiful**
   (0389A/0389B *Quam pulchrae*, *pulchriora*; 0395A *non nisi pulchritudo*) ·
   *decor* / *decus* = **comeliness** / **grace**. All four stand within two
   columns of each other at 0394C–0395A and the passage is built on their
   difference. **Offered to the merge as the extension of brief §5's triple into
   the beauty field.**
3. ⚠ **`species` splits, and the split is contextual — flagged rather than
   asserted.** In the aromatic catalogue it is **"spice"** (0397B *sine altera
   **specie** vel mistura* · 0398A *praemissis **speciebus*** · 0398C *ad aliorum…
   charismatum **species*** · 0396D *multa subdit genera **specierum***, which
   introduces 0089's spice list). In the garden passage it is **"kind"** (0394D
   ***species** vigent rerum* · *multiplicitas **specierum***, where no spice is in
   view and the subject is the things growing). Both are right in context and the
   work has no single English exponent; **logged so a merge sweep does not
   flatten one into the other.**
4. **`fistula` = "sweet cane" (Douay), not carried Latin.** Philip's gloss —
   *coloris est subrubei, corticem habens durum* — is of cassia bark, and "cassia"
   was considered. **Douay's "sweet cane" was kept** because a cane has a rind and
   the gloss survives it, and because nothing in the chapter is an etymology of the
   word. *crocus* = saffron · *cyprus* = cypress (Douay; Philip's *arbusculae…
   graciles* whose *grana* are boiled in oil is henna, but again no etymology
   depends on it) · *nardus* = spikenard · *malum Punicum* = pomegranate.
   **Logged as judgment calls, all reversible.**
5. **Bed- and chamber-words: none occur in this range**, so cruces-0060 §5.1's
   *lectulus* / *lectus* / *cubile* / *cubiculum* set is untouched. ⚠ But
   *thalamus* does occur twice — 0390B *primum **thalamo** virginali* and 0395D
   *Nuptialem itaque **thalamum** virginis* — and is rendered **"bridal chamber"**
   / **"nuptial bed"** respectively by the immediate context. **Offered as a fifth
   term of that set, and flagged as the one I am least sure of**: a merge that
   wants one English word for *thalamus* should pick "bridal chamber" and change
   0395D.
6. **`charitas` = charity · `dilectio` = love — held, and the range contains the
   pressure point.** 0388C–0388D prints *sub una redigit **charitate*** and
   ***charitatis** redimiculo* and then, four lines later, *cum per
   **dilectionem** quod lex divina praecipit operatur* — where the underlying verse
   (Gal. 5:6) has *caritatem*. **Rendered "charity" / "charity" / "love"**, per
   brief §5, which is what preserves the fact that Philip has changed the noun.
   Also 0388C *si **dilectionis** vinculo non ligatur* = "the bond of love" against
   0400B *unguenta geminae **charitatis*** = "the twin charity."
7. **Pattern 3 held on the one bare instance** (§3c): 0391C *Scripturarum* →
   "the Scriptures".
