### 1.2 · ⭐⭐⭐ Cant. 5:9 at 0429B — Migne prints *ex dilecto* ONCE where the Vulgate prints it twice, and **Philip collates the variant himself, in the text, and reports his manuscripts**

> *Qualis est dilectus tuus **ex dilecto**, o pulcherrima mulierum: qualis est
> dilectus tuus, quia sic adjurasti nos?* (0429B, head lemma of *Caput* XVI)

Vulg. Cant. 5:9: *Qualis est dilectus tuus **ex dilecto**, o pulcherrima
mulierum? qualis est dilectus tuus **ex dilecto**, quia sic adiurasti nos?* The
phrase stands in **both** questions. Migne's second question has lost it.

⭐⭐⭐ **And the omission is not merely expounded — it is adjudicated.** Philip
builds his whole Christological reading of the doubled question on the asymmetry
(0430B–C):

> *Prima enim inquisitio cum dixisset:* Dilectus *statim* ex dilecto *supponit,
> secunda,* dilectus *repetens* ex dilecto **juxta quosdam codices** *non
> apponit: per hoc dans intelligi Deum dignum diligi ex Patre esse nihilominus
> diligendo, hominem vero etsi diligi, tamen dignum esse ex homine non aequaliter
> amplectendo… quarum prima posuit* ex dilecto, *secunda voluit reticere.*

— and then, extraordinarily, he reports the collation against himself:

> *Quod si dixerit quispiam quia illud et prima posuit, et secunda non tacuit,
> nolo nimis contendere, pertinaciter reluctari, **quia multos inveni codices
> huic testimonio suffragari.** Nam, etsi non omnes, quos invenire potui codices,
> **plerique tamen ex eis**, sicut in prima inquisitione, sic et secunda habent*
> dilectus ex dilecto; *neque satis compertum habeo utrum* ex dilecto *repetitum
> fuerit ab auctore, an potius scriptum vitio vel errore.* (0430C)

**This is a twelfth-century commentator doing textual criticism on his own Bible,
naming the two readings, conceding that MOST of the codices he could find carry
the phrase twice, and declining to decide whether the repetition is the author's
or a scribe's error.** Migne's lemma prints the minority reading — the one
Philip's doctrine of the two substances requires — and the four columns of
*Caput* XVI stand on its absence.

`[var:]` fired at the head lemma, naming the Vulgate's repetition and pointing at
0430B–C. It does not speculate about origin (Pattern 14) — Philip's own two
sentences do that better than any note could.

⚑ **For the merge, this is a member of brief §6's class with an internal witness,
which none of the other four members has.** Zech. 4:14, Gen. 3:19, *Bethel* and
Eccles. 9:1 are all inferred from recurrence; here the author states the variant.
⚠ It is also the one instance in this work where a merge might reasonably demote
the marker on §10.7's logic ("Migne's own *[alias]* bracket… the divergence is
already adjudicated on the page"). **I fired it anyway, and the reason is that
§10.7 concerns MIGNE'S apparatus.** This adjudication is Philip's, inside the
body text, in the twelfth century, in Latin, sixty lines below the lemma — it is
not apparatus and a reader meeting the lemma has no signal at all. Flagged so the
merge can rule the class once.

⚑ **The phrase recurs at 0448A (chunk 0108)** — *Supra enim dixerant:* Qualis est
dilectus ex dilecto — where the daughters recall the question. **Printed once
there too** (and with *tuus* dropped). So Migne is internally consistent: two
printings, both single. That is the copy-text shape.

### 1.3 · ⭐⭐ Cant. 5:7 at 0427A — Migne prints ***vigiles*** for the Vulgate's *custodes*, and Philip's cross-reference note proves it is copy-text, not a slip

> *Invenerunt me **vigiles** qui circumeunt civitatem: percusserunt me, et
> vulnerarunt me, tulerunt pallium meum custodes murorum.* (0427A)

Vulg. Cant. 5:7: *Invenerunt me **custodes** qui circumeunt civitatem; percusserunt
me, et vulneraverunt me; tulerunt pallium meum **mihi** custodes murorum.*

Two divergences, and the first is material:

1. ⭐⭐ ***vigiles* for *custodes*.** In the Vulgate the verse uses **one** word
   twice — *custodes* find her, *custodes murorum* take her cloak. In Migne it
   uses **two**, and **Philip's *Caput* XIV is built on the distinction.** He
   keeps the two parties apart for two columns (*vigiles* five times, *custodes
   murorum* → the apostles → the walls), and closes the chapter by subordinating
   one to the other:

   > *civitatem ambiunt ad custodiam fortiter expediti, **si tamen a vigilibus
   > digne fuerint custoditi**.* (0428B)

   The keepers of the walls are themselves kept by the watchmen. **On the
   Vulgate's text, where both are *custodes*, that sentence cannot be written.**

2. **The Vulgate's *mihi* is gone** — a deletion, named inside the same `[var:]`
   so it cannot be elided. Nothing turns on it (Philip supplies the dative
   himself at 0427D, *gaudet Virgo pallium **sibi** tolli*), but it is recorded
   as a deletion because §8a's John 19:39 case is exactly the shape that leaves
   nothing on the page to notice.

⭐⭐⭐ **The proof that *vigiles* is copy-text and not a compositor's reach is
inside the work, and Migne prints the pointer.** The very next words carry
`[n: (sup. l. III, c. 22)]` — Migne's only note in the chunk, an internal
cross-reference — and Philip writes *Longe retro eamdem Virginem dicti sunt
**vigiles** invenisse.* I resolved it: **Book III, *Caput* XXII is chunk 0065,
col. 0346A, and its head lemma is Cant. 3:3 — *Invenerunt me vigiles qui
custodiunt civitatem*.** *vigiles* is the **Vulgate's own word at 3:3.**

So Philip identifies the finders of Cant. 5:7 with the finders of Cant. 3:3, and
the identification works **because his text uses the same noun in both places**.
On the Vulgate they are different nouns and the cross-reference is a looser
gesture. Migne's Cant. 5:7 has been conformed to Cant. 3:3 — and the exposition
that follows requires exactly that conformation.

⚑ **Two by-products worth the merge's ledger:**
- **`(sup. l. III, c. 22)` is CORRECT**, resolving exactly (§8b wants the right
  ones logged as much as the wrong). It is also the only *internal* cross-
  reference siglum this range met; it is a locator, so it stays `[n:]`, not
  `[nt:]`.
- The lemma prints syncopated ***vulnerarunt*** while the re-quotation eleven
  lines later at 0427A–B prints full ***vulneraverunt***. Migne differs from
  himself inside one column. No sense change; **logged, no marker.**

### 1.4 · ⭐⭐ 0418B — Jer. 6:7 quoted with only an `[n:]`, and it IS a quotation: **the range's one `[n:]`-only hit**

> *Sicut enim perhibente propheta frigidam cisterna **facit** aquam
> `[n: (Jer. VI)]`; sic frigidam fecit malitiam suam.* (0418B)

Vulg. Jer. 6:7: *Sicut frigidam **fecit** cisterna aquam **suam**, sic frigidam
fecit malitiam suam.*

**No guillemets, no italics — only the note.** And unlike every other `[n:]`-only
span this range met, this one is not Philip's prose: the whole verse is there,
both limbs, and the second limb (*sic frigidam fecit malitiam suam*) is **word
for word the Vulgate and stands entirely OUTSIDE the note**, unmarked in any way.
Migne's note falls at the semicolon, so the extraction could only ever see the
first half.

Divergences in the first limb: present ***facit*** for the Vulgate's perfect
*fecit*, and ***suam*** dropped, with the word order rebuilt (*frigidam cisterna
facit aquam* for *frigidam fecit cisterna aquam suam*). The tense-break is the
interesting one — the Vulgate's parallelism is perfect/perfect, and Migne's is
present/perfect, so the simile becomes a general truth rather than a matched act.

**Ruling applied: logged in full, `[var:]` NOT fired** (brief §8a — where Migne
marks the words as neither quotation nor lemma we cannot establish the
quotation's boundary, and here the boundary problem is visible on both edges: the
left edge runs into Philip's *Sicut enim perhibente propheta*, and the right edge
runs past the note into unmarked verbatim Vulgate). The English renders what is
printed: "For as, the prophet bearing witness, a cistern maketh water cold; so
hath she made her malice cold."

⭐ **Reported as a hit, and it is the class's third in the whole run.** The
running rate before this range was 2 of 109 (see §2c). Mine is **1 of 5**.

### 1.5 · ⭐⭐ 0416D — 1 Cor. 11:3 quoted with an explicit *Paulo teste* and printed bare, and it is the chapter's controlling text

> *Caput Christi, **Paulo teste**, Deus est.* (0416D)

Vulg. 1 Cor. 11:3: *caput **vero** Christi, Deus.* Every printed word is Paul's
(the connective *vero* falls away where Philip's own frame takes over, and *est*
is supplied from the Vulgate's ellipsis). **Philip names his authority — *Paul
bearing witness* — and Migne prints the words in neither guillemets nor italics,
with no `[n:]` of their own.**

⭐ **And it carries the chapter.** The lemma under exposition is *quia **caput
meum** plenum est rore*; the identification *caput Christi = Deus* is what turns
the dew into the Jews' refusal of Christ's divinity, and the next four sentences
are entirely about that refusal (*dum Christum hominem Deum esse, Dei Filium non
intelligunt*), proved from John 10:33 and John 5:18. Remove the Pauline sentence
and *Caput* VII has no hinge.

**Ruling applied: logged, no marker** (brief §8a). This is the class the brief
describes exactly — an expounded quotation Migne leaves unmarked — and, like the
Virgil at 0385A, it is **adduced as an authority with an attribution formula and
printed bare**, which is the shape §7's counter-instances take.

⚠ **On the siglum, and I record it as CORRECT rather than wrong.** The nearest
note is `[n: (I Cor. II)]`, one sentence later, at the end of *…friget iniquitas
Judaeorum*. It is tempting to read it as a corrupted **XI** (a lost `X` would give
exactly `II`) misplaced from *Paulo teste* — but 1 Cor. 2:8 (*quam nemo principum
huius saeculi cognovit: si enim cognovissent, nunquam Dominum gloriae
crucifixissent*) is a genuinely apt imagery pointer for *the iniquity of the
Jews*, which is the clause it actually stands on. **I therefore record `(I Cor. II)`
as a correct pointer and 1 Cor. 11:3 as simply unnoted**, and log the numeral
hypothesis here only so the merge has it. Brief §8b's warning about manufactured
patterns cuts directly against counting this as an error, and `(I Cor. II)` is
the very siglum §8b says has been vindicated twice already (0305C, 0318A).

## §5 · CONVENTIONS SET OR HELD IN THIS RANGE

1. ⭐⭐ **`districtio` = "strictness" · `discretio` = "discretion" — and
   *discretio* is NOT split when it means "separation."** The whole of *Caput* XI
   is a pun held across two senses: the fingers *foras tamen **discreti**
   prodeunt… eorumque **discretio** tam prudenti naturae artificio fabricatur*
   (0422C, the anatomical sense) becomes, four words later, *virtus recte
   accipitur **discretiva*** — the virtue of discretion. **English "discretion"
   carries both senses and the pun survives; "distinction" for the first would
   sever it.** This is the Song section's case 2 (*stylus*/*epistylium*,
   *murenulae*/*ferculum*) applied to a pun rather than an etymology. Held with
   *severitas* = "severity" and *moderatio* = "moderation" kept separate, since
   0422C sets all four in one sentence (*discreta moderatio, vel moderata
   discretio*). ⛔ **This is the one cross-chunk decision in my range the merge
   must not undo**; *districtio*/*discretio* recur through 0098 twenty-two times.
2. ⭐ **`probata` / `probabilis` / `probatissima` = "approved" / "approvable" /
   "most approved", against Douay's "choicest".** Forced by 0423A, where Philip
   builds the argument on the degree: *non **probatam**, sed magis,
   **probatissimam** nominavit* · *Probabilis vero vel probata illa congrue
   potest dici* · *Probata est quae… Probatissima vero est quae…* Douay's
   "choicest" has no positive or comparative in English and the four-term chain
   would collapse. **Cant. 5:5's lemma therefore reads "the most approved myrrh."**
3. ⭐ **`vigiles` = "watchmen" · `custodes` = "keepers" — kept strictly
   distinct, and it is load-bearing (§1.3).** "Watchmen" is already the work's
   English for *vigiles* (21 instances, set at 0346A on Cant. 3:3), so this range
   inherits rather than sets it; what is new is that **the same verse now carries
   both words** and the English must show it. *custodes murorum* = "the keepers of
   the walls"; *custodia* = "guard"/"keeping". ⛔ Do not harmonize.
4. **`otium` = "leisure" · `negotium` = "business" — held**, matching the work's
   37 / 22 existing instances. The pair is the axis of *Caput* VIII–XII and
   appears sixteen times in this range. The Latin's *neg-otium* etymology has no
   English exponent and nothing in the argument turns on it.
5. **`distillare` = "drop down" · `stillare` = "drop" · `stillicidium` = "a
   dropping"** — set here so that Migne's lemma and Philip's gloss of it remain
   visibly different words on the English page (see §2b, 0098). Consistent with
   0086's "dropping honeycomb" for Cant. 4:11's *distillans*.
   `annuntiare` = "announce" · `nuntiare` = "tell", on the same principle (0101).
6. ⚠ **`stratum` = "bed" — flagged, because brief §5's ruled triple has no fourth
   term.** Ps. 62:7's *super stratum meum* (0424D, and glossed at 0424D–0425A,
   *Stratum, quietem vocat*) needs a plain English noun, and Douay's is "bed" —
   but §5 assigns "bed" to *lectus*, "little bed" to *lectulus* and "couch" to
   *cubile*. ⚑ **None of those three occurs anywhere in this range**, so nothing
   collides on the page; but a merge enforcing the triple work-wide should know
   that *stratum* is a fifth word with no slot left, and that "bed" is what I
   used. Reversible in one line. (`cubiculum` = "chamber" and `thalamus` =
   "bridal chamber" are held from cruces-0084 §5.5; *conclave* at 0420D = "inner
   room".)
7. **`pessulum` = "bolt" · `sera` = "lock" · `vectis` = "bar"** — three
   fastening-words in one sentence at 0416B (*detrahe **vectem**, **seram**
   excute*) and the whole of *Caput* XII on the bolt. Kept distinct, per Douay
