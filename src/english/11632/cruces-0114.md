## §1 · ⭐⭐ THE FOUR FINDINGS THE MERGE SHOULD CARRY

### 1.1 · ⭐⭐⭐ Cant. 7:13 at 0473B — Migne DIVIDES THE VERSE differently from the Vulgate, and *Caput* XXXI has nothing else to stand on

This is the range's largest finding and the only one that reshapes a chapter.

Migne prints two lemmata, heading two chapters:

> **CAPUT XXXI** — *Mandragorae dederunt odorem **in portis nostris**.* (0473B)
>
> **CAPUT XXXII** — *Omnia poma nova et vetera, Dilecte mi, servavi tibi.* (0474A)

Vulg. Cant. 7:13: *Mandragorae dederunt odorem. **In portis nostris omnia
poma**: nova et vetera, dilecte mi, servavi tibi.*

**Every word is present; the division is not.** The Vulgate's *in portis nostris*
belongs to the apples; Migne's belongs to the mandrakes, and *Caput* XXXII's
lemma accordingly opens at *Omnia poma* with the gates already spent.

⭐ **And the whole of *Caput* XXXI is an exposition of the gates as the
mandrakes'.** Philip re-quotes the divided lemma with his own *jam* wedged into
it — *Odorem,* inquit, *in portis nostris mandragorae* jam *dederunt,* the roman
*jam* standing inside the italic run and reproduced there as printed — and
then builds:

> *Et ii quidem necdum in aedibus, necdum in thalamo fructum proferunt
> pleniorem, sed **in portis**, id est sensibus spiritalibus **gratum virgini
> dant odorem*** (0473D)

The Jews-as-mandrakes are *not yet* in the house or the bridechamber; what they
give **in the gates** is a *smell*. Under the Vulgate's division the gates hold
apples and the mandrakes' smell has no location at all, and the antithesis
*aedibus / thalamo / portis* — the chapter's frame — is unwritable.

**`[var:]` FIRED**, immediately after the lemma, naming the Vulgate's division
and what it gives to which fruit. The marker is available here for the reason
cruces-0102 §1.2 gives: Migne italicises the run, so the quotation's boundary is
printed and the `[var:]` asserts nothing the plate does not.

⚠ **This is a division, not a wording divergence, and I want the merge to see it
as its own shape.** Pattern 8 governs punctuation and says mirror the plate;
Pattern 14 governs scripture divergence and says mark what changes the sense.
Here they meet: the *only* difference from the Vulgate is where the sentence
breaks, and the sense changes completely. I judged Pattern 14 to govern because
what diverges is the **extent of the quotation**, which is a textual fact, not a
compositor's comma. If the merge rules the other way the fix is a one-line
deletion; nothing in the English depends on it.

### 1.2 · ⭐⭐ Cant. 6:12 at 0458D — *Sunamitis* for the Vulgate's *Sulamitis*, seven printings, and the whole chapter is an etymology of the divergent name

Migne prints, as the running lemma of *Caput* XIII:

> *Revertere, revertere, **Sunamitis**: revertere, revertere, ut intueamur te.*

Vulg. Cant. 6:12 (6:13 Heb.): *Revertere, revertere, **Sulamitis**; revertere,
revertere, ut intueamur te.* And Cant. 6:12b at 0459D: *Quid videbis, aiunt, in
**Sunamite**, nisi choros castrorum?* for Vulg. *in Sulamite*.

**Three lines later Philip glosses the divergent form:**

> *Sunamitis interpretatur **captiva** vel **despecta**, sive **coccinea**.*
> (0459A)

Those are Jerome's interpretations of **Sunamitis** (*coccinea sive despecta*),
not of *Sulamitis*, which is the peace-name. And they are not decoration: they
are the chapter's three-part architecture, and Philip works each one —

> *naturaliter filia fuit irae… adhuc tamen gravatur vinculo, et naturae legibus
> est **captiva*** · *infectionis merito mundioribus Dei oculis est **despecta***
> · *et **coccinea** digna sit nominari merito, non colore. **Coccus** ignis
> speciem tenet, amorem significans* … *ut **coccus** sit **bis tinctus**, ad hunc
> amorem geminum bis invitat* (0459A–C)

— and the *coccus bis tinctus* leg then carries the chapter to its end, twinning
the Bridegroom's two natures with the two dyeings and the two *Revertere*'s.
**Conform the lemma to *Sulamitis* and there is no captive, no despised, no
scarlet, and the twice-dyed scarlet has no verse to hang from.**

⭐ **I greped all 126 Latin chunks. *Sulamit-* occurs nowhere in the work; the
verse is printed only here, and the name seven times in the body of one chunk**
(four *Sunamitis*, three *Sunamite*). So there is **no internal control** — this
is unlike Eccles. 9:1, which contradicts itself at 0049, and unlike Ps. 44:3,
which Migne prints nine times in four orders. What it has instead is the
authorial witness: **the exposition names the form and derives from it.** That
is §6's Zech. 4:14 shape (Philip builds on the addition) rather than its
distributional shape.

**`[var:]` FIRED** at the lemma, once, naming Vulg. *Sulamitis*. The English
carries *Sunamite* throughout, including in the gloss.

### 1.3 · ⭐⭐ Cant. 7:5 at 0465B — the lemma prints *juncta canalibus*, and Philip's own gloss twice keeps the Vulgate's *vincta*

Migne prints:

> *Caput tuum ut Carmelus, et comae capitis tui sicut purpura regis **juncta**
> canalibus.* (0465B)

Vulg. Cant. 7:5: *…sicut purpura regis **vincta** canalibus* — "bound in
channels." Migne repeats the divergent word in the exposition: *purpurae regis
**junctae** canalibus similantur* (0465D).

⭐ **But the dyer's excursus that immediately follows is written on *vincta*.**
Philip explains the craft twice, and both times the verb is binding or
straitening, never joining:

> *solet purpura per fasciculos in angustis canalibus **alligari**, cui
> **alligatae** aqua superfunditur* (0465D)
>
> *lana subtilis et candida in canalibus **angustatur*** (0465D)
>
> *Comae autem capitis in **angustis** canalibus **disponuntur*** (0466A)
>
> *Quos in canalia, id est in **depressas et humiles angustias** voluit
> **introduci*** (0466C)

Four times the channel constricts the purple. *Junctus* ("joined to") says the
opposite thing about the same preposition-less ablative, and the moral it
carries — thoughts driven down into humble narrows — needs the binding.

**`[var:]` FIRED**, naming Vulg. *vincta* and pointing at Philip's *alligari*.
⚑ *Iuncta* is not a phantom: it is a known variant in the Vulgate tradition. That
is an argument for `[var:]`'s wording ("Vulg. *vincta*"), not against firing —
Pattern 14 states two readings and lets the crux argue.

⭐ **Grep result, offered because it is the cheap kind:** *canalibus* occurs in
this work **only in chunk 0116**, five times, all in this chapter. There is no
second printing anywhere to control against.

### 1.4 · ⭐⭐ 0464C — Migne prints *cinis* where his own etymology, six lines above, requires *cingulum* — and it stands inside the only autobiographical paragraph in the range

At 0464A Philip gives the name's meaning, italicised:

> *Hesebon interpretatur **cingulum moeroris**.* — "the girdle of sorrow"

He then applies it: *ingressos portae angustias moeror necessarius nos
**accingit*** ("a necessary sorrow **girdeth** us", 0464A–B) — the verb built on
the same root. Twelve lines later, in the personal passage, Migne prints:

> *hujus [0464C] **cinis** Hesebon mea ipsius moestitia cogeret deperire, si non
> Virgo dignaretur extenta manu citius subvenire.*

***cinis*, ash, is a real Latin word**, so Pattern 12's own boundary clause keeps
it on the English side and no `[sic:]` fires. **Rendered as printed** — "the ash
of this Hesebon, mine own sadness, would have compelled me to perish" — taking
*cinis* and *moestitia* as the two nominatives in apposition that the plate
supplies. ⛔ **The conjecture is ***cinguli*** / ***cingulo***, and it is close to
certain**: the sentence is the payoff of the *cingulum moeroris* gloss, the
girdle is what the whole chapter has built, and ash has appeared nowhere in the
book. **Not emended.** ⚠ This is the range's most repairable place: one word
restores the argument and destroys the evidence.

⭐ **And note what the paragraph is.** *Expertus sum quod aio* — "I have
experienced what I say" — introduces Philip speaking in his own person about
being turned on by his own community: *Cum divino perurgente judicio **filii
matris meae contra me pugnaverunt**, cum exteris parcentibus, ii quos mea

pugnaverunt contra me*), taken out of the lemma-machinery entirely and turned on

- ⭐ **Plural → *you*, and there are exactly two places.**
  1. **0459D–0460C, the angels to the Bridegroom** — *aiunt*, *inquiunt*, and they

  2. **0469B, Matt. 25:41–42** — the only true plural address in the range:
     *Ite… non dedistis mihi bibere* → "**Go, you cursed**… and **you** gave me
     not to drink."
  ⭐ **The pair the merge should like is 0467A vs 0469B, six columns apart**: the
  Bridegroom says *quam pulchra **es**… statura **tua*** to one woman and the same
  Bridegroom says *Ite… **dedistis*** to a crowd, and under Pattern 17 an English
  reader sees the number change with no apparatus. Under a flat "you" the Bride
  and the reprobates take the same pronoun in the same book.
- **0464B's *nos* passages are first-person plural**, not second, and take no
  archaism: *nos restringit*, *nos accingit*, *vult nos esse*.

## §4 · PATTERN 7 / 8 / 9 / 10 — five `[sic:]`, and the readings rendered as printed

### 4a · The five carried non-words — and four of them are one mechanical class

⭐ **`n` set for `u`, three times in three chunks, and `o` for `u`/`i` twice.**
Recorded together because the profile is a compositor's, not a copy-text's, and
because a merge counting "defects" would otherwise count five unrelated events:

| col | printed | read | marker |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0464B | *qnod* | *quod* | `[sic: *qnod*]` |
| 0465B | *proficiendnm* | *proficiendum* | `[sic: *proficiendnm*]` |
| 0466A | *pnrpuream* | *purpuream* | `[sic: *pnrpuream*]` |
| 0462D | *virita* | *virili* | `[sic: *virita*]` |
| 0467A | *recundito* | *recondito* | `[sic: *recundito*]` |

- **0462D *fide virita constat*.** Not a Latin word in any form. ***virili*** is
  near-certain: the clause it stands in is an antithesis — *quae apostolis
  defluentibus timore carneo et **mollitie feminina**; fide **virita** constat* —
  and *feminina* two words earlier demands *virilis*. Carried, in italics,
  untranslated: "standeth firm by a [sic: *virita*] faith." ⚑ The English keeps
  "firm" from *constat*, not from the marked word, so nothing inside the marker is
  doing translation work (Pattern 7a′).
- **0467A *vino recundito*.** ***recondito*** ("stored up") is near-certain — a
  stored wine is the whole point of *botris fertilibus… laetabundis*, and
  *recunditus* exists nowhere. It is a single-vowel misprint like *pnrpuream*, and
  Pattern 12's test is whether the run is a word at all, not how small the error
  is. Carried: "joyous with [sic: *recundito*] wine."
- ⚠ **The `[sic:]` content of all five was checked as a verbatim substring of the
  Latin twin** (Pattern 12's safety rule) — the check that stops a `[sic:]` being
  wrapped round English.

### 4b · Real words merely wrong or merely odd — rendered as printed, NO marker

Pattern 12's boundary clause keeps every one of these on the English side.

- ⛔⭐⭐ **0467D, *cujus ascensio, id est carnis assumptio **fideli cuique** ficus
  fatua reputatur*.** As printed: the Incarnation "is reckoned by every
  **faithful** one a foolish fig" — and the second half of the same sentence says
  *ei autem qui digne suspicit, et humiliter suscipit, fructum salutiferum
  operatur*. **The sentence contradicts itself in six words.** Read
  ***infideli***, near-certain: the *fatua ficus* is the barren fig of Matt. 21,
  the antithesis needs an unbeliever, and *cuique* + *autem* is a standard
  either/or frame. ⛔ **NOT repaired.** Rendered "is reckoned by every faithful one
  a foolish fig," which is meant to read wrong. **This is brief §9's exact case —
  a dropped negative that makes the author assert the opposite of his own
  argument — and it is a DELETION of a prefix, which brief §8a names as the
  hardest member of the class because nothing on the page signals it.** ⚠ It is
  also the range's cheapest repair: two letters. **The single most important line
  in this file after §1.1.**
- ⛔ **0464C, *hujus cinis Hesebon*.** See §1.4. Read ***cinguli*** / ***cingulo***,

### 4c · An untranslatable etymology, logged because §5's rule made the call

**0462B–C, *A muniendo quippe vel monendo, ut nonnulli aiunt, **monilia**
nominantur, eo quod **munita** quaeque tali **munere moneantur***.** The whole
second half of *Caput* XVI turns on *monile* ← *munire* / *monere*, and English
"necklace" shares no root with "fortify" or "admonish."

**I translated it ("necklaces") rather than carrying *monilia* in italics**, on
brief §5's stated principle: *italic Latin is reserved for words with no English
exponent*, which is the ground on which *vermiculatas argento* was translated and
*muraenula* and *ferculum* were not. *Monile* is a plain object with a clean
English word; *muraenula* required a fish. The English keeps the argument's shape
(fortify / admonish / fortified / admonished, all four cognate pairs rendered by
cognate English) and loses only the sound-link to the noun. **Logged so the merge
can see the call was made deliberately and can reverse it cheaply** — it is five
occurrences in one paragraph of one chunk.

⚑ Two other lemma words where I did **not** reach for italic Latin, on the same
principle: *crater tornatilis* → "a turned bowl" (Philip glosses *tornatura*,
which English carries) and *frixorium* → "frying-pan" (0466A; odd, but it is a
frying-pan and the oddity is Philip's).

### 4d · Negation audit (brief §9), second pass, against the Latin

Every printed *non / nec / neque / nisi / nihil / nil / nullus / nunquam /
necdum / nequaquam / nequire / nescire / nullatenus / ne* in the six Latin chunks
was extracted **after** the prose was drafted and matched one by one against the
English. **No negation is missing and none was added.** The places I checked
twice, because they are the ones a smooth English would have eaten:

- **0468C *ut **non** gaudere **nequeat*** — a double negative. Rendered "**is not
  able not** to rejoice," not "cannot but rejoice," which would have dropped one.
- **0464B *nisi moestos et miseros **non** admittit*** — rendered "admitteth
  **none save** the mournful and the wretched," carrying both particles.
- **0469C *nullum prius, nullum posterius inconveniens apparere*** — first drafted
  as "no unfitting thing… neither before nor after," which fused the two *nullum*
  into one; corrected on this pass to "**nothing** unfitting appeareth before,
  **nothing** after."
- **0467D *ficus fatua*** — see §4b. The negation that is **missing from the
  Latin** was not supplied.
- **0469D *non dormitat… neque stertit… faciem non avertit*** — three, all kept,
  including the psalm-adjacent pair where the familiar English ("shall neither
  slumber nor sleep") was the pull.
- **0459A–B *non potuit… non est efficacia… non suspensa… non puellaris… non
  retorsit… non extorsit*** — the six-negation cluster of *Caput* XV, all
  present.

⭐ **And the one caught in my OWN English, per brief §8's ⭐ instruction.**
Drafting 0114 I wrote "For he regarded **the lowliness** of his handmaid" for
Migne's *Respexit enim **humilitatem** ancillae suae* — the Douay-adjacent
wording — nine words after correctly rendering *humilitas* as "**humility**" in
the sentence that sets up the rhyme *despecta* / *respecta*. Not a conformation to
a word Migne does not print, but the same mechanism: **the remembered English
arrived before the printed Latin was read, and it split one word into two.**
Fixed to "humility" in both places. It was found by the second pass, not by
reading, and I record it because brief §8's measured claim is that the two acts —
collating and writing — come apart.
