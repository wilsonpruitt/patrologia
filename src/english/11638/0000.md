## HERE BEGINS THE FIRST TOME.

## PROEM

[0489C]

To his lords and, in all things, by the merit of holiness reverend fathers, MILO bishop of the Morini, HUGH abbot of the Premonstratensians: that worse and last one of the servants of God, whose name is contained in the first five letters of the first five parts of the first Tome, [wishes them] to have the divided hoof of the foot of Ezekiel's calf.

The little work set out below upon the Song of Songs I began, divine grace going before, at the exhortation and encouragement of a certain faithful man who had once heard me disputing upon certain sentences of the Divinity. Besides, a certain infirmity, from my boyhood — though by intervals — yet frequently assails me and has assailed me, which for the greatest part urged me on to this work. [0489D] For I judged that, if I pressed upon this work the more ardently, I should the less feel meanwhile the troubles of the infirmity that assails me. I reckoned, I say, that the pressing of this work would drive from me the harassment of the aforesaid infirmity. Truly I confess to all the truth of the matter. The work indeed was begun by me at God's inspiring, but nevertheless the aforesaid infirmity has as yet been little [0490C] mitigated for me. But perhaps there will be some, of my own order or of another, who, feigning that they have the zeal of God — though not according to knowledge — will say: Who is he, or what is his person, or his own life, or his wisdom, that he has presumed to do this? To whom I say: I indeed, as I said above, am the worse and the last of the servants of God, infirm in the person of my body, contemptible in speech, in life despicable enough before God, and my wisdom is truly none.

« Great, he says, is the multitude of thy sweetness, O Lord, which thou hast hidden for them that fear thee [n: (Psal. XXX)]. » [var: Vulg. *Quam magna multitudo*, "How great is the multitude"] Truly great and wonderful is the multitude of the sweetness of God, which God hides for those who fear and love him, both here in the Holy Scriptures and in the age to come in the contemplation of his majesty. But this sweetness [0490D] the lovers of the world cannot take in, because, filled with empty delights and deadly desires, they in no wise sigh after the unspeakable joys of the heavenly fatherland. This brief preface, therefore, we send before, ere we come to the book, that we may make our hearer studious and solicitous [0491A] for the understanding of the mysteries of the divine reading, lest in this little book he seek anything bodily or carnal. For the letter of this little book is quite alien from that which it seems to sound; and unless we believed it to have been assuredly published by the Holy Spirit, we should adjudge it in all things unworthy of the charisms of the Holy Spirit. For who, hearing kisses, and the eyes and cheeks and lips of the Bride named, would not at once and deservedly be indignant at the very hearing, in that such things were found in the divine oracles, unless it were believed that these things were written — the Holy Spirit dictating — concerning the coming of the Son of God to the human race, and concerning the salvation of faithful souls? But the more harshly the letter drives us back from the carnal and outward understanding, the more sweetly it leads us in to that inward understanding of itself which is lovely to God. Assuredly in this it is believed that by the sevenfold [0491B] grace of the Holy Spirit this book is composed: because all and every one of the sentences that are found in it seem to contain in themselves at the least seven things, namely, *287* the divinity and the humanity of Christ; the conjunction of the Bridegroom and the Bride, that is, of Christ and the Church. And according to the rhetorical flowers, a one-membered or two-membered or three-membered or four-membered sentence. And according to secular men, the disciplines of the seven liberal arts, the three parts of philosophy: logic, ethics, physics, that is, rational, moral, natural — without which parts of philosophy this book cannot be expounded.

Moreover, the title which is set before this little book seems to contain in itself the sense of the whole book. For as the sun, set in heaven, with its rays poured out everywhere and [0491C] its wonderful brightness spread abroad, lights up the whole world by its presence, so this title, which is set before this little book, already for the greatest part makes plain to us what this little book means to say, saying: *The voice of one desiring the coming of Christ.*

It is asked, in this first title — since two persons seem to be brought in, namely Christ's and that of a certain one desiring — why the proper name of that one desiring is not expressed, when the name of Christ is manifestly named? To which it must be said: that if any person whatever were expressed by name or set down properly, the other persons who in this book speak in various ways would perhaps seem to be shut out. But the name of Christ is set down manifestly and without a veil for this reason, that concerning [0491D] the coming of no other, save of the Son of God alone — his coming and incarnation — is the prophecy of this book woven together. Then, besides, some may wonder why Christ, who is the worthier and more excellent person, does not first begin to speak in this book. But a just reason, propped upon authority, demanded this: that the inferior person should speak among the first, and should humbly entreat him, without whom she could not be saved, to come for her deliverance, and should say: *Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth [n: (Cant. I)]*. The blessed apostle Paul instructs us, saying: Since indeed all the things that befell our fathers, befell them in a figure [n: (I Cor. X)]. [var: Vulg. *Haec autem omnia in figura contingebant illis*, "Now all these things happened to them in figure"]

If therefore among them there was a figure, among us there ought to be the truth. Turning over therefore much Scripture of the Old Testament [0492A] within myself, I sought after the figure of this truth, whether perchance any of the patriarchs, or of the prophets, had anywhere kissed his wife, so that the figure going before might make the one that follows more clearly plain to us. Nevertheless I found this written of no other save of Jacob the patriarch alone. For of him alone it is written that he once kissed his wife, that most famous Rachel, namely, of whom it is doubtful to none that she holds the figure of holy Church. But Jacob, both by the interpretation of his name and by the working of virtue, is discerned to have held the figure of Christ.

For it is written in the book of Genesis [n: (cap. XXIX)] that this same patriarch Jacob once was journeying to the eastern land; who, coming to a certain [0492B] well, was speaking with the shepherds of that region; and behold, says the Scripture, Rachel was coming with her flock, for she herself was pasturing her father's flock. When therefore Jacob had known her to be his cousin, he watered her flock and kissed her.

But afterwards Jacob wept, and made known to her that he was her kinsman. Who then is he who journeys to the eastern land, save Christ, of whom the prophet says: *Behold a man, the Orient is his name [n: (Zach. VI)]*. And what is the eastern land, save the blessed and everlasting Mother of God, the Virgin Mary, of whom Christ deigned to arise, whom Ezekiel [n: (cap. XI)] calls the eastern gate? What did that well prefigure, save the mystical and hidden Scripture which [0492C] foretold that Christ was to be born? What, finally, did Rachel's flock signify, which Jacob watered, save the flock of the prophets, which Christ gave to drink of the water of the fountain leaping up unto life everlasting? And what did Jacob's weeping intimate, save the compassion which Christ had upon us when he purposed to die for us? And what is it that holy Jacob makes known to Rachel, that he is her kinsman, save this which the Lord said through the prophet: *I will declare thy name to my brethren [n: (Psal. XXI)]*, and in the Gospel: *Go, tell my brethren* etc. [n: (Matth. XXVIII.)] And what does the kiss betoken which Jacob gave to Rachel, save this: that Christ joined to himself the nature of the human race through the mystery of the incarnation? [0492D] whence the same human race in this place says of the same Christ: *Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth.* Let that same one, who once was prefigured by the patriarchs and foretold by the prophets — let him, I say, come, and take unspotted flesh of my nature, and wondrously lead me back to the joys of paradise which I lost through Adam. Let holy Church also say of Christ: *Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth,* that is, let the Word be made flesh; and as of old Eve was fashioned from the sleeping Adam [n: (Gen. II)], so let the same Word made flesh [n: (Joan. I)] — that is, God made man, dying upon the cross — betroth me to himself as a bride pledged with his own blood. Let blessed Mary also, the mother of Christ, say — let her too, I say, say of Christ: *Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth,* that is: as long ago he promised through Isaiah the prophet, [0493A] saying: *Behold a virgin shall conceive [n: (Isa. VII)]*, let the Son of God come into me, and of my virgin flesh take a most holy body, that as a bridegroom out of his chamber [n: (Psal. XVIII)], so he himself, adorned with the purple cloak of my flesh, may come forth out of my womb. Let every faithful soul also say of Christ: *Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth,* that is: so let him join me to himself by faith and by working in this world, that in eternal happiness *288* I may deserve to be made one spirit with him. Of this kiss of Christ and holy Church, that is, of the mystery of the Incarnation, David says to the prophet: *Justice and peace have kissed [n: (Psal. LXXXIV)]*. Join therefore, if it please such a one, the lily to the violet or to the rose: such is the kiss of the Bridegroom and the Bride, of Christ and the Church, that is, the conjunction of the divine and the human nature. [0493B] *Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth.* In this sentence thou wilt find seven mysteries: of Jacob and Rachel, of God and the human race, of Christ and holy Church, of the Son of God and blessed Mary, of Christ and the faithful soul, of peace and justice, of the lily and the violet.
