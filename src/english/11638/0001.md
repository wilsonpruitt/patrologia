## RECAPITULATION FROM WHAT WENT BEFORE.

With the utmost intention and with a pure heart God must be entreated by us, the declaration of whose words gives light and makes manifest spiritual understanding to those who are little ones in malice [n: (I Cor. XIV)], so that he may deign to unveil the eyes of our heart, that we may be able to consider the wonders that are in his law. That the author of this book, therefore, is the Holy Spirit, no man of sound wisdom doubts. And that Solomon [0493C] was the instrument of the Holy Spirit at the time when he published this book, no one steeped in ecclesiastical letters is ignorant. For without the grace of the Holy Spirit he could not have brought forth so many and such mysteries of Christ and the Church. Since therefore we hold it settled that the Holy Spirit made this book, it must be asked by us why the same Holy Spirit began the same book from the letter O, or what pleased him in this letter more than in the other letters. Nevertheless, of how great authority and dignity is the mystery of this letter which is O, he himself deigned to show, who laid the name of that same letter upon himself, saying: *I am* α *alpha and* ω [n: (Apoc. I)], that is, the beginning and the end. I am the beginning, he says, giving to all creatures the beginnings of being. I am the end, [0493D] that is, the consummation; because, when all men fail, I alone remain, since I am God and am in no way subject to mutability; whence the apostle James says: *With whom there is no change, nor shadow of alteration [n: (Jacob. I)]*. And that Christ is the end, that is, the consummation, Paul makes plain when he says: *The end of the law is Christ, unto justice to everyone that believeth [n: (Rom. X)]*. Finally, if we should wish to search out yet more subtly the mystery of this letter which is O, in no other letter shall we find so great a figure, not only of the whole world, but even of God himself. For it is found round and perfect on every side of itself, just as the world of this age is described by the philosophers as round after the manner of a sphere. Moreover, if according to the Greek letters we look more attentively, [0494A] we find that this letter ω is beyond doubt three-membered, that is, having three members. What then do the three members in the one letter ω, looking upward, betoken, save three persons in the substance of the one most high Trinity? Not by chance, therefore, but by a sure mystery of the Godhead does the Holy Spirit begin the opening of this book from the letter ω, because this book through the whole text of itself preaches him who created the whole world out of nothing, and who declares in the Gospel that he himself is one person in the Trinity, saying: *I and the Father are one [n: (Joan. X)]*. The three members, therefore, of the letter ω looking toward heaven declare, as we have said above, three persons in one substance of the Trinity. And well do the members of that same letter look upward, and, as though coming down from heaven, make below one letter [0494B]; because the knowledge of the holy Trinity came down to us through the Lord Jesus, and set its seat for itself in one holy catholic Church through the unity of faith. For which cause the Lord Jesus himself says in the Gospel: *And this is life eternal, that they may know thee, and Jesus Christ whom thou hast sent [n: (Joan. XVII)]* — it is understood withal: and the Holy Spirit, to be one and true God. [var: Vulg. *ut cognoscant te solum Deum verum, et quem misisti Iesum Christum*, "that they may know thee, the only true God"] Assuredly neither do I judge that this is to be passed over carelessly by a prudent reader, that this book takes its opening from a kiss (which is the sign of charity and of peace). For it intimates to us in mystical and open utterances him at whose nativity the angelic multitude cries out: *Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men of good will [n: (Luc. II)]*. [var: Vulg. *Gloria in altissimis Deo*, "Glory to God in the highest"] There is besides another mystery which can yet be said concerning the kiss [0494C]: that a kiss cannot be made save by two. Two teeth of the same one draw near from opposite sides, they meet in one kiss of peace, and so, with lips joined together, they accomplish the kiss in charity. For the two drawing near from opposite sides and meeting into one ought not unfittingly to be understood as the divinity and the humanity of Christ, which drew very near from opposite sides when the one from heaven and the other from earth met together into the one womb of blessed Mary ever Virgin, and these two natures gave, as it were, a perfect kiss to one another, because they are so inseparably leagued together, the grace of the Holy Spirit working it, that henceforth it must not be said, without peril to faith, that there is God without man, nor man without God; and this is what our same nature seems to say [0494D] in this our book: *Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth;* as though it should say: I desire that God the Son of God so take me up into the womb of the Virgin — that is, that the Word be made flesh — that he may remain always what he was, true God, and eternally *constit* me, changed from mortality to immortality, into one person of God and man together with himself [ed: The sentence breaks off in mid-word here: our digitized source ends the paragraph with a truncated verb and supplies nothing further. Recovery would require reading the column on Migne's plate.]

By the authority of Holy Scripture we have learned [n: (Job V)] that nothing is done on the earth without a cause, for the reason *289* that we are in the hand of God, both we and our words. Since therefore it stands beyond doubt that, because we are in his hand, both we and our words, it plainly and clearly appears that we cannot speak anything well, unless either with him working together with us, or being permitted by him. Finally, if nothing is said to be done on earth without a cause, neither [0495A] do we judge that this at all was done without a reason: that at the beginning of the exposition of this little book we should use lilies and violets and the other flowers. It was plainly worthy that our exposition should take its opening from flowers, since with his help we set ourselves to speak of him who says: *I am the flower of the field and the lily of the valleys [n: (Cant. II)]*. For he himself is that wonderful flower who, according to Isaiah the prophet [n: (cap. XI)], comes up from the root of Jesse — that is, Christ, who took flesh of the Virgin Mary; and as a flower sweetly fragrant sends forth on every side its most pleasing odor, so he himself scattered the knowledge of his divinity and his humanity throughout the whole world. Moreover, as flowers without human tillage bring forth of themselves honeycombs and honey, so without man's touch did the [0495B] Virgin Mary bring forth of herself God and man. Let us say, therefore, that this book, which speaks of that wonderful flower, is the flower of flowers, that is, is the flower of the other books, saving that of the highest flower himself, that is, of Christ.

For all the saints are called flowers in holy converse, whence is that word: *The just shall flourish like the palm tree,* etc. [n: (Psal. XCI.)] The just shall flourish like the lily before the Lord. Assuredly all the books of Holy Scripture can be called flowers, for this reason, that they make us to flourish with flowers, that is, with spiritual virtues. Therefore, as Christ is the flower of the other flowers — that is, the honor, the glory and the comeliness of his saints — so this book, which is called the Song of Songs, is, as we said above, saving the Gospel of Christ, the flower of flowers, that is, the flower of the other books. Behold, as we have heard, [0495C] the Bridegroom, that is, Christ, calls himself in this book a lily; and this because in almost all places of the sacred oracles the lily signifies virginity and chastity and cleanness. And since he himself was a virgin and the son of a Virgin, therefore above all in his Gospel, among the rest of the virtues, he commended chastity to us, saying: *Let our loins be girded [n: (Luc. XXII.)]* [var: Vulg. Luc. XII, 35 *Sint lumbi vestri praecincti*, "let your loins be girded"] As though he should say: Since you do not doubt that I am a lover of virginity, of chastity and of cleanness, see that in thought, in speech and in work you keep that chastity which is pleasing to me above all virtues. Moreover, finally, I was also nourished in Nazareth, which is interpreted *flower,* because that soul delightfully feeds and nourishes me with pious works, which breathes virginity [0495D], burns with chastity, loves cleanness.

As, therefore, by the lily the Bridegroom, the same Christ, can be understood in this book, so also by the flower of the violet his Bride, that is, holy Church, ought to be interpreted. And rightly by this flower, namely the violet, which is of a purple or airy color, is the loving Mother Church betokened, who without ceasing exhorts her sons to the glorious contests of the martyrs and to the joys on high of everlasting blessedness. By the purple color, then, is taken the glory of martyrdom; by the airy, the happy and glorious expectation of the kingdom of God. But neither does it seem that this should be passed over in silence, that the same flower, namely the violet, is described as being of a tempering nature, so much so that it can heal those whom the immoderate ardor of the flesh wearies beyond their strength. [0496A] Which assuredly ought elegantly to be assigned to holy Church, who applies to her sons, wounded by the harassment of temptations of every kind, the poultices of loving confession and penance; whence also every one of the faithful ought to say to his mother the Church: *According to the multitude of my temptations in my heart, thy consolations have gladdened my soul.* [var: Vulg. Psal. XCIII, 19 *dolorum meorum*, "of my sorrows"]
