## THE FIRST MORALITY CONCERNING JACOB.

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0498C]In the old law the Holy Scripture teaches us [n: (Levit. I)] that he who offers to God from the flock a ram, or any animal fit for sacrifices, should first draw off the skin, secondly bring the flesh to the sacred fires, thirdly offer the fat and those things which are veiled by the fat as a holocaust to the Lord. ⟦s2⟧ For the ram or the heifer is any sentence whatever taken out of the Old Testament. ⟦s3⟧ From which sentence we draw off, as it were, the skin, when, handling the historical understanding, we establish it to be true. ⟦s4⟧ And the flesh is then delivered to the sacred fires, when the allegory, understood in spiritual senses, is made clear. ⟦s5⟧ But the fat, and those things which are beneath, we judge that we offer to God, when we search out the morality, and those things which hang upon manners, for the profit of the souls of the faithful and for [0498D] the praise of God himself. ⟦s6⟧ Since, therefore, in the earlier treatise we interpreted the historical Jacob in an allegorical and spiritual sense, we have, as it were, with the skin drawn off, burned up the flesh with sacred fire. ⟦s7⟧ It remains, therefore, that we bring the fat to the divine fire, that is, that we bring forth into the light the morality with a moral and loftier understanding. ⟦s8⟧ Our flesh indeed is then made the Eastern land, when in it the vices are mortified and the spiritual virtues arise. ⟦s9⟧ Which land assuredly the spiritual Jacob, that is, Christ, will deign to visit, if it shall be such as to be held worthy of his visitation. ⟦s10⟧ But that well to which the historical Jacob came signified our heart; which, if it shall perfectly have loved God and kept his word, Christ will come to it and will abide in it, whence he himself in the Gospel [0499A] says: *If any man love me, he will keep my word, and my Father will love him, and we will come to him, and will make our abode with him [n: (Joan. XIV)]*. ⟦s11⟧ And the stone with which the well is closed is rightly understood as the hardness of our heart. ⟦s12+13⟧ Which hardness indeed the Lord lovingly promises that he will take away from us, when he says through Ezekiel the prophet [n: (cap. XI et XXXVI)]: *I will take away from you the stony heart, and I will give you a heart of flesh, and I will cause you to walk in my precepts.* [var: Vulg. Ezech. XXXVI, 26 *auferam cor lapideum de carne vestra*, "I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh"] The three flocks of sheep with their shepherds which are found by Jacob beside the well are the three kinds of thoughts which turn about our heart. ⟦s14⟧ For in our thoughts we for the most part remember the evils which we have done; the present we guard against with God's help; and for the things to come, lest they overwhelm us and sink us down, we entreat God. ⟦s15⟧ And well [0499B] indeed does the history relate that, when the stone was removed, those flocks were watered; because, when the hardness of our heart has been taken away, the aforesaid thoughts are made drunk with spiritual compunction and with the healthful bedewing of tears. ⟦s16⟧ And the shepherds who guard the aforesaid sheep are faith, hope, charity. which nourish our simple thoughts. ⟦s17⟧ It is clear, therefore, that he who mourns for past sins, guards against present ones, and fears for those to come by entreating, shows himself in every way free from all sin. ⟦s18⟧ So at last he returns to that virtue which is called sinlessness, in which God created the first man after his own image. ⟦s19⟧ Finally, the history describes with an elegant style how indeed there Jacob knew again his cousin Rachel; because without doubt the creature is then more perfectly known again by its Creator [0499C], when, according to his power, a man keeps himself from sin. ⟦s20⟧ Whence deservedly Rachel is interpreted *a sheep* or *a beginning seen*, because so plainly does the creature return to the beginning of its creation, when through simplicity and sinlessness, without the blemish of sin, it is presented to its Creator. ⟦s21⟧ But the weeping with which Jacob wept when he saw Rachel signifies the unspeakable joy and the incomprehensible jubilation which God, appearing, will give to his faithful ones. ⟦s22⟧ For very often we are wont to weep for exceeding joy, just as this Jacob, when he recognized his cousin Rachel, is known to have wept for exultation. ⟦s23⟧ But the kiss which Jacob gives to Rachel betokens that unity by which we shall be united to God himself. ⟦s24+25⟧ Of which unity indeed the apostle Paul seems to me to speak: *He that cleaveth to God is made one spirit [0499D] with him [n: (I Cor. VI)]*. [var: Vulg. I Cor. VI, 17 *Qui autem adhaeret Domino, unus spiritus est*, "but he who cleaveth to the Lord is one spirit"] Moreover, this which the history does not pass over — that Jacob made known to Rachel that he was her kinsman — we can not unfittingly interpret thus: that, the judgment being ended, we shall know again our own substance in our Creator, which long since, having pity on us, he took up, when he willed to be born of the blessed Virgin Mary.
