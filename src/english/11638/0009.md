## RECAPITULATION FROM THE FOREGOING, ON THE IVORY THRONE WHICH SOLOMON MADE, HOW IT OUGHT TO BE REFERRED TO THE PERSON OF CHRIST.

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Above we said beforehand that the Lord Jesus, [0511C] who in the Canticle of Canticles is called the Bridegroom, he himself indeed is called in the book of Kings the oracle of the temple and the ivory throne. ⟦s2⟧ Moreover we added this, that by the two doors of the oracle the clemency and mercy of God, namely the breasts of the Bridegroom, ought to be understood. ⟦s3⟧ We subjoined finally a sufficient reason, namely that as those entering the oracle entered through those doors, so assuredly through the clemency and mercy of God shall they be saved who are to be saved. ⟦s4⟧ It remains therefore that we approach the throne built of ivory, and set forth, according to our small measure, in what manner the same throne was made by Solomon in a figure of Christ. ⟦s5⟧ Then at length it will be necessary that we search out, allegorizing in the spiritual sense, how those two hands of which we spoke in the preceding place, [0511D] which hold the seat in the ivory throne, spiritually hold the figure of clemency and mercy, that is, of the breasts of the Bridegroom. ⟦s6⟧ Nevertheless it must be piously considered by us whether anywhere in the divine utterances anything be said of a throne, so that what we mean to say may be strengthened by the authority of the sacred utterances. ⟦s7⟧ In the Apocalypse John says: *From the throne proceed lightnings, and voices, and thunders [n: (Apoc. IV)]*. ⟦s8⟧ As if he should say: From Christ, who is the golden throne in which the fulness of the divinity rested, there go forth lightnings, that is, the tokens of miracles, and the voices of evangelical preaching, and the thunders, namely the terrors of the judgment to come and of the last damnation.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Moreover let this be hidden from no one, that the throne of which we are busy to speak, and which we purpose spiritually to refer to the person [0512A] of Christ, is described to have been not only of ivory, but also of the brightest gold. ⟦s2⟧ It is plain therefore, and we are brought by this into no ambiguity, that by the gold we ought to understand the divinity *299* and by the ivory his humanity. ⟦s3⟧ But that gold holds the signification of the divinity of Christ needs no difficult interpretation, because as gold, for its own preciousness, goes before all metals, so without doubt the divinity of Christ, for the excellence of its dignity, surpasses not only his humanity but also all creatures. ⟦s4⟧ Whence elegantly the Holy Spirit, after he had said through the mouth of the historian that Solomon made *a great throne of ivory,* thereupon added: *And he overlaid [sic: *vestitvit*] it with the finest gold,* that it might be made manifest [0512B] how far the prerogative of the divinity excelled the assumed matter of the humanity. ⟦s5⟧ For it is certain, and no one doubts, that a thing which clothes another thing is set above that very thing which it is seen to overclothe. ⟦s6⟧ As therefore the most splendid gold with its splendour and its brightness adorned, bedecked and made bright the throne which it clothed, so without doubt the divinity carried that man whom it assumed forward even unto this its own glorification, that the man himself, by participation of the divinity, was made God. ⟦s7⟧ Much therefore did our humanity profit, which ascended, glorified unto the participation of the supernal deity in the assumption of the assuming divinity. ⟦s8⟧ Finally, by how much in the first Adam his descent to the lower regions was the worse, [0512C] by so much in the second Adam his ascent to the heavens was the more glorious and the more excellent. ⟦s9⟧ And as there was nothing whereby it could be cast down more unhappily from paradise, seduced by the devil, so there had been nothing whereby it could be lifted up more happily and more gloriously by Christ.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Our flesh therefore has been exalted in Christ above every creature and above itself; above itself, I say, because it in no wise deserved that dignity to which it has come. ⟦s2+3⟧ And this indeed is what the prophet had prophesied long before it came to pass: *Let thy ancient, true counsel be done, O Lord [n: (Isa. XXV)]* [var: Vulg. Isa. 25:1 *cogitationes antiquas fideles amen*]. ⟦s4⟧ This counsel assuredly was from eternity, and before eternity (if it be lawful so to say!), in the disposition of the highest deity, that our flesh, to be assumed and glorified by Christ, should be raised on high even unto the contemplation [0512D] of the divine majesty. ⟦s5+6⟧ Concerning this glorification indeed of our humanity, or its assumption, the prophet Ezechiel [n: (cap. XVII)], refreshed by spiritual contemplation, seems to me to say: *A great eagle, upheld by the great defence of her wings and adorned with much variety of feathers, came to the cedar which was in Libanus, and took of the marrow of the cedar and carried it away, and set it upon a holy and lofty mountain.* [var: Vulg. Ezech. 17:3–4 *aquila grandis magnarum alarum, longo membrorum ductu, plena plumis et varietate, venit ad Libanum et tulit medullam cedri*; the setting *in monte sancto et excelso* answers rather to Ezech. 17:22–23] Who then is this eagle, but that one of which the prophet says: *As an eagle provoketh her young to fly, and fluttering over them spreadeth her wings, and hath taken them up and carried them on her shoulders? [n: (Deut. XXXII)]* And as though some one should ask of him of what eagle he was speaking, he straightway subjoined manifestly that he had said this of God: *The Lord alone was his leader [n: (ibid.)]*. ⟦s7⟧ This eagle therefore [0513A] came to the cedar which was in Libanus, and took to herself of its marrow, when the Word, brought forth from the heart of the Father, came to blessed Mary, and from her virgin flesh received to himself a most holy and undefiled body. ⟦s8⟧ And as the prophet says of that eagle, that she set upon a holy and lofty mountain the marrow which she had taken from the cedar, so indeed the Son of God, namely the Word of the Father, raised on high with himself, in the seat of his majesty at the right hand of his Father, our flesh which he assumed from the blessed Virgin Mary. ⟦s9⟧ But that blessed Mary is called the cedar of Libanus is plainly known from ecclesiastical usage rather than from any difficult interpretation. ⟦s10⟧ For thus holy Church says in her daily office: *As a cedar I was exalted in Libanus (Eccli. [0513B] XXIV)*. ⟦s11⟧ And because Libanus is interpreted *a whitening*, not undeservedly is it compared to the virginity of blessed Mary. ⟦s12⟧ For as the material cedar was exalted in Libanus, so blessed Mary, for the singular privilege of her virginity, is glorified and chosen out before God. ⟦s13⟧ But let us now at length return to the golden and ivory throne treated above, and behold with spiritual eyes in what manner it held the figure of the Lord's body. ⟦s14⟧ *Solomon made,* he says, *a great throne of ivory [n: (III Reg. X)]*. ⟦s15⟧ Truly that throne was great with a wonderful loftiness, because, as the Psalmist says: *Great is the Lord, and exceedingly to be praised, and of his greatness there is no end [n: (Psal. CXLIV)]*. ⟦s16⟧ Moreover we declared a little before for what cause that throne was clothed with most precious gold; [0513C] now it remains that our mind bring forth into the light why the same throne, which bore the figure of the Lord's body, is of ivory. ⟦s17⟧ For as they have said before us who with prudent skill have disputed of the natures of things, ivory is of so wonderful a power and coldness that if a linen cloth be laid upon that same ivory, and upon that same linen cloth a live coal be set above, by its own power the ivory keeps unharmed the cloth which cleaves to it, lest it be able to be utterly consumed or burnt by the live coal set above. ⟦s18⟧ Who therefore, even to one who knows but slightly, does not manifestly grow clear, how this comparison ought to be fitted to the body of Christ? ⟦s19⟧ For as ivory by its natural power keeps the cloth laid upon it unviolated from the burning of fire, so the divinity which in the body of Christ was overshadowed [0513D] kept that same body immune from every blemish of sin. ⟦s20⟧ Wherefore also the blessed apostle Peter, speaking of the Lord Jesus, said: *Who did no sin,* he says, *neither was guile found in his mouth [n: (I Petr. II)]*. ⟦s21⟧ But as to the six steps which are related to have been in the throne, by which one ascended step by step to the seat of the throne, sacred History has taught us with manifest reason that *300* through the six ages of the passing world God the Son promised us in mystical and hidden ways that he would come to his incarnation. ⟦s22⟧ For in the first age, which was from Adam unto Noe, he pledged that he would come when he declared to the woman, saying: *Thy seed shall crush the head of the serpent [n: (Gen. III)]* [var: Vulg. Gen. 3:15 *ipsa conteret caput tuum*, "she shall crush thy head"]. ⟦s23⟧ But in the second age, which was from [0514A] Noe unto Abraham, he showed that he would come, when he said to the sons of Noe: *Increase and multiply [n: (Gen. VIII)]*. ⟦s24⟧ But in the third age, which ran from Abraham unto Moses, he prefigured that he would be present, when he blessed the offspring of that same prophet, saying: *In thy seed shall all nations be blessed [n: (Gen. XXII)]*. ⟦s25⟧ But in the fourth age, which was from Moses unto David, he intimated in the manifold religion of sacrifices that he would be at hand. ⟦s26⟧ But in the fifth age, which was from David unto the nativity of blessed Mary [and] flourished, because he himself is King of kings and priest for ever according to the order of Melchisedech [n: (Psal. CIX)], he declared figuratively in kings and priests that he would come. ⟦s27⟧ The sixth age is that which is now passing, from the birth of the blessed Virgin Mary unto the day [0514B] of judgment, in which the Son of God clemently deigned to come to his incarnation, just as by the sixth step one came to that glorious seat which was in the midst of the golden and ivory throne [n: (III Reg. X)]. ⟦s28⟧ But that it is said that the top of the throne is round in the hinder part is by no means to be passed over slothfully by the prudent reader. ⟦s29⟧ For scarcely anyone is ignorant who abounds in any sense, that whatever is round is also perfect in every part of itself. ⟦s30⟧ For of Christ, whose figure that throne held, holy Church preaches to her sons, saying: *Perfect God, perfect man, subsisting of a rational soul and human flesh*. ⟦s31⟧ But the two hands which in the aforesaid throne hold the seat on this side and on that ought not unfittingly to be understood as the clemency and mercy of our [0514C] Saviour; which wrought this, that for the redemption of the human race Christ should take flesh, and should for a time taste the death which he utterly knew not. ⟦s32⟧ Whence also the prophet David says: *He shall drink of the torrent in the way, therefore shall he lift up the head [n: (Psal. CIX)]*. ⟦s33⟧ These, I say, are the spiritual breasts of the Bridegroom in the Canticle of Canticles, the two hands which hold the seat of the golden and ivory throne in the book of Kings, namely clemency and mercy, which brought it about that God should become man, and should wonderfully lead us back to the joys of paradise which we lost through Adam.
