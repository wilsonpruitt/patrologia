## RECAPITULATION FROM THE FOREGOING ACCORDING TO THE MORAL SENSE.

Although it is exceedingly grave and perilous that an unskilled and ill-mannered man should dispute about the good morals of men, yet we ought in no wise, with drowsy senses, to neglect that we are bidden always and without interval to bless and praise God, as [0533A] the Psalmist says: « I will bless the Lord at all times, his praise shall be ever in my mouth [n: (Psal. XXXIII)]. » For indeed if we wish to wait so long to speak of the good, until we are good; or if we keep silence about perfection so long until we are perfect. And if again we are silent from the praises of God up to the point at which we shall be worthy to praise God, it is plain assuredly that we shall nowhere praise God, since, as must be, we shall in no wise ever in this life be good and perfect and worthy of the praises of God. Let not, therefore, good will fail us, although effectual power be lacking; and so at length we shall be able, God enlarging us, to attain that we may be good and perfect and *311* fit for the praises of God. But now let us examine this saying under the moral interpretation, namely: « Thy name is oil poured out; therefore the maidens [0533B] have loved thee [n: (Cant. I)]. » Blessed is that man, though he be mortal, whose name for the desert of a good life has already obtained so great a prerogative that it can truly be said to him: « Thy name is oil poured out, » therefore the maidens have loved thee. Assuredly such a man is greatly to be sought, and when he has been found, he is to be loved in no middling degree. For he who lives well and religiously in the world, and out of his substance gives large alms for God, and does not do to another what he does not wish to be done to himself, and who in all his works seeks that God be praised, and who so shows himself that he is venerated by all men for his holiness — to him doubtless it can be said: « Thy name is oil poured out, » therefore the maidens, that is, the faithful souls, have loved thee. In like manner he who goes to conversion [0533C] and in the cloister lives well and holily and religiously, and who by the good report of a blameless life animates and draws others, that they may be converted to the Lord, and moreover who by dove-like peace and brotherly love makes himself lovable to the minds of his brethren, so far that he is loved by all with a pure intention of mind — to him without doubt it ought to be said: « Thy name is oil poured out; therefore the maidens, » that is, the souls of the brethren, « have loved thee. » Moreover, as to what we set down above in the first recapitulation, that the Lord charged Moses to tell the children of Israel to bring oil from the olive trees: if we are true Israelites, in whom there is no guile, our spiritual Moses *(Exod. [0533D] XXVII)* likewise charges us — namely the Lord Jesus Christ — that we bring him oil from the olive trees, that is, that we follow and imitate the examples and actions of the holy Fathers, who by the works of mercy are known to have pleased God in former times; but nevertheless, since obscurity in the divine page is not good, nay is exceedingly harmful to the less capable, I judge it worthy to discourse somewhat more widely about the olive trees and about the oil, so far as what we wish to say may answer to a clearer sense. The olive trees, therefore, are our holy Fathers, of whom blessed Paul says: "These are men of mercy, whose righteousnesses have not received forgetfulness before God." These indeed are the olive trees of which the Lord says in the Gospel: « A good tree cannot bring forth evil [0534A] fruits [n: (Luc. VI)]. » [var: Vulg. Luc. VI, 43 *non est arbor bona quae facit fructus malos*; the wording here is that of Matth. VII, 18] The oil of these trees is every work good, loving, modest, chaste, religious and holy. But that by the oil a good work ought to be understood, the Gospel according to Matthew most plainly declares: « The kingdom of heaven is like, he says, to ten virgins, and the rest which follows there [n: (Matth. XXV)]. » [var: Vulg. Matth. XXV, 1 *simile erit regnum caelorum*, future "shall be like"] With which saying Solomon seems well enough to agree, who among his other words speaks thus: « At all times let thy garments be white, and let oil not be lacking from thy head [n: (Eccle. IX)]. » Our garments, therefore, are the members of our body, with which [sic: *cuibus*] our soul is clothed, while it tarries in this little body according to the disposition of God. Which members ought always to be white, since they ought not to lie under any sin. [0534B] For thus says Isaiah the prophet: « A garment, he says, mingled with blood shall be for the burning of fire [n: (Isa. IX)]. » [var: Vulg. Isa. IX, 5 *erit in combustionem et cibus ignis*, "shall be for burning, and fuel for the fire"] And moreover the Apostle says: « Let not sin reign in your mortal body, so that you obey its concupiscences [n: (Rom. VI)]. » And again: « As, he says, you presented your members to serve uncleanness unto iniquity, so now to present your members to serve justice unto sanctification [n: (ibid.)]. » [var: Vulg. Rom. VI, 19 *ita nunc exhibete membra vestra*, imperative "so now present ye your members"] But oil is never lacking from our head when our mind always abounds in loving work and in good desire. And it wishes to do continually more, if it could be done, than it can ever bring to perfection; whence the Psalmist says: « My soul hath coveted to long for thy justifications at all times [n: (Psal. CXVIII)]. » Oil, therefore, [0534C] from the olive trees we offer to God when through works of piety and of justice we strive to please him, by which we do not doubt that our fathers of old pleased him. Moreover, that the oil which was offered to God was of the purest, Moses did not without cause so express, since he foreknew that it would come to our knowledge. For that same prophet could have said [sic: *Potera*] pure or purer oil, but he chose rather to set down "purest," that he might silently intimate to us in what manner we ought to examine our work, whereby we intend to please God himself, who is the highest purity. For oil is pure then, when in its outward showing our work lacks sin. But then it is made purer, when in our speech also [0534D] no sin is repeated. And if our mind, when it girds itself to perform a good work, is able inwardly to shun vain glory, then assuredly it is the purest oil which it will offer to God in the sacrifice of its own heart. And that the same oil is said to have been pounded with a pestle, I judge in no way to be passed over out of negligence. For the pounding of the pestle signifies the tribulations and pressures of this world. What then does the oil beaten with a pestle intimate to us, but that we ought to imitate the actions and examples of those fathers whom we know, according to the Apostle [n: (Hebr. XI)], to have entered into the kingdom of God through many tribulations? But that it is commanded that a lamp burn always before God in the tabernacle of the testimony [n: (Exod. XXVII)], this doubtless signifies that the intention *312* of our heart ought never [0535A] to be withdrawn from the consideration and contemplation of the majesty of God, as the heart of him never withdrew from God who said: « The meditation of my heart is ever in thy sight [n: (Psal. XVIII)]. » For the lamp of thy body, as the Lord says in the Gospel [n: (Marc. VI)], is thine eye. Which eye our holy Fathers have interpreted as the intention of the mind, or the sincere affection of the mind. And by the tabernacle of the testimony our body can not undeservedly be understood, whence blessed Peter says: « I know, brethren, that the laying aside of my tabernacle is swift, according as the Lord Jesus Christ has vouchsafed to reveal to me [n: (II Petr. I)]. » [var: Vulg. II Petr. I, 14 *certus quod velox est depositio tabernaculi mei… significavit mihi*, "being sure… hath signified to me"] In this tabernacle, therefore, that is, in our body, a lamp ought always to burn before the Lord, since a simple and pure [0535B] intention of the mind ought unremittingly to have dominion over our body, and we ought never to suffer anything to have dominion over our mind which we know to be displeasing in the sight of our judge. Or, the lamp shines unremittingly in our tabernacle when the fervour of charity never grows lukewarm in our heart. For thus that lamp of charity shone again in the heart of Paul with the brightness of a wondrous clearness, so that the same Paul said: « The charity of God is poured forth in our hearts by the indwelling Spirit, who is given to us [n: (Rom. V)]. » [var: Vulg. Rom. V, 5 *per Spiritum Sanctum, qui datus est nobis*, "by the Holy Spirit"] Or, the lamp shines without failing of brightness in the tabernacle of the testimony, when our heart, through the infusion of the Holy Spirit, unremittingly thinks upon the things which are of God. As therefore we have examined the chapter in the book of Exodus according to the moral sense, [0535C] so it is fitting that we discourse morally upon that saying taken from Isaiah. « A vineyard, he says, was made for my beloved on a horn, the son of oil. » O how blessed is that soul which, by working truly and lovingly, is made the vineyard of God! But now let us consider what a vineyard is, and of what a vineyard consists, and let us transfer its earthly working into moral and spiritual building-up. A vineyard indeed is seen to consist of vines, and it is called *vitis* because by force it holds itself in the place where it is planted. Whosoever therefore desires to be a vine or a vineyard, it is needful that he in no wise let go the purpose of holiness and religion which he took up for God, that he be not dissolved by prosperity, nor broken by adversities, knowing that the kingdom of heaven suffers violence and the violent bear it away [0535D] [n: (Matth. XI)]. But this too, that the vine, against the custom of other trees, transmits through its pith to the tops of its branches the moisture which it draws from the earth, is by no means to be considered without wonder at a mighty mystery. For other trees take up the moisture from the earth between the bark and the strength of their wood, and pour that same moisture into the little branches proceeding from themselves, that they may be able to bear fruit. What then does this signify, that the vine conceives its moisture in the pith, that is, in its inner part, which afterward it diffuses abundantly through the outer branches? Doubtless the perfect soul of each faithful man, whose body, though it appear publicly before men, yet in the presence of God remains in secret, since he strives to please God more inwardly than men [0536A] or the world outwardly; whence the Psalmist says: « All the glory of the king's daughter is from within [n: (Psal. XLIV)]. » Hence Paul also says: « This is our glory, the testimony of our conscience [n: (II Cor. I)]. » A mystery of this kind can further be considered in the vine, which, against the manner of other trees, carries its sap in the inner pith, wherewith little by little it afterward waters the outer branches that spring from it by natural birth. For holy men pour into religious men, through the showing forth of good work and the preaching of the heavenly word, the charisms of the Holy Spirit which they receive by God's hidden bounty in the secret place of their heart; and after the manner of a good vine, what is wrought in them invisibly is shown forth visibly without. There is moreover another thing which can be said of the vine [0536B] with reasonable authority, and which can be found in the divine sayings by the prudent skill of illustrious clerics. For this tree, namely the vine, is lowly in its stature, and, so long as it lives, adorned with leaves, fragrant with flowers, pleasing to all eyes with its budded fruits. But if it has been cut off, by however much it was before more dear, more precious, more acceptable and more lovable, by so much afterward it stands more vile, more degenerate and more useless. Which indeed the Lord makes plain through the prophet Ezekiel, when he says: « Son of man, what shall be done with the wood of the vine, which is precious above all the other woods of the forests? [n: (Ezech. XV.)] » [var: Vulg. Ezech. XV, 2 *quid fiet ligno vitis ex omnibus lignis nemorum quae sunt inter ligna silvarum*, without *pretiosum*, "precious"] Shall anything of work be made of it, if it has been cut off? Shall a peg be fashioned of it, that any vessel may hang upon it? It is useful for nothing, save [0536C] that it be cast into the fire. And what these things signify is more plainly opened when a discourse is found concerning the children of Israel in what follows. But the children of Israel themselves, so long as they obeyed the commandments of God, and did not depart from his worship, stood fast in their comeliness like a good vine with flower and fruit. But after they began to contradict his precepts, and moreover inflicted the punishment of the cross upon him who is the author of life, they were assigned to eternal fire like a useless and lopped-off vine. In like manner also the Christians who have been imbued with the ecclesiastical sacraments, who have been instructed in faith and work by the doctors of the Church, so long as within the bosom of that same Church they exercise their faith by good works, so long do they flourish, like a fruit-bearing vine in the vineyard of God, with the greenness of faith. But if, [0536D] which God forbid! they slip into some heresy, and, the grace to which they were long ago handed over being abandoned, of their own accord are tainted with depraved doctrines: do not these deservedly, for their *313* iniquity, hand themselves over to avenging flames like a lopped-off and useless vine? To whom beyond doubt that most elegantly agrees which Moses the prophet says in his canticle: « Their vineyard is of the vineyard of Sodom, as far as that place where he says in the person of God: Vengeance is mine, and I will repay them — it is understood, on the day of judgment, which they deserve [n: (Deut. XXXII)]. » But what shall I say of converts and religious men who, as the Lord declares in the Gospel [n: (Luc. VIII)], believe for a time, and in the time of temptation fall away? For I have known not a few men of great religion and of a purpose lofty enough, [0537A] whom would that I had not known, who, so long as it is allowed them in their cloisters to exercise their own will, or to wander and run about through the workshops of the cloister, and through the whole world, and among their kinsfolk, so long are they kept within the enclosures of the monastery under the appearance of religion of some sort, in name only, although, as I said above, with a lost mind and a flighty spirit they stray through the whole world. To whom indeed that well applies which the Lord says through the prophet: « Ephraim is become as a dove seduced, having no heart; strangers have devoured his strength, and he knew it not [n: (Osee. VII)]. » For the strangers are the demons, and the vices that fight against us, and evil thoughts, and carnal concupiscences, which estrange us from God, if by our own sloth they obtain the chief place in us. [0537B] Of these strangers indeed David complains, saying: « Since indeed strangers have risen up against me, and the strong have sought after my soul [n: (Psal. LIII)]. » Solomon too says: « Give not thy honour to strangers [n: (Prov. V)]. » And as though we were asking of Solomon who these strangers were, he straightway added: « and thy years to the cruel one [n: (ibid.)], » namely, the devil, of whom the Lord testifies: « Because he was a murderer from the beginning [n: (Joan. VIII)]. » For he who has been evilly given over to the aforesaid strangers, that is, to the demons and to the vices and concupiscences, while he lives in the race-course of this present life, he beyond doubt after this life shall be delivered into the power of Satan, that is, of this cruel one. For this is that cruel one who in Isaiah [n: (cap. XIX)] is written to persecute men cruelly.
