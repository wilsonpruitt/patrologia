## RECAPITULATION FROM THE FOREGOING ACCORDING TO THE ALLEGORICAL AND MYSTICAL SENSE.

*317* By the help of him who said: « If I be lifted up from the earth, I will draw all things to myself [n: (Joan. XII)], » we have spoken above of that sentence which is in the Song of Songs, namely *draw me after thee.* It remains now that, by the same one's aid, we speak concerning the sevenfold or eightfold work of God, to his praise, and bring to light for what reason each several work agrees with the eight ages of this world; nevertheless [0544B] it is first needful that we distinguish the world by eight ages, and so at length we shall the more rightly assign each several work, one by one, to the several distinctions of the ages. The first age therefore of this world was from Adam unto Noe. The second, from Noe unto Abraham. The third, from Abraham unto Moses. The fourth was from Moses unto David. The fifth, from David unto the coming of Christ. In the sixth age came the Lord Jesus Christ, which age lasted until that time in which persecution ceased in holy Church. The seventh age is that very one which is now being passed, until the day of judgment. The eighth will be the age after the day of judgment is done, which shall lack an end. Truly this is exceedingly marvellous, that the things which God is read to have done in those seven days agree, in an elegant order and [0544C] most wise reason, with those seven or eight aforesaid ages. Let us hasten therefore to the discussing of the work of the first day, and, relying on the most holy inspiration of him who made the first day, let not our mind be afraid to bring to light what of sacrament that work contains in itself, saving the exposition of the holy Fathers in every kind. « In the beginning, says Moses, God made heaven and earth [n: (Gen. I)]. » [var: Vulg. Gen. 1:1 *creavit*, "created" — as the same verse is printed below in this very chunk] O Moses, God's lodger! O secret unsealer of the divine mysteries! Who made known to thee that God in the beginning made heaven and earth, unless that same one who founded all these things to the praise and honour and glory of his name? But in what beginning? In that one assuredly who says in the Gospel: « I am the beginning, who speak to you [n: (Joan. VIII)]. » In him, I say, of whom [0544D] John the evangelist testifies: « In the beginning was the Word, and all things were made by him [n: (Joan. I)]. » In him, I say, of whom the Psalmist says to the Father: « And thou in the beginning, O Lord God the Father, in thy Son didst found the earth, and the heavens are the works of thy hands [n: (Psal. CI)]. » [var: Vulg. Ps. 101:26 *initio tu Domine terram fundasti et opera manuum tuarum sunt caeli*, without *Deus Pater* and without *hoc in tuo Filio*] Consider, O most kindly hearer, and most placid lover of spiritual eloquences, that only two things shine forth in the work of the first day: namely the Creator who founded the creature, and the creature which was made by her God, and which now serves him. Nothing else therefore wilt thou be able to find at all in that work, though thou search that work of the first day for a thousand years. For the creature is there declared to have been made by God the Father through the Son; [0545A] the Holy Spirit over the waters, that he might sanctify them for us unto the grace of his baptism, is likewise proclaimed to be borne there. And as the holy Trinity is shown in Father and Son and Holy Spirit by the effect of the work, so the work itself which was made in a certain manner represents in itself the likeness of the most holy and undivided Trinity. For everything that was created by God, from the moment it was created, had being, lay subject to quantity, and it behoved it to lie under quality. Let us make clear then what we have said, lest perchance we leave the sense of a simple hearer beset with obscurity. It is manifest that whatever is, has being either through itself, or with another, or through another, or in no way. God alone is only through himself, but that which was created by him, so that it may subsist through him who [0545B] created it, must needs be through itself; and to be without another is discerned to be one, which properly pertains to God. To be through another, and not to be able to be without another, is proved to be proper to a creature, which apart from its Creator is not able to be, even if in some manner it seem to be. But that which was created lies subject to quantity, because it is either small, or great, or middling. And a creature is necessarily under the laws of quality, since it is either white, or black, or coloured with a middle colour. Behold, in a few words, God inspiring, our stylus makes plain how reasonably every creature represents in itself the image of the holy undivided Trinity. Moreover, if we shall have searched out still more subtly with inward eyes the very surface of this sacred page, [0545C] we shall find all the laws of those seven arts which according to philosophy are called liberal. For, that we may come to the art of rhetoric, according to the modes of speech which are called flowers, the first sentence, namely: « In the beginning God created heaven and earth, » is discerned to be of one member. But the second sentence, to wit « the earth was void and empty, and darkness was upon the face of the deep, and the Spirit of God was borne over the waters, » they will confess with a clear voice to be of three members, who glory in the skill or the prerogative of that art. Truly this we ought by no means to pass over in silence, that that first sentence which is of one member preaches to us one God; but the second, which is of three members, no less confirms and declares to us that the same God is true, who consists of one substance and [0545D] of three persons. For the confirming of the firmament of our faith, which proceeds from the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, and which is repeated not only in this book, *318* but also in all the divine volumes, let it suffice to have said these things. The blessed Augustine puts forth such an interpretation upon heaven and earth: « God, says he, made in the beginning heaven and earth, » that is, the angelic or heavenly and the earthly creature. We also can, saving the sense of the ecclesiastical rule, refer heaven and earth allegorically to the person of our first father Adam. For he himself, before he sinned, stood as it were heaven, but after sin he heard from God: « Thou art earth, and into earth shalt thou go [n: (Gen. III)]. » [var: Vulg. Gen. 3:19 *pulvis es et in pulverem reverteris*, "dust thou art, and into dust shalt thou return"] And well indeed can Adam before the perpetrating [0546A] of sin be called heaven, because without doubt, as saint Augustine says, had he not run into that wretched guilt, he would have come to the heavenly glory without the taste of death. Adam can further in another manner be named heaven and earth, because he himself had a soul of heavenly nature, and his body drew its origin from the earth. For thus Scripture says: « God made man of the slime of the earth, and breathed into his face the breath of life [n: (Gen. II)]. » And there is besides another reasonable understanding whereby our first parent ought to be called heaven and earth. For of him Eve was made; as it is written: « God sent a deep sleep upon Adam, and took one of his ribs, and built that rib into a woman [n: (ibid.)]. » If therefore by heaven we take Adam, and by earth [0546B] we take Eve, our understanding walks by a straight path along the ecclesiastical rule. Let us say otherwise: Not undeservedly can we interpret heaven and earth in Adam, because of his offspring holy Church was to go forth, which in the elect can be called heaven, and in the reprobate whom she contains, earth. Or else Adam may not unfittingly be called heaven and earth for this cause, that it was to come to pass that from his stock, through the lines of the generations, he should draw his origin who by his blood should join together, by making peace, things heavenly and earthly at once; and should restore, out of the sons of the same Adam, though regenerated by the saving font, the loss which that heavenly court had borne in the fall of Sathael and of his companions. But as to what is said, that the earth was void and empty, it must be so understood, that indeed [0546C] Adam and Eve, after they consented by sinning to the devil rather than to God, remained at once void and empty of the grace of the Holy Spirit and of the remaining virtues wherewith they before shone. With a like sentence also is that to be taken which follows: « Darkness was upon the deep [n: (Gen. I)]. » The deep, as saint Augustine says, is a certain depth which lacks a bottom; which assuredly, the deeper it is, so much the more, ambiguity removed, is it dark. Do they not seem to thee to be dark enough, who thought they could lie hid from the face of God? For thus after the transgression he himself said for himself and for his wife: « I heard, O Lord, thy voice, and I hid myself [n: (Gen. III)]. » Darkness therefore was upon the face of the deep, because the obscurity of sin, and the boldness of self-defence, and the wrapping of lying beset [0546D] the hearts of those who strove to deceive with words him to whom Solomon says: « Thou alone knowest the hearts of the sons of men [n: (II Par. II)]. » But as to that, that the Spirit of God was borne over the waters, it behoves us so to interpret it, that we understand that already from that time the grace of baptism was consecrated by the power of the Holy Spirit, by which baptism the offspring of Adam was to be purified from this transgression. And that also which God said: « Let there be light, and light was made [n: (Gen. I)], » can fittingly enough be applied to Adam himself. For he shone forth as it were a light before the other creatures over which he was set, and therefore by participation of the most wise light which lay hid in him he imposed names upon all things. « And God saw the light, that it was good [n: (ibid.)] » [0547A] It would surely have pleased God if Adam had remained in that same state in which he had been founded by him. But because he himself sinned, there was a great division between that state which he had before the fall, and that in which he remained after the tasting of sin; and this is the division « of the light from the darkness. And he called the light day, and the darkness night [n: (ibid.)], » which is to say: Before the transgression Adam stood just, that is, light and day; after the guilt of transgression he remained night and darkness, that is, a sinner. « And there was evening and morning, one day [n: (ibid.)]. » If by the morning we would understand the soul of Adam, which was of heavenly nature, and by his body the evening, such a solution of this sentence may be had: « Evening and morning were made one day, » [0548A] that is, of soul and body one man was made. Or else, if by the morning, which pertains to light, we would interpret that glorious estate in which God created Adam, and if by the evening, which regards the darkness, we interpret the unhappy guilt which Adam ran into, we can say that indeed Adam, whom God founded just, although he made himself a sinner, yet remained the very same, save that he lacked not the blemish of sin. Our intention therefore tends to this: that, by the furnishing of him who made the first day, we may refer that which was done in that first day, by allegory, to the things that were done in the first age of the world.

[0547] *Here ends the fourth Tome upon the Song of Songs.*
