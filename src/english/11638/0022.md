## RECAPITULATION FROM THE FOREGOING ACCORDING TO THE MYSTICAL AND ALLEGORICAL SENSE UPON THAT SENTENCE: « THE KING HATH BROUGHT ME INTO HIS STOREROOMS. »

[0550C]The men who are perfumers, who make up fragrant spices out of divers kinds, and who rather often handle balsams or the other things of that sort that give out aroma: they indeed first enjoy, as it were for the wage of their labour, the wondrous odour of those same spices or of the balsam; then they that come after and draw nearer are steeped in the most pleasing fragrance and enjoy it. Further, this stands most certainly, that the more the aforesaid perfumers break their spices small, or grind them minutely, or pour out the balsams and shift them from vessel to vessel, so much the more beyond doubt do they bring a fresher odour to their own nostrils, and offer a more pleasing one to them that stand at a distance. What then are these spices or precious balsams, if not the [0550D] most sacred eloquences of the divine books? Whence the Bride in the Song of Songs says: « My beloved is gone down to the little bed of spices, that he may gather the lilies [n: (Cant. VI)]. » What then is the little bed of spices, if not the catholic Church, in which is contained a mighty abundance of the divine books? But the perfumers I judge can be understood as none other, more rightly, than the rulers and preachers of the Church of God, who after the manner of spices grind and divide into small parts, by expounding, the Scriptures divinely inspired, and, as balsams when they are poured from vessel to vessel breathe out a most sweet odour, so these bring forth reasonable and pleasing senses, while they pass from history to allegory, and from allegory to morality. *321* If therefore, as we have said, the spices are the sacred eloquences, and if they give out so much the more odour the more [0551A] they shall have been ground, let us see what odour this sentence gives out, or what of fragrance it contains in itself. *The king hath brought me into his storerooms.* Indeed the storerooms of this king, according to allegory, are all the books generally of the divine law, into which we are then spiritually and mystically brought in by the Lord, when we are instructed and taught by him to understand the hidden and mystical senses. Thus finally were those blessed disciples brought in by the Lord Jesus within these storerooms, of whom it is said in the Gospel: « Then he opened their understanding, that they might understand the Scriptures [n: (Luc. XXIV)]. » A certain secret storeroom indeed, abounding in all divine free gifts and spiritual riches, is the Gospel. Whence the Bride cries out exulting in the Song of Songs: « The king hath brought me into the wine [0551B] cellar, and hath ordered charity in me [n: (Cant. II)]. » For the Gospel by teaching declares how charity ought to be had and possessed among Christians: namely that principally, before all creatures, and above all creatures, and before all things, God be loved; then in the second place, that the neighbour be loved with the sincere affection of charity. For this is the most blessed and most holy ordering of pure charity, which every one of the faithful can find in the storeroom of Christ's Gospel. And there are besides other storerooms of this great and most rich king: namely the book of Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, the book of Numbers and the book of Deuteronomy, Isaias, Jeremias, Ezechiel, Daniel, and the twelve prophets, and very many other books of the divine law — these are the storerooms of Christ; but what shall I say of the chief and [0551C] excellent one among the prophets, David, who makes clearer than light, in the first part of his book, whose storeroom it is, when he says: « Blessed is the man who hath not gone in the counsel of the ungodly, and hath not stood in the way of sinners, and hath not sat in the chair of pestilence? » [n: (Psal. I.)] We do not therefore judge it now to be the time in which we should run through all the books one by one, and expound how each several book is a storeroom of this highest king, since for the accomplishing of that we by no means possess fit senses, and we know that our holy Fathers of old accomplished this more fully. Nevertheless, lest anyone suppose that we wish to lie hid under the silence of a sluggish body, let us enter that storeroom of which we spoke above, and let us bring forth into the open what it contains hidden in itself [0551D] or what it gives out for odour, by the bounty of him whose the storeroom is. « Blessed is the man who hath not gone in the counsel of the ungodly, etc. » In the divine page, O searcher of the spiritual law, three things lie subject to thy inquiry, namely history, allegory, and morality, which last always walks about and inseparably cleaves to our condition. Truly in the nature of things these three are, and are not easily found, unless they be sought out rather carefully by a prudent reader. Let therefore the historical understanding of this verse, namely, « Blessed is the man, » be referred to the person of the earthly king David; let the allegory be ascribed to the Lord Jesus Christ; let the morality be assigned to the correcting of our own acts. But how can we refer historically to David this verse, namely, « Blessed is the man, » etc., whom we know to have sinned in thought, in speech and in [0552A] work? For it is certain that David sinned by thinking, when, looking incautiously from his couch, he saw Bethsabee the wife of Urias the Hethite washing, and coveted her. But because he in no wise repressed within himself the unlawful thought, he came down as far as the guilt of speech, when through go-betweens he inquired who that woman might be. And who knows not that he fell in work by a wretched lapse, when he committed adultery with the same Bethsabee [n: (II Reg. II)], and afterwards, lest the adultery should lie open to all, delivered her husband to be slain by the swords of the sons of Ammon? With what blessedness then shall David be endowed, who is proved to be guilty in thought, in speech and in work? And who will dare to call David a blessed man, who committed adultery so lightly, and to the same adultery, [0552B] which is worse, joined homicide? But according to the word of the apostle Paul [n: (I Cor. VI)] he was washed, he was justified, he was sanctified in the name of our Lord and in the Spirit of our God. He was justified, I say, by confession, when he said to Nathan the prophet: « I have sinned against the Lord, pray for me; » and Nathan the prophet to him: « Fear not, said he, thou shalt by no means die the death, because the Lord hath taken away thy sin from thee [n: (II Reg. XII)]. Behold how swiftly this man was washed by confession, was justified, was sanctified. Moreover let us hear what the Lord says of him: « I have found, said he, David my servant, the son of Jesse, who doeth all my wills [n: (Act. XIII)]. » [var: Vulg. Act. 13:22 *inveni David filium Jesse virum secundum cor meum qui faciet omnes voluntates meas*, "I have found David the son of Jesse, a man according to my own heart, who shall do all my wills"] And in another place: « I have found David my servant, with my holy oil I have anointed him [n: (Psal. LXXXVIII)]. » [0552C] My hand, says he, shall help him [n: (ibid.)] » , and the rest which follows in the same psalm, which the plain wording proves to have been said to the praise of that same David. Who then would dare to condemn him whom God with words of this sort washes, justifies and sanctifies? But surely let us seek whether it may perchance occur to us that somewhere David did not go in the counsel of the ungodly, and did not stand in the way of sinners, and did not sit in the chair of iniquity. For neither does it befit the majesty of God to justify the ungodly, as neither does this, to condemn the just. By no means indeed would God justify or praise the just David, unless some good work were found in him which might please God himself. Known indeed is the history of the book of Kings, which relates that at a certain time Saul, that most impious king, was unjustly persecuting David, [0552D] and it fell out on a certain day that the same *322* Saul entered a certain cave for the requirements of nature, and behold, in that same cave David with his companions, fleeing from the face of that same Saul, lay hid. His companions therefore said to him: « Behold the day of which the Lord spoke to thee, saying: I will deliver thy enemy to thee, that thou mayest do to him whatsoever thou wilt. Rise up therefore and kill him. And David broke the words of the men who were with him, and did not permit that they should kill him, but said: The Lord liveth, that unless the Lord shall strike him, or his day shall come that he die, or going down into battle he shall perish, I will not put forth my hand upon the anointed of the Lord [n: (I Reg. XXIV)]. » Behold how David went not in the counsel of the ungodly. [0553A] For although the law said: « Thou shalt love thy friend, and thou shalt hold thy enemy in hatred [n: (Levit. XIX)]; » yet he, despising the institutes of the ancient law, was already making his passage to the sacraments of the new law. For in the new law it is said: « Love your enemies, do good to them that hate you [n: (Matth. V)]. » [var: Vulg. Levit. 19:18 *diliges amicum tuum sicut temet ipsum*, "thou shalt love thy friend as thyself"; the clause *et odio habebis inimicum tuum* stands at Matth. 5:43, as the saying of them of old] But after we have shown, God helping, how David went not in the counsel of the ungodly, it remains that, by his aid, we demonstrate in what manner he stood not in the way of sinners. For in the same book of Kings it is likewise found that on another occasion that same most shameful king Saul, at the goading of envy, was persecuting holy David. And it came to pass, while the sun drew toward its setting, and the evening coming on was now willing to set a term to the day, that Saul in the place where he was, his [0553B] tents and pavilions being spread, was disposing himself to rest. And when quiet slumber held the limbs of all, David, having marked the place in which Saul was sleeping, said to his companions: « Who will go down with me to Saul into the camp? » [n: (I Reg. XXVI.)] And there went down with him some of his companions. But when he had come to the place in which Saul was sleeping, one of those who were with him said: « Behold, the Lord hath given thy enemy into thy hand. Now therefore let me go, and I will pierce him with the spear once into the earth, and there will be no need of a second time. And David said: Do him no evil at all, nor stretch out thy hand upon him. For who, putting forth his hand upon the anointed of the Lord, shall be innocent? » [n: (Ibid.)] Let the Lord know this, that unless the Lord himself shall strike him, or his day shall come [0553C] that he die, or going down into battle he shall perish, « I will not put forth my hand upon the anointed of the Lord [n: (ibid.)]. » Behold how David stood not in the way of sinners, because he refused to hear the counsel of those who were persuading him to homicide. Certain arguments therefore having been shown from the works of holy David, whereby we have proved him by manifest authority to have at one time neither gone in the counsel of the ungodly nor stood in the way of sinners, it lies upon us still with watchful mind to show that he sat not in the chair of pestilence. Now after Saul ended this temporal life with the sons of Israel under the lot of battle on Mount Gelboe [n: (I Reg. XXXI)], it is written that the house of Juda in Hebron anointed David to itself as king, and set him after the royal manner on the royal chair. [0553D] Behold, says the history, certain men came and announced to David that the men of Jabes Galaad had honourably buried Saul his enemy, and he sent to them, saying: « Blessed be ye of the Lord, who have done this mercy with Saul your lord. But I also will render you thanks for the work which ye have done in him [n: (II Reg. II)]. » Moreover in what follows of the book of Kings it is said that David took the bones of Saul and of Jonathas, and buried them very honourably in the land of Benjamin [n: (II Reg. XXI)]. Truly, with what words could we worthily set forth how David wept when he heard of the death of that same enemy of his Saul, at whose destruction surely thou oughtest to have rejoiced with exulting spirits, as it is written: « David made a lamentation of this sort [0554A] over Saul, and over Jonathas his son: Ye daughters of Israel, weep over Saul, who furnished ornaments of gold for your attire, and clothed you in delights. O Saul and Jonathas, how lovely and comely were ye in your life! ye were swifter than eagles, and stronger than lions. Ye mountains of Gelboe, let neither dew nor rain come upon you, nor let there be fields of firstfruits in you, because there was cast away the shield of the mighty, the shield of Saul, as though he had not been anointed with oil. The arrow of Jonathas never went back, and the sword of Saul returned not empty. O how are the strong and the noble and the excellent men fallen, and the weapons of war perished! » [n: (II Reg. I.)] And this also, that David commanded him to be slain who announced to him, saying: « I have slain Saul on Mount Gelboe, » agrees exceedingly [0554B] with the works of tenderness and mercy. Behold, as we have said, David sat not in the chair of pestilence and of iniquity, who for the death of his enemy not only poured out lamentable wailings, but even stirred up others with most loving words to lament with him. Above therefore we premised that the history of that verse, namely « Blessed is the man, » ought to be referred to David, the allegory to Christ, the morality to each one of us. Nevertheless the allegory of that verse can easily be fitted to the Lord Jesus, since indeed he who did no sin, he assuredly went not in the counsel of the ungodly, and stood not in the way of sinners, and in no wise sat down in the chair of pestilence and of iniquity. Let us consider then with subtle investigation how the morality of this verse [0554C] pertains to each one of us, and how in our inward action it besets us on every side. Three kinds indeed of the lapses of human frailty, O thou lover or hearer of the divine word, it behoves thee to consider in this verse, namely « Blessed is the man, » and do ye weigh *323* with diligent regard by what ways our nature lies subject to sin. For as in this verse these three, namely walking, standing, sitting, are denoted, so every sin to which we are liable is perpetrated by us in three manners. But surely what these three manners are we know rather by daily experience, which we cannot speak of without heavy sorrow, than by the discourse of any sentence pointing it out. For in thought, or in speech, or in work, it is hard that the human condition should not slip. For it seems as it were to walk in the counsel of the ungodly [0554D] when our mind, given over to the vain thoughts of malign spirits, strays hither and thither; and it stands in the way of sinners when it speaks those things which are contrary to God. But it sits in the chair of pestilence when, the fear of God being cast away backward, it works evil.
