## RECAPITULATION ACCORDING TO THE ANAGOGE AND THE MORALITY UPON THAT SENTENCE: « THE KING HATH BROUGHT ME INTO HIS STOREROOMS. »

All the arts of human knowledge, in the midst of the teaching — that is, when they are taught or practised — offer to their hearers certain delightful refuges outside themselves, as for example are the polishers, or the hewers of wood [0555A], or those who are busied in the stone-quarries, or goldsmiths or builders, or of any other art whatsoever that is discerned to pertain to human judgment. For all these have within their working certain empty consolations. But while the polishers polish marbles, they talk, they tell tales, they laugh, they make merry; and sometimes even certain things which are proved to pertain nothing at all to that same art. But our art, namely that of spiritual letters, in the learning or in the teaching wills to have the whole man intent upon it. For either a man shall offer his whole self intent upon our art, or he shall by no means come to a clear mastery of the skill of that same art. For who ever, with a halved heart, or with the senses turned away, or with a wandering and unstable mind, was able to be a good clerk [0555B]? It is needful therefore that not only the things which are of this world, but even himself for the greatest part, if it be lawful to say so, he hand over to oblivion, who longs perfectly to attain those things which in the divine books lie subject to human intention. Earthly pleasures therefore being lulled to sleep, and depraved works despised, which are wont very often to plunge incautious men into the depth of the gulf, let us come with no halved heart, but with a whole mind and sincere affection to the Bridegroom, and let us beseech him with humble words together with his most dear Bride, that he may deign to bring us in, devoted to him, into his storerooms. Above therefore I confess I said that the storerooms of this Bridegroom and king were, allegorically, all the books of the divine law. But now I say likewise that of the same Bridegroom and king the storerooms are, morally, [0555C] all the virtues that can be found scattered here and there throughout the sacred eloquences. Nevertheless, because we desire to begin a discourse concerning the virtues, it follows that we direct our stylus to the head or beginning of all the virtues. Now the head or beginning from which all the virtues go forth is without doubt discerned to be charity. Whence the blessed Gregory says: « As from one stock of a tree many branches go forth, so from one charity many virtues are begotten, nor has the branch of good work anything of greenness if it has not abided in the root of charity. » Let charity itself therefore be itself the opening of our narration, since it is believed to be the storeroom of this great Bridegroom and king. But with what presumption shall we dare to speak of charity [0555D], who do not perfectly love that same charity, as would be fitting, nor possess it; and appear in no wise charitable with your brethren? Nevertheless this presumption leaps forth out of the love of charity, which stirs us to this, that we should willingly dispute of the works of charity. « God, says John, is charity [n: (I Joan. IV)]. » This virtue could not be compared to a greater thing than that it should be made equal to the omnipotent Creator. For although it is lawful to say, God is obedience, humility, chastity, peace, patience, and so of the other virtues; yet of none is it so specially said, as of charity, God is that or that virtue. God therefore is charity. Deservedly then is God said to be charity, and justly is God declared to have charity, because when he was alone in his glory, [0556A] in his divinity, in his majesty, he willed not alone to hold the kingdom of heavenly beatitude, but sought companions to possess with him the happy and immortal joys of everlasting delight, namely angels and men, whom he created to the praise and glory of his name. Of whose inestimable charity still, incomparable tenderness, unspeakable mercy, the blessed Augustine speaks thus, saying: « O inestimable love of charity which thou hast had! that thou mightest redeem a servant, thou didst deliver up a Son. » For he was considering with what loving charity, and with what charitable love, God almighty grieved with man wretchedly fallen, for whose raising up he made his own Son, consubstantial and coeternal with himself and with the Holy Spirit, to put on the servile girding of flesh, and to endure the hangings of death. But the prophet also [0556B] [n: (Isa. LXIII)] saw the Lord Jesus *324* in the spirit of prophecy clothed with the cloak of zeal and treading alone in the winepress, and that of the Gentiles there was not any man with him. But let this lie hid from no one, that what it is to be clothed with the servile girding of flesh, this it is to be wrapped in the cloak of zeal; and what betokens the suffering of the losses of an undeserved slaying, this assuredly signifies to tread alone in the winepress. For greatly did the Lord Jesus Christ love us, who for us perished in the winepress, that is, on the cross, pouring out his blood. Whence also Isaias says: « But he was wounded for our iniquities, he was bruised for our wickednesses, and by his death we are saved [n: (Isa. LIII)]. » [var: Vulg. Isa. 53:5 *et livore ejus sanati sumus*, "and by his bruise we are healed"] Hence indeed John says: « Who hath loved us, says he, and washed us with his blood [n: (Apoc. I)]. » Moreover this [0556C] incomparable charity of the Lord Jesus Christ, this inestimable love, this inexplicable compassion, was well indeed of old prefigured in holy David. For the history of the book of Kings relates of him that at a certain time Absalom his son, greedy of his father's kingdom, was cruelly persecuting him, and, unless difficulty had stood in the way, was contriving to bring an undeserved death upon him [n: (II Reg. XVIII)]. But when, according to God's disposition, that same death was turned back upon him who brought it, and a most dense oak held Absalom hanging between heaven and earth (for neither was he worthy — I will not say that he should look upon heaven as he died, but neither indeed was he such as the earth should worthily bear up; seeing that against all the laws of nature he strove to bring the perils of death [0556D] upon a father's sweetness) — David himself, even as a most loving father, hearing of the slaying of his son and of his persecutor, if indeed he can be called a son who stood forth a most abominable persecutor, he himself, I say, David, poured out tears inconsolably. For thus it is written of him: « Now king David, walking with covered head, mourned his son, saying: Absalom my son, my son Absalom, who will grant me that I may die for thee, my son Absalom? » [n: (Ibid.)] Behold how David had been brought into the storerooms of charity, who wept with irremediable tears for the death of his persecutor. But someone can say: Thou doest violence to Holy Scripture; whence therefore dost thou know that that mourning which David had over the destruction of his son stood forth as a figure of the passion and death which the Lord Jesus Christ endured for the salvation [0557A] of the human race? Hear the apostle Paul [n: (I Cor. X)], not me, saying that all things happened to our fathers in figure, and were written for our correction. If therefore David stood forth as a figure, without doubt in the Lord Jesus Christ the truth abided undefiled. Nevertheless let us search out clearly whether David's lamentation agrees with the passion and death of Christ, and this being searched out, there will be left to our objector nothing which he may justly object against us. For in the Gospel the Lord Jesus Christ is read to have wept in two places, and afterwards to have endured death for us: « For seeing the city Jerusalem, he wept over it, saying: If thou also hadst known [n: (Luc. XIX)], » — there is understood, the things that are to come upon thee, thou wouldst weep; which, because thou now knowest not, for a little space of time thou art [0557B] dissolved in unfitting gladness and rejoicest. In a like manner also the Lord Jesus Christ, as being true man, deigned to weep at the burial of Lazarus [n: (Joan. XI)]. Of which weeping assuredly saint Augustine says that he wept not the dead man, whom he himself raised, but the death which the human race acquired for itself by sinning. For he was considering in how great glory the human race had been founded by its Creator, and to how great misery it had come by disobeying. But after that twofold weeping, the Lord Jesus went forward to pay the undue debt of death, because he had for this been sent by the Father into the world, that by dying he might redeem the human race. Let us see then how the wailing of David fittingly agrees with the twofold weeping and the single death of our Lord Jesus Christ. « King, says he, David [0557C] walking with covered head [n: (II Reg. XIX)]. » But what do we judge can be signified by David walking with covered head, if not that the assuming divinity lay hid in the assumed humanity? For the head of Christ, according to the apostle Paul » is God [n: (I Cor. XI)]. And as it were assuredly David walked with covered head, when God lay hid in the man he had assumed. Does it not seem to thee that the Lord Jesus walked as it were with covered head, when human opinion reckoned him to be either Elias, or Jeremias, or one of the ancient prophets? But Peter the apostle uncovered the head of the Lord Jesus, when he said to him with a truth-speaking voice: « Thou art Christ the Son of the living God [n: (Matth. XVI)]. » When therefore the Lord Jesus by the power of the divinity [0557D] wrought miracles, then David walked with covered head, because God lying hid in the man appeared by manifest tokens. But when, passing through the field of this world, he suffered and did for us only human things, then David, as it were walking with covered head, mourned his son, saying: « Absalom my son, my son Absalom [n: (II Reg. XVIII)]. » For this cause finally did the Lord Jesus endure the injuries of our nature, that he might save the human race which is designated by Absalom, which he himself created to the praise and glory of his name. Whence Absalom is well interpreted *the father's peace,* or *the devil,* because that ancient enemy gloried that our race had laid itself low through disobedience, and because the Lord Jesus Christ, through the flesh which he assumed of the human race, reconciled and [0558A] made peace again for that same human race with his Father. [sic: *repacicavit*] But that David, walking and weeping, tearfully doubles the name of his son, saying: « Absalom my son, my son Absalom, » this surely signifies *325* that the Lord Jesus twice poured out tears in the Gospel, and truly underwent death for the salvation of the human race. By the twofold and tearful repetition of that name may be understood the compassion and death of our Saviour, which he bore for the salvation of our soul together with our body; because whatever perished in our first parent, the second parent took up wholly and saved. For by his death he redeemed the human race. Let us be lying, and let us be convicted of falsehood as false interpreters of the divine law, if the history that follows does not indicate this very thing which we say, [0558B] when it proclaims with manifest voice, saying: « Who will grant me that I may die for thee, my son Absalom? » [n: (Ibid.)] If therefore, as we premised above, by David we understand our Lord Jesus Christ, and by Absalom we take the human race, it stands for certain that the death which David desired for the destruction of his son insinuates to us the death which the Lord Jesus endured for the redemption of the human race. I beseech thee, O hearer of this lesson, that thou spare me, and be not indignant against me for this, that I have said that by Absalom, metaphorically, the human race ought to be understood, and by David also, that most loving and charitable king, our Lord Jesus Christ. For this usage is very often found in the divine letters, namely that one person is taken under an [0558C] allegorical sense for various understandings. Thus finally the blessed Gregory, metaphorically, understands by Saul, that most impious king, our Lord Jesus Christ; but by the mountains of Gelboe, on which the same was slain, the Jewish people. But behold, while we have discussed the charity of David and of Christ figuratively according to the capacity of our sense, we have tarried a little too long in the storerooms of charity. But let us contemplate still more subtly whether any of the ancient fathers dwelt in these storerooms of charity, besides David alone, who mercifully prayed for his persecutors, namely Absalom and Saul. O Moses, thou lodger, tell us what thou didst, and how, set in persecution, thou didst enter the storeroom of charity. For at a certain time the sons of Israel made a golden calf [0558D] to be adored, by which crime they provoked to wrath the God of majesty, who had bestowed on them so many benefits. Which misdeed being discovered, Moses, who was the meekest above all men that dwelt on the earth, said to the Lord: « I beseech thee, O Lord, this people hath sinned a very great sin, and they have made to themselves gods of gold; either forgive them this trespass, or if thou dost not, blot me out of thy book which thou hast written. To whom the Lord answered: He that shall have sinned against me, him will I blot out of my book. But go thou, and lead forth this people [n: (Exod. XXXII)]. » And in what follows it is written that the people wished to stone Moses. And the same Moses said to the Lord: « I beseech thee, O Lord God, forgive the sin of this people according to the multitude [0559A] of thy mercy. And the Lord said to Moses: I have forgiven according to thy word [n: (Num. XIV)]. » [var: Vulg. Num. 14:19 *secundum magnitudinem misericordiae tuae*, "according to the greatness of thy mercy"] Behold in what manner he had been brought into the storerooms of charity; behold how holy Moses abounded in the bowels of charity, who with so great affection besought the majesty of God for idolaters and for his persecutors, that he even asked to be blotted out of the book of eternal life, in which he was already held enrolled, if this offence were not remitted them by God. But behold, while I make discourse of the charity of holy Moses, I cannot hand over to oblivion the charity of David. For the second book of the history of Kings relates that once God was exceedingly angry with the people of Israel, over whom David was set: and he sent his angel, and slew of the people of the Jews as many as seventy thousand men. But when [0559B] David had seen the angel of the Lord striking the people, moved with tenderness he said to the Lord: « I am he who sinned: I have done wickedly; these who are sheep, what have they done? let thy hand, I pray, be turned against me, and against the house of my father [n: (II Reg. XXIV)]. » God therefore, seeing the charity of David which he had, who longed to die for the people committed to him, said to the striking angel: « It is enough now [n: (ibid.)]. » And the raging plague was restrained from Israel, and the Lord was made favourable again to his land. Moreover Samuel the prophet was of old cast out as it were reprobate by that same people from his principality, and yet that same people entreated that same prophet whom they were driving from his seat, that he would pray the Lord for them. What he said, or what he answered, or what he spoke to his enemies, [0559C] let those hear who scarcely, or perhaps never, pray with a sincere heart for their friends. « Far be this sin from me in the Lord, that I should cease to pray for you, and I will show you the good way by which you ought to walk [n: (I Reg. XII)]. » [var: Vulg. I Reg. 12:23 *absit a me hoc peccatum in Dominum … et docebo vos viam bonam et rectam*, "against the Lord … and I will teach you the good and right way"] Likewise the same prophet is found in the same book to have mourned for Saul, that most impious king, on whose account he himself had also been cast out from his principality. Wherefore the Lord also said to him: « How long dost thou mourn Saul, when I have cast him away? » [n: (I Reg. XVI.)] Behold how these three men from that time already then observed that which the Lord was to say: « Love your enemies, do good to them that hate you [n: (Matth. V)]. » For no man has that greater charity, than that he should pray for his persecutors [0559D] and deliver up his own soul unto death for them [n: (Joan. XV)]. Scarcely at last shall we be able to come forth out of the storerooms of charity, so many and so great works and examples of that same charity meet us. There are still other storerooms also of this great and illustrious Bridegroom and glorious King, namely all the virtues, as we have already premised, which are found in the books of the divine law. Of which *326* we shall here take care to express some by name, lest we seem to have passed them over untouched through negligence. Charity will always be the principal and the first, which humility shall follow, faith, hope, thrift, sobriety, virginity, continence, chastity, mercy, obedience, peace, patience, long-suffering, perseverance, penitence for sins, and pure confession. The keeping of God's commandments out of true love [0560A], goodness, concord, kindness, meekness, brotherly affection, spiritual fellowship in God, the bestowing of alms, a reasonable ordering of manners, contempt of self, despising of the world, casting away of worldly glory, renunciation of one's own will, spiritual love, poverty, fastings, watchings, prayers, tears, mortification of the flesh, assiduous meditation in the law of God, our soul's ever untiring contemplation of heavenly things, joy, exultation, jubilation: not in earthly things, but in the Holy Spirit, temperance in prosperity, fortitude among adversaries. Behold, God breathing upon me, I have composed in this catalogue forty-two virtues, in which I have prepared without obstacle the way as far as heaven for those willing to ascend. For by these virtues, if anyone shall have willed step by step [0560B] to climb, he will be able without doubt to penetrate as far as the summits of heaven. Lastly, this is to be noted, that the principal virtue in this catalogue is charity itself, and the last is unfailing fortitude itself, since indeed, as Solomon says in the Song of Songs: « Charity, or love, is strong as death [n: (Cant. VIII)]. » And charity can by no means be kept without spiritual fortitude, nor fortitude without true charity. If therefore we truly believe that both we and our discourses are in the hand of God, then assuredly it is believed to have been done now beside the purpose, seeing that forty-two virtues are found set in the series of this catalogue. For forty mansions and two there were, through which the sons of Israel, going forth out of Egypt, came to the land of promise. [0560C] Further, the Lord Jesus Christ, when at the command of God the Father he came down from heaven to the lands for the salvation of the human race, came through forty-two generations as far as the womb of the blessed and perpetual and ever Virgin Mary. O if there were one who were a scribe learned in the kingdom of God! O if there were one who could bring forth new things and old out of the treasure of his heart! O, I say, if there were one so instructed in the divine law that he knew worthily and elegantly to fit and assign single things to single ones — namely how the single virtues which we have already tasted beforehand agree in a reasonable order with the single generations and mansions. And again, how the single generations accord with the single virtues and mansions; and yet again, how in order the single mansions may be fitted to the single virtues and generations. [0560D] For truly who is so great and such a one as to dare to say: « I opened my mouth, and drew in the Holy Spirit [n: (Psal. CXVIII)], » or to whom God may speak in the secret of his heart, and say: « Open thy mouth, and I will fill it? » [n: (Psal. LXXX.)] [var: Vulg. Ps. 80:11 *dilata os tuum*, "open wide thy mouth"] He assuredly would be able to explain or unravel the profundity of such great sacraments, which lies hid within the compass of these virtues, of the generations and of the mansions together. But, although that work exceed the poverty of our little wit, yet trusting in him who says to his disciples: « For it is not you that speak, but the Spirit of your Father that speaketh in you [n: (Matth. X)], » we shall attempt to taste beforehand only the beginnings of these virtues and generations and mansions, and under that understanding [0561A] which we shall bring forth we shall leave to our hearers the things that remain to be understood. For the first mansion of the sons of Israel is called Ramasse, but in the opening of the generation of Jesus Christ the patriarch Abraham is set; and in the catalogue of the virtues which we have composed charity is put first. Abraham is interpreted *father of many nations,* Ramasse *the stirring of the moth,* and God is charity according to John the apostle [n: (I Joan. IV)]. What therefore do we understand by the many nations of which Abraham is said to be the father, if not a vast multitude of divers virtues? And what ought we to take by the stirring of the moth, if not the insolent infestation of sins and of vices? God therefore, who is charity, himself gives to whom he wills by his grace that he may be able to be a father of many virtues, and himself [0561B] drives out and roots up and removes from him on whom he has mercy all the stirrings and infestations, and the insolences of all vices and sins. Under this sense therefore, and under a better, if God shall have revealed it to anyone, it behoves us to understand the second mansion of the sons of Israel and the second generation of Jesus Christ and the second virtue of our catalogue. For thus it is written: « The sons of Israel set forth from Ramesse to Sochoth, which place is interpreted *tabernacles [n: (Exod. XII)]*. » In the series also of the generation of our Lord Jesus Christ, [0562A] Isaac is discerned to have been the son of Abraham, and in the catalogue mentioned above humility follows next in order after charity: a very wondrous thing, and worthy for ever of admirable and manifold praise! Behold, it is now pleasing enough to consider how the tabernacles agree with the generation and the generation with the virtue. For Isaac, as saint Paul says [n: (Hebr. XI)], dwelt in tabernacles with the coheirs of the heavenly kingdom, and in him shone forth assuredly the tokens of an incomparable humility. Who indeed could worthily explain how admirable a humility Isaac had [n: (Gen. XXII)], who in no wise contradicted his father when he wished to immolate him to God? Moreover the same Isaac is interpreted *laughter* or *joy,* because the voice *327* of exultation and of salvation is in the tabernacles of the just [n: (Psal. CVII)]. Further, true [0562B] salvation is not gained save in holy humility, since, as Holy Scripture says, « Only the humble in spirit shall be saved [n: (Psal. XXXIII)]. » [var: Vulg. Ps. 33:19 *humiles spiritu salvabit*, "he will save the humble in spirit"] If therefore anyone knew well how to run through, by expounding, the remaining mansions, generations and virtues, I confess the truth of the matter, the greatest part of time would fail him rather than the abundance of matter for speaking. We shall therefore offer our hearers matter for thinking, because if we expounded all things they themselves would remain numb with weariness and idle.
