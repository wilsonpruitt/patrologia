## RECAPITULATION FROM THE FOREGOING, IN WHAT MANNER THIS SENTENCE OUGHT TO BE REFERRED TO THE PERSON OF THE FATHER, NAMELY, « SHOW ME, » ETC. (cont.)

Therefore, the eternal mountains being shown, the waterings from above [0580D] being made plain, our mind, breathed upon by the dew of the Holy Spirit, will now endeavor to elucidate what that happy and blessed earth is which is satisfied from the fruit of God's works. Assuredly, being about to speak of earth, let us not withdraw far from ourselves, because we ourselves are earth. For thus the Lord says to Adam: « Earth thou art, and into earth thou shalt go [n: (Gen. III)]. » [var: Vulg. Gen. III, 19, *pulvis es, et in pulverem reverteris*, "dust thou art, and into dust thou shalt return"] Let us beware lastly lest perchance — which God forbid! — we be that earth which was granted by the Lord to the serpent for food. For the book of Genesis [n: (ibid.)] says that God struck the devil in the serpent with this curse, namely that he should eat earth all the days of his life, for that he deceived by his guile the man who had been created by God himself of an upright [0581A] and simple nature. Which Scripture also attests, saying: "God made man simple and upright [n: (Eccle. VII)]"; simple, that he should never do evil, and upright, that he should never withdraw from God himself. [var: Vulg. Eccle. VII, 30, *quod fecerit Deus hominem rectum*, without *simplicem*] But because our first parent, led on by an empty hope, thought that he could obey both God and the devil, he incurred duplicity in place of simplicity; and because the rectitude of mind which he had received from God, and after which he ought to have pleased him alone, *338* he twisted — being softened by the blandishments of the woman — toward the knowledge of evil, it was brought about according to God's dispensation that he lost the state of eternal rectitude, and since, when he could stand, he would not stand, now indeed, when he would stand, he cannot stand. But now let us return to the earth of which we had begun to speak a little before. The earth which was given [0581B] to the devil for food was the Jews, who desired to kill the Lord Jesus. To whom the Lord himself also says: « You are of your father the devil, and the desires of your father you will to accomplish. He was a murderer from the beginning, in the truth he stood not [n: (Joan. VIII)]; » as if he should say: You are the earth of the devil, therefore you desire to be satisfied from the fruit of his works; and because he is proved to be a murderer, and in no wise to have stood in the truth, you, following your father, will to kill me, and in me, who am the truth, you will neither to stand nor to believe. Let us then be that earth which, bearing fruit, has received blessing from the Lord, as David says: « The Lord will give kindness, and our earth shall yield her fruit [n: (Psal. LXXXIV)]. » The Lord therefore gives kindness to our earth when he breathes into our [0581C] heart the sweetness of his love, that for him we may delightfully bear things hard and harsh, and may utterly cast away from us those vices of human flesh which dissolve the soul into death. The Lord gives us, I say, his kindness when he makes known to our minds the knowledge of his own self; by which knowledge our minds, made drunk as it were with a spiritual draught, count the joys of this world as nothing, because, the deceitful glory of the world being despised, they strive to come to him whose knowledge is eternal life. Whence the same Lord says in the Gospel. « This is eternal life, that they may know thee, and him whom thou hast sent, thy Son, to be the one and true God [n: (Joan. XVII)]. » [var: Vulg. Joan. XVII, 3, *Haec est autem vita aeterna: ut cognoscant te solum Deum verum, et quem misisti Jesum Christum*] And our earth yields her fruit when our mind, caught up in supernal contemplation, [0581D] sighs after God, and, forgetting its own frailty, desires to be made one spirit with him whom, with the sincere love of charity, it loves above all creatures as is worthy. Our earth, I say, will then yield her fruit when our body, proved by many temptations through the good works which it now performs, shall after the end of this frail life render its spirit to God, saying with the Psalmist: « Into thy hands I commend my spirit [n: (Psal. XXX)]. » The fruit of which flesh, namely, will then be doubled with a happy joy, when that same flesh, made bright by the glory of the resurrection, shall together with its spirit be endowed with eternal beatitude; so that, as in this world both endured pains and tribulations for God, so after the day of judgment they may with equal joy receive [0582A] the mansions of that highest and incomparable glory promised by God. Of which the same Lord says to his disciples: « In my Father's house there are many mansions [n: (Joan. XIV)]. » Therefore, since the intention of our mind, according as its powers support it, has also labored to show what that earth is which is satisfied from the fruit of God's works, it now remains that it speak of those works of God themselves, and of the fruit of his works, unto the praise and glory of his name. And the works of God, generally or universally, are all creatures, which were created by God himself with a most wise ordering. Of which wondrous creation the authority of Holy Scripture thus testifies: « He that liveth for ever created all things together [n: (Eccle. XVIII)]. » Whence David the prophet says to the Lord: « Thou hast made all things in wisdom *(Psal. [0582B] CIII)*. » For truly God made all things in his wisdom, because in his Son, who is his wisdom and word and power, he ineffably founded all things. Which the same prophet, perceiving in the Holy Spirit, declares in another place, saying: « And thou in the beginning, O Lord, didst found the earth, and the heavens are the works of thy hands [n: (Psal. CI)]. » [var: Vulg. Psal. CI, 26, *Initio tu, Domine, terram fundasti*, without *in principio*] And here the Prophet calls the Son of God the beginning, whom in a lower psalm he had named his wisdom. And by heaven and by earth we ought to understand the things which are contained in these two elements; since we believe that all creatures which were created by God are within the compass of these elements. Which they judge that Moses, the choicest of the prophets, perceived in the Holy Spirit, who, discoursing of the creation of the world, thus speaks, saying: « In the beginning [0582C] God created heaven and earth [n: (Gen. I)]; » as if he should say: In his Son, who is the beginning of every creature which he himself founded, he created heaven and earth, and the things which are contained in them. Nevertheless, among the other works which God almighty is believed to have founded through his Son, the same Son is read to have done a certain wondrous and special work. Of which work Isaiah the prophet, touched by the divine spirit, thus testifies: « His work was alien from him; that he might do his own work, he first did an alien work [n: (Isa. XXVIII)]. » [var: Vulg. Isa. XXVIII, 21, *ut faciat opus suum, alienum opus ejus: ut operetur opus suum, peregrinum est opus ejus ab eo*] Which words of the holy prophet, because they are exceedingly obscure, and since they cannot easily be found in the other prophets, ought to be weighed by us with anxious attention, in that they were by no means uttered by so great a prophet [0582D] without the weight of a great mystery. For these words have within them a vast mystery, covered by the outer rind of the letter as by a kind of veil. Which words, unless they be more thoroughly discussed, the hidden mystery which is within is in no wise brought forth into the light. What then is it that the prophet says, that the work of the Son of God was alien from God himself? For what is it that he says, that in order to do his own work he first did an alien one? Let us therefore seek some example, by which, when it has been brought forward and discussed, we may more lucidly show the hidden mystery which in this sentence we desire to lay open. A certain lord speaks to a certain servant of his, saying [n: (Matth. XXI)]: "Go first, work in my vineyard, and after this thou shalt work *339* in thine own vineyard; and know for certain that thou shalt in no wise work in thine own vineyard, unless thou shalt first have worked in [0583A] my vineyard." Hearing this therefore, he works his lord's vineyard, and after this he does the work upon his own proper vineyard; and thus he first does an alien work, that afterwards he may do his own. So assuredly, so did the Lord Jesus Christ act. He first did an alien work, that afterwards he might do his own work. For to be born, to suffer, to be crucified, to die and to rise again was a work alien from his divinity. He himself invisible, impassible, immortal, being as he is coeternal and consubstantial with God the Father and the Holy Spirit. For the proper work of the Son of God was the redemption of the human race. But unless he were first born, and suffered, and died, and rose again, in no way would he redeem from death the human race, lost by its own free will. The Son of God therefore did an alien work, that afterwards he might do his [0583B] own work, because he first endured the death which did not befit his divinity, that afterwards he might redeem the human race, which did befit his divinity. Behold, we have shown — yet not as is worthy — what are the general works of the Father and of the Son, or what are the works special to the Son only, such as to suffer death, which befits neither the divinity of that same Son nor of the Father himself. For human death regards the humanity of Christ only, but the divinity of the Father, and of that same Christ and of the Holy Spirit, remains and has remained utterly without experience of death or of any suffering. Let us then demonstrate what the fruit of God's works is, with which fruit we, who are earth, ought to be satisfied. For we reckon this to be the fruit of God's works: that, the works of the Lord himself [0583C] who made all things being considered, we should praise and glorify them. Let us know, I say, this to be the fruit of God's works: that from the consideration of creatures we should render thanks and praises, as is worthy, to the Creator himself. Who indeed out of no existing matter wondrously founded all things that were made, and by ineffable power created the first man from the slime of the earth, and breathed into him the insensible breath of life. Moreover — which is of greater glory and of more excellent dignity — he deigned to give to that same man, above the other creatures, his own image and likeness. For of no creature save of man alone does Holy Scripture bear witness [n: (Gen. I)] that it was formed or created to the image and likeness of God. Render therefore, O man, [0583D] render, I say, thanks to the clemency and goodness of thy Creator. Be not, I beseech thee, be not ungrateful for the benefits conferred upon thee by him: answer, as much as thou canst, the bowels of his charity. To whom, according to thy possibility, thou canst answer on this condition, if thou wilt: if thou art willing to obey his commandments, which he delivered to thee for thy salvation, and if thou keep thyself free from all sin, [0584A] and persevere in his service unto the last term of thy life. Of great authority therefore and of great dignity art thou, O man! God almighty created thee to his own image; his Son the Lord Jesus Christ redeemed [thee] with his own blood; by the Holy Spirit thou hast been mercifully enlightened; by the sevenfold grace of that same Holy Spirit thou hast been imbued unto the knowledge of the holy Trinity.
