## PRELIMINARY NOTICE.

[0663C]

The present letter, or little commentary on the cited passage from the Song, is believed to have been first published by Canisius, in Tome VI of the *Lectiones Antiquae*, pages 366-368, or in Tome II of Basnage's edition, Part I, page 540, from the library of the most renowned monastery of St. Gall; although Antonius Possevinus, in Tome I of the *Apparatus Sacer*, page 35, records an older edition, made in the year 1568, together with a commentary on the Psalms. The distinguished Quercetanus followed the edition of Canisius, and recorded it among the *Works of the Blessed Flaccus Alcuin*, page 306. Which edition we too follow, collated with an old Salzburg manuscript codex, whence we have noted certain variant readings. Alcuin was still a young man, if I am not mistaken, says Basnage in the passage cited, when he wrote this letter; for he desired to lie hidden among his intimates, dreading the judgments of the learned, nor did he dare to present himself to their sight. The writers of the *Histoire littéraire de la France*, Tome IV, page 305, note 6, also judge this letter to be of little importance [0664C].

Concerning Daphnis, or Daphinus, all is unknown to us, except that he was a disciple of Alcuin, to whom he made known his grief over the perverse manner of life to which another disciple, Cuculus by name, had given himself, whom he laments in a singular poem, to be presented also in its own place from the *Analecta* of the distinguished Mabillon.

## LETTER TO DAPHNIS

To my most beloved son Daphinus [ Ms., Daphni], Albinus the Father, greeting.

Since some time ago you treated with me of the theory of numbers, asking how in the most excellent Song of Solomon the number sixty should suit the queens, and eighty the concubines, and why the young maidens were without number: therefore, even though I know that you have read the full [ Ms., plenariam] account of this in the treatises of the holy Fathers, nevertheless, lest I sadden your solicitude, [0665A] I will say what seems able to suit my own unskillfulness, and what I would wish to lie hidden only among familiar ears, because I do not dare to present myself publicly [ Ms., publico] to the judgment of the great doctors.

For we read, in the most subtle theory of numbers, that some numbers are even, others odd; and likewise that of the even numbers some are perfect, others imperfect: likewise also that of the imperfect ones some are superabundant, others deficient. Now even numbers are those which can be divided into two equal parts: as eight into twice four, and four into twice two. Odd are those which cannot be divided into two equal parts, as seven or nine; which if you divide, you will not be able to find in them two equal [parts]: likewise of these even numbers some are perfect, others imperfect. A perfect number is [0665B] one that is filled by its own parts, and is neither broken by diminution nor superabounds by the multiplication of its parts, as the number six. For the number six has as its half three, as its third two, and as its sixth one, which joined together fill up the same six; for one, two, three, make [six], which is filled by its own parts. It is neither broken by diminution nor overgrows by abundance; therefore the most perfect Creator, who created all things very good, founded the creatures of the original world in that number, to show that all things which he founded are perfect in their own kind.

Likewise the number eight [ Edit., octogenarius], if you divide it into the parts named from itself, is found to be less than itself; for it has as its half four, as its fourth two, and as its eighth one: which [0665C] drawn together do not fill up eight, but seven; one, two, and four make seven, not eight: and *409* therefore the second origin of this kind [ F., humani generis] began to grow from the number eight. For we read that there were eight souls in the ark, from whom the multitude of the whole human race sprang forth, that it might be shown that the second origin is more imperfect than the first, which was created in the number six. Hence also our Redeemer and Restorer of the first perfection: as Adam was created on the sixth day from the virgin earth, so he in the sixth age was made man from the Virgin Mary, that he might consecrate by his coming the perfection of the number six, which he showed in the founding of the first man.

Likewise we see the progression of numbers by joints, as by [0665D] certain units, growing to infinity through certain finite forms. For the first progression of numbers is from one up to ten. The second from [0666A] ten up to a hundred. The third from a hundred up to a thousand. And it is necessary that the same rule of perfection [or of imperfection] which [ Leg. quam] the first unit keeps in its numbers up to [ Ms., sub] ten, the second also keep in its tens up to a hundred. For as the number six, set in its parts by unit [ Ms., positis], is perfect in itself, so also sixty, its parts being set by ten, is known to be perfect, so that in sixty the ten always holds the place of the unit in six, and its division by its parts is of this sort: the half of sixty is thirty, as three is of six; and its third part is twenty, as two is in six; and let ten hold in it the place of the unit; which sum, if it is gathered, fills up the same sixty. [0666B] Ten therefore, and twenty, and thirty, are sixty, just as three, and two, and one are six.

You will be able also, by yourself, according to the same rule, to explore the deficiency of eighty. For forty is the half of eighty, and twenty its fourth part, and ten its eighth: which drawn together make seventy, not eighty, for ten, twenty, forty are seventy [ Ms., faciunt].

But the sixty queens and eighty concubines are the rulers of the holy Church. But some of them teach for the sole charity of Christ; others, pursuing earthly advantages, labor in the Church, not by the labor [ Ms., ardore] of the heavenly fatherland, but sweat in teaching for the sake of earthly gain. These, in their perfection [ F., imperfectione], are compared to the number eighty: those [0666C], by perfect blessedness, are signified by sixty, so that they are made worthy of the name of queens, because they do not cease to beget spiritual offspring — solely for the love of the Bridegroom, and for the multiplying of sons into the heavenly fatherland — by baptizing or preaching, a happy progeny. The former, however, are denoted by the name of concubines, because, through worldly ambition or for the sake of temporal honor, by preaching or baptizing they often beget noble sons indeed, but they themselves remain ignoble in themselves. Flee, most dear son, I beseech thee, the fellowship of these; and if at any time the mercy of God should deign to make thee a teacher, cease not thou to labor for the love of him who did not hesitate to shed his blood for thy salvation, so that there may be to thee, as reward [ Ms., sit merces], riches that shall not perish, but perpetual glory in [0666D] the heavenly bridal-chamber of [our] Lord Jesus Christ, to whom be praise and glory unto all eternities. Amen.
