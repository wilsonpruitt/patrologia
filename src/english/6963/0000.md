## PROLOGUE.

[1851]

[1851A] *1545* 1. *Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth [n: (Cant. I, 1)]*: here it is not the incitements of foulness, but the mysteries of chastity, that are celebrated; where the matter is handled spiritually, and by a certain contemplation of the affections, concerning the marriage and the joining of Christ and the Church, of the uncreated Spirit and the created, of flesh and spirit. Let us therefore circumcise our heart: let us seek nothing base, nothing bodily; and everything earthly is base. Let us therefore set down in these heavenly utterances nothing earthly, nothing carnal, nothing worldly, nothing bodily, nothing light and changeable: for *the words of the Lord are chaste words;* that in them the spotless and modest sincerity of the heavenly mysteries may *1546* shine forth by a spiritual interpretation. Let us not mingle earthly things with divine by some adulterous notion, nor violate that inviolable mystery of prophetic vision or of the everlasting oracle by an estimate drawn from our own nature: for whether it be of kisses or of such things, the whole of it is seemly, whatever is offered to religion; so that we blush at [1852A] no service that avails toward the worship and the observance of Christ: as in that dance of David before the ark; for that dancing was no companion of delights and of wantonness, but that each man should lift up an unsluggish body, and not let his limbs lie sluggish on the ground, or grow numb with slow steps. For this mastery we did not find out, but received; thus has heavenly doctrine established the order of the mystic song. *1547*

## 2.

*Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth; for thy breasts are good above wine, and the odor of thine ointments above all spices. Thy name is an ointment emptied out [n: (Ibid.)]*. [var: Vulg. *osculo oris sui*, "with the kiss of his mouth," singular and without *ab*; *meliora sunt ubera tua vino*, "better than wine"; *fraglantia unguentis optimis*, "fragrant with the best ointments"; *oleum effusum nomen tuum*, "oil poured out"] This whole passage of delights rings of play, stirs up applause, provokes love. *Therefore,* she says, *the maidens have loved thee and have drawn thee; let us run behind the odor of thine ointments. The king has brought me into his tabernacle [n: (Ibid., 3)]*. [var: Vulg. *trahe me post te; curremus in odorem unguentorum tuorum*, "draw me after thee: we will run," an imperative; and *introduxit me rex in cellaria sua*, "into his storerooms"] She began from kisses that she might arrive at the tabernacle; and she, impatient of the Lord's labor and of exercised virtue, [1853A] is driven to open the bars with her hand, to go out into the field, to abide in the villages; yet at the beginning she runs behind the odor of the ointment, and when she has come into the tabernacle she will exchange the ointment for the villages. See, then, where she comes out.

## 3.

*If she is a wall,* he says, *let us build upon her towers of silver [n: (Cant. VIII, 9)]*. [var: Vulg. *propugnacula argentea*, "bulwarks of silver"] She who was playing with kisses now raises up towers, so that, turreted with the precious pinnacles of the saints, she may not only frustrate the assaults of enemies, but may also build safe bulwarks of good merits. Even in these temporal marriages the bride is applauded before she is commanded, lest harsh commands give offense before love, warmed by caresses, has grown into her. The heifer learns to love the sound of a certain clapping, that she may not refuse the yoke upon her neck; and so she is first accustomed to the word *1548* of playfulness before the lash of discipline. But when she has put her neck [1853B] under the yoke, the rein also constrains her, and the goad presses, and her yokemates draw, and the yoke-fellow invites. So too our Virgin ought first to hammer with holy love; to marvel at the golden supports of the heavenly couch, and in the very porch [1854A] of the wedding to behold the doorposts crowned with garlands of leafage, and to drink in the delights of the choir sounding within; lest, made afraid, she withdraw herself from the Lord's yoke before, being called, she bow to it. For we must know certain degrees of advancement, and their order — what ought to come first and what after: for to know what thou shouldst do, and not to know the order of doing it, is not of perfect knowledge; for things done back to front most often give offense. And so, since there are as many opinions as there are men, if anything in our discourse has been cleared, let all read it; if anything is boiled down, let the riper approve it; if anything is modest, let it cling to their breasts and paint their cheeks; if anything is flowery, let the flowering age not disapprove it. We ought to have stirred up the love of the bride; for it is written: *Thou shalt love the Lord thy God [n: (Deut. VI, 5)]*: [1854B] we ought to have adorned the bride's hair at the wedding with certain curling-irons of prayer; for it is written: *Clap with the hand, and strike with the foot [n: (Ezech. XXI, 14)]*: we ought to have strewn the everlasting bridal chambers with roses.

## HERE BEGINS THE TEXT.



## CHAPTER ONE.

[1853B] *1547*

## 1.

Life is to be sought before doctrine; for a good life has grace even without doctrine, while doctrine without life has not wholeness. *For wisdom does not fall into an ill-willing soul [n: (Sap. I, 4)]*: [var: Vulg. *non introibit sapientia*, "will not enter in"] and therefore he says: *The wicked shall seek me, and shall not find [n: (Prov. I, 28)]*: [var: Vulg. *tunc invocabunt me et non exaudiam; mane consurgent et non invenient me*, "then shall they call upon me, and I will not hear"] because the eye of the mind is blinded by wickedness, and, with iniquity darkening it, it cannot find the deep mysteries. First, therefore, the soldiery of life must be exercised, [1853C] and morals corrected. When we have set these things toward their due course, so that there may be correction of offense and the grace of purity, then let us come to the pursuits of taking in knowledge, in their own order and way. First, then, come the moral matters, second the mystical: in the former is life, in the latter knowledge; so that, if thou seek perfection, there be neither life without knowledge nor knowledge without life, but each bear witness with the other. Following this arrangement, Solomon wrote the book of Proverbs, in which he set forth the moral topic more richly, the natural in Ecclesiastes, the mystical in the Song of Songs: although, if thou examine diligently, thou wouldst find very many mystical things in Proverbs too, and in the Song the sweetness of moral matters. For surely this is mystical: *Wisdom has built herself a house, and has set seven columns beneath it; she has slain her victims, [1853D]* etc. [n: (Pov. IX, 1)]. [var: Vulg. *excidit columnas septem; immolavit victimas suas*, "hewed out seven columns; sacrificed her victims"] For in the Song that shines as mystical which is moral, wherein the sweetness of endearments and the affection of a lover is expressed.

## 2.

*[n: (Vers. 1, 2.)] Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth; since thy breasts are best above wine, and the odor of thine ointments above all spices.* [var: Vulg. *meliora sunt ubera tua vino*; the plate has printed the same lemma with *bona*, "good," above] Set before thee, then, a Virgin betrothed a long while, and burning with a just love, who has come to know many glorious works of her beloved by the assertion of approved witnesses; often put off, her desires held in suspense, bearing delays no longer, who would have done all things so that she might [sic: *deret*] the bridegroom: [1854B] who at length, having obtained her vows, troubled with joy at the unforeseen coming of the bridegroom, does not seek the first beginnings of greeting, nor the exchanges of words, but at once demands what she had desired. So then also the holy Church, betrothed in the first beginnings of the world in paradise, prefigured in the flood, announced through the Law, called through the prophets, when she had long awaited the redemption of men, the beauty of the Gospel, the coming of her beloved, impatient of delay, rushes into kisses, saying: *Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth;* and, delighted with the kisses, [1854C] she adds: *since thy breasts are best above wine.*

## 3.

And to speak more morally, I understand for myself that flesh which had been steeped in Adam with the serpent's venom, which was withering with the stench of crimes, which went forth among the daughters of Sion with neck held high and with beckonings of the eyes, trailing her tunics in the going of her feet and playing with her own feet, with ringlets of hair and composed countenances and headbands, and in every affected comeliness the more unseemly; and yet that same flesh, taught by very many *1548* oracles that he would come who, when the serpent's enticements had been shut out, would pour in the grace of the Holy Spirit, so that all flesh might see the salvation of God, that all flesh might come to God — that it had withered with longing: but fearing lest, as impatient, as wanton, as luxurious, as querulous, it should displease as it had been before, [1854D] although it was shaken by an expectation, longer than it could now bear, of the Lord's delaying coming: yet not murmuring nor transgressing, but lifting up pure hands in every place, without wrath and dispute, in adorned habit with modesty and sobriety, adorning herself not with plaited hair or gold or pearls or costly raiment, but with those things that befit the grace of chastity and of good conversation, praying and saying: *Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth; since thy breasts are best above wine.* Now the flesh wished to cleave to Christ, now she hastened to be wedded; [1855A] that she might be one spirit, and that she might become the flesh of Christ who before was a harlot's. *Let him kiss me,* she says: the Word of God kisses us when the spirit of knowledge illumines our understanding.

## 4.

Therefore it is either the Church, held in suspense through many seasons by the Lord's coming long since promised to her through the prophets, or the soul which, raising herself from the body, luxury and delights and carnal pleasures being renounced, stripped also of the anxiety of worldly vanities, has this long while yearned for the inpouring of the divine presence into her and for the grace of the saving Word: that she is worn away because he comes late, and is afflicted; and therefore, as one wounded with charity, since she cannot bear his delays, turning to the Father, that she asks God to send her the Word, and declares the cause why she is so impatient, saying: *Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth.*
