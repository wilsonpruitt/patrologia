## 45.

*[n: (Vers. 12, 13.)] A binding-together of the drop is my brother to me; in the midst of my breasts he shall remain,* or *shall dwell* [var: Vulg. *Fasciculus myrrhae dilectus meus mihi, inter ubera mea commorabitur*, "A bundle of myrrh is my beloved to me; he shall abide between my breasts"]. She had indeed said before that her spikenard had given her the odour of the bridegroom, and that through the ointment with which she had anointed him she had received the fragrance of his odour: but now she says: My brother smells to me of the drop, and this not poured abroad nor scattered as it may chance, but [1869A] bound together and drawn tight, so namely that the odour of its own sweetness may be rendered denser and stronger. And he, she says, *1558* being such as he is, dwells and abides in the midst of my breasts, and makes his rest and his mansion in the place of my bosom. *A binding-together of the drop is my cousin, a cluster of cypress is my brother to me; between the midst of my breasts he shall rest.* For the Lord Jesus, taking a body, bound himself with the chains of charity, and joined himself not only to our members and to natural sufferings, but even to the cross; and therefore, like a cluster, he rests in the faith of the Church and in her moral grace.

## 46.

*Spikenard of cypress is my cousin in the vineyards of Engaddi* [var: Vulg. *Botrus cypri dilectus meus mihi, in vineis Engaddi*, "A cluster of cypress is my beloved to me"]. If we seek the place of a certain region which is in Judaea, that place is so called in which the balsam-sap is produced: if we seek the interpretation, in Latin it signifies temptation. [1869B] But in those vineyards there is a wood which, if one prick it, sends forth ointment; this is the fruit of the wood. If the wood be not cut, it does not so give fragrance and odour; but when it has been pricked by the craftsman's hand, then it distils a tear. So also Christ, crucified upon that wood of temptation, wept over the people, that he might wash away our sins, and out of the bowels of his mercy poured forth ointment, saying: *Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do [n: (Luc. XXIII, 34)]* [var: Vulg. *non enim sciunt quid faciunt*, "for they know not"]. Then therefore, pricked upon the wood with the lance, there went out from him blood and water, sweeter than any ointment, a victim acceptable to God, pouring out through the whole world the odour of sanctification. And as balsam out of the tree, so virtue went forth out of his body, whence he says: *I perceive that virtue has gone out from me* [1869C] [n: (Luc. VIII, 46)] [var: Vulg. *nam ego novi virtutem de me exiisse*, "for I know that virtue has gone out from me"]. Thence it is called more expressly *opobalsamum*, in that by the pricking of the wood the balsam bursts out through the hollow of the puncture.

## 47.

Jesus therefore, being pricked, poured forth the odour of the remission of sins and of redemption. For he was also drawn tight, when the Word was made man: and he was made poor, when he was rich, that we might be enriched by his want: he was powerful, and offered himself to be despised, so that Herod scorned him and mocked him: he moved the earth, and clung to the wood: he covered heaven with darkness, and crucified the world, and was crucified: he bowed his head, and the Word went forth: he was emptied out, and filled all things: God descended, man ascended: the Word was made flesh, that the flesh might claim for itself the throne of the Word at the right hand of God: he was a wound, and [1869D] ointment flowed: he was heard as a beetle, and was acknowledged as God. And Christ answered.

## 48.

*[n: (Vers. 14.)] Behold thou art good, my neighbor, behold thou art good: thine eyes are a dove's* [var: Vulg. *Ecce tu pulchra es, amica mea, ecce tu pulchra es; oculi tui columbarum*, "Behold thou art beautiful, my friend… thine eyes are doves' eyes"]. Because the Church had known the mystery, and was preaching the Lord Jesus crucified for the redemption of the whole world, she deserves to hear: *Behold thou art good,* thou who callest me good; *Behold thou art good,* thou who hast seen the comeliness of my glory, and art thyself shapely and comely. But what is it for Christ to say: *Thou art good,* or *Thou art comely,* if not that word of the Gospel: *Be steadfast, daughter, thy sins are forgiven thee [n: (Matth. IX, 22)]* [var: Vulg. Matt. 9:22 *Confide, filia, fides tua te salvam fecit*, "Have confidence, daughter, thy faith hath made thee whole"; the clause on forgiveness stands at Matt. 9:2]? Therefore he to whom Christ has forgiven sins [1870A] rightly says: *Reward thy servant, that I may live and keep thy commandments [n: (Psal. CXVIII, 146)]* [var: Vulg. Ps. 118:17 *Retribue servo tuo, vivifica me, et custodiam sermones tuos*, "quicken me, and I will keep thy words"]. He has nothing in the recompense whereof to despair; because the Lord Jesus came to save the world, not to destroy it. Therefore he is unmindful of injury, mindful of grace, as he himself bears witness in the prophetic book, saying: *I am, I am he who blot out thine iniquities, and I will not be mindful of them: but be thou mindful, and let us be judged; tell thou first thine iniquities, that thou mayest be justified [n: (Esai. XLIII, 25 et 26)]* [var: Vulg. *Ego sum, ego sum ipse qui deleo iniquitates tuas propter me, et peccatorum tuorum non recordabor. Reduc me in memoriam, et judicemur simul: narra si quid habes ut justificeris*]. Whosoever therefore tells his iniquities to God is justified: and whosoever is justified fears not the recompense, but demands it: he who fears not the recompense, lives.

## 49.

*[n: (Vers. 15, 16.)] Behold,* she says, *my cousin is well-formed, and indeed beautiful. Our reclining-place is shady, the beams of our houses are cedars, our panelled ceilings [1870B] cypresses* [var: Vulg. *Ecce tu pulcher es, dilecte mi, et decorus. Lectulus noster floridus; tigna domorum nostrarum cedrina, laquearia nostra cypressina*, "Behold thou art beautiful, my beloved, and comely. Our little bed is flowery"]. The Church praises the comeliness of the bridegroom, whom each one praises the more with silent affection; and the faithful interpreter of the mysteries preaches the more by keeping silence. For she who divulges secret things diminishes the comeliness of Christ; therefore let no one cast his pearls before swine, lest he cause precious ornaments to be trodden down. Not then in the tent of the talkative and the chattering — because by much speaking sin is admitted — but in that of a serious man, who is sparing of address, and not intemperate of speech, and by the sobriety of his words avoids the drunkenness of talkativeness, does Christ lay back his head.

## 50.

And with reason does she signify a shady reclining-place; because the virtue of the Most High overshadows those who are set in the Church. In this shadow David asked to be protected, lest the sun burn him by day, or the moon by night *(Psalm. [1870C] CXX, 6)*. This shadow spiritual grace *1559* ministers to those who flee the parched and summer heats of this age and of the world. Shady therefore is the reclining-place of Christ and of the Church, upon whom that eternal rest of God the Father breathes. In this shadow therefore let us rest, wearied by the heats of our sins: whomsoever lust has scorched, let the cross of the Lord refresh them, upon which he laid himself back, that he might take up our debts. Whomsoever guilt has wearied, let Jesus receive them in his bosom, and cherish them with a soft embrace; whence I dare to say that the flesh of Christ is the reclining-place of the Church.

## 51.

*The beams of our houses are cedars.* Under the figure of the cedar the glory of those elders who were just is designated. *For the just shall flourish like the palm tree, and like the cedar which is in Lebanon he shall be multiplied [n: (Psalm. XCI, 13)]* [var: Vulg. *sicut cedrus Libani multiplicabitur*, "like the cedar of Lebanon"]; for as the cedar [1870D] does not rot, so neither is the glory of the elders corrupted by any old age.

## 52.

*Our panelled ceilings cypresses.* This kind of tree never loses its greenness; in winter as in summer it feeds its foliage, nor is it changed with a different colour. This tree alone the wind never despoils of its honour; it alone is never stripped of its old cloak, nor clothed with a new blossom. So too apostolic grace knows no failing, but flourishes by its own age. The soul therefore knows not how to be corrupted, which is vigorous with flourishing members, and always upholds with patient greatness of mind the summits of justice and of the other virtues; [1871A] and therefore it neither flows away nor fails, because there is nothing in it chinked or slack, nothing shifting, nothing slippery, nothing which could be poured out of it by a fault of speech. There follows the Church, or the faithful soul — the soul, I say, because man is nothing so much as soul.

## CHAPTER THE SECOND.



## 1.

*[n: (Vers. 1, 2.)] I am the flower of the field, and the lily of the valleys, as a lily in the midst of thorns* [var: Vulg. *Sicut lilium inter spinas*, "As a lily among thorns"]; for into the whole earth the faith of the believing people has gone out, and Christ has set his feet in a spacious place; and therefore he beautifully says that he is the flower of the field. A flower too was Paul, who said: *We are the good odour of Christ unto God [n: (II Cor. II, 15)]*. And truly a flower, who could bring forth new things and old out of the treasure of his own heart. *I am the flower of the field.* And well is [1871B] the Church a flower, who announces the fruit, that is, the Lord Jesus Christ, of whom it was said to Mary: *Blessed art thou among women, and blessed is the fruit of thy womb [n: (Luc. I, 42)]*.

## 2.

In another sense also I show thee a flower to be plucked, that one surely who said: *I am the flower of the field, and the lily of the valleys.* Very many think this comparison more apt according to the nature of the flower and the use of the shoot set before us; in that lilies require no tending of any yearly use, nor, as in the case of the yield of the other fruits, does the labour of the year come round again in its circuit for anxious husbandmen with regard to the generation of this flower; for whatever the droughts of the field, all that which is nourished into flower is quickened out of itself by a certain generative power, and in itself by a sap always green; so that when [1871C] thou seest the stalk of its grown leaves to have withered, yet the nature of the flower is green; for the greenness is hidden, not lost. But when it has been called forth by the caresses of spring, it takes up again the clothing of the shoot, the foliage of the flower, or the adornments of the lily.
