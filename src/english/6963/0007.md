## 3.

It delights one also to notice this, that lilies are not begotten in the roughnesses of mountains and the uncultivated places of woods, but in the pleasantness of gardens. For there are certain gardens fruitful with divers virtues, according to that which is written: *A garden enclosed,* etc. [n: (Cant. IV, 12)], in that where there is integrity, where chastity, where religion, where the faithful silences of secret things, there is the brightness of angels, there are the violets of confessors, the lilies of virgins, the roses of martyrs. Well is Christ a lily, because where the blood of the martyrs is, there is Christ, who is the flower on high, [1871D] unspotted, harmless, in whom the roughness of thorns gives no offence, but the grace poured about him was shining. For the thorns of the roses are the torments of the martyrs; the unoffended Godhead, which felt no torments, has no thorns.

## 4.

*As a lily in the midst of thorns.* Which is of plain evidence, that virtue is beset by the brambles of spiritual wickednesses, whence no man may carry off fruit save he who comes warily. Take wings therefore, O virgin, but wings of the spirit, that thou mayest fly above the vices, if thou desirest to touch Christ, [1872A] *Who dwelleth on high, and beholdeth lowly things [n: (Psalm. CXII, 5)]*. And his form is like the cedar of Lebanon, which sets its foliage in the clouds, its root in the earth; for his beginning is from heaven, his latter parts on earth, *1560* and they have brought forth fruits nearest to heaven. Search out more diligently so good a flower, to see whether thou mayest find him anywhere in the valley of thy breast; for he is often breathed upon lowly things. He loves to be begotten in gardens, in which Susanna, while she walked, found him: ready rather to die than to be violated.

## 5.

Otherwise: *I am the flower of the field, and the lily of the valleys, as a lily in the midst of thorns.* Behold another place in which the Lord is wont to abide — nay, not in one, but in many: *I,* he says, *am the flower of the field;* because he frequents the open simplicity of a pure mind. [1872B] *And the lily of the valleys;* for Christ is the flower of humility, not of luxury, not of pleasures and wantonness, but the flower of simplicity, the flower of humility. A flower keeps its odour even when cut down, and heaps it up when bruised, nor does it lose it when plucked away; so also the Lord Jesus, upon that gibbet of the cross, neither withered when bruised, nor vanished when plucked away, and, cut by that pricking of the lance, he blossomed the more shapely with the sacred colour of the blood poured out, himself not knowing how to die, and breathing out upon the dead the gift of eternal life.

## 6.

*[n: (Vers. 3.)] As a lily in the midst of thorns* — does not the flower of good odour rise up amid the roughnesses of labours and the contritions of minds? For by a contrite heart God is appeased. This is the desert, O daughters, which leads to the kingdom; this is also the desert which [1872C] flowers as a lily, according to that which is written: *Rejoice, thou barren one, and exult, thou desert, and let it flower as a lily [n: (Esai. XXXV, 1)]* [var: Vulg. *Laetabitur deserta et invia, et exsultabit solitudo, et florebit quasi lilium*, "The desert and the pathless land shall be glad… and shall flower like a lily" — indicative, not imperative]. In this desert, O daughters, that good fruitful tree, which makes good fruits, begins to spread abroad the arms of its own deeds, to lift up the crown of its divinity, beside which the trees of our wood may put forth their shoots; because, as the apple tree among the trees of the wood, so is my brother in the midst of the sons; so that the Church, seeing this, and already glad at the success of our faith, may say: *In his shadow I have desired and sat down, and his fruit is sweet in my jaws* [var: Vulg. *Sicut malus inter ligna silvarum, sic dilectus meus inter filios. Sub umbra illius quem desideraveram sedi, et fructus ejus dulcis gutturi meo*, "Under the shadow of him whom I had desired I sat, and his fruit was sweet to my throat"].

## 7.

[n: (Vers. 3, 4.)] Note that often in this book of Songs Solomon has plainly expressed that threefold wisdom, namely the moral, the natural, and the mystical, as he had said in the Proverbs; that he might write in threefold fashion [1872D] for him who should wish to hear his wisdom. He says therefore in the Songs, the bride concerning the bridegroom: *Behold thou art beautiful, my cousin, behold thou art beautiful and comely; our reclining-place is shady, the beams of our houses are cedars, our panelled ceilings cypresses [n: (Cant. I, 15 et 16)]*. We can take this of moral things; for where does Christ rest, and the Church, if not in the works of his own people? And indeed where there was unchastity, where pride, where iniquity, there the Lord Jesus says: *But the Son of man has not where to lay back his head [n: (Matth. VIII, 20)]*. But what shall we take [1873A] of natural things? *In his shadow,* she says, *I have desired and sat down, and his fruit is sweet in my jaws;* for he who steps over earthly things, and to whom worldly things die (since the world is crucified to him, and he to the world), flees and despises all things which are under the sun. Of mystical things also she says: *Bring me into the house of wine, set charity in me.* For as the vine embraces its own vineyard, so the Lord Jesus, as an eternal vine, embraces his own people with certain arms of charity [var: Vulg. Cant. 2:4 *Introduxit me in cellam vinariam; ordinavit in me caritatem*, "He brought me into the wine cellar; he set charity in order in me" — indicative, not imperative].

## 8.

Consider them one by one: in moral things the flower among thorns is the lily, as he himself says: *I am the flower of the field, and the lily of the valleys [n: (Sup., v. 1)]*. In moral things therefore he is a flower, as in natural things the sun of justice, who, rising and rising again, gives light, and setting, overshadows. Beware lest he set upon thee, for it is written: *Let not the sun go down upon [1873B] your wrath [n: (Ephes. IV, 26)]*. In mystical things he is charity, because Christ is the fullness of the law. And therefore the Church, which loves Christ, is wounded with charity.

## 9.

Beautifully also is the Church called a lily; for as the lily shines, so also do the works of the saints shine forth. Most beautifully also was it said: *The lily of the valleys;* because in lowly things grace shines out the more. But this lily is in the midst of thorns, that is, among the Jews and the heretics, that is, among the anxieties of this age, which prick the mind and the spirit of man. We can take it also otherwise: that as the lily stands out among thorns, so the Church of God shines above all assemblies.

## 10.

It is worth considering also that this lily [1873C] is compassed about with splendour, but that what it has within is red, and this too of good odour; in that the flesh of Christ, hedged about as it were with the moral brightness of the Godhead, had the protection of heavenly grace. And indeed in the latter parts he says: *My kinsman is white and red [n: (Cant. V, 10)]* [var: Vulg. *Dilectus meus candidus et rubicundus*, "My beloved is white and ruddy"]: white with the brightness *1561* divine, red with the appearance of human colour, which he took up in the sacrament of the incarnation. And deservedly that also which is red smells well, because the flesh of Christ is without sin — which the faithless, in handling, defiled their own hands; the saints, in venerating, were fragrant with the odour of piety.

## 11.

The Lord Jesus received this odour of his fragrant Church, and says: *Behold my neighbor is good.* And the Church says to Christ: *Behold thou art good, my kinsman, behold thou art good, as an apple among the trees of the wood.* [1873D] A most beautiful comparison! For what could be said more beautifully or more aptly than that she should compare the bridegroom to an apple tree? For a fruit of this kind has a pleasing odour, so that it surpasses the fragrance of the other fruits. And Christ, fastened to the wood, like an apple hanging on the tree, poured out the good odour of the world's redemption, which wiped away the heavy stench of sin, and poured forth the ointment of a life-giving draught.

## 12.

*As an apple,* he says, *among the trees of the wood, so is my cousin in the midst of the sons;* in that above [1874A] the prophets and apostles he soothed the inmost hearts of men with the sweetness of his words. But not only odour, but also sweet food is in the apple: therefore Christ is sweet food.

## 13.

*In his shadow I have desired, and sat down;* for she had received the law, this she followed, this way she ran: resting therefore in the law, she was resting in the shadow of Christ. Good is the shadow which defends us from the sun of iniquity! And to whom is it a doubt that the law of God is the shadow of Christ? What is the law of the feast day, of the new moon, of the sabbaths, if not a shadow of things to come? What days are they according to the law of Moses: That I may see six days, and again the seventh day, and so perpetual ages? What month is there according to the law of Moses, that, knowing the month, I may know the first new moon of the month, and the things truly holy which are to be offered on the new moons? What year is there according to the law of Moses, [1874B] consisting of those six days of which he says: *Six years shall the Hebrew serve, and in the seventh year he shall be set free [n: (Deut. XV, 12)]* [var: Vulg. *serviet tibi sex annis: in anno septimo dimittes eum liberum*, "he shall serve thee six years: in the seventh year thou shalt let him go free"]; so that there be found six years in which a man works the land, but in the seventh year he releases it to the strangers, and to the poor, and to the beasts of the earth? What is the seventh year, in which all debts are remitted to the Hebrews?
