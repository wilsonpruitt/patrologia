## 19.

*[n: (Vers. 6.)] Who is this that ascends from the desert?* [1896A] Be thou like that soul which stirs up in itself the charity of Christ, at whose ascent the powers of the heavens marvel; that she ascends without stumbling *1576* and ascends out of this world with gladness and delight, like the shoot of a vine, and lifts herself like smoke to the things above, fragrant with the odour of a holy resurrection and with the sweetness of faith, as thou hast it written: *Who is this that ascends from the desert, like the shoot of a vine, made odorous with the smoke of incense, with myrrh and frankincense, out of all the powders of the ointment.* [var: Vulg. *sicut virgula fumi ex aromatibus myrrhae et thuris et universi pulveris pigmentarii*] Beautifully has he expressed her fineness by the comparison of powder and by the mention of ointment; because in Exodus we read of a fine incense compounded of many things, that this is the prophetic incense which is the prayer of the saints, that it may be directed in the sight of the Lord, as David also says: *Let my prayer be directed as incense in [1896B] thy sight [n: (Psalm. CXL, 2)]*.

## 20.

*Who is she that ascends from the desert?* This desert appears as a place of earth and untilled, overgrown with the briars and thorns of our offenses. They marvel, plainly, how the soul which before was being left in hell should cleave to the Word of God, and should have ascended like the shoot of a vine, raising herself into the higher regions, like smoke born of fire and seeking the heights, and should moreover be fragrant with good odours. And that odour of holy prayer breathes out a sweetness which is directed *as incense in the sight of the Lord [n: (Psal. CXL, 2)]*. And in the Apocalypse we read that *The smoke of the incense went up from the prayers of the saints [n: (Apoc. VIII, 4)]*. which are offered as incense through the prayers of his angels, namely upon that golden altar which is before the seat of God. And it is fragrant like the sweet [1896C] ointment of holy entreaty; because it is compounded out of the asking for eternal and invisible things, not for bodily ones: yet chiefly it smells of myrrh and frankincense, in that she is dead to sin and lives to God. Whence also in what follows: *I will go,* he says, *for myself to the mountain of myrrh, and to the hill of frankincense.* There follows:

## 21.

*[n: (Vers. 7.)] Behold, sixty mighty men round about her shoot, armed with drawn swords, and trained in the disciplines of war.* [var: Vulg. Cant. III, 7–8 *En lectulum Salomonis sexaginta fortes ambiunt ex fortissimis Israel*, "sixty valiant men surround Solomon's bed"] Consider who these strong men are, and trained in the disciplines of war. It belongs to a strong and churchly man not to dissemble when something threatens, but to look out ahead and, as it were, to scout from some watchtower of the mind, and to meet with provident thought the things that are to come; lest afterwards he say: I fell into this because I did not think it could happen. In short, unless adverse things are scouted out, [1896D] they quickly overtake us. As in war an unforeseen enemy is scarcely withstood, and if he finds men unprepared he easily overwhelms them: so evils unexplored break the mind the more. In these two things, then, lies that excellence of mind: first, that thy mind, exercised in good thoughts, may see with a clean heart what is true and honourable: for *Blessed are the clean of heart, because they also shall see God:* and that it may judge that alone to be good which is honourable. Then, that it be disturbed by no occupations, that it waver with no covetous desires. [1897A]

## 22.

Nor indeed does anyone do this easily: for what is so difficult as to look down, as from some citadel of wisdom, upon riches and all the other things which seem to most men great and lofty? Then, that thou shouldst confirm thy judgment by settled reason, and despise the things thou hast judged trifling as things that will profit nothing? Then, that if anything adverse should befall, and be thought grievous and bitter, thou shouldst bear it in such a way as to think that nothing has happened contrary to nature, since thou hast read: Naked was I born, naked shall I go out: the things which the Lord gave, the Lord has also taken away [n: (Job. I, 21)]; [var: Vulg. Job I, 21 *Nudus egressus sum de utero matris meae, et nudus revertar illuc*] and he had indeed lost both his sons and his goods: and that in all things thou shouldst keep the character of a wise and just man, as he kept it who said: *As it pleased the Lord, so it has been done, blessed be the name of the Lord [n: (Ibid.)]*. And below, *Thou hast spoken as one of the foolish women: if we have received good things [1897B] from the hand of the Lord, shall we not bear the things that are evil [n: (Ibid., 2)]* ? [var: Vulg. Job II, 10 *si bona suscepimus de manu Dei, mala quare non suscipiamus?*]

## 23.

The fortitude of the mind, therefore, is no middling thing, nor one set apart from the rest, that it should wage war with the virtues; but it is that which alone defends the ornaments of all the virtues, and guards their judgments, and which contends in inexpiable battle against all vices, unconquered in labours, brave in dangers, more rigid against pleasures, hard against enticements, to which it knows not how to lend an ear, nor (as the saying is) to bid them hail; which neglects money, flees avarice as a kind of stain that unmans virtue; for nothing is so contrary to fortitude as to be overcome by gain. Often, when the enemy has been driven back and the line of the adversaries turned to flight, while the fighter is taken up with the spoils of the slain, he has fallen, a man to be pitied, among the very men [1897C] whom he laid low: and legions cast down by their own triumphs, while they are busied with the *1577* spoils, have called back upon themselves the enemy who had fled.

## 24.

Let fortitude therefore drive back and trample down so monstrous a pest, and let it not be tempted by covetous desires, nor broken by fear; because virtue is consistent with itself, so that it pursues all vices bravely, as the poisons of virtue. Let it drive off anger as with certain weapons, since anger takes away counsel, and let it shun it as a sickness. Let it beware also of the appetite for glory, which has often done harm when sought too immoderately, but always when usurped. For to be softened by flattery seems not only not to belong to fortitude, but even to belong to cowardice. Seeing in the spirit the camp of these strong men, Jacob said: *These are the camps of God [n: (Gen. XXXII, 2)]*. [1897D]

## 25.

But what are the sixty? We know that the hebdomad belongs to the Old Testament, and the ogdoad to the New — namely when Christ rose again, and the day of the new salvation shone upon all: on which day the brightness of the full and perfect circumcision poured itself into human breasts. Now seven and eight make fifteen. These, then, are the seven and the eight, that is, the fifteen, for which Hosea hired the harlot whom the Lord had commanded him to hire. And so the hebdomad and the eighth signify the consummation of the two Testaments [1898A] and the fullness of faith: while the number fifteen taken four times, which is sixty, signifies the perfection of the Gospel.

## 26.

But what is the shoot round about which these mighty men are? *Nephthalim,* he says, *a vine let loose, putting forth beauty in its bud.* [var: Vulg. Gen. XLIX, 21 *Nephthali, cervus emissus*, "a hind let loose"] One branch of the vine is cut away which seems useless, lest the vine run to waste in an empty exultation of branches, and another is pruned back a little and let loose, that it may bear fruit, whose beauty is put forth in its bearing; because while it lifts itself to the things above it embraces the vine, and climbing to the top it clothes the necks of the vines' yoke-poles with a certain necklace of precious shoot. That one too is comely in its bearing, because with full shoots it pours out many fruits. This is comely, but that other is far more beautiful, which signifies the shoot [1898B] clinging to the spiritual vine, of which we are the branch, and we can bear fruit if we remain in the vine; but if not, we are cut off. The holy patriarch Nephthalim was an abounding shoot, whence Moses also says: *Nephthalim, the satisfying of those who receive, shall be filled with blessing from the Lord: he shall possess the sea and the south-west wind [n: (Deut. XXXIII, 23)]*; [var: Vulg. Deut. XXXIII, 23 *mare et meridiem possidebit*, "shall possess the sea and the south"] explaining what Jacob had said, what *a vine let loose* is, that is, one stripped of the bonds of death through the grace of faith: in which is signified the people of God, called to the liberty of faith and the richness of grace, spread through the whole world, which clothes the yoke of Christ with good fruit, and encircles the wood of that true vine, that is, the mysteries of the Lord's cross, and does not fear the peril of confessing him: but rather, even when set in persecutions, glories in the name of Christ. [1898C]

## 27.

Now these strong men hold swords in their hands, which they do not refuse for the name of Christ, or by which they distinguish bodily things from spiritual, the shadow from the truth. *For the word of God is living and effectual, and more piercing than any two-edged sword, and reaching unto the division of the soul and the spirit, of the joints also and of the marrow, and a discerner of the thoughts and intentions of the heart: and there is no creature invisible in his sight, but all things are naked and open to his eyes [n: (Hebr. IV, 12 et 13)]*. For the word of God is a sword twice sharpened. There are also two other swords in the Church, of which it is said: *Lord, behold, here are two swords,* namely of the churchly power and of the worldly, against which a false mercy fights.
