## 33.

*[n: (Vers. 12.)] A garden enclosed, my sister, bride, a garden enclosed, a fountain sealed. Thy shoots,* etc. Because in gardens of this kind the image of God, impressed by the seals, shines back in the wave of the unmixed fountain, nor are the streams, bespattered with mire, disturbed by the wallowing-places of spiritual beasts. Hence that modesty, hedged about by a wall-like spirit, is shut in, lest it lie open to plunder. And so, like a garden inaccessible to thieves, it is redolent of the vine, it breathes the olive, it shines with the rose; that in the vine religion, in the olive peace, in the rose the modesty of consecrated virginity may grow. This is the odor which Jacob the patriarch breathed, when he deserved to hear: *Behold the odor of my son, as the odor of a full field [n: (Genes. XXVII, 27)]*. For although the field of the holy patriarch was full of almost all fruits, yet he brought forth crops by a greater [1910C] labor of virtue, this one flowers. Gird thyself therefore, virgin, and if thou wishest such a garden to breathe upon thee, shut it with the prophetic precepts: *Set a guard to thy mouth, and a door of enclosure to thy lips [n: (Psal. CXL, 3)]*. [var: Vulg. *Pone, Domine, custodiam ori meo, et ostium circumstantiae labiis meis*, "set, O Lord, a guard to my mouth"]

## 34.

*A garden enclosed, my sister, bride, a garden enclosed, a fountain sealed; thy shoots a paradise of pomegranates, with the fruit of the apples of Cyprus.* [var: Vulg. *cum pomorum fructibus, cypri cum nardo*, "with the fruits of apples, cypress with spikenard"] The Bride is praised in that she is a garden, having in herself the odor of that full field, of which Isaac says: *The odor of my son, as the odor of a full field [n: (Genes. XXVII, 27)]*. A good soul therefore breathes out the odors of justice. And perhaps the field is the patriarch, the garden is the soul of some lesser one, as it were a portion of the field: and *a garden enclosed,* lest it be assailed by beasts: and *a fountain sealed,* she who by the integrity of the seal [1910D] and by the perseverance of faith has washed away her own sins. For he who has received from the Church; has that which he may refer to the grace of virginity; in that, placed in the paradise of delight, she takes spiritual fruits without labor, so that the souls of the patriarchs confer their own fruits upon her by a certain rustic labor of their own soul, whereby she may be able to take perpetual sweetness: who is rightly called a fountain sealed, in that the image of the invisible God is expressed in her.

## 35.

*A garden enclosed, my sister, bride, a garden enclosed, a fountain sealed.* All the teachers agree [1911A] upon paradise, and upon the tree of life rooted there and the tree of knowledge, by which one may discern good and evil: and the other trees full of vigor, full of quickening, breathing and rational. From which it is gathered that paradise itself can be seen as earthly, not in any soil, but in our ruling part, by which the soul is animated and quickened with virtues and with the infusion of the Holy Spirit. In short Solomon in the spirit plainly declared that paradise is in man. And because he expresses the mysteries either of the soul and the Word, or of Christ and the Church; therefore he says of the virgin soul or the Church, whom he wished to assign as a chaste virgin to Christ: *A paradise enclosed, my sister, bride, a paradise enclosed, a fountain sealed.* Paradise in Greek is in Latin called a garden. In short Susanna was in a paradise, and so it is read in Latin: and Adam was in [1911B] paradise, and so we read. Therefore let it not disturb thee that some Latin codices have *hortum*, others *paradisum*. There then is the chaste wife, where the virgin also is. Although the virgin, set apart, has her own enclosures and seals, yet both are in paradise; that against the heats of the body and the burning of the flesh she may be cooled by the shades of the virtues.

## 36.

Therefore paradise is in our ruling part, growing wild with the nurseries of very many opinions, in which God chiefly set the tree of life, that is, the root of piety. For this is the substance of our life, if we render to the Lord our God *1586* the worship due to him. He set also a seed-plot of the knowledge of good and evil; for man alone among the other earthly living creatures has the knowledge of good and evil. There too are other [1911C] divers nurseries, whose fruits are the virtues. But because the affection of man, capable of knowledge, was known to God, that it would sooner incline to cunning than to the height of prudence; nor could the quality of his own work lie hidden from him, the judge who set fixed bounds in our soul: he willed to cast out cunning from paradise, as the provident author of our salvation, and to pour in the pursuit of life and the discipline of piety. And therefore he commanded man that of every tree which is in paradise he should taste, but that of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil he should not taste. But because every creature is liable to passion, therefore delight crept into human affections as by a serpent's gliding.

## 37.

And so not without reason did holy Moses figure delight under the likeness of a serpent; for it is prone [1911D] upon its belly like a serpent, not walking upon feet, nor raised upon any legs: but slippery, as it were, with the winding bend of its whole body. Earth is its food, as it is the serpent's, since it knows no heavenly meat; for it feeds upon bodily things, and is changed into various kinds of desires, and is bent into tortuous windings. It has venom in its teeth, with which every luxurious man disembowels himself, the glutton slays himself, the squanderer destroys himself. How many have wines burst, drunkenness undone, indigestion distended! Now I understand for what cause the Lord God breathed into the face of man; for there are all the senses, there the seat and the enticement of delight, in the eyes, the ears, the nostrils, and in the mouth; that he might make our senses stronger [1912A] against delight. This then poured cunning into us as the serpent did; for it is not delight, but labor and long meditation with the grace of God, that gives perfect prudence. Yet because the inheritance of the human race is wrapped in the deceits of the serpent, let us in this follow the serpent's cunning, that we cast not our head into dangers, but keep it whole before all else; and our head is Christ. Let this remain unharmed, that the serpent's venoms may not be able to hurt us: for wisdom is good with an inheritance, that is, with faith; since there is an inheritance of those who believe in the Lord.

## 38.

But if the first man, who spoke with God, set in paradise, could fall so easily, created from virgin earth, which had been formed and created at the word of God in a fresh springing forth, that earth not yet clotted with parricidal [1912B] blood and the blood of the rest, not yet polluted with crimes and disgrace, our flesh not yet condemned by the curse of a liable inheritance: how much more easily afterwards, the road to sinning being slippery, did it bring a greater downfall upon the human race, when the worse succeeded the more tolerable through the turns of generation! For if the magnet stone has such force of nature that it draws iron to itself, and pours itself over into its likeness; so that, when many who wished to make trial have brought several iron rings to that stone, it holds them all in like manner: then if to that ring to which the stone has clung thou bring another ring, and again set one under each in order; although the force of nature from that stone penetrates them all in order, yet it binds the earlier ones with a more vehement bond, the later with a slacker: [1912C] how much more has the condition and nature of the human race fallen from a purer state into a worse, the more wicked the state it has reached! For if nature is diminished through those kinds of things which are not capable of guilt: how much more is its strength blunted through minds and limbs polluted with the stain of crimes! Whence, because when malice prevailed innocence had been abolished, there was none that did goodness, there was not so much as one. The Lord therefore came, to reform the grace of nature, nay rather to increase it; that where sin had superabounded, grace might superabound.

## 39.

It is clear therefore that God is the author of man, and that there is one God, not many gods, but one who wrought the world, and one world, not many worlds, as the philosophers say. First then he created the world, [1912D] then the inhabitant of the world, to whom the whole world should be a fatherland: for if today the wise man goes anywhere, everywhere he is a citizen, everywhere he understands what is his own, nowhere does he judge himself a stranger, nowhere a guest: how much more was that first man an inhabitant of the whole world, and, as it is said in Greek, *cosmopolites*, a fresh *1587* converser with God, an assiduous citizen among the saints! planted about with virtues, set over all living creatures of the land, of the sea, of the air, he counted the whole world his own possession, which the Lord watched over as his own work, nor did he, as a good parent and author, desert it. In short he so cherished the creature he had made, that he redeemed him when disowned, received him back when cast out, raised him up when dead by the passion of his only-begotten Son. God therefore is the author of man, [1913A] and as a good workman he loves his own work, nor does the good father forsake him whom, like a rich householder, he has redeemed at the cost of his own inheritance.
