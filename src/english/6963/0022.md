## 40.

Let us beware therefore lest that woman, that is, the passion of our senses, herself deceived and mocked by delight, make effeminate that man, that is, the νοῦν, and draw him, circumscribed, into her own laws and judgment. Let us flee delight as a serpent, it has many arts and chiefly in man. For other living creatures are caught by greediness for food; man, since he has more senses of the eyes and of the ears, has by so much the greater dangers. And so beware lest the vigor of thy mind, bent by a certain coupling with bodily pleasure, be softened, and be dissolved into all its embraces, and open its fountain, which ought to be closed and hedged about by earnestness of purpose [1913B] and by the consideration of reason: *For a garden enclosed, a fountain sealed.* For when the sense of the mind is unstrung, bodily delights pour themselves forth, exceedingly destructive, and flowing on into an appetite full of grave danger; and if the considered guard of a living mind had remained, it would have reined them in.

## 41.

*[n: (Vers. 3, 4.)] Thy shoots,* he says, *are a paradise of pomegranates with the fruit of apples, cypress with spikenard, spikenard and saffron, sweet cane and cinnamon with all the trees of Lebanon, myrrh and aloes with all the chief ointments.* The gifts of the soul are praised which were sent by the Bridegroom, with which she came dowered (and to God the good odors are those of a devout soul), myrrh, aloes, saffron and the rest, with which the grace of the gardens breathes, and the stench of sins is done away. And so, made secure by so great a proclamation, [1913C] she asks that the north wind, that heavy wind, be still, lest it shake off the flowers: that the south wind blow, that is, that the winter as it were pass by, and that the mildness of a softer breath come as spring. She invites the Bridegroom into her garden, the bridegroom comes down, and delighted by the diversity of its fruits, he rejoices that he has found a stronger food, and has found a sweeter also. For there is as it were a certain bread of the Word and a honey, one discourse more vehement, another persuasive. There is also one faith more fervent, like wine; another clearer, as is the juice of milk. This food Christ feasts upon in us, this cup he drinks, and by the drunkenness of his draught he provokes us to make a passing over from lower things to better and best.

## 42.

The soul hearing these things drank in the drunkenness of the heavenly mysteries, and as though lulled to sleep by wine, and set as it were in [1913D] ecstasy or stupor, says: *I sleep, and my heart waketh [n: (Cant. V, 2)]*. Then, struck by the light of the Word present, she is roused by the Word. And this is the fourth advance of the soul. For first, impatient of love, and not bearing the Word's delays, she asked that she might merit kisses, and merited to see him whom she desired. Secondly, brought into the king's chamber, while she exchanged mutual speech, she rested in his shadow, and suddenly the Word departed from her in mid-discourse; yet he was not long absent from her as she sought him, but came leaping upon the mountains and skipping over the hills. And not long after, like [1914A] a roe or a young hart of the stags, while he speaks to his beloved, he leapt away and left her. Thirdly, when in her bed and by night, in the city and the market place, in the streets, she did not find him whom she sought, and at length grace by her prayers called back the one who was seeking, so far even that she was called nearer by the bridegroom. Fourthly, she herself, now sleeping, is roused by him, although she was waking in heart; so that at once she heard the voice of him that knocked: but having suffered delay, while she rises (because she could not grasp the swiftness of the Word), while she opens the door, the Word passed by, and she herself went out at his word: and having sought him through wounds, but the wounds of love, at last she scarcely found him, and held him, that she might not afterwards lose him. In sum I have touched upon these things in a compendious discourse, now let us examine them one by one.

## 43.

*[n: (Vers. 15.)] A fountain of gardens, a well of living [1914B] waters, which flow with force from Lebanon.* Thou hast these things in Solomon, since his Proverbs are moral, Ecclesiastes natural, in which he as it were despises the vanities of this world: mystical is his Song *1588* of Songs. Thou hast it also in the prophet: *Sow for yourselves unto justice, gather the vintage unto the fruit of life, light for yourselves the light of knowledge [n: (Ose. X, 12)]*; [var: Vulg. *Seminate vobis in justitia, et metite in ore misericordiae; innovate vobis novale*, "sow for yourselves in justice, and reap in the mouth of mercy; break up your fallow ground"] for this is the light of knowledge, to have the perfection of charity. And therefore it is said: *Fear not [n: (Joan. XII, 15)]*; [var: Vulg. *Noli timere, filia Sion*, "fear not, daughter of Sion"] for charity casteth fear out of doors.

## 44.

But that we may know that Solomon so interpreted these three wells, which Jacob dug, as to refer them to moral and natural and mystical teaching, in each of his books, which he wrote concerning moral and natural or mystical things, he set these wells. [1914C] For in the Proverbs too, when he said that the appearance of worldly enticement must be turned aside, he says: *Drink water out of thine own vessels, and out of the fountains of thine own wells, and let the waters overflow for thee out of thine own fountain [n: (Prov. V, 15)]*. [var: Vulg. *Bibe aquam de cisterna tua, et fluenta putei tui*, "drink water out of thy own cistern, and the streams of thy own well"] And below: *Let the fountain of thy water be thine own, and rejoice with the wife who is from thy youth [n: (Ibid., 18)]*; [var: Vulg. *Sit vena tua benedicta, et laetare cum muliere adolescentiae tuae*, "let thy vein be blessed, and rejoice with the wife of thy youth"] in that true wisdom is a remedy for us against the temptations of the world. Moral teaching also, which may wash away with its own watering the image of worldly pleasure smeared with certain harlot's paints, and wipe it off with the stream of its own fountain.

## 45.

Of natural things too thou hast the same in Ecclesiastes: *I made myself pools of waters, to water from them a sprouting wood [n: (Eccles. II, 6)]*. [var: Vulg. *Exstruxi mihi piscinas aquarum, ut irrigarem silvam lignorum germinantium*, "I made myself pools of water, to water the wood of sprouting trees"] And let it not move thee that he put pools for a well, since Moses too spoke of a well of breadth; because he is loosed from all anxiety [1914D] and from straits who has passed beyond this world with a devout mind. Not without reason therefore has Ecclesiastes pools, who saw that there is no abundance under the sun: but if any wish to abound, let him abound in Christ.

## 46.

Of mystical things there remains for us also the well, which we find in the Song of Songs itself, Scripture saying: *A fountain of gardens, a well of living water, and coming down with force from Lebanon.* For if thou pursue the depth of the mysteries, it seems to thee a well, as though mystical wisdom were set in the deep: but if thou wouldst draw the affluence of charity, which is greater and richer [1915A] than faith and hope, then it is a fountain for thee. For charity overflows, so that thou canst both draw it close at hand, and water with its affluence thy garden, abounding in spiritual fruits. And because he himself is a well of breadth who has charity; therefore he said that where charity is, there a great force comes down from Lebanon.

## 47.

But lest anything move thee, that he called the same thing both a well and a fountain; let the Gospel also instruct thee, in which it is written: *That Jesus came into a city of Samaria, which is called Sichar, near the estate which Jacob gave to Joseph his son [n: (Joan. IV, 5 et 6)]*. And the fountain of Jacob was there: *And being wearied,* he says, *he sat thus upon the well.* Whence we recognize there also that this well is referred to mystical teaching, because there that Samaritan woman, that is, a keeper (and a keeper of the heavenly [1915B] precepts) drew from that well the divine mysteries, knowing that God is a spirit, and is not adored in a place, but in spirit: and that the Messias comes, who is Christ. Having heard these things, that woman, who bears the figure of the Church, knew the sacraments of the Law and believed.

## 48.

*A fountain of gardens, a well of living waters, and a force coming down from Lebanon.* These forces the Church has drawn down from Lebanon, by this force sins are washed away, by this force of the pure fountain of the Holy Spirit the Bride came from Lebanon, and from the beginning of faith she passed over the world, and passed through to the kingdom. To some spiritual grace is a fountain, to others a well, according to our capacity: to some a garden enclosed, a fountain sealed, to others a fountain of gardens, which is numbered in the Church's dowry: to others a force coming down from Lebanon, [1915C] and a great force which never fails. For the breasts fail not from the rock, nor the snow from Lebanon, nor the water which is borne by a strong wind to the virgin Jerusalem.
