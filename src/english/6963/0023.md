## 49.

The force came down from Lebanon, when, the apostles and many believers being gathered into one, there was suddenly a sound from heaven, as though a spirit were borne with great violence, and they were all filled with the Holy Spirit, who granted diversities of tongues [n: (Act. II, 1, 3)]. A good force, which knows not how to hurt, but knows how to fill. If anyone therefore wishes to merit this force of grace coming upon him from heaven, let him also himself go down with his eyes into the runnings of waters. He who has first poured out this force will merit that one. She went down with her eyes into these channels of waters, she who in the Gospel watered the Lord's feet with tears [1915D] [n: (Luc. VII, 38)], and therefore by the price of her faith bought health for her soul and for her body, flowing now no longer with blood, but with spiritual grace.

*1589.* 50. David the prophet therefore went down, and therefore he brought back grace out of sin. He went down into the channels of waters, that is, he filled them, and heaped up the streams of the running waters with his own tears, or filled the empty and void channels with weeping alone. Or, because he shows certain senses of moral utterance, he went down into the channels of waters, he passed over them. And we might say, he transcended [1916A] them, and passed above them: but the force of the utterance is lessened, whereby a greater force of the affluence coming down than of that going up is expressed. See, I pray, what the words have from usage, that the prophetic discourse may not lose its own force, although usage itself has been accustomed, with greater fitness, to serve the sense of the writers.

## 51.

*A well of living waters;* for a well, if thou draw nothing from it, is easily corrupted by inert idleness and degenerate mould: but when it is used it grows bright to the sight, sweet to the drinking. So too a heap of riches, sandy in its pile, is comely in use, but in idleness is held useless. Draw off therefore something from this well: *For water quencheth a burning fire, and alms resisteth sins [n: (Eccli. III, 33)]*. But standing water quickly breeds worms: let not thy treasure stand, lest thy fire stand. [1916B] It will stand in thee, unless thou turn it away by the works of thy mercy.

## 52.

*[n: (Vers. 16.)] Arise, north wind, and come, south wind, breathe upon my garden, and my spices flow forth.* [var: Vulg. *Surge, aquilo, et veni, auster; perfla hortum meum, et fluant aromata illius*, "arise, north wind… and let the spices thereof flow"] Thou hast heard, bride of Christ, that the beauty of thy virtues pleases him; thou hast heard that he has preferred the spices of thy garments, that is, the good odor of integrity, above all odors. Thou hast heard that thou art a garden enclosed, filled with the produce of sweet fruit-trees. Ask therefore that the Holy Spirit may breathe upon thee, may breathe above thy bed, and may heap up the odor of a devout mind and of spiritual grace. Rouse the Holy Spirit, saying: *Arise, north wind, and come, south wind; breathe upon my garden, and let my spices flow forth. Let my kinsman come down into his garden, and eat the fruit of his fruit-trees.* [1916C] The garden of the Word is the affection of the soul in its spring, and in the fruit-trees is the fruit of virtue. He comes therefore, and whether thou eat or drink, if thou callest upon Christ, he is present, saying: *Come, eat my loaves, and drink my wine [n: (Prov. IX, 5)]*. [var: Vulg. *Venite, comedite panem meum, et bibite vinum quod miscui vobis*, "come, eat my bread, and drink the wine which I have mingled for you"]

## 53.

*Arise,* he says, *north wind, and come, south wind; breathe upon my garden, and let my spices flow forth.* For from all parts of the world the odor of holy religion has grown, with which the limbs of the beloved virgin have been fragrant. Whence the Church also, keeping the loftiness of the heavenly mysteries, casts from herself the heavier storms of the wind, and invites the sweetness of springlike grace; and knowing that her garden cannot displease Christ, she calls her Bridegroom: *Arise,* he says, *north wind, and come, south wind.* Very many take these words thus, as though the north wind were cast out and the south wind invited. [1916D] But if they so take it, the icy harshness of unbelief is driven out from the Church, lest our flight be in winter or on the sabbath, and the southern springtime mildness is invited.

## 54.

Or certainly: *Arise, north wind,* that is, *rise, thou that sleepest, and arise from the dead [n: (Ephes. V, 14)]*. Thou people of the nations, who hast long slept before, awake at last, and Christ shall shine upon thee. Lastly all are invited to the Church, both the people of the Synagogue and that of the Gentiles: but the Synagogue's first, because the apostles from among the Jews believed first [1917A] and through them afterwards the peoples of the nations were gathered. See therefore our sun coming to the south, afterwards wheeling round to the north. *Jerusalem, Jerusalem:* he came indeed to her, whom he even deigned to call: but this Jerusalem is the earthly one, which slays [sic: *eccidit*] the prophets, that is, the Synagogue of the Jews: *How often,* he says, *would I have gathered thy children, as a hen her chickens, and thou wouldest not? Behold your house shall be left desolate [n: (Matth. XXIII, 37 et 38)]*. He wheeled therefore to the nations: and in wheeling the Spirit of God wheeled, and was turned into his own circles, that God might become all in all.

## CHAPTER FIVE.



## 1.

*[n: (Vers. 1.)] Let my beloved come into my garden, and eat the fruit of his apples.* [var: Vulg. *Veniat dilectus meus in hortum suum*, "let my beloved come into HIS garden"] Hence Plato framed for himself that garden [1917B] which he called in one place the garden of Jove, in another the garden of the mind; for he called Jove both God and mind. Into this, he says, the soul entered, which he names Venus, that she might fill herself with the abundance and riches of the garden, in which, filled with drink, she might lie, with a drink that poured out nectar. This therefore he framed out of the book of the Songs; in that the soul cleaving to God has entered into the garden of the mind, in which there was an abundance *1590* of divers virtues, and the flowers of discourses. And who does not know that out of that paradise, which we read in Genesis to have the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil [n: (Gen. II, 8)], and the other trees, he thought an abundance of virtues was to be transferred, and to be planted in the garden of the mind?

## 2.

Which garden in the Song of Songs signifies the soul, or the soul herself; for so it is written: [1917C] *A garden enclosed, bride, my sister, a garden enclosed, a fountain sealed.* And below the soul says: *Arise, north wind, and come, south wind; blow through my garden, and let thine ointments flow down, let my brother come down into his garden [n: (Cant. IV, 12 et 13)]*. How much more beautiful is this, that the soul adorned with the flowers of the virtues should be a garden; or should have in herself a sprouting paradise, into which garden she invites the Word of God to come down; that that soul, watered by the shower of the heavenly Word and by his abundance, may bear fruit! But the Word of God is fed with the virtues of the soul, as often as he has found the soul obedient to him and rich: and he plucks her fruits, and is delighted with them. And when the Word of God has come down into her, the ointments of healthful words flow down from her, and the breathings of divers [1917D] graces glow, redolent far and wide. Whence the Bridegroom says (and the Bridegroom of the soul is God the Word, to whom the soul is joined by a certain lawful covenant of wedlock):

## 3.

*[n: (Vers. 2.)] I have entered into my garden, my sister, bride; I have gathered my myrrh with my ointments, I have eaten my bread with my honey, I have drunk my wine with my milk. Eat, my neighbors, and drink and be drunken, my brethren. I sleep, and my heart waketh.* [var: Vulg. *Veni in hortum meum… messui myrrham meam cum aromatibus meis; comedi favum cum melle meo… Comedite, amici… carissimi*, "I came into my garden… I reaped my myrrh with my spices; I ate the honeycomb… eat, friends… dearest ones"] Let us learn what fruits or foods God feasts upon, or in what he is delighted. He is delighted in this, if any [1918A] mortify his own sin, blot out his own fault, bury and abolish his iniquities; for myrrh is the burial of the dead, and dead are the sins which cannot have the sweetness of life. And certain wounds of offenses are bathed with the ointments of the divine word, and are healed with the stronger food of the word as with bread, and with sweeter discourse as with honey. And Solomon teaches elsewhere too that words are foods, saying: *Good words are honeycombs of honey [n: (Prov. XVI, 24)]*. [var: Vulg. *Favus mellis composita verba*, "well-ordered words are a honeycomb of honey"] In that garden therefore there are good words, one that may restrain fault, another that may rebuke iniquity, another that may make insolence die, and as it were bury it, when one who has been rebuked renounces his errors.

## 4.

There is also a stronger word, which strengthens the heart of man [1918B] with the more powerful nourishments of the heavenly Scripture. *I have eaten the honeycomb with my honey, I have drunk my wine with my milk.* For there is a persuasive word, sweet as honey, and yet in its very sweetness pricking the conscience of the sinner. There is also a word of more fervent spirit, which makes drunken like wine, and gladdens the heart of man. There is finally a milky word, pure and white. These foods of sweet and useful words the Bridegroom bids his neighbors feast upon: *Eat, my neighbors, and drink, and be drunken, my brethren.* And the neighbors are those who follow him, and are present at his marriage. With which food and drink (for every man drinks [n: (Prov. V, 15)] water out of his own vessels, and out of the fountains of his own wells) the soul, filled and made drunken, was sleeping to the world, was waking to God. And therefore, as what follows teaches, God the Word demanded that her door be opened to him, that he might fill her with his entering. [1918C]
