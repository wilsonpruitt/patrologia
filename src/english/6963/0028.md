## 47.

The keepers of the walls found her. Perhaps there are also other keepers, whom we ought rather to understand. For there is a city which does not have the gates of its walls shut, of which it is said: *And the gates thereof shall not be shut by day [n: (Apoc. XXI, 25)]*; for night shall be no more in it, the nations shall bring glory and honor into it. That therefore is the city Jerusalem which is in heaven, within which thou art now kept as one perfect and unspotted; for no common thing enters into it. Chastity which is written in the book of life is not common, modesty [1930B] is not common.

## 48.

If therefore we have found the city, let us enter into it, let us see its light, let us see the walls, let us see the foundations of the walls, let us see also the keepers of the walls. But how shall we enter into it? In this city is life, and there is one way which leads to life. The way therefore is Christ; let us therefore follow Christ. But that city is itself in heaven; how then shall we ascend to heaven? The Evangelist teaches, who says: *And he led me forth in the spirit into a mountain great and high, and showed me the holy city Jerusalem coming down from heaven [n: (Apoc. 21, 10)]*. [var: Vulg. *sustulit me in spiritu*, "he took me up in the spirit"] Let us therefore ascend in the spirit, because the flesh cannot ascend to it. Let us ascend meanwhile into heaven, that afterwards it may come down from heaven to us: in which the light is like [1930C] a most precious stone, and as of the jasper stone and of crystal it has a wall great and high.

## 49.

Thou hast learned of the light, thou hast learned of the wall; learn of the gates, learn of the keepers. *It has,* he says, *twelve gates, and in the gates twelve angels, upon which the names of the twelve tribes of the children of Israel are written above [n: (Ibid., 12)]*. In the gates the names of the patriarchs are contained, in the wall those of the apostles; for the foundations of the city are the apostles, and the corner-stone is Christ, in whom the whole structure rises up. God is without, God is within, God is everywhere: *For the city has,* he says, *the majesty of God [n: (Ibid., 11)]*. [var: Vulg. *habentem claritatem Dei*, "having the brightness of God"] Therefore you also, holy virgins, and whosoever of you are just, and bear the unspotted chastity of the soul, are fellow-citizens of the saints and of the household of God. But then shall you possess that nobility of your fatherland, if you seek Christ within the enclosures of that city, having entered [1930D] by faith and by precious acts, made bright by the light of the patriarchs, founded upon the apostles, dwelling among the angels.

## 50.

How therefore are those keepers angels, who take away the cloak of a chaste soul? The cloak of virgins is one thing, that of a girl who haunts the streets another. She who seeks Christ in the market has lost even the cloak she had. For prudence is possessed not in the market, not in the streets, but in the Church. And perhaps, that we may come into favor with them also, and may teach that God is merciful to all — since they too [1931A] at some time find Christ, if only they seek continually — this cloak is the garment of the body. Therefore he who has sought Christ in his chamber, if only he has so sought as he who said: *So have I been mindful of thee upon my bed [n: (Ps. LXII, 7)]*; [var: Vulg. *Si memor fui tui*, "If I have been mindful of thee"] if he has sought him in the nights, according to what is written: *In the nights lift up your hands to the holy places [n: (Ps. CXXXII, 2)]*: if he has sought him in the city, in the market and in the streets, in the city of our God: in the market perhaps *1599* in which that judge of divine right sits, in the streets from which were gathered those who came together to the Lord's supper — he can, while he seeks long, meet with the angels who keep the city of God. Nay more, from the heavenly nature of the keepers we can understand the city to be heavenly, and the market a heavenly market of everlasting justice, and the streets not cheap streets, but perhaps those in which [1931B] that fountain was wont to overflow, of which it is written: *Let thy waters overflow from thy fountain, and let thy waters be poured out in thy streets [n: (Prov. V, 16)]*. [var: Vulg. *Deriventur fontes tui foras, et in plateis aquas tuas divide*, "Let thy fountains be conveyed abroad, and divide thy waters in the streets"] He therefore who so seeks Christ comes to the angels.

## 51.

But if it is by good merits that one comes to the angels, why is he who has come wounded? There is also a good sword, and the wound of that sword is good. The Word of God wounds, but does not ulcerate. There is a wound of good love, there are wounds of charity; and therefore she said: *I am of wounded charity [n: (Cant. II, 5)]*. [var: Vulg. *quia amore langueo*, "for I languish with love"] She who is perfect is wounded with charity. Good therefore are the wounds of the Word, good are the wounds of the lover: *For more useful are the wounds of a friend than the willing kisses of an enemy [n: (Prov. XXVII, 6)]*. [var: Vulg. *Meliora sunt vulnera diligentis quam fraudulenta oscula odientis*, "Better are the wounds of one that loves than the deceitful kisses of one that hates"] Of wounded charity is Rebecca, who, leaving her parents, journeyed to her bridegroom. Of wounded charity [1931C] is Rachel, who was jealous of her sister and loved her husband; for she, still barren, envied her sister in that she abounded in sons, because she bore the type of the Church, to whom it is said: *Rejoice, thou barren that bearest not, break forth and cry, thou that travailest not [n: (Esai. 54, 1)]*. [var: Vulg. *Lauda, sterilis, quae non paris; decanta laudem, et hinni, quae non pariebas*, "Praise, O thou barren that bearest not; sing forth praise and cry out, thou that didst not bear"]

## 52.

The keepers therefore found her and wounded her, and took her cloak from her: that is, they carried off the wrapping of bodily action, that the naked simplicity of the mind might seek Christ; because no one clothed with the garment of philosophy, that is, in the habit of worldly wisdom, can see Christ. And well is the garment of philosophy taken from her, lest anyone despoil her through philosophy. Well is the cloak taken from her who draws near to Christ, that, being about to see God, she may walk with a clean heart: for *Blessed are the clean of heart, for [1931D] they shall see God [n: (Matth. V, 8)]*. In short, when she had cleansed her heart, she found the Word, she saw God. Seek him therefore, O virgin — nay rather, let us all seek him; for the soul has no sex. But perhaps it received a feminine name for this reason, that a more violent heat of the body drives it: while it soothes the onset of the flesh and of its own love by a certain soft and gentle reasoning. Therefore by prayers and supplications we ought to invite God, that he may deign to breathe upon us like a good south wind. And let him breathe upon us the breeze of the heavenly Word: which is wont to stir the fruit-bearing trees not with a heavy wind, but with a light breathing and a soft blast. [1932A]

## 53.

*They took away my cloak from me.* A good garment is the eternity of life; this cloak of the Bride the keepers of the walls wished to take away, of which the first man had been stripped; but the soul devoted to God, by holding fast the long-sought and not letting go her beloved, clothed herself with the precious wrapping of divine charity. Blessed therefore are they who are clothed with such a cloak, and have deserved a garment of this kind by the observance of the law; because they have not been forgetful of the law, but have done the things that were of the law.

## 54.

*[n: (Vers. 8.)] I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem, if you find my beloved, that you announce to him that I languish with love.* [var: Vulg. *ut nuntietis ei*, "that you tell him"] But what is this languor of the soul? The moral sense makes it needful that we remember the old history, which is of holy Moses, who asked God to show himself to him, and that he might see him face to face. Assuredly [1932B] the holy seer of the Lord knew that he could not see the invisible God face to face. But holy devotion oversteps measure, and he thought that even this was possible to God, that he should cause that which is incorporeal to be comprehended by bodily eyes. This error is not blameworthy, but rather a pleasing and insatiable desire, in that he longed to hold his Lord as it were with the hand, and to see him with the gaze of the eyes. For he knew that man had been made to the image and likeness of God; for when, chosen by the Lord God to free the people, he was filled with the spirit of wisdom, he had seen that angel, and his face in glory; in short, he was made afraid by his shining light, and saw the bush burning and not burnt up. By sight he took in the brightness, and marvelling at it he drew near; provoked by desire and by grace, that he might more diligently [1932C] look upon that brightness in the bush. And so, if he received so great an ardor and underwent so great a desire when he had seen the angel in the flame of fire out of the bush, that, though seized with dread he dared not look, yet he wished to look: how much more did he desire to see the countenance of the Lord bodily, saying within himself: That countenance full of light, *1600* full of grace, full of virtue, full of divinity — what shall I say it is? I can say or feel no more of God; for when a man has finished, then he begins; and when he has made an end, then shall he be at a loss, as it is written, because *the eternal majesty of God is incomprehensible [n: (Eccles. XVIII, 6)]*.
