## 76.

*[n: (Vers. 15.)] His appearance is as of Lebanon, choice as the cedars.* He dwells in the heights, and looks upon lowly things, because his appearance is like the cedar of Lebanon, which sets its foliage among the clouds and its root in the earth; for its beginning is from heaven, and its hinder parts in the earth have brought forth fruits nearest to heaven. This kind of tree never loses its greenness, in winter as in summer it feeds its foliage, nor is it changed with a different color: this tree alone the wind never despoils of its honor: this alone is never stripped of its old covering, nor clothed with a new bloom. So apostolic grace too knows no failing, and [1938C] blossoms with a perpetual comeliness of its own. The soul therefore knows not how to be corrupted, which is vigorous with flourishing members, and always upholds with patient greatness of heart the summits of justice and of the other virtues; and therefore it neither flows away nor fails, because in it there is nothing ruinous or slack, nothing shifting, nothing slippery, nothing which could be poured out of it by a fault of speech.

## 77.

*[n: (Vers. 16.)] His throat is most sweet, and he is wholly desire. The judgments of the Lord are true, justified in themselves, to be desired above gold and much precious stone [n: (Psal. XVIII, 10 et 11)]*. [var: Vulg. Cant 5:16 *et totus desiderabilis*, "and he is wholly desirable"] What is severe in others, in Christ is sweet, in Christ is pleasant; because he himself is pleasant: *In short, taste and see that the Lord is sweet [n: (Psal. XXIII, 9)]*. Sweet are the judgments to him who confesses, because he himself says: *I am he, I am he, who blot out [1938D] thine iniquities, and I will not be mindful of them: but be thou mindful, and let us be judged. Tell thine iniquities, that thou mayest be justified [n: (Esai. XLIII, 25 et 26)]*. [var: Vulg. *Ego sum, ego sum ipse qui deleo iniquitates tuas propter me, et peccatorum tuorum non recordabor. Reduc me in memoriam, et judicemur simul: narra si quid habes ut justificeris*] Sweet are the judgments to him who does penance, because he himself said: *There shall be joy over one sinner doing penance, than over ninety and nine just who need not penance [n: (Luc. XV, 7)]*. If therefore the judgments of the Lord are sweet, let us labor to receive the fruit of his sweetness. *1604* Wilt thou know how sweet the judgments of the Lord are? *He who believes in him is not judged [n: (Joan. III, 18)]*. Sweet therefore are the judgments to them that believe: but they who [1939A] believe not have been condemned not by the judgment of Christ, who came not to judge the world but to save and redeem it; rather they have undergone the judgment of their own impiety, who would not believe unto the remission of sins. For they cannot belong to the benefit of him whom they have refused to acknowledge. Therefore they who have not believed in him are seen to be unworthy of his judgment. For learn what his judgment is, since he himself says: *And this is the judgment, whereby the light came into this world [n: (Ibid. 19)]*. Sweet therefore is that which is light, sweet is the judgment which mercy goes before; for so it is written, *Mercy and judgment will I sing to thee, O Lord [n: (Psal. C, 1)]*. Sweet also and most pleasant is the throat, whence this mercy goes before and this judgment.

## 78.

*[n: (Vers. 16.)] And he is wholly desirable. Shapely in form [1939B] above the sons of men [n: (Psal. XLIV, 3)]*. He was indeed man according to the flesh, but beyond man, according to his divine working. In short, when he touched the leper, he was seen to be man: but beyond man, when he cleansed him. And when he wept for Lazarus dead, he wept as man: but he was above man, when he bade the dead man come forth with his feet bound. He was seen to be man, when he hung upon the cross: but above man, when, the tombs being unbarred, he raised the dead. Nor let the venom of Apollinaris flatter itself, because it is read thus: *And in appearance found as a man [n: (Philip. II, 7)]*; [var: Vulg. *et habitu inventus ut homo*, "and in habit found as a man"] for Jesus was not denied to be man, since elsewhere Paul himself says of him: *The mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus [n: (I Tim. II, 5)]*; but he was confirmed. For it is the usage and custom of Scripture so to signify, as we read also in the Gospel: [1939C] *And we saw his glory, the glory as of the only-begotten of the Father [n: (Joan. I)]*. As therefore he is called "as the only-begotten," and yet the only-begotten Son of God is not denied; so also he is called man, and the perfection of man in him is not denied.

## 79.

Since therefore he was in the form of a servant, humbled even unto death, he was nevertheless in the glory of God. What then did servitude harm him? But he is read to have been made a servant, because he is read to have been made of a Virgin, and created in the flesh; for every creature serves, as the prophet says: *For all things serve thee.* This is the glory of Christ, that he took servitude upon himself in his own body, that he might restore liberty to all: he bore our sins, that he might take away the sin of the world: he was made a servant, sin, a curse, that thou mightest cease to be the servant of sin, and that he might absolve thee [1939D] from the curse of the divine sentence. He therefore took upon himself thy curse: for *Cursed is every one that hangeth upon a tree:* he was made a curse upon the cross, that thou mightest be blessed in the kingdom of God: he was dishonored and cheapened, and accounted as nothing: he said, *I have labored in vain;* through whom Paul deserved to say, *I have not labored in vain [n: (Philip. II, 16)]*; that he might confer upon his little servants the fruit of good work, and the glory of evangelical preaching, whereby all men might be released from the burden of labor.

## 80.

*[n: (Vers. 17.)] Such is my beloved, and my friend, O daughters of Jerusalem.* So the Church, or the [1940A] holy soul, mingled with the daughters of that heavenly city, seeks the Word, and by seeking stirs up his charity within herself: while they indeed ask and say: *Whither is thy beloved gone, O most beautiful of women?* Whither has thy beloved turned aside, that we may seek him with thee?

## CAPUT SEXTUM.



## 1.

*[n: (Vers. 1.)] My beloved,* she says, *is gone down into his garden, to the little bed of spice, that he may feed in the gardens, and gather lilies.* She recognizes where she should seek the Word: she knows that he tarries among the prayers of the saints, and that he cleaves to them, and she understands that he feeds his Church, or the souls of his just ones, among the lilies. This mystery he showed thee in the Gospel, when on the sabbath he led his disciples through the sown fields. [1940B] Moses led the people of the Jews through the desert, Christ leads through the sown fields, Christ leads through the lilies; because through his passion the desert flowers as a lily. Let us follow then, that on the day of that great sabbath, in which is the great rest, we may gather fruits. And fear not that the Pharisees may accuse those who gather from the sown fields: even if they accuse, Christ excuses, and makes the souls which he wills, following him, like to that David who ate the loaves of proposition above the Law, already then conceiving in mind the prophetic sacraments of the new grace.

## 2.

*[n: (Vers. 2.)] I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine, who feeds among* *1605* *the lilies.* The utterance seems easy and common, but it belongs to few. For rare enough is he who can say: *I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine.* What is it to say, I am thine? For he says it who cleaves to [1940C] God with all his senses, who knows not how to think of anything else; he uses this utterance who can say to the Lord: *Show us the Father, and it sufficeth us [n: (Joan. XIV, 8)]*. Does the man greedy of money, of honor, of power use this utterance? For many it is not enough to know God — indeed for very many. So many peoples, so many nations, so many rich men think it poverty to serve the Lord; and he who is above all is to them a small and narrow thing: for them the Son of God, in whom are all things, is not enough. In short, that rich man in the Gospel to whom it was said: *If thou wilt be perfect, sell all that thou hast, and give to the poor [n: (Matth. XIX, 21)]*, [var: Vulg. *vade, vende quae habes*, "go, sell what thou hast" — without *omnia*] judged that God was not sufficient for him. In short, he was also made sorrowful, as though he were bidden to leave what was of more worth, and to choose what was of less. He therefore says, I am thine, who can say: *Behold, we have left [1940D] all things, and have followed thee [n: (Ibid., 27)]*.
