## 3.

This is the voice of the apostles, and yet not of all the apostles; for Judas too was an apostle, and reclined among the apostles at the banquet of Christ. He too was saying, I am thine; but with the voice, not with the heart. Satan came and entered into him, and began to say: He is not thine, Jesus, but he is mine. In short, he thinks of the things which are mine, he turns over in his breast the things which are mine: he banquets with thee, and feeds with me: from thee he received bread, from me money: with thee he drinks, and to me he sells thy blood: he is thine as apostle, and mine as hireling. The worldly man cannot say, I am thine; for he has [1941A] many masters. Lust comes and says: Thou art mine; because thou lustest after the things which I am, the things of the body: in the love of that young girl thou hast sold thyself to me: in the embrace of that harlot I have counted out the price for thee. Avarice comes and says: The silver and gold which thou hast is the price of thy servitude: the possession thou holdest is the purchase of thy right, the sale of thy liberty. Luxury comes and says: Thou art mine; the banquet of a single day is the price of thy life: that outlay of feasts is the bidding for thy head, it is the sum of thy contract. And what is worse, thou hast been bought at a dear price, thou art cheaper than thy food: thy table for one day is more precious than the life of thy whole time: among cups I have redeemed thee, among feasts I have acquired thee. Ambition comes and says to thee: Plainly thou art mine. Knowest thou not that I therefore made thee command others, that thou thyself [1941B] mightest serve me? Knowest thou not that I therefore conferred power upon thee, that I might subject thee to my power? Or art thou ignorant that it was said to the Lord Savior himself by the prince of this world, when he had shown him all the kingdoms of the world: *All these will I give thee, if falling down thou wilt adore me [n: (Matth. IV, 9)]*? Before all, then, he himself is made subject who wishes to have others subject. All the vices come, and each says: Thou art mine. He whom so many claim, how cheap a chattel is he! How therefore canst thou, who art of this sort, say to Christ: I am thine? He will answer thee: *Not whosoever saith to me, Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven [n: (Matth. VII, 21)]*: [var: Vulg. *Non omnis qui dicit mihi*, "Not every one that saith to me"] not whosoever says to me, I am thine, is mine.

## 4.

Plainly thou art mine, if conscience does not refute the voice, if thy mind or thy works do not disprove thy speech. [1941C] I do not deny him to be mine who does not deny himself, or at any rate if for my sake he denies his very self to himself. I will not have a little servant who serves very many masters. For how is he mine, if he says to me in word: I am thine; and denies it by his works, and by his deeds adjudges and binds himself to the devil? He is not mine whom lust inflames, because chastity is mine: he is not mine whom the care of despoiling lesser men drives about, because bounty is mine: he is not mine whom the breeze of fickleness disquiets, because tranquillity is mine. He is not mine who is drunken with the surfeit of wine, unclean with the filth of ambition, greedy of worldly glory, drunk unto peril, who cannot keep the track of sober moderation. I am peace, I know not how to quarrel: to what end is he mine, concerning whom the devil may come and say, He is mine; for to me he has bowed his neck, [1941D] I find more of my own in him: he claims thy name for himself, and my gift. He is not Christ's, therefore, save he who is a stranger to crime: he is not Christ's, save he who can always show himself Christ's little servant.

## 5.

*I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine, who feeds among the lilies.* She seems to say, among the beloved things of the beloved: *My secret to me, my secret to me.* For she had understood, in the ecstasy of her love, certain hidden mysteries of the faith, which she judged were not to be published even to the daughters of Jerusalem themselves. *It is good to hide the mystery* *1606* *of the king;* for he sins against God who thinks that the mysteries committed [1942A] to him are to be divulged to the unworthy. [var: Vulg. Tob 12:7 *Sacramentum regis abscondere bonum est*, "It is good to hide the sacrament of the king"] And so it is a danger not only to speak false things, but even true ones, if a man insinuates them to those to whom he ought not. Which fault is of four parts, either of flattery, or of avarice, or of boasting, or of incautious talkativeness; because while a man wishes to flatter the one to whom he speaks, he pours out the mystery: some too, from zeal for gain, pursue the wage of betrayal, so as to sell by speaking what ought to be covered with silence: others, that they may seem to know more, and may vaunt their own knowledge, open what they ought to conceal: very many, while they speak without judgment, let go a word which they cannot call back. Therefore a serious man is praised, and not an idle one. There was a man hiding his own words, as the apostle Paul, who knew what he should speak to individuals, and when he ought to speak anything. [1942B]

## 6.

*[n: (Vers. 3.)] Thou art well-pleasing, my sister, shapely as Jerusalem, a wonder as one set in order.* [var: Vulg. *Pulchra es, amica mea; suavis, et decora sicut Jerusalem; terribilis ut castrorum acies ordinata*, "Thou art beautiful, my love; sweet and comely as Jerusalem; terrible as an army set in array"] The truth of love is proved by its perpetuity. And so the Bride is praised by the Bridegroom, because she has sought him so well and so constantly. And therefore she is now not only called *sister*, but is even named *well-pleasing*, as being pleasing to him; because she has pleased the Father: and, *shapely as Jerusalem, a wonder as one set in order;* because she has all the mysteries of the eternal city: and is a wonder to all who see her; because she is full as equity is, and perfect: and, having borrowed her brightness from the light of the Word, while she ever strains toward it, she is even terrible, being advanced by a certain order to the summit of the virtues.

## 7.

*Thou art beautiful,* he says, *my love, sweet and comely,* etc. And truly beautiful is she who strains toward him of whom [1942C] it is said: *Shapely in form above the sons of men [n: (Psal. XLIV, 3)]*. Whence let us too strain our mind toward him as much as we can, and be in him. Let us hold in mind that which is beautiful, comely, good; that by his illumination and brightness our soul may become shapely, and our mind clear. For if our eyes, when they are overspread with any dimness, are fed with the greenness of the fields, and by the appearance of a grove or a shady hill drive off all the unevenness of a sickening gaze, and the very pupils and orbs seem to be colored with a certain healthful appearance: how much more does this eye of the mind, when it beholds that highest good, and is engaged in it, and is fed by it, grow bright and shine forth. *Beautiful as Jerusalem.* [var: Vulg. *decora sicut Jerusalem*, "comely as Jerusalem"] This therefore is not the beauty of a perishable body, to perish either by disease or by old age, but a beauty subject to no [1942D] mischances, never to die in the esteem of good deserts; since it is worthy to be compared now not with human things, but with heavenly, whose life she lives on earth. And therefore the Bridegroom says to the Bride, as to one already perfect:

## 8.

*[n: (Vers. 4.)] Turn away thine eyes from me, because they have made me fly away.* Strain not against me with too great devotion and faith. Thou hast passed beyond the possibility of thy nature and of thine own condition, because to gaze straight upon the inaccessible light is a heavy thing. *Turn away,* he says, *thine eyes from me:* for this reason, that she cannot bear the fullness of his divinity, and the splendor of the true light. [1943A]

## 9.

Yet we can also take it thus: *Turn away thine eyes from me;* even if thou art perfect, there are other souls still to be redeemed by me, others to be propped up. For thou liftest me up by seeing me: but I came down for this, that I might lift up all. Even if I have risen, and have the seat of the Father, yet I will not leave you orphans, destitute as of a father's protection, but by my presence I will strengthen you. Thou hast this written in the Gospel: *Behold, I am with you even to the consummation of the world [n: (Matth. XXVIII, 20)]*. Turn away then thine eyes from me, because thou liftest me up. For the more a man strains toward the Lord, so much the more does he lift up the Lord, and is himself lifted up. Whence he too says: *I will exalt thee, O Lord, because thou hast upheld me [n: (Psalm. XXIX, 1)]*. For the holy man exalts the Lord, the sinner humbles him. He wishes therefore that she turn away her eyes, lest, [1943B] beholding her, because she can now follow to higher things, he be lifted up and leave the other souls behind. Therefore in the Gospel also he showed his glory not to all the disciples, but to the more perfect [n: (Matth. XVII, 1)]. Set before thyself now some teacher who wishes to open an obscure matter to his hearers: how, even if he himself be powerful in speech and knowledge, he yet condescends to the ignorance of those who do not understand, and uses a simple and plainer and more familiar speech, that he may be able to be understood. If therefore there be among the hearers one quicker in sense, who can easily follow, he lifts him up and rouses him. Seeing this, the teacher calls him back, that he may rather allow the teacher to dwell upon lowlier *1607* and plainer things; whereby the rest also may be able to follow.
