## 10.

[n: (Vers. 5-7.)] The Bride is praised also because she is faithful, [1943C] because she is powerful in word, because she is fruitful with diverse fruits, because she is one dove, having the unity of the Spirit, in which is peace, which makes both one: and because she is not composed of the diverse elements of a distinct and warring nature. For what is so diverse as fire and water, air and earth, out of which the creature of our body consists? But every blessed soul is simple, which imitates him who says: *That all may be one, as thou, Father, in me, and I in thee, and that they themselves may be one in us [n: (Joan. XVII, 21)]*; for this is the consummation and the perfection, whence he added also: *That they may be one, as we also are one: and I in these, and thou in me, that they may be consummated in one [n: (Ibid., 23)]*. This therefore is the one and perfect dove, which, being simple and spiritual, is not disturbed by the passions of this body, in which [1943D] there are fightings without, fears within. In short, Scripture teaches us that by this word of unity concord and peace are signified, saying: *And the multitude of them that believed had one heart and one soul, and there was no separation at all among them [n: (Act. IV, 32)]*. [var: Vulg. *Multitudinis autem credentium erat cor unum et anima una*, without the final clause]

## 11.

Nor is the soul praised idly for its fruitfulness, not only because it is fruitful in virtues, but also because it has nothing of evil in itself; for that is comely and shapely in which there is nothing of evil. For that is comely which is good; but that which is uncomely, this is evil. Shapely is the fruitfulness of good works, contrary [1944A] therefore to shapeliness is barrenness; since in him who is deprived of shapeliness or comeliness there is every evil: but that which is evil, this is barren and unfruitful. And of this matter what plainer sign is there than the earth? For the earth which is good is fertile and fruitful: but that which is bad is meager and barren; but that which is fertile, this is also comely. For what is more beautiful than a full field, when the crop waves, when the apples redden, or when the garlands of grapes hang down, or the olive, laden with berries, bends over, or when the tops of the mountains and the low places of the valleys are clothed with green herbage?

## 12.

For God is the author of all good things: and the things which are his are assuredly all good, and there is no evil there; and if our mind abide in him, it knows no evil. The soul therefore which does not abide in God is [1944B] to itself the author of evils, and so it sins: but *the soul that sinneth, the same shall die;* for being loosed from the golden chains of the virtues, it is borne headlong down the precipice, and slips to lower things. But blessed is the soul which no adverse battles of the body storm. This soul, like a sparrow when the snare is broken, flies away. For the baits of evils are the pleasures of the body: whosoever strains after these entangles his own soul in the snare. But he who abstains from its baits, and comes out of its darkness, his soul shines as the dawn, of which it is said:

## 13.

*[n: (Vers. 9.)] Who is this that looks forth as the dawn, beautiful as the moon?* [var: Vulg. *Quae est ista quae progreditur quasi aurora consurgens, pulchra ut luna*, "Who is she that cometh forth as the morning rising, fair as the moon"] For she looks forth as from a free house, nor does she say: *Darkness covers me, and walls surround me, and who knows whether the Most High sees [n: (Eccli. XXIII, 26)]*? [var: Vulg. *Tenebrae circumdant me, et parietes cooperiunt me, et nemo circumspicit me: quem vereor?*, with the verbs the other way about and a different close] But she rather seeks the light [1944C] as in the upper parts of her house, that is, of her body: and, set above the world, she gazes upon divine things and lifts herself to eternal things, that she may stand near to God; now carrying the light of her works round her own orb, as the moon does, over the whole world.

## 14.

*[n: (Vers. 10.)] I went down into the garden of the nut tree, to see in the birth of the torrent, and to look whether the vine had flowered, and the pomegranates had budded.* [var: Vulg. *Descendi in hortum nucum, ut viderem poma convallium*, "I went down into the garden of nuts, to see the fruits of the valleys"] The Word of God is invited, in short, into the garden of the nut tree, in which is the fruit of prophetic reading, and the priestly grace, which is bitter in temptations, hard in labors, fruitful in the inward virtues. Whence Aaron's rod of nut-wood also flowered, not now by its own nature, but by a hidden power. Let him come down therefore into the garden, that he may gather the vintage of faith, take in the odors, find the heavenly fodder, feast upon the sweetness [1944D] of our honey.

## 15.

For while she is praised by the Bridegroom, modestly shrinking from being praised to her face; then, called back by the love of the Bridegroom, she says: *I went down into the garden of the nut tree to see in the birth of the torrent.* For where is the Church, save where the priestly rod and grace flowers? There she is frequently, that she may be tried in bitternesses and in temptations. By the nut we understand bitternesses, by the torrent temptations, yet tolerable ones, because it is written: *Our soul hath swum through the torrent [n: (Psalm. CXXIII, 5)]*. [var: Vulg. *torrentem pertransivit anima nostra*, "our soul hath passed through the torrent"] And so she went down into the place of bitterness, where the vine flowers, [1945A] and the fruit various and manifold in the appearance of pomegranates, which as it were under one covering of the whole body is fortified by faith and charity. *1608*

## 16.

In that bitterness, therefore, the soul did not know itself, *For the corruptible body weighs down the soul, and the earthly dwelling is quickly bowed down [n: (Sap. IX)]*. [var: Vulg. *Corpus enim quod corrumpitur aggravat animam, et terrena inhabitatio deprimit sensum multa cogitantem*] But it ought always to know itself. Yet Peter was tempted, and Peter too did not know himself; for if he had known himself, he would not have denied his author. But Christ knew him. In short, he knew him, because he also looked back at him; for the Lord knows who are his: and, as a good ruler, by the reins of his mercy he called back his own from the fall: our ruler therefore is Christ. Whence there follows also:

## 17.

*[n: (Vers. 11.)] The chariot of Aminadab has set me:* [var: Vulg. *Nescivi: anima mea conturbavit me propter quadrigas Aminadab*, "I knew not: my soul troubled me because of the four-horse chariots of Aminadab"] in that the soul is joined to the body, and, like a certain [1945B] chariot of raging horses, seeks a charioteer to rule it. For Aminadab was the father of Naason, as we read in Numbers [n: (Num. I, 7)], who was prince of the people of Juda, whose figure is referred to Christ, who, the true prince of the people, mounting the soul of the just man as a chariot, guides it as driver with the reins of the Word, lest by the fury of violent horses it be swept headlong and over precipices. For his four horses, as it were, are the four affections: anger, desire, pleasure, fear; and when it has begun to be driven by these in their raging, it in no way knows itself. For the corruptible body weighs down the soul, and, like a chariot of irrational animals, sweeps it away unwilling, rushing forward as by a certain onset while cares roll on, until the aforesaid passions of the body grow mild by the power of the Word. [1945C]

## 18.

This is the foresight of the Word as of a good driver: that the mortal body joined to that soul which is not in itself subject to death may not make the driving of it difficult. First therefore let him tame these swift motions of the body, and bridle them with the bond of reason: then let him beware lest, by unequal motion, they entangle themselves like horses; so that either the wicked one discolors the good, or the slow one hinders it, or the turbulent one disquiets it. For the horse of malice snorts, and by tossing itself injures the chariot, and weighs down its yoke-fellow. This the good charioteer soothes, and sends forth into the field of truth, and turns aside from the windings of deceit. Safe is the course to higher things, perilous the descent to lower. Thence, as though well discharged from service, they who have borne the yoke of the Word are led even to the Lord's manger, in which the food is not hay, but the bread which came down from heaven. Of this [1945D] chariot there are wheels, of which the Prophet said: *And the spirit of life was in the wheels [n: (Ezech. I, 20)]*; in that the chariot of the soul, smooth and round, rolls on without any stumbling.

## 19.

*The chariot of Aminadab has set me.* The soul therefore is a chariot, which bears a good ruler. If the soul is a chariot, it has horses either good or bad. The good horses are the virtues of the soul: the bad horses are the passions of the body. The good ruler therefore restrains and calls back the bad horses, and urges on the good. The good horses are four: prudence, temperance, fortitude, justice: the bad horses are wrathfulness, concupiscence, fear, iniquity. Sometimes the horses themselves disagree among themselves, and either wrathfulness stretches forward, or [1946A] fear: and they hinder one another, and slow the course. But indeed the good horses fly, and raise themselves from the earth to higher things, and lift up the soul; especially if they have the sweet yoke and light burden of him who says: *Take my yoke upon you; for my yoke is sweet, and my burden light [n: (Matth. XI, 29)]*.

## 20.

He himself is the ruler, who knows how to govern his own horses, so that the course of all may be even: he slows the swifter ones by prudence, justice admonishes the more sluggish with its own lash; temperance makes them gentler, fortitude makes them harder. He knows how to couple the discordant, lest perchance they shatter his chariot. And so it is possible to see, by an intelligible spectacle, every soul caught up to heaven with the highest striving, the horses hastening to come first to the prize of Christ, upon whose necks the palm may first be laid. [1946B] These are the horses subjected to the yoke of faith, bound fast by the chain of charity, by the reins of justice, by the halters of sobriety.
