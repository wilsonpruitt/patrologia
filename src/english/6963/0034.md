## 21.

Beautifully therefore she says: *The chariot of Aminadab has set me,* that is, the father of the people: but he himself who is the father of the people is likewise the father of Naason, that is, of serpents. Now do thou call to mind who hung upon the cross like a serpent for the salvation of all men, and thou wilt understand that soul to be peaceable whose president is God the Father, and whose driver is Christ; because this name too is written in our books: *Father, father, driver of Israel [n: (IV Reg. XI, 12)]*. [var: Vulg. 4 Kings 2:12 *Pater mi, pater mi, currus Israel et auriga ejus*, "My father, my father, the chariot of Israel and its charioteer"] This driver therefore says:

## 22.

*[n: (Vers. 12.)] Turn, O Sunamite, turn.* [var: Vulg. *Revertere, revertere, Sulamitis*, "Return, return, O Sulamitess"] Well does he say it as a driver, and as one speaking to a chariot: *Turn, O Sunamite,* that is, O peaceable one; for the soul which is peaceable turns and corrects itself quickly, even if it has sinned before: and the more does *1609* Christ mount it, [1946C] and deign to rule it, to which it is said: *Mount upon thy horses, and thy riding is health.* And elsewhere: *Thou hast sent thy horses of Tharsis [n: (Habac. III, 8)]*. [var: Vulg. Hab 3:8 *qui ascendes super equos tuos, et quadrigae tuae salvatio*, "who wilt mount upon thy horses, and thy four-horse chariots are salvation"] These are the horses of Christ. Christ therefore mounts his own horses: the Word of God mounts holy souls. When our soul has become peaceable, so that it is said to it: *Turn; turn, O Sunamite,* which signifies "peaceable," then it will receive into itself Christ as a seal, that is, the image of God, that it may be unto the image; since *As is the heavenly, such are they also that are heavenly, and we must bear the image of the heavenly [n: (I Cor. XV, 48)]*, that is, peace. And that we may know it to be true, thou hast in the same Song of Songs, at the very end, this said to the soul now made perfect, which may the Lord Jesus say to me also: *Set me as a seal upon thy heart, as a seal upon thy arm [n: (Cant. VIII, 6)]*; that peace may shine in my heart, and Christ in my works, [1946D] that wisdom, and justice, and redemption may be formed in me.

## CAPUT SEPTIMUM.



## 1.

*[n: (Vers. 1.)] How shapely are thy steps made in sandals, O daughter of Aminadab! The measures of thy thighs are like collars, the work of a craftsman.* [var: Vulg. *Quam pulchri sunt gressus tui in calceamentis, filia principis! Juncturae femorum tuorum sicut monilia quae fabricata sunt manu artificis*, "How beautiful are thy steps in shoes, O prince's daughter! the joints of thy thighs are like jewels"] There is no doubt that here too the steps of the Church, or the advance of the soul, are signified. *How shapely are the feet of them that preach the gospel of peace, of them that preach good things [n: (Esai. LII, 7)]*! [var: Vulg. Isa 52:7 *Quam pulchri super montes pedes annuntiantis et praedicantis pacem*, "How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him that announceth and preacheth peace"] Assuredly he calls shapely the advances of evangelical preaching and disputation, as it is said elsewhere: *Pass over the rivers [n: (Esai. XLVII, 2)]*, that is, run across the streams and slippery places of this world with a steadfast [1947A] gait of the mind. That this is said of the going of the soul, David plainly shows, when he brings forward that his soul had passed over the torrent of iniquities [n: (Psal. CCXIII, 5)].

## 2.

But what does he mean by saying that the steps of the Church are shapely in sandals? We read it said to Moses: *Loose the sandal of thy feet (Exodi III, 5)*: whereby he seems to be admonished not to be held bound by bodily chains. Therefore in the Song of Songs he signifies the shapely loveliness of the soul, which uses the flesh as a sandal, and in that very sandal suffers no hindrance, but excels in the comeliness of its going. Let the soul therefore shoe itself with ecclesiastical grace, that it may pass over the course and passage of this life with comeliness. Which comes about if it does not befoul its own sandal with bodily mud, does not plunge it into the abyss of vices: if it chastise [1947B] its own flesh, lest it delay in the course, and be weighed down with the burden of fat grease. The good sandal of the soul is modesty, the good step is in the track of chastity. But wisdom is the mantle of the soul, whence it is written: *Honor her, and she will embrace thee [n: (Prov. IV, 8)]*. [var: Vulg. *Arripe illam, et exaltabit te; glorificaberis ab ea cum eam fueris amplexatus*, "Take hold of her, and she shall exalt thee"] Let us use the body therefore as a sandal for the works of a lower virtue: for ministry, not for command: for compliance, not for delight: for obedience, not for dissension; and let us set our footstep in the way of wisdom, lest some force of a torrent shut in our steps.

## 3.

Therefore it was said to Moses: *Loose thy sandal (Exodi III, 5)*. It was said to Jesus son of Nave also [n: (Josue V, 16)]. But of Christ it was not said, but rather it is written, the Baptist saying: *After me there comes a man, [1947C] whose sandals I am not worthy to bear [n: (Matth. III, 11)]*; [var: Vulg. Matt 3:11 *qui autem post me venturus est, fortior me est*, "he that shall come after me is mightier than I"; *post me venit vir* is the wording of John 1:30] because they are rightly admonished to loose their sandal who could not be without sin. But he not only did not loose his sandal, but even unlooses the sandals of others; because he not only kept his own body immune from sins, but also gave pardon of the sins of all.

## 4.

Therefore the Church, in imitation of Christ, is shapely, and in its sandals washed from every fault. And perhaps when it speaks wisdom among the perfect, it is shapely in its upper members: but when men even of a lower rank or of lower teaching follow the Word, do not forget the order of faith, keep the precepts of the priest, it is shapely in its sandals. Very often the clergy has erred, the priest has changed his sentence, [1947D] the rich have sided with the earthly king of this world, and the people has kept its own faith.

## 5.

Whence of the Lord Jesus also we can rightly say that even in those things which are bodily the Word has shapely steps, when there is disputing about moral matters. And the apostles are perhaps for this reason sent with bare feet, lest their disputation be overshadowed, but rather shine out. And so the Church is the daughter of Aminadab, that is, of the willing or the well-pleasing one; because a willing or well-pleasing one gathered her, and she is shapely in her sandals. And deservedly it is added:

[1948A] *1610* 6. *The measures of thy thighs are like collars, the work of the hands of a craftsman;* that the ornaments of the Church's posterity might be sung. For by the thigh we recognize the badge of generation, according to that word: *Gird thy sword upon thy thigh, O most mighty [n: (Psal. XLIV, 4)]*: whereby it is signified that the Son of God, when he had emptied himself, girt with the divinity of the Word, and shod with human generation, should come forth from the Virgin to give salvation to all. But "measures" are the precious ornaments which are wont to be hung upon the necks of matrons. So great therefore is the advance of the Church that is signified, that it is compared to the most precious ornaments, and to the collars of those who triumph; for these are the ornaments of warriors. Whence Symmachus also said *Epitrachelia*, that is, the things which are about the neck. Whether therefore the generation of Christ [1948B] from the Virgin, or the propagation of the Church, in appearance indeed as by the hand of a craftsman with adorned collars, but with the spiritual badges of true virtue, has adorned the necks of the faithful. In short, this whole description of the members of the Church is full of comeliness and of praise. For also:

## 7.

*[n: (Vers. 2.)] Her navel is as a turned goblet,* it is proclaimed, not lacking in the mixture; in that, being turned upon the lathe in every doctrine, in the fullness of knowledge, it does not lack the spiritual draught either. And her belly is not only fattened with the heap of wheat, that is, with the stronger foods of the heavenly mystery, but is also filled as with certain lilies with the sweetness of moral things. Whence she too, as one well deserving, is crowned as a queen with the blood of Christ, as it is written: *And the adornment of her head is as purple.* [var: Vulg. Cant 7:5 *comae capitis tui sicut purpura regis*, "the hairs of thy head as the purple of the king"] The blood of Christ is the purple, which dyes [1948C] the souls of the saints, resplendent not only in color, but also in power; because it makes them kings, and better kings, to whom it gives an eternal kingdom.

## 8.

*Thy navel is as a turned goblet, never wanting cups.* The navel of the soul and the belly of her who ascends to Christ is fine; and therefore she is praised by the voice of the Bridegroom saying: *Thy navel is as a turned goblet, not lacking in the mixture; thy belly a heap of fortified wheat among the lilies.* [var: Vulg. Cant 7:2 *numquam indigens poculis… venter tuus sicut acervus tritici vallatus liliis*; the first form Migne prints in this same paragraph agrees with the Vulgate, the second does not] For it is turned upon the lathe in every doctrine, and the spiritual draught is not lacking in fullness, and in the knowledge of heavenly secrets. The belly of the soul also is mystical, as the navel is; by which belly it takes not only the strong food by which hearts are made firm, but also the sweet and flowery food by which they are delighted.

## 9.

*[n: (Vers. 4.)] Thine eyes are pools in Hesebon, in the gates [1948D] of the daughter of many, Thy nostrils are as the tower of Lebanon.* [var: Vulg. Cant 7:4 *Oculi tui sicut piscinae in Hesebon quae sunt in porta filiae multitudinis. Nasus tuus sicut turris Libani*, "Thy eyes like the fishpools in Hesebon which are in the gate of the daughter of the multitude. Thy nose is as the tower of Libanus"] What are the pools in Hesebon, save an abundance in reasonable thoughts, which is in the gates of the Church, to which the multitude of doctrine is deservedly brought? For the daughter of many is the posterity and the effect of very many doctrines, whose nostrils are the odor of sacrifices, surpassing all flowers.

## 10.

*Thy nose is as the tower of Lebanon:* exceedingly lofty beyond measure, and therefore it looks forth upon the face of Damascus, smelling out, that is, the gentile people, and the faith of him by whose grace it might wipe away the stench of its own faults. The face [1949A] therefore of Damascus is the faith of the Gentiles, overshadowed by no covering, hidden by no clothing, naked and free, more intent upon heaven than upon earth. This the nostrils of the Church watch and look forth upon, gathering what is sweet in it both of the breathings of odors and of grace.
