## 1. Work-wide conventions (fixed at merge)

Migne prints an **Old Latin / LXX-derived Song of Songs** throughout, and Ambrose's
exposition repeatedly argues *from the divergent word*. The controlling rule for this
work is therefore: **one English word per Latin word, held across all forty chunks**,
because the moment two Latin words share an English word the author's own argument —
and our `[var: …]` apparatus, which exists to show Migne's Latin against the
Vulgate's — stops meaning anything.

### 1.2 What the merge actually had to repair

Four divergences between agents were found and reconciled **in the English chunks**,
not merely noted here. Each is recorded so a later sweep does not churn it back.

1. ***proxima mea* had three Englishes.** "my neighbour" (0005, 0006, 0007, 0019),
   "my neighbor" (0011, 0012, 0023, 0024) and **"my nearest one"** (0025, three
   occurrences plus the gloss *Nearest by charity*). All now read **neighbor**, in
   the American spelling used by the style anchor (`9741`) and by the corpus at
   large. 0025's gloss now reads *Neighbor by charity, a dove by simplicity, perfect
   by virtue*, which is what *Charitate proxima* says. At 1864C the adjectival prose
   *quae proxima eras* was "thou who wast nearest" and now reads **"thou who wast a
   neighbor"**, keeping the *aliena / proxima* antithesis on the same word as the
   lemma.
2. ***formosa* had collapsed into *pulchra*.** 0011, 0012 and 0019 rendered *formosa*
   "beautiful" — the word already carrying *pulchra* — so 1870A's *formosus … et
   quidem pulcher* and 1905C/1907A's *formosa* against the Vulgate's *pulchra* both
   went silent. 0011/0012/0019 now read **well-formed** (nine occurrences), matching
   0006's *Behold, my cousin is well-formed, and indeed beautiful*.
3. ***speciosa* had collapsed into *pulchra* and *decora*.** 0004 and 0034 rendered
   *speciosi/speciosa* "beautiful"; 0011 "fair"; 0032 "beautiful"; 0033 "beautiful";
   0036 "comely". The apparatus at **1946D** marks Migne's *Quam speciosi pedes*
   against the Vulgate's *Quam pulchri*, and at **1942B–C** Migne prints *speciosa
   sicut Hierusalem* and then, one paragraph later, *Pulchra ut Hierusalem* — with
   both Englishes reading "beautiful", the `[var:]` note contradicted itself and the
   plate's own three forms of Cant 6:3 became one. All epithet uses now read
   **shapely** (0004 ×2, 0011, 0031, 0032 ×3, 0033 ×3, 0034 ×10, 0036 ×3), which is
   also what 0005/0006/0007 already had (*How shapely are thy cheeks become*,
   *shapely and comely*, *blossomed the more shapely*).
   ⚠ **Deliberate carve-out:** *speciosus* in free prose, where it is not an epithet
   of the bride or of the preacher's feet, keeps ordinary English — 1918B's
   *speciosum aliquem locum* ("some beautiful place") and 1918D's *speciosus est usu*
   ("beautiful in use"). "Shapely place" is not English. This is recorded so the
   carve-out is not read as residual drift.
4. ***decora inter mulieres* was rendered "beautiful among women" once** in 0004
   against "comely among women" four times in the same chunk; now **comely**
   throughout. Two `[var:]` glosses of the Vulgate were normalized to the same table
   at 1953B/1953C (Vulg. *pulchra* → "beautiful as the moon", Vulg. *formosus* →

   from the running text.

## 2. ⛔ Plate divergences deliberately left UNHARMONIZED

**These are not agent drift. Migne's text visibly sits on two Latin traditions at
once, and that is the finding this edition exists to show.** A tidy-minded pass that
conforms any of them deletes it. Each is rendered as printed at every occurrence.

1. ***amica mea* (1901C) vs *proxima mea* (1905C, 1907A)** for the same address.
   Migne prints both; the English prints **"my love"** and **"my neighbor"**
   respectively. Do not merge them.
2. **Cant 5:10 *ex millibus* (§§58–59, 1934A) vs *ex decem millibus* (§60, 1934C).**
   Ambrose needs "ten thousands" for the Ephraim/David argument and quotes it that
   way two paragraphs after printing "thousands."
3. **Rom 13:10: *plenitudo legis est Christus* (1873B) vs *…est charitas* (1878D),
   and again *plenitudo enim Legis charitas est* (0035 §12).** The work carries both
   predicates.
4. **Cant 5:1: *manducavi panem meum* (1909C, 1917D, 1919A, 1920B) vs *Comedi favum*
   (1918B); *bibi vinum meum* (1917D, 1920A) vs *bibi potum meum* (1919A).**
   "my bread" / "the honeycomb", "my wine" / "my drink".
5. **Cant 6:3 in three forms within two columns** — the LXX-derived *Beneplacita es…
   speciosa sicut Hierusalem, admiratio sicut ordinata* (1942B §6), then the Vulgate
   form *Pulchra es, amica mea, suavis et decora* in the very next paragraph
   (1942B §7), then *Pulchra ut Hierusalem* (1942C §7).
6. **Cant 7:4 *Nares tuae* (1948D §9) then *Nasus tuus* (1949A §10)**, one paragraph
   apart — plural/feminine against the Vulgate's singular; and *Nares ejus* again at
   1949A §11.
7. **Cant 7:2 both readings four lines apart inside one paragraph (1948C §8)** — the
   Vulgate's *numquam indigens poculis* and the LXX's *non deficiens mixto*, with
   Ambrose glossing the LXX one.
8. **Cant 8:13 masculine *Qui sedes in hortis* (1959C §28) vs the feminine *Quae
   sedes* that Ambrose reports from Symmachus and Aquila (§31).** He is knowingly
   holding both, and §31 exists to say so.
9. **Cant 8:5 in three forms in two columns (1951D §1, 1952B §2, 1953B §6)** —
   *ascendit candida* / *ascendit dealbata* / *ascendit* with no epithet: "white" /
   "made white" / bare.
10. **Cant 4:16 *perfluunt* (indicative) vs *perfluant* (subjunctive), 1916B, four
    lines apart** — "and my spices flow forth" / "and let my spices flow forth".

## 3. SETTLED — 1893B–C, *nex* for *nec*, and the two sites that made it a class

**⚠ The ruling below REVERSES the merge-pass ruling of earlier the same day.** That
first ruling — carry the type, let the English stand affirmative, log the consequence
— was made on the printed page alone. The blind polarity read then brought corpus
evidence the merge did not have, and it decides the question.

**Ruling (2026-08-14, blind polarity read): carry the negation, keep the marker.**
The English now reads *She who was speaking Christ, [sic: *nex*] was **not** stripped
of her cloak.*

**The evidence that overturned it** is inside the work and did not need an outside
witness. §8 of this same passage prints *et non exutus est pallio*, "and he was not
stripped of his cloak"; and chunk 0028 asks *qui castae animae pallium tollunt?* The
crux Ambrose is worrying at, across thirty-seven columns, is that **this soul kept her
cloak**. The affirmative English contradicted both its own paragraph and a passage far
downstream. Note that this is exactly the alternative the translating agent proposed
and did not take.

**And it is not one case — it is a class**, found the same afternoon by three
independent readers in three chunks far apart:

- **1893B** — `[sic: *nex*]` swallowed the negative; the English asserted the opposite.
- **1899A** — `[sic: *car*]` swallowed *cur*; a reproach ("why does he **not** take
  away…?") became an assertion that he does, in a paragraph closing *Non potest igitur
  haec dici justa misericordia*. Now reads *[sic: *car*] why does he not take away…*
- **1917A** — `[sic: *eccidit*]` swallowed the clause's only verb, leaving "which …
  the prophets" with no predicate. Now reads *which slays [sic: *eccidit*] the
  prophets*.

**The principle, now written into `translation-style.md` as 7a⁗: a `[sic: …]`
quarantines the defective TYPE, never the FORCE that type governs.** The marker
isolates what the plate got wrong; it is not a place to park a negation, an
interrogative, or a clause's only verb and let the English go silently affirmative.
The reader still meets the broken type at the point of loss — nothing is concealed —
but the sentence still says what the author is arguing.

**This does not reopen Pattern 7.** Supplying the force the marked run governs is not
a conjectural emendation of the plate: the negation is *in* the printed run, which is
why the run is marked at all. What was conjectural was the earlier reading, which had
the English assert a thing the surrounding Latin twice denies.

The stint's own entry, in full:


###### 1893B–C — *nex* for *nec* (§7) ⚠ READ THIS ONE
Migne prints: *Quae Christum loquebatur, **nex** exuta est pallio, et quem
quaerebat, invenit.*

*nex* ("slaughter, violent death") is a real Latin word with **no grammatical
slot** here — a bare nominative noun stranded between a relative clause and a
finite passive verb (Pattern 12, test 1). Carried untranslated in italics.

**Conjecture, near-certain: *nec*.** Ambrose's own preceding sentence sets the
question — *qua ratione hic ei **non** tulerint pallium, haereo reperire* ("by
what reasoning they did **not** take away her cloak here, I am at a loss to find
out") — so the clause must read *nec exuta est pallio*, "she was **not** stripped
of her cloak," and §8's closing sentence repeats the point affirmatively: *et non
exutus est pallio, postea quam coepit esse perfectior.*

**⚠ Consequence for the English, stated plainly per Pattern 7a′.** Because *nex*
is not a negative particle but a noun, carrying it leaves **no negation in the
English clause**, and our page therefore says that she *was* stripped of her
cloak — the contrary of the argument the paragraph is conducting. This is not a
repair hidden in a marker: the marker sits exactly where the negation belongs,
and the reader sees the broken type at the point of loss. It is logged here in
full because it is the one place in this stint where the plate's defect inverts
the sense. If a later reader judges that the printed letters do carry the
negation (i.e. that *nex* is simply *nec* with a broken **c**), the remedy is to
render "was **not** stripped of her cloak" and keep the `[sic:]` — not to leave
the affirmative standing silently.

**The five that matter most, and why:**

1. **1861A, *non est intuitus me sol*.** The Vulgate says the sun *has discolored*
   her; Migne prints that the sun *has **not** looked upon* her (LXX
   παρέβλεψέν με ὁ ἥλιος). The negative is the hinge of the whole of §§21–24 —
   "the sun of justice has forsaken me… I did not open the windows… when I shall have
   opened them, he will enlighten my eyes." On the Vulgate text the exposition is
   unintelligible. The *non* is carried everywhere it is printed.
2. **1864A, *quem dilexit anima mea*.** Migne's perfect against the Vulgate's present
   — and Ambrose's next sentence is *Cur* quem dilexit anima mea *dicis, et non quem
   diligit?* The commentary is an argument **about the tense**. A conformed lemma
   would have destroyed the paragraph.
3. **1861B, *Fusca sum **et** decora*.** §24 turns on the conjunction itself
   ("Ambrose Bassus" vs "Ambrose *and* Bassus"), and the Vulgate prints *sed*. The
   *et* is load-bearing type.
4. **1859C / 1860D, *cubiculum* against *cellaria*.** Ambrose adjudicates the
   divergence himself at 1860D: *Graecus in promptuarium suum et in cellarium suum
   habet* — the plate records the author noticing the very variant this apparatus
   tracks.
5. **1865B, *in calceamentis gregum*.** "In the **sandals** of the flocks", against
   the Vulgate's "after the **footsteps** of the flocks" — the Greek πτέρναις
   ("heels") read as footwear. §34's entire gloss is about *unshod* feet and Moses's
   loosed sandal, so the divergent word is the peg the exposition hangs on.

###### 1.1 — 0000, col 1854A/B: *deret*
> *…quae omnia fecisset ut sponsum* [1854B] *deret*

`deret` is no Latin verb. The clause needs a transitive verb governing *sponsum*;

###### 1.2 — 0001, col 1856A: *cupieos*

Printed: *Itaque sicut aurum bonum, ira Ecclesia cum utitur, detrimenta non sentit,
sed magis fulgor ejus augetur.* *ira* is a real word occupying a real slot (ablative
with *utitur*), so it is rendered in place — "when the Church makes use of wrath" —
per Pattern 12's boundary for a real word that is merely wrong. Conjecture: **ita**
("so the Church, when it uses [gold], feels no loss"), which the *sicut … ita*
correlation and the following *donec Christus veniat* both want. **Not** emended.

###### 1878C — *in virtutibus e fortitudinibus agri*
The same lemma at 1875B prints *et*. Here *e* is a preposition governing the
ablative *fortitudinibus* and does construe, so it is rendered "from" rather than
carried as broken type. Conjecture: **et**, by the parallel three columns earlier.

###### 1872A — *necubi eum in pectoris tui convalle reperias*
Rendered with its negative intact ("lest anywhere thou find him in the valley of thy
breast"), which reads against the encouraging drift of the sentence (*humilibus enim
frequenter inhalatur*, "he is often breathed upon lowly things"). *necubi* may be
doing duty for an indirect question ("whether somewhere thou mayest find him"). The
negation is printed and is carried; the difficulty is recorded rather than smoothed.

###### 1867A — *sed absoluto pro equitatu*
Elliptical: an ablative absolute with no expressed noun, the point being that the
singular *equa* stands for the collective *equitatus*, "as we have often said."
Rendered "but a word put absolutely for the cavalry"; the two supplied English words
carry no sense the Latin does not, but the ellipsis is noted here.

###### 1869C–D (§47) — the antithesis series
*terram movebat, et haerebat in ligno … vulnus erat et fluebat unguentum: scarabaeus
audiebatur, et Deus agnoscebatur.* Rendered flat and paratactic as printed;
*scarabaeus* ("beetle," the old Latin rendering at Hab. 2:11) is kept as the plate
has it and not glossed in the text.

### 6d — cols 1879D–1893B: flags — printed readings that contradict their own context

##### Flags — printed readings that will not construe, or contradict their own context

Each is rendered as printed. None is repaired in the English.

1. **1885A (0012 §50) — *Videte* where Ambrose's own gloss requires *Orate*.**
   Migne prints Matt 24:20 as *Videte ne fiat fuga vestra hieme vel sabbato*.
   At **1891A (0014 §69)** Ambrose paraphrases the same verse: *et ideo **suadet
   orandum**, ne fiat fuga nostra hieme aut sabbato* — "and therefore he urges
   that prayer must be made." The paraphrase presupposes *Orate* (Vulgate),
   which the quotation five columns earlier does not print. Rendered "See that
   your flight be not…" as printed, with `[var:]`; the contradiction with §69 is
   left standing.

2. **1884C (0011 §48) — a negation missing from the third member.**
   *quia **non** sequuntur Domini passionem, **non** tollunt crucem suam, **et**
   Christum sequuntur.* The first two members are negative and the third is
   affirmative, so the sentence asserts that those who do not follow the
   passion nevertheless follow Christ — the opposite of the argument (they are
   the ones whose flight is in winter). *nec* for *et* is near-certain.
   **Rendered as printed**: "they do not follow the Lord's passion, they do not
   take up their cross, and they follow Christ." This is a Pattern 7a case and
   the English carries the contradiction deliberately.

3. **1881B–C (0010 §§37–38) — *domus panis* / *domus Dei*, and a doubled clause.**
   The last sentence of §37 and the second sentence of §38 are the same clause
   printed twice: *salit enim super Ecclesiam quae est **domus panis**, eo quod
   fidelium corda confirmet* / *salit enim super Ecclesiam, quae est **domus
   Dei**, eo quod fidelium corda confirmet.* The lemma is *super montes
   **Bethel***, and Bethel is *domus Dei*; *domus panis* is Bethlehem. Both are
   rendered as printed. **No `[d: …]`** — the two runs are not identical (the
   variant word is the whole interest of the doubling), and Pattern 11's marker
   wraps a repeated run, not a variant one. Whether §37's sentence is a
   compositor's anticipation of §38 or Ambrose's own doublet cannot be settled
   from the plate.

4. **1888D (0013 §63) — *Frater meus* in the lemma, *Dilectus meus* in the gloss.**
   The verse 16 lemma is printed *Frater meus mihi, et ego illi*; three lines
   later the same verse is quoted *Dilectus meus mihi et ego illi*, and again at
   **1890B** *Dilectus meus mihi, et ego illi.* Migne's plate carries both text
   forms of Cant 2:16 within one section. Both rendered as printed ("My
   brother", "My beloved"); no harmonization.

5. **1891B / 1891C (0014 §1) — *in nocte* in the lemma, *in noctibus* in the re-quotation.**
   The block lemma reads *In cubili meo, **in nocte** quaesivi*; the re-quotation
   eleven lines later, and again at **1892B (§3)**, reads *In cubili meo **in
   noctibus** quaesivi*. Singular against plural in the same section. Both as
   printed.

6. **1879C (0010 §31) — *ego me vulneratam charitatis puto*.**
   A bare genitive with *vulneratam*; the construction wants *charitate* (as at
   1882A §40, *vulneratur charitatis decore*, where the genitive depends on
   *decore*). Rendered "I deem myself wounded of charity", which keeps the
   genitive visible. Not emended.

7. **1879D (0010 §32) — no stop between two sentences.**
   *Videamus salientem Salit de coelo in Virginem.* The plate prints no
   punctuation and a capital *S*. Under Pattern 8 no stop is supplied: "Let us
   see him leaping He leaps from heaven into the Virgin."

8. **1880B (0010 §33) — mixed persons.**
   *si **non potest** esse mons et non **praevales**, esto vel collis* —
   third-person *potest* and second-person *praevales*/*esto* in one
   conditional. Rendered literally ("if one cannot be a mountain and thou dost
   not prevail, be at least a hill"); both negations carried.

9. **1881B (0010 §37) — *maturus exspectes*.**
   *Si in tempore messis quo diriguntur isti metere, maturus exspectes…* The
   nominative *maturus* is stranded (the ears of corn, not the watcher, are what
   ripens in the surrounding figure). Rendered "thou shouldst wait ripe", which
   keeps the case relation as printed.

10. **1889A (0013 §64) — *Quomodo … est.* punctuated as a statement.**
    *Quomodo idem et mihi sacerdos et Deo est.* An interrogative adverb closed
    by a full stop. Under Pattern 8 the mark stands: "How the same is a priest
    both for me and for God." No question mark supplied.

11. **1886D (0013 §53) — *dum ligatur, exuitur*.**
    The three-member figure runs *dum ligatur, **exuitur**: dum humiliatur,
    attollitur: dum reciditur, coronatur*, and the two following members invert
    their verbs where the first does not (bound → stripped is not an
    inversion). *erigitur* (as in the immediately preceding vine clause, *ut
    vinea dum ligatur, **erigitur***) would give the expected sense. Rendered as
    printed: "while it is bound, it is stripped."

12. **1886A (0012 §51) — the fig-tree excursus will not fully construe.**
    *Namque succedentibus aliis quae quasi degenerantia despuuntur, et arenti
    infirma radice, renovatis quibus sit succus utilior, exuuntur.* Two ablative
    absolutes, a relative clause without a clear antecedent, and a plural
    *exuuntur* with no expressed subject. Rendered as closely as the printed
    words allow ("For when others succeed, which are spat out as though
    degenerate, and the weak root being dry, they are stripped away, those being
    renewed whose sap is more serviceable"). No word supplied, none dropped.

13. **1891D (0014 §2) — first person inside a second-person address.**
    *Tunc maxime quando tenebrae **in circuitu meo**, et parietes **me**

    noctem…*, *Ne dixeris ergo…*). Rendered as printed ("when darkness is round
    about me and walls cover me"); the shift is Migne's.

14. **1890C (0013 §67) — *montes convallium*.**
    "Mountains of the valleys," against the lemma's *lilium convallium* and the
    work's steady mountain/valley opposition (1880A: *Deus montium est, non
    vallium*). Rendered literally.

15. **1888B (0013 §61) — *Allophylorum*.**
    Migne prints the LXX transliteration (ἀλλόφυλοι) where the Vulgate of Judges

17. **1885D / 1884D (0012) — the section number 50 is printed twice.**

## 5.3 Secondary repairs — not polarity, but the same read found them

###### *custodes* was splitting into two English words, inside one verse
The Song lemma at 1928C prints *custodes* twice — *Invenerunt me **custodes** qui
circumeunt civitatem … tulerunt pallium meum a me **custodes** murorum* — and the
English had "**guards** who go about the city" against "**keepers** of the walls".
Across the work: 0015 all "guards" (5), 0027 mixed 5/2, 0028 all "keepers" (8).
**This invents a distinction the plate does not print, and 0028 §47 is built on the
identity of the word** — *Fortasse sunt et alii custodes*, "perhaps there are also
*other* custodes", an argument that only runs if it is the same word. Unified to
**guards** throughout (the dominant form, and the one 0015 already used for the same
scene). This is the class the merge pass exists to catch and did not: the two chunks
were written by different agents, neither could see the other, and both were
internally consistent.

###### 0012's ratio warning — adjudicated, NOT padding
The verifier flags 0012 as the work's highest EN/LA ratio. Its batch's report was lost
in the crash, so this was closed here instead. Expansion is **distributed**, not
localized: per-paragraph ratios run 1.48–2.19 across all 21 paragraphs, and the two
highest are explained without residue. 1886D–1887A (*Hanc vineam diligens agricola
fodere, rigare, contendere consuevit…*) is one dense synthetic period — five
infinitives, three *ne*-clauses, two ablative absolutes — unpacked clause for clause
with nothing added. 1887B's inflation is **apparatus, not prose**: the `[var:]` note
carrying the Vulgate's *in foraminibus petrae, in caverna maceriae* against Migne's
*in tegimento petrae, juxta praemunitionem* is 24 of its 44 extra words. The work's
whole range is 1.50–1.87; 0012 is not an outlier in it.

###### 1877D (0009) — John 7:23 conformed to the familiar interrogative
Migne prints *dicens: Mihi indignamini quia totum hominem sanum feci in sabbato*,
indicative, no question mark — the Vulgate points it as a question, and the English
had followed the Vulgate: "*Are ye angry with me because…?*" Now *Ye are angry with
me because I made a whole man sound on the sabbath.* **This one was never adjudicated
by the first pass** — its reader's report was lost in the crash — and it is exactly
the 7a″ pressure (familiar wording arriving over the plate), reaching the page through
punctuation rather than vocabulary.

###### 1872B (0007) — the one question mark KEPT against the plate
Migne prints *nonne inter asperitates laborum contritionesque animorum boni flos
odoris assurgit;* with a semicolon, and the English closes it with "?". **Kept**, and
this is not the same case as 1927C: *nonne* is an unambiguous interrogative particle,
so the clause *is* a question and English has no way to carry an interrogative without
its mark, whereas 1927C's *Quomodo transivit, id est…* is a gloss on a lemma word and
was never interrogative at all. The remaining 0002/0004 warnings are the same
punctuation class, resolved in §5.4.

###### 1927C — a printed statement turned into a rhetorical question
Migne prints *Quomodo transivit, id est, mentis interna penetravit* with a comma, as
a gloss on the lemma word. The English had "**How did he pass by?** That is, he
penetrated…". Migne punctuates questions freely in this very chunk (§38, §42), so the
comma is a real signal, not plate noise. Now: *How he passed by: that is, he
penetrated…* — which also resolves 0027's question-mark warning.

## 5.4 Ruled and deliberately NOT changed

###### 1959B — *quam illi utique tribuit ipse quem diligit*
A reader proposed binding *quem diligit* to *illi* ("assigns to that one whom he
loves"), on the strength of the parallel Ambrose immediately draws — *sicut dilexit et
Paulum, **cui** dedit* — which makes the loved one the recipient. **Rejected as an
edit, logged as a crux.** The Latin is genuinely muddled, the present English renders
the printed word order literally, and the proposed reading requires a case mismatch
(*illi* dative, *quem* accusative). Pattern 7 says render the printed reading and log
the doubt; the reader's own confidence was low. Recorded so a later sweep does not
churn it.

###### 1948B — *verae autem virtutis **insignis** spiritualibus*
Rendered "with the spiritual badges of true virtue", which reads the printed
*insignis* as *insignibus*. A quiet normalization with no crux — now logged. Not
reverted: the clause is unconstruable as printed and no rendering of it is innocent,
so the honest record is this entry rather than a churn.

###### The two question-mark warnings, 0002 and 0004 — **resolved, nothing missing**
Both extra English `?` are punctuation normalizations of interrogative clauses Migne
closes otherwise: 1860B *«Ego a te debeo baptizari, et tu venis ad me;»* (semicolon)
and 1865D–1866A *Quid est se noscere, nisi ut sciat unusquisque…* (full stop). Every
Latin interrogative is present in the English in both chunks. This check exists
because it caught a dropped 46-word sentence in 11632; here it caught punctuation.

###### 1960B–C — Migne's *Quae sedes* against his own *Qui sedes*
At 1959C–D the plate prints the masculine *Qui sedes in hortis* (speaker: the soul,
addressee: Christ). At 1960B, reporting Symmachus and Aquila, it prints the **feminine
*Quae sedes*** — and Ambrose's whole appeal to them is that reversal (speaker: Christ,
addressee: the Church). English "Thou that sittest" cannot inflect, so the distinction
is invisible on the page; the frame sentence ("Christ says to the Church") carries the
reversal, but not its textual basis. **No inline marker:** `[var:]` names an outside
witness, and this is Migne disagreeing with himself — the verifier correctly refused
it. Recorded here instead.
