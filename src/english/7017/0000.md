## *Thales to Pherecydes.*

⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1147A]

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ I hear that you are preparing to be the first of the Ionians to discourse openly among the Greeks concerning divine matters. ⟦s2⟧ But it would perhaps be a juster judgment to read among friends what you have written than to hand it about indiscriminately to any and all, since to them it will bring no profit. ⟦s3⟧ For my part, if I learn that it would be agreeable to you, I wish to be privy to the things you are writing. ⟦s4⟧ And if indeed you bid me, I will set out to you in good time. ⟦s5⟧ For we are not so senseless and dull, I and Solon of Athens, that, when we sailed to Crete for the sake of seeing it, and likewise made our way into Egypt to meet there with the priests and astronomers, we should not sail to you with equal zeal. ⟦s6⟧ For Solon himself will come too, if you consent. ⟦s7⟧ And you, held fast by love of that place of yours, cross but rarely into Ionia, nor are you touched by any longing for foreign [1147B] men. ⟦s8⟧ But you apply yourself, as I hope, to the one business only of writing. ⟦s9⟧ Yet we, who write nothing, travel through Greece and Asia. ⟦s10⟧ Farewell.

## *Thales to Solon.*

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ If you depart from Athens, you will be able, as I for my part think, to dwell most conveniently at Miletus. ⟦s2⟧ For it is your colony, and you will suffer nothing grievous there. ⟦s3⟧ But if among the Milesians too you abhor tyranny — for everywhere alike you find tyrants hostile — yet to live with us among friends will be most pleasant. ⟦s4⟧ Bias has written to you that you set out for Priene: if you would more gladly dwell in that city, we too will fly there to you.

## *Pisistratus to Solon.*

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ I am neither the only one of the Greeks to have seized the tyranny, nor [1147C] have I usurped a thing that was another's, seeing that I trace my descent from Cecrops. ⟦s2⟧ For I claim for myself by my own right what the Athenians once, when they had confirmed it by oath to Codrus and his line that they would render it, afterward took away. ⟦s3⟧ For the rest, I sin in nothing else against the gods or against men. ⟦s4⟧ The laws that you yourself gave the Athenians I command to be kept, and to live according to them; and indeed they are better kept than they could be if affairs were conducted by the rule of the multitude. ⟦s5⟧ For I permit no one to be wronged; and I, though a tyrant, differ from the common people in nothing but rank and honor, content with only those revenues that were owed also to those who reigned before me. ⟦s6⟧ In short, the individual Athenians set apart the tithes of their crops, not to be consumed for our uses, but for the public sacrifices and the common advantages; and if [1147D] at any time a war should press upon us, to be assigned to the expenses. ⟦s7⟧ With you, for my part, I am in no way angry, that you laid bare my mind and design — seeing that you laid it bare rather out of zeal for the commonwealth of the city than out of hatred of me; and besides, because you did not know what sort of [1148A] king I would prove to be. ⟦s8⟧ For had you learned this, you would perhaps have borne my endeavors too with an even mind, and would by no means have committed yourself to flight. ⟦s9⟧ Come home, then, in good faith, believing me even unsworn, that there is no danger to Solon from Pisistratus. ⟦s10⟧ For you know that no one, even from among my enemies, has suffered any evil at my hands. ⟦s11⟧ In short, if it should please you to enjoy our friendship, you will be among the first; for I perceive in you nothing of fraud or perfidy. ⟦s12⟧ But if for other reasons you are unwilling to dwell at Athens, you will do so at your own discretion; only do not depart your fatherland on our account. ⟦s13⟧ Farewell.

## *Solon to Periander.*

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ You write that very many lie in wait for you; yet you, even if you should take them all out of the way, will not even so gain your end. ⟦s2⟧ For someone will lie in wait for you from among those whom you least hold suspect — partly indeed fearing for himself, partly reproaching you because you dread everything, and there is nothing you do not fear, [1148B] partly likewise desiring to gratify the city. ⟦s3⟧ It would therefore be best to abstain from the tyranny, that you might escape all causes of fear. ⟦s4⟧ But if you judge that you must cling to the tyranny at all costs, you must needs procure for yourself external forces and foreign auxiliaries, greater than the forces of the city, so that now none may be hostile to you, and you may drive no one into exile. ⟦s5⟧ Farewell.

## *Solon to Epimenides.*

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Neither, in truth, were my laws going to profit the Athenians much; nor, when you had made them ancient, were you of any advantage to the city. ⟦s2⟧ For God, and the givers of laws, cannot by themselves aid cities, but those who lead the multitude, into whatever opinion they wish. ⟦s3⟧ For to these, [1148C] if they administer the matter rightly, God and the laws are useful; but if ill, they profit nothing. ⟦s4⟧ Nor indeed did my laws and ordinances, which I wrote, avail anything; but those who transgressed them brought great harm upon the commonwealth — they who did not withstand Pisistratus, that he should not seize the tyranny. ⟦s5⟧ Nor was any faith placed in me foretelling what would come. ⟦s6⟧ He was reckoned more trustworthy, flattering the Athenians, than I speaking the truth. ⟦s7⟧ So, having laid down my arms before the senate-house, I said that I was wiser than those who did not perceive that Pisistratus was straining after the tyranny, and braver than those who feared to assert the freedom of the commonwealth; but they construed it as Solon's madness. ⟦s8⟧ At last, having thus called my fatherland to witness, I departed: O Fatherland, here indeed is that Solon, ready and prepared [1148D] to bring you aid both by deed and by word; but to these men I even seem to be mad. ⟦s9⟧ And so, forsaking you, I set out, alone of all men the enemy of Pisistratus; but they are ready both to favor and to obey him. ⟦s10⟧ For you know the man, my friend — with what cunning, with what wit, with what art he seized the tyranny. [1149A] For he began winningly to entice the common people, then even inflicted wounds upon himself; and coming forth in public, and crying aloud that he had received these from enemies, he begged that they give him as guards four hundred of the most robust young men. ⟦s11⟧ And they, though I indeed struggled and cried out against it, consented. ⟦s12⟧ Now these were armed with stout cudgels; and so, hemmed about by these, he overthrew the state of the commonwealth. ⟦s13⟧ And while those men strive with vain hope to free their poor, that they might not serve for hire, all together were sent under the yoke, and serve Pisistratus alone.

## *Solon to Pisistratus.*

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ I believe indeed that I shall suffer no evil from you. ⟦s2⟧ For before the tyranny I was your friend, and not even now am I more hostile than any other of the Athenians who do not love the tyranny. ⟦s3⟧ Whether, moreover, [1149B] it be more useful to them to be ruled by the command of one, or for the affair to be administered publicly, let each determine according to his own judgment. ⟦s4⟧ Certainly I confess you to be the most excellent of all tyrants. ⟦s5⟧ But I recognize that it is not useful for me to return to Athens, lest anyone justly reproach me — I who long ago had handed over to the Athenians the commonwealth to be administered on equal terms, and, being present, had of my own accord declined the tyranny offered to me — should now, returning, seem by a sudden repentance to approve your deed.

## *Solon to Croesus.*

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ I marvelously embrace your goodwill toward us; and by the immortal gods, had I not long since resolved with myself to have my seat where the commonwealth is free, I would rather pass my life with you, in your kingdom, than at Athens, while Pisistratus violently exercises the tyranny. [1149C] But there we live more sweetly, according to our principle, where there are laws equal and common to all. ⟦s2⟧ Yet I will come to you, that I may for a while enjoy at least your hospitality.

## *Chilon to Periander.*

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ You bid me be free of military service, and live in exile — as though you yourself were going to be in safety. ⟦s2⟧ And yet I think that not even his own affairs are safe for a monarch; and I judge that tyrant happy to whom it has fallen to die in his own house without bloodshed.

## *Pittacus to Croesus.*

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ You bid me come into Lydia to behold your wealth. ⟦s2⟧ Though I have by no means beheld it, I in no way doubt that the son of Alyattes is the most opulent of all kings, and most crammed with gold. ⟦s3⟧ Nor indeed shall we have anything more, if we come to you at Sardis. ⟦s4⟧ For gold we ourselves do not lack, being content with a modest provision of life, [1149D] as much as suffices for me and my friends. ⟦s5⟧ Yet I will come, that I may become the intimate of a man so very kindly and hospitable. ⟦s6⟧ Farewell.

## *Cleobulus to Solon.*

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ You have indeed very many friends, and each of them has a house; but I judge that Lindus will be most convenient for Solon to dwell in, a free city. ⟦s2⟧ And it is a maritime island, where, if you should wish to tarry, you will dread no evil from Pisistratus, and your friends will flock to you from every side. ⟦s3⟧ Farewell.

## *Periander to the Sages.*

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1⟧ I render immortal thanks to Pythian Apollo, that my letters have found you gathered into one, and will themselves, as I trust, lead you through to Corinth. ⟦s2⟧ And so I await [1150A] you. ⟦s3⟧ Certainly you yourselves will see how courteously I receive you. ⟦s4⟧ As, therefore, last year at Sardis, you came into Lydia, so now I pray that it may not irk you to set out to me also, the tyrant of Corinth; for the Corinthians will see you, not without rejoicing, coming to the house of Periander.

## *Periander to Procles.*

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ To us indeed the crime committed against my wife was not according to our wish; but you, if of your own accord you do anything to my son who is ungrateful, do wrong. ⟦s2⟧ Either, therefore, restrain your savagery against my son, or I will bring him aid; for he too has paid the penalty long enough.

## *Thrasybulus to Periander.*

⟦p14⟧ ⟦s1⟧ To your herald indeed I dissembled nothing, but leading him into the standing corn, and striking the more eminent ears with my staff, I beat them down, he following after; and to you, if [1150B] you should ask, he would report whatever he saw or heard from me. ⟦s2⟧ Do you therefore act thus, if indeed you desire to hold the tyranny in safety and to be established firmly in it. ⟦s3⟧ Take away the leading men of the city, whether they seem friends or enemies; for to a tyrant even friends are often suspect.

## *Anacharsis to Croesus.*

⟦p15⟧ ⟦s1⟧ I, O king of the Lydians, have come into Greece, to learn the manners and pursuits and institutions of the Greeks. ⟦s2⟧ But I have no need of gold, and it is enough for me that I may return to the Scythians better and more learned. ⟦s3⟧ Yet I will come to you at Sardis, making very much of becoming intimate and friend to you.
