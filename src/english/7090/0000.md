## [nt: This is one of those letters which Holstenius published in the first part of the *Roman Collection*, inserted in the acts of the Roman synod under Boniface II. Since Innocent calls this letter the *first-fruits* of his episcopate, which he entered upon *at once* on the death of Anastasius, we may not without reason conclude that it was written about the month of May of the year 402.] EPISTLE I, OF POPE INNOCENT I TO ANYSIUS, BISHOP OF THESSALONICA. *Innocent confirms to Anysius that authority over the Churches of Illyricum which Anastasius and his predecessors had granted him.*

[0463D]

*To his most beloved brother ANYSIUS, INNOCENT.*

Since our God Christ has deigned to call to himself the man of holy memory, Bishop Anastasius, albeit swiftly, [0464D] reckoning his merits to be so great and of such a kind that they already exceeded the fellowship of human conversation, and that age, by reason of the purity of his life and the abundance of his teaching, with which he ruled the people of God with the whole rigour of ecclesiastical authority: lest his Church should remain even a little while without the helm of a ruler, at once, according to his mercy, with the consent of the holy priests and of all the clergy and people, with the peace which God, returning to heaven, [0465A] deigned to bestow on his Church, it was fitting to recognize me ordained in his place, dearest brother [n: ( f. te cognoscere)]; and to announce my first-fruits swiftly to none but a man [nt: The same Anysius is honoured by Palladius, in the *Dialogue on the Life of Chrysostom*, ch. 3, with the encomium of *an excellent old man*, τοῦ χαλογήρου. There it is also recorded that he entrusted his own letter as well to Eulysius, bishop of Apamea in Bithynia, who was carrying to Rome the letters of fifteen bishops in support of Chrysostom.] most excellent and ever faithfully labouring in God [n: (V. epist. 2 Nicolai I)]. To whom also my predecessors in the episcopate before me, men so great and of such a kind, that is, Damasus of holy memory, Siricius, and the man mentioned above, so deferred, that they committed to thy holiness, which is full of justice, the cognizance of all things that were done in those parts; it becomes thee to recognize that my littleness also holds this judgment and has the same will. For it was not right either that I should attempt to go against the judgment of men so great and so good, into whose place I am known to have succeeded, or that anything should seem to be taken away from thy merit, on which illustrious men [0465B] are seen to have conferred so great a favour of this authority. I therefore openly declare that I acknowledge this very thing to be reserved to my littleness also: that with equal judgment and in like form I grant, both to the good alike and to thy charity, that which thou deservest.

## NOTICE ON THE FOLLOWING LETTER.



## 1.

*The things in it copied from Siricius do not make its authenticity doubtful.* --Many things in this letter are copied from Letter 4 of Siricius. But, as Tillemont observed, this ought not to seem at all surprising, since Innocent, asked by Victricius to hand on to him the rules customarily kept in the Roman Church, could not have done this more happily than by copying out the decrees of his predecessors. Hence, therefore, no fit reason is found why either the aforesaid letter should be taken away from Siricius, or this one from Innocent. Nor are the grounds lighter which assign this one [0465C] to Innocent than were those which assign that one to Siricius. It is cited under his name by the Second Council of Tours, c. 20, by Cresconius in the *Breviarium canonum* passim, and by Pope Zacharias in Sirmond, tome I of the *Concilia Galliae*, Epistle 9, cc. 14 and 20. Moreover, of the old collections, of which we have examined a great many, in none is it lacking, in none is it not presented inscribed to Innocent. It is ascribed to him by the Corbie exemplar copied in the times of Pope Vigilius, by the Colbert one written in Lombardic capital letters, by the old Codex of canons published by Quesnel, by the exemplars of the Dionysian and Hadrianic collections, and also by the Spanish collection, the Isidorian, and many others.

## 2.

*That it is not to be put back to the year 405.* --In all these collections [nt: (if you except the Dionysian, in which the consular date is omitted)] the ending of the letter is entirely the same, and with the greatest agreement it is dated *Honorio aug.* VI *et Aristeneto conss.* Hence no room is left for the conjecture of a learned man, by which he suspects that this consular date was added to this letter of Innocent [0465D] by another hand, or at any rate that *post consulatum Honorii*, etc., should be read. For though we grant him that the words *post consulatum*, which copyists are accustomed to express by the first letters *P. C.*, could easily have fallen out; in that case surely *Honorii et Aristeneti*, not *Honorio et Aristeneto*, would have been retained in at least some old codex, nor ought the word *conss.* to have been added in any. Nor shall we be any more compelled to yield to that conjecture if we weigh what cause there may be why he thinks this letter written in the year 405 rather than 404. He is moved, namely, solely by the words of no. 5, in which Innocent mentions what he had endured on account of the curials, with the emperor present, and with Victricius placed beside him. From which he also thinks it follows that this letter was written not only after the emperor had come into the City, [0466A] but also after Victricius had returned from Rome to Rouen. And yet, he says, the Emperor Honorius came to Rome only in the year 403, in the month of December, and had not come there before, as is gathered from Claudian's poem: and Victricius, in so small an interval as runs from the emperor's arrival in the City to the 15th day of February of the year 404, could not return from Rome to Rouen, and send a letter thence to Innocent, so that it was delivered to him before that day. From which he concludes that this letter was dated not in the consulship of Honorius Augustus, but after the consulship of Honorius Augustus, that is, in the year 405, not 404. But if, on account of a fact of which no certain knowledge is had, it be lawful to deny credit to titles so many, so diverse and so ancient, what will be certain in history? Let it be, then, that Honorius came to Rome at the close of the year 403, and that Victricius was in the City at that time: what compels us to agree at the same time that the same bishop had returned to Rouen before he received this letter of Innocent? Innocent mentions no one sent to him in Victricius's name, no [0466B] letter of his delivered to him. But, says the learned man, the word *misi*, which Innocent uses in the opening, implies one who is absent. But if it implies one absent, it certainly does not signify one returned to Rouen, not one gone out of Rome. For what prevents Victricius, about to depart [sic: *asbcessurus*] from Rome, from having asked of him a book of the ecclesiastical rules while he was bidding Innocent farewell, and him, not having the book at hand at that time, from having taken care that it should be put together as soon as possible and delivered to Victricius before his departure from the City. Nor indeed is it necessary that those to whom we are said to send something should be far from us. Friends daily send one another little gifts, books, letters, etc., even when contained within the walls of the same city. Let Damasus and Jerome be an example of this, above, p. 579 and following.

## 3.

*It is called the book of rules. The division of the chapters.* --Further, the book of rules mentioned does not seem to be reckoned anything other than this very letter, inasmuch as in it Innocent, after a brief preface, sets out several chapters of discipline [0466C] by which Churches are to be ruled. These chapters, moreover, are presented summarily in most manuscripts, though not in all, with titles set before them. But neither in those is this division or summary of the chapters one and the same; rather it varies according to the bent and judgment of those who collected the pontifical letters. More ample than the rest, however, is the one which the exemplars of the Codex published by Quesnel present. Wherefore we should have held it enough to represent that one alone here, were it not worth the labour to add to it the other, from the collection of Dionysius Exiguus, with which the Spanish collection too, from which the Isidorian was copied, agrees in nearly everything. For since Cresconius and other collectors of canons are accustomed to cite the decrees of the Roman pontiffs according to the division into titles arranged by Dionysius, it is necessary, in order that their citations may be found, to know beforehand what that division is. Now Dionysius Exiguus comprised in twenty-nine titles all the decrees which he collected from Innocent, among which the first place is held by this pope's letter to Decentius, divided into eight [0466D] titles. Then this one to Victricius follows it, which he divides into twelve titles. Wherefore the first decree of this letter is called title 9 in Dionysius, and the last title 20, as is to be seen below.
