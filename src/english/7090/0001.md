## 4.

*The laws which forbade curials to move out of the curia.* --In those decrees, moreover, that which is said in cap. 11 about the curials must be explained here somewhat more fully than is permitted in the annotations, because mention of the same matter will occur again and again in other letters of other pontiffs as well. Those were called curials, or decurions, who discharged the municipal magistracies and offices. Of them the Emperor Majorian, at the beginning of Novel 1, speaks thus: « No one is unaware that the curials are the sinews [0467A] of the commonwealth and the vitals of the cities: whose body antiquity rightly called the lesser Senate. » But, as the same emperor observes, the judges or governors of the provinces reduced many by their unjust exactions to such a point that they deserted their native place. To the curials belonged the charge of taxes, of buildings, of public shows or games, etc. For this reason the laws of the emperors were severe, by which no one was permitted to be freed from the curia unless he had discharged all the offices of his curia. The Emperor Constantine ordained [n: (Cod. Th. lib. XII, tit. 1, leg. 11)]: « Let no judge attempt to grant to any curial exemption from civil duties, or free anyone from the curia at his own discretion: » Of those too who, leaving their curias, take refuge in the garrisons of the army, he orders [n: (Leg. 1)] « all who are not yet found liable to the primipilate to be released from the army and to return to the curia. » Likewise he laid down [n: (Leg. 12)], as the interpreter Anianus puts it, « that whatever curial wishes to pass from the city in which he was born to another, may in no way [0467B] . . . nor by any plea of necessity escape the condition owed to the curia, or the services of his own city, on the ground that he wished to change his city. » By another law too [n: (Leg. 20)] he commands « that no curial, unless all the offices of the curia have been performed in order (Anianus adds, *which are owed to the native place*), ought to undertake the office of curator or defensor. » Nor was the indulgence of the emperors any greater towards those who migrated from the curia to the clergy. Ambrose, Epistle 41, to the Emperor Theodosius, n. 29, thought he must remonstrate with him on this matter in this manner: « How shall I excuse myself before the bishops, who now lament grievously because certain men who have discharged the rank of presbyter, or ministers of the Church, through thirty and innumerable years, are dragged back from the sacred office and assigned to the curia? » And that remonstrance was all the more just because Constantine had earlier [n: (Leg. 13)] wished the curias to be admonished that henceforth « they should drag back to the curia only those whom they found, within twenty years' service in the offices, either to have fled their origin, [0467C] or to have spurned nomination and thrust themselves into the service. » Indeed Julian had afterwards decreed [n: (Leg. 56)]: Those who, born of curial origin, have loved the military office, when the service of ten years has been completed, shall by the authority of our commands be exempt from the curia.

## 5.

*On what condition curials were permitted to give their names to the clergy.* --Curials were not so removed from the clergy that every approach to it was closed to them. Valentinian and Valens permit them to give their names to the clergy on a certain condition, which they explain thus in a law given in the year 364, on the 10th day of September [n: (Leg. 59)]: « He who chooses the part of the Church, let him either, by conferring his own goods on a kinsman, make him a curial in his stead, or cede his means to the curia which he has left, he who has done neither being of necessity recalled: » that is, so that he who did not cede his goods either to a kinsman or to his curia is by all means to be recalled to the curia. On the same matter there is also this constitution of Theodosius, dated the year 384, on the 7th day of November [n: (Leg. 104)]: « Curials who prefer to serve the churches rather than the curias, if they wish [0467D] to be what they feign, let them despise those things which they withdraw. For we free them on no other terms than that their patrimonies be despised. » Ambrose had this in view when he wrote in Epistle 18, to Valentinian, n. 13: « If a priest seek the privilege of declining the curial burden, he must give up the possession of his paternal and ancestral property and of all his means. » Constantius had been somewhat more indulgent: for in the year 361, on the 29th day of August, he first decreed in favour of the bishops [n: (Leg. 49)]: « Let no one compel a bishop only to make over his means to the curia, as had formerly been laid down. » Then he wills that those should enjoy the same privilege « who have attained the ranks of presbyters, and of deacons also, or subdeacons and the rest, with the curia assisting, and giving its consent under the eye of the judge . . . above all if they are asked for by the voices of the whole people. » Otherwise he orders [0468A] that two portions of their substance be handed over, either to their kinsmen substituted for the curial duties, or to the curia; but reserves the third to themselves. And of those who had failed of the conditions set out above, what is added at the end of the same constitution seems to have been said: « If the superintendents of the granaries, and those who are about to undertake a magistracy, the superintendents of the peace also, or the receivers of the various kinds of goods, have thought they should aspire to the Church (that is, to the ministry of the Church), after they have entered upon the offices of the charge or honour laid on them; it will be fitting for the prelates of the heavenly law first to resist them, and, they themselves being the first to strive for it, for these to be recalled to the same duties: or if they neglect this, they are to be dragged back by the curials, with the judgment of the office supporting. » Indeed Arcadius, derogating from his father's law, issued a constitution dated the year 398, on the 27th day of July, and reported in the *Codex Justinianus*, book I, tit. 3, law 12, by which he orders that a curial, if he has been ordained a cleric, be restored at once upon prior summons: « For we do not suffer, he says, that law to profit clerics any longer which, upon cession of patrimony having followed, did not forbid decurions to be clerics. » That this, however, was said of the minor [0468B] clerics alone is proved by what the same emperor laid down at the close of the year 399 [n: (Leg. 163)]: « If any, from our father's second consulship, leaving the curia, have made themselves over to the fellowship of the clergy, if they have already been found worthy to be bishops, or presbyters, or deacons, let them indeed persevere in the sacred and more secret mysteries; but let them be compelled either to offer substitutes to the curia, or, according to the law long since passed, to hand over their means to the curia. All the rest, readers, subdeacons, or clerics to whom the privileges of clerics are not owed, are to be presented forthwith to the duties owed to their native place. » With laws so many and so diverse, therefore, the judges did not lack an occasion by which to reclaim importunately curials admitted into the clergy, and thereby to make trouble for the prelates of the churches.
