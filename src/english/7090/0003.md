## 10.

That no one presume to ordain a cleric from another's Church [n: (Dist. 71, c. 2)], unless his bishop, [nt: In the Corb. manuscript and another Colb., *precibus* is lacking. In the Council of Nicaea, can. 16, and in that of Sardica, can. 19, it is forbidden that another's cleric be ordained *without the consent and without the will of his own bishop*. In the *Codex canonum Ecclesiae Africanae*, c. 54, Bishop Epigonius, having first said that *it was laid down in many councils that no bishop snatch another's cleric for himself without the judgment of him whose cleric he was*, remonstrates with Julian because he had usurped his own cleric for himself and ordained him deacon without consulting him. To whom *Bishop Numidius says: If Julian appears to have done this without thy good pleasure being asked or consulted, we all judge it done unjustly and unworthily*; then he decrees that he be separated, unless he make satisfaction by returning the cleric.] won over by prayers, has been willing to grant it. This too the synod of Nicaea laid down [n: (Can. 17)], that one church should not receive a cleric cast off by another.

## CHAP. X and XI.



## 13.

Or at any rate they must prove that the same pope, and the framers of the canons older than he, judged that for a woman who has married another while her former husband lives, this latter marriage is not to be dissolved. The mind of Innocent is illustrated by the First Council of Toledo, celebrated in the year 400. For can. 16 sets down first *that a devoted woman who sins is not to be received in the Church, unless she has ceased to sin, and, ceasing, has done fitting penance for ten years*: and then adds: *But she who has taken a husband, let her not be admitted to penance, unless, while that husband is still living, she has begun to live chastely, or after he himself has died.* But how could she be proved to live chastely, unless by withdrawing from the adulterer? The fathers of the same council explain this more openly in can. 19, where, concerning the daughter of a bishop or presbyter or deacon, *if she has been devoted to God, and has sinned, and has taken a husband*, they lay down *that she be not admitted to communion, unless, her husband being dead, she has done penance; but if, while he lives, she has withdrawn, and repented . . . . . , let her receive penance when failing at the last*. For it first copies out his words, then professes that it is not lawful for it to depart from his decrees. And at last, having added to them the canon 52 of the Second Council of Arles and some others, it decrees concerning a consecrated virgin who has married, and concerning him whom she has married: *They are to be slain with the sword of the mouth, and deprived of communion, if they have been unwilling to observe the decrees of the elders left to them, and have disdained to hear their pastor, and have been unwilling to be separated. Those therefore who have wished to endure in this obstinacy, and to remain in the wallowing-place of evil conversation rather than to separate themselves from the forbidden marriage, let them be condemned by perpetual excommunication.* For if by *decrees of the elders* it there means, above all, the words of Innocent adduced, which cannot be denied, since it judges that they are despised by those *who have been unwilling to separate themselves from the forbidden marriage*; it remains that it judged Innocent to have commanded the dissolution of a marriage of this kind. So the Fifth Council of Orleans, can. 19, with which the First of Mâcon, can. 12, agrees, permits the same persons to be recalled to the grace of communion only on this condition, *if they have healed the fault by separation*. And indeed that marriage, which by the judgment of the Council of Mâcon *is rather a debauchery than a marriage*, is by the sentence of Siricius, Epistle 10, n. 3, *incest*, and on the *adulterer* nothing but the false name of husband can be laid. Innocent also, with the same pope, judges consecrated virgins to be truly *married*, inasmuch as *they had joined themselves to an immortal bridegroom*. Whence it follows that, since their bridegroom never ceases to live, neither can the law of the marriage entered into with him ever be dissolved. The Second Council of Tours praises the law 2 of the *Codex Theodosianus*, tit. 25, by which the Emperor Jovian decrees: *If anyone shall have dared, I will not say to carry off, but even to make an attempt on consecrated virgins for the sake of joining marriage, even unwilling ones, he shall be struck with capital sentence.*] had joined themselves has departed from the world. For if this principle is kept in the case of all, that whatever woman has married another while her husband is living is held an adulteress, and leave to do penance is not granted her unless one of them has died: how much more is it to be held in the case of her who had before joined herself to an immortal bridegroom, and afterwards has passed over [nt: Merlin and Crabbe, with the Colb. Langob. manuscript and also other codices of the Spanish and Isidorian collections: *transire elegit*.] to human nuptials!

## NOTICE ON THE FOLLOWING LETTER.

[0481B]

## 1.

*This letter is shorter in some books, fuller in others. Who cut it down.* --Some books present this letter shorter, others fuller. In the shorter form it was first published by Jacques Merlin, and was then reprinted in the same manner both in the collection of pontifical letters and in various editions of the councils, until Jacques Sermondus, having found it entire in old books, judged that it should be published for the public good. In the exemplars of the Spanish collection, such as the libraries of the Churches of Laon, Noyon and Beauvais preserve, as in all the exemplars of Isidore Mercator, it is had only mutilated. Hence we suspected that it was first truncated by the one who put together the Spanish collection of canons. But that suspicion was then removed by the Colbert codex 408, *On the Institution of Clerics*, written 700 years ago, and another like it and no more recent, the Coislin, which, although they are composed of the monuments of the Spanish collection alone, [0481C] nevertheless retain those chapters of this letter which are lacking in the shorter copy. Finally, when we learned that sound and genuine exemplars of the Spanish collection still exist in the Churches of Urgel, Gerona and Ripoll, in which this letter is read entire, we were fully persuaded that it had been truncated not by him who arranged that collection, but by some other. And since in all the exemplars of Isidore Mercator without exception it is had only mutilated and cut down, who would think another author of this deed should be sought? Nor is it surprising that a man who made no scruple of forging many things, interpolating others, altering more in various ways, did not fear to suppress some things as well. Content, namely, to have copied out Innocent's general decrees, he withdrew the rest, which pertain to special persons or causes, as more foreign to his purpose. Nor do the aforesaid books of Laon, Noyon and Beauvais stand in the way of this opinion. For although [0482A] they were copied from the Spanish collection, yet it is clear, expressly from the letter of Pope Vigilius to Profuturus, that he by whom they were written or dictated also had the collection of Isidore by him, and sometimes, although rarely, preferred it to the Spanish. Further, this letter of Innocent is preserved entire not only in the genuine exemplars of the Spanish collection but also in the very old one of Corbie, and in that of Saint-Maur-des-Fossés, now Colbert's. Since these things are so, that nothing remains for the reader to desire, we now present it entire from the fidelity of the sounder codices, in such a way that we also indicate, at its end, how it was published by Mercator.
