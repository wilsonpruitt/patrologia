## 7.

*That a council was held at Toledo in the year 400.* -- But we have so restored that council to the year 447 as not to deny that another was held at Toledo in the year 400, but rather to assert it plainly from that very fact. Of this council of Toledo Idatius speaks thus in his *Chronicle*, at the year 400: « In the province of Carthagena, in the city of Toledo, a synod of bishops is gathered, in which, as is contained in the acts, Symphosius and Dictinius and other bishops of the province of Gallaecia with them, followers of Priscillian, condemn his most blasphemous heresy, together with its very author, by the subscription of their profession. Certain things also are laid down to be observed concerning the discipline of the Church, Bishop Ortygius, who had been ordained at Celenae, taking part in the same council. » Now the council held by the order of Leo in the year 447 first reviews the canons pertaining to discipline which the synod of Toledo issued *Stilichone consule*, that is, in the year 400: and afterwards, from the [0484C] acts of the same synod, dated according to Spanish custom *era* 438, for the year 400, it presents not only the professions of Symphosius, Dictinius and others condemning the heresy of Priscillian together with its author himself, but also the sentence of the same council, by which their honour is preserved to them, and which is closed, in Ortygius's favour, with these words: « But to our brother Ortygius we pronounce that the churches from which he had been driven are to be restored. » Whence the agreement of those acts with the *Chronicle* of Idatius is manifest. Moreover, in the sentence of the synod of Toledo just mentioned Simplician is recorded as the one who then ruled the Church of Milan. But Simplician succeeded Ambrose only in the year 397, and he himself, before the sixteenth day before the Kalends of July of the year 401, had Venerius as successor, as is clear from the Council of Carthage inserted in the *Codex canonum Ecclesiae Africanae*, c. 56. And hence, therefore, the time at which the synod of Toledo was celebrated is confirmed.

## 8.

*In what sense the following [0484D] letter can be said to have been written to this synod.* --These things being laid down, since it is ascertained and beyond doubt that that synod of Toledo, whose canons and acts are reported in the aforesaid council of the Spains, was celebrated in the year 400, no controversy now seems to remain but that the following letter of Innocent was inscribed to this synod of Toledo. For first, in it, as may be read at nos. 6 and 7, the acts and decrees of this synod are praised more than once. Next, Innocent, at no. 10, notes that he is addressing the bishops « who decreed by their sentence that ordinations are to be made according to the Nicene rules. » And indeed the prelates of the First Council of Toledo, when in the very preface they had decreed « that the constitutions of the Nicene council are first of all to be kept in perpetuity, and that there must be no departing from them », proceed to lay down what ought to be observed in ordinations. Not, however, that Innocent is to be understood as writing to the synod of Toledo as though still assembled, but rather to those bishops who had come together in it. Unless one prefer [0485A] that here by the name *of the synod of Toledo* the bishops belonging to the synod of Toledo are understood. Certainly the praised pope speaks of the First Council of Toledo, no. 6, as celebrated *long since*. Nor does that *Patruinus of venerable memory*, in whose place he records, no. 9, that Gregory was substituted, seem other than the one who is read to have subscribed first to the canons of the First Council of Toledo. Finally, Innocent's zeal in this letter aims chiefly at this, that he may suppress and settle the schism which arose from Symphosius, Dictinius and others having been received with their honours at the synod of Toledo, and which was spreading more widely day by day. Wherefore it is manifest that some interval came between this letter and that synod. But how much came between cannot be defined with certainty. We may only conjecture that this letter was written before Spain was thrown into disorder by the tumults of war, and thus before the year 408, in which Constantine snatched that province from Honorius. But whether the letter is later than the preceding one or not, although we do not know, yet on account of the affinity of the matters defined in both concerning the ordinations of clerics, [0485B] it will be pleasing and convenient to the reader to have both next to one another. Nor is it now of much concern to him to have ascertained the time at which it was written, since he holds to whom, on what occasion, and with what design it was written. Here perhaps someone will wonder how the same pontiff now so earnestly praises and approves the indulgence of the synod of Toledo towards the followers of Priscillian, who afterwards so constantly refused that clerics ordained by Bonosus should keep their honours. But a wise priest ought to discern between leprosy and leprosy, and to search out when the severity of discipline is either to be retained or relaxed. For although the holy leaders of the churches are animated by the same spirit, yet at different times they have laid down different things concerning clerics returning to the Church from heresy or schism. The Nicene fathers, can. 8, willed the Novatians to remain in the clergy. On the testimony of the Africans, a council beyond the sea, in the times of Siricius and Ambrose, forbade that clerics coming from heresy [0485C] should keep their honours. But the Council of Toledo, in the year 400, judged that this was to be granted to the followers of Priscillian, and Innocent now approves its sentence *for the purpose of unity*, who in the year 414 will sharply vindicate the severity of ecclesiastical discipline in the case of the Bonosians. The Council of Carthage, however, held in the year 401, judged that this moderation should be used: that for clerics returning, where by their return some compensation of unity is made, their honours should be preserved, and where there is no compensation of this kind, they should be refused [n: (Cod. can. eccl. Afr., c. 68)].

## EPISTLE III. On [nt: We have borrowed this title and those that follow from Sirmond, not from the manuscripts.] the dissension and corrupted discipline of the Churches of Spain.



## I.

*That some bishops wrongly dissent from others on account of converts received from heresy.* --II. *On bishops [0486A] ordained by Rufinus and Minicius in other men's churches against the canons.* --III. *That the cause of Bishop John be examined, and of the others who were shunning the fellowship of communion.* --IV. *On the faulty ordinations of many, that what has been usurped hitherto be so passed over that it be forbidden for the future.* --V. *That the complaint of Gregory, bishop of Merida, be examined.* --VI. *Whom it is lawful to admit to the order of the clergy: and of what sort they are to be who are chosen. And that she too makes a man a bigamist who was his wife before baptism.*

*INNOCENT to all the bishops assembled in the synod of Toledo, most beloved brethren, greeting in the Lord.*

## 1.

*A schism in Spain. And contempt of the canons.* -- Though an excessive care has often held me anxious concerning [nt: Already before the First Council of Toledo a certain dissension had prevailed among the Spaniards concerning the ordinations of clerics. Wherefore Bishop Patruinus, in the preface of this council, speaks first thus: *Since we have each begun to do different things in our churches, and from this there are such great scandals, which reach even to schism, if it please you, let us decree by common counsel what is to be followed by all the bishops in ordaining clerics.* But after the council just mentioned, to this cause of schism was added another, when some admitted, and others contended should be rejected, those converted from the heresy of Priscillian to catholic communion whom the same synod had received in their honours. Innocent now tries to heal that twofold cause of so great an evil.] [0486B] the dissension and schism of the Churches, which report declares to be creeping more widely day by day through the Spains, and to be advancing at a quicker pace, the necessary time has come in which so great an amendment could not be put off, and a fitting medicine ought to be provided. For our brethren, our fellow bishop [nt: Among those who subscribed the First Council of Toledo, Hilarius is read in the fourth place.] Hilarius and the presbyter Elpidius, partly moved by love of unity, partly roused, as was fitting, by the ruin with which the province labours, have come to the apostolic see, and with grief and groaning have set forth that in the very bosom of the faith the peace within the province has been violated, the order of discipline confounded, and many things committed against the canons of the fathers, order being despised and the rules neglected, in the usurpation of churches, and that the concord in which the whole stability of our faith consists cannot be retained: which things [0486C] are kept in the record of acts drawn up in the session [nt: That is, of the Roman clergy, this name being taken from the more worthy rank. To the same session, above all in causes of this kind, the bishops who were staying at Rome were also accustomed to be summoned.] of the presbytery, and can be shown to you by reading.

## 2.

*The bishops of Baetica separate themselves from the rest on account of the Gallaecians who were received.* --First, then, as regards the faith itself, a dissension has arisen because the bishops of Baetica or of Carthagena, on account of communion with the Gallicians, have withdrawn [nt: So the Corb. and Colb. manuscripts. But in Sirmond, *dissederunt*. *Discesserunt* would rather have to be restored. Earlier, in the Corb. manuscript, *Galliarum*; in the Colb., *Galliorum*; perhaps in place of *Gallicorum* or *Gallaeorum*. For, as we heard above from the *Chronicle* of Idatius, the bishops of Gallaecia were Symphosius, Dictinius and others, who at the synod of Toledo condemned the heresy of Priscillian, together with its very author, by their subscription; that the Baetici and Carthaginians took ill their being received into peace, and therefore fell away from the communion of the rest, is not at all surprising. Gregory, bishop of Elvira in the province of Baetica, had sown deep the seeds of the Luciferian error. Nor is it astonishing that they propagated an unhappy fruit down to the times of Innocent.] from the peace of all: which is not only not diminished, but is even increased day by day by the zeal of contention, since, each one purposing to obtain what he wished, they have made out of such animosity an eternal round of evil and a kind of circle: when surely for every good man it is better in matters of this kind to be overcome than by an evil habit to obtain a perverse [0487A] purpose which has once pleased him. For what other cause, in former times too, was there of that Lucifer, besides obstinacy, which drew him back from concord [nt: In the manuscripts, *cum illorum*. The sentence would be more complete in this manner: *cum illorum qui Arianorum haeresim prudenti conversione damnaverant, tum illorum qui satisfactionem eorum susceperant.* On the same matter Sulpicius speaks thus, book II: *Lucifer so condemned those who had been at Ariminum that he separated himself even from the communion of those who had received them under satisfaction or penance.* *And to whom*, says Augustine, Epistle 185, n. 47, *an indulgence of this kind was displeasing, he fell into the darkness of schism, the light of charity being lost.* Since in these places Innocent, Sulpicius and Augustine reproach Lucifer with schism only, the error by which they dared to repeat baptism seems to be attributed to his disciples alone, not to himself.] with those who had condemned the heresy of the Arians by a prudent conversion? With the same zeal, [nt: Sirmond and Labbe: *post Priscilliani detestabilem sectam omnium detestatione damnatam*. The preposition *post* is absent from the manuscripts, in which moreover *consensione* is found, more elegantly than *detestatione*. To this refers what Jerome writes to Ctesiphon, that Priscillian was *condemned by the authority of the whole world*.] the detestable sect of Priscillian having been condemned by the deserved consent of all, we have learned that some took it very ill that those were received into the catholic faith who were converted by sounder counsel: to whom a useful deed, and the very peace of the churches, is discovered to have been displeasing. For when, for the purpose of unity, [nt: The Colb. manuscript: *Symposius*. That Symposius is not to be believed the one who subscribed the synod of Saragossa held against the Priscillianists in the year 380. How Symphosius, Dictinius and their companions, having confessed their error and professed the faith, were admitted to catholic communion is explained in the council which was held under Pope Leo, from the acts of the council of Toledo, tome II of the *Concilia* of Labbe, p. 1229 and following. In the sentence, however, which the fathers of Toledo pass on them, it is to be noted that, permitting Paternus to hold the church in which he had been established, they add: *about to receive* (him) *also into our communion, when the apostolic see shall have written back*; and, decreeing the same of the remaining bishops of Gallaecia who had always persisted in the communion of Symphosius, they wish them too at the same time to await, *by a like example, what the pope who now is, what St Simplician, bishop of Milan, and the other priests of the churches write back.* Where by the way it may be observed that the Roman bishop is designated by the simple name of *pope*. The fathers of Toledo continue: *But let Symphosius, an aged religious man, who did what we have written above, remain in his church . . . thence* (that is, from the pope and from the bishop of Milan) *he will await communion, whence he had before received the hope of future peace. Which we have decreed is to be observed also for Dictinius and Anterius.* Then there follows next: *But we have laid down that, before communion is restored to them through the pope* (see again the name of pope for the Roman pontiff) *or through St Simplician, neither bishops nor presbyters nor deacons are to be ordained by them.* Why do they pass sentence on all those bishops in such a way as to wish it to be awaited until communion is restored to the same by letters of the apostolic see and of the bishop of Milan? Light is thrown on this question by similar decrees both of the Council of Carthage held in the year 401, on the 18th day of June, and of another celebrated in the same year on the 13th day of September, by which, as may be seen in the *Codex canonum Ecclesiae Africanae*, c. 65, the Africans resolve that it should be sent to Anastasius the Roman pontiff and to Venerius bishop of Milan, that it might be lawful for them through these both to promote to the clergy infants baptized by the Donatists, and to receive in their honours clerics who wished to pass over from the Donatists to catholic unity, where this seemed to profit Christian peace. For they subjoin the reason of this matter, *because this had been forbidden from these sees*. Nor do the fathers of Toledo teach obscurely that they had determined that the aforesaid bishops should await the communion of the Roman and Milanese prelates for this reason, that some kind of judgment concerning them too had already gone before from those sees, inasmuch as Ambrose and Siricius had prescribed to them by letters certain conditions of peace. Whence the sentence of the Spaniards concerning Vegetinus is more absolute, in this manner: *But Vegetinus, against whom no sentence had been specially pronounced before, having given the profession which the synod accepted, we decree is to be restored to our communion.*] Symphosius himself also and [nt: That Dictinius had not only followed the tenets of Priscillian, but had also asserted them in books. Afterwards, however, in the Council of Toledo, detesting whatever he had either thought or written amiss, he said: *All my presumption in my writings I censure and condemn.* And soon after: *Again I repeat, whatever I wrote in my former comprehension and in the beginnings of my conversion, I reject all with my whole heart. . . . Except the name of God, I anathematize all . . . All things that are found against the faith I condemn together with their very author.* Yet his writings could not be suppressed so carefully but that down to the times of Pope Leo they were read by many with veneration, to whom indeed that pope, Epistle 17, n. 19, counsels that *they ought rather to love his restoration than his fall*.] Dictinius, condemning the depraved heresy, were received, so that, such persons being cut off, the inbred dissension might be utterly extinguished; there were found some to whom the very correction rightly made [n: (Mss. facit)] was displeasing. And now [0487B] the churches are at variance, which with no small animosity dissemble from one another ( *Or* disagree and go off into enmities). But if the correction had been kept by the priests with sounder counsel, both the state of the catholic faith would [0488A] have remained entire, and no scandal would have corrupted the concord useful in all things.
