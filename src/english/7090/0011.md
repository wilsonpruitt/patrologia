## EPISTLE XII. [nt: In the edition of the Councils, 28. The one which was 12 is Isidore Mercator's. This letter, like the preceding VII, we owe to Sozomen alone. Of both he speaks thus by way of preface, book VIII, c. 24: *When Innocent was striving to assemble an ecumenical synod, he wrote to John, and separately to the clerics of the city of Constantinople.* By which words he seems to signify either that both letters were sent at the same time, or at least that this one is not later than the seventh. But it is not unusual for this writer to put together and confound things done at distant times. Certainly, since there is deep silence in this letter about assembling the synod, on which Innocent had placed all his hope, this is no light argument that it was written when he had fallen from this hope. It is not far from the truth that Innocent, moved by the last letter of John, wrote this one to console the holy prelate in the evils by which he had learned that he was afflicted. And if so, it could scarcely have been delivered to John while he survived.] OF POPE INNOCENT TO JOHN CHRYSOSTOM, BISHOP OF CONSTANTINOPLE. *He consoles John by setting before his eyes the commendation of patience.*

*To his beloved brother JOHN, INNOCENT.*

Although the innocent man ought to expect all prosperous things and to ask mercy of God, yet from us also, who counsel patience, fitting letters have been sent by [nt: The same Cyriacus, with Paul, likewise a deacon, had already in the year 404 been sent to Rome by John to deliver his earlier Epistle 4 to Innocent, as is declared in the same letter, n. 1.] Cyriacus the deacon, lest [0513B] insult avail more to crush than a good conscience to raise up to hope. For neither art thou to be taught, the teacher and pastor of so many peoples, that all the best men are always and constantly tried, whether they abide in the vigour of patience, and do not give way to any labour and trouble. And truly conscience is a firm defence against all things that happen unjustly: which unless one has overcome by patience, he offers an argument of evil suspicion against himself. For he ought to bear all things who relies on trust first in God, and then in his conscience. For a good and upright man can indeed be exercised toward patience, but cannot be overcome; since the divine Scriptures fortify and guard his mind. For the sacred readings which we hand on to the peoples abound in examples, which testify that almost all the [0513C] saints were vexed in various ways, and proved as by a kind of examination, and so came to the crown of patience. Let conscience itself, then, console thy charity, brother greatly to be revered, [nt: Valesius rendered this into Latin thus: *which in calamities is wont to be the consolation of virtue*. We cleave to the Greek wording, in which it is not conscience that is proclaimed the consolation of virtue, but rather virtue the consolation of conscience. Innocent asserts more surely what the Orator had declared somewhat more timidly: *Punishments which are undergone through virtue have some consolation in themselves.* And by the name of virtue Innocent chiefly understands patience, of which in Epistle 7, to the clergy and people of Constantinople, having set out their calamity, he writes in n. 1: *Which thing indeed the consolation of patience alone can heal.*] which in calamities has the consolation of virtue. For under the eyes of the Lord Christ a pure conscience stands in the haven of peace.

* To the beloved brother John, Innocent. * [0514A]

Although the blameless man ought to expect all good things, and to ask mercy from God, nevertheless from us also, who counsel forbearance, the fitting letters have been sent by Cyriacus the deacon; so that insult may not be able to do more [0514B] in crushing, [Add. ἢ] the good conscience in hoping. For thou oughtest not to be taught, thou the teacher and shepherd of so many peoples, that the best men are always and often tried, whether they abide in the vigour of endurance, and fall under no labour of suffering. And conscience is in truth a sure thing against all that befall unjustly; which unless one overcome by enduring, he brings forth a proof of a base suspicion. For he ought to endure all things who trusts first in God, and then also in his own conscience. Whereas the good and noble man can above all be exercised toward endurance, but not be conquered; since the divine writings guard his understanding. And the divine readings which we hand on to the peoples abound in examples, which have assured that almost all the saints were worn down in divers ways [0514C] and continually, and tried as in a kind of examination, and so came to the crown of endurance. Let conscience itself console thy charity, most honoured brother, which in afflictions has the consolation of virtue. For while the Master Christ looks on, the conscience, having been cleansed, will stand in the haven of peace.

## EPISTLE [nt: Formerly recited in the Roman synod under Boniface II, thereafter also cited by Nicholas I, Epistle 2, it was afterwards brought to light by Lucas Holstenius together with the other monuments of the aforesaid Roman synod. The time at which it was written is indicated by the consular note. But the most illustrious man Tillemont, vol. X, p. 829, thinks that there is a fault in this note, and that for *Honorius IX and Theodosius V* there should be restored *Honorius VII and Theodosius II*. From which it would follow that this letter was written in the year 407. And he strives to prove this opinion by this induction. First, indeed, he states that of the letters which Innocent wrote to Rufus this is the first, and was given by him as soon as he was informed of Rufus's ordination; which is not to be denied. Then he adds that that Rufus to whom Innocent now writes seems to be no other than he to whom the same pope, Epistle 17, to Marcian, mentions that he had already given letters about the clerics of Naissus. Which being likewise granted, he subjoins: *But Innocent wrote this letter to Marcian while staying at Ravenna, whither he came at the beginning of the year 409, and where he was still living in the month of August of the year 410; and there is no trace in history from which we might conjecture that he returned besides to the same city.* From which he thinks it follows that neither Rufus's ordination nor this letter is to be put later than the year 408. But since by no means all things that happened have been written, and he who went to Ravenna once could also go there a second and a third time; and since, as the most illustrious man himself admits, a cause and occasion of this was not lacking in the year 413, in which Heraclianus was preventing grain from being carried to Rome: it seems by no means safe to change a certain epoch by an uncertain conjecture. Surely that slip of the copyists would be exceedingly unusual, if, when they had erred in expressing the years of two consuls who had borne the same dignity several times, their error nevertheless so aptly held together in designating their consulships, that with the ninth consulship of Honorius they joined the fifth of Theodosius, not the fourth or the third, and in turn with the fifth of Theodosius the ninth of Honorius, not the seventh or any other whatever.] XIII. TO RUFUS, BISHOP OF THESSALONICA. *The care of various provinces is committed to him, in which he is constituted Innocent's vicar and the first among the primates.*

*To his most beloved brother RUFUS, INNOCENT.*

## 1.

To the most choice and most glorious Moses, in the freeing and ruling of Israel, the Lord so committed all things that he commanded him, in a manifest report, that the counsel of his father-in-law Jothor was to be followed [n: (Exod. XVIII)]: namely that he should constitute men nearly equal in the rank of judicial dignity, who should settle the multitudes of causes among the peoples according to the place entrusted to them, he himself being free chiefly, or even, for divine [nt: Having found by experience that in old books, where the sequence of the discourse requires the word *consultationis*, the word *consolationis* is not rarely, indeed for the most part, wont to be read, we suspect the same fault here also, and think there should be restored *ipso maximis vel etiam divinis consultationibus vacaturo*. For Innocent looks to these words of Exodus XVIII, 19 and 22: *Be thou to the people in those things which pertain to God, to refer what is said to him... But whatever is greater, let them refer to thee, and let them judge the least things themselves.*] consolations. Nor was the form of the Apostles promulgated otherwise [n: (Act. VI, 2 et 3)] than [0515B] that they themselves, constituted princes of the Gospel, should commit to their disciples the causes and needs of other matters, to be cared for and attended to. So, in short, Paul, wonderful in all compassion, committed to Titus what he should care for in Crete [n: (Tit. I, 5)], to Timothy what he should dispose throughout Asia [n: (I Tim. I)], as we learn from the reading of the sacred epistles.

## 2.

Divinely, therefore, this grace running forward, let it learn so to take counsel for the churches separated from me by long intervals, that they may judge that to thy prudence and gravity [nt: That those at whose suggestion Justinian wrote in Novel 11 that only in the times of Attila, that is after the year 440, was the dignity of vicar through Illyricum attributed to the bishop of Thessalonica, imposed upon this emperor, is manifest from this.] are to be committed the care and the causes, if any arise throughout the churches of Achaia, Thessaly, Old Epirus, New Epirus, and Crete, Dacia Mediterranea, Dacia Ripensis, Moesia, Dardania and Praevalis, with Christ [0516A] the Lord assenting [n: (Ad hunc locum respicit Nicolaus I epist. 2)]. For truly, by his most sacred admonitions, we enjoin this solicitude upon the providence and virtue of thy most choice sincerity; not establishing these things so for the first time, but imitating our apostolic predecessors, who willed that these things be enjoined upon the most blessed [nt: That this charge was enjoined upon Acholius at least from the year 380, against the opinion of Haller, we observed in the Notice on the 8th letter of Damasus.] Acholius and Anysius according to their merits. For it is most just that those who have deserved well be so adorned with honours, as it seems necessary that the arrogant be beaten down. It pleases, therefore, that there be reward for the good, and for the less good [nt: *Censio* commonly signifies a mark of censure from the censor. Presently by the name *primatum* are understood the metropolitans of the provinces named before.] censure: for so both the one is corrected, and the other is ennobled.

## 3.

Take up, therefore, most beloved brother, in our stead, the care throughout the churches written above, saving their primacy: and first among the primates themselves, whatever [0516B] it shall be necessary for them to send to us, let them not ask without thy judgment. For so either through thy experience whatever it is will be finished; or we direct that by thy counsel it is to be brought as far as us. Know, moreover, that it is lawful and permitted to thy fraternity by the favour of the apostolic see that, when any ecclesiastical question is to be treated and examined either in thy province or in the provinces mentioned, thou mayest take with thee whom thou wilt of the bishops as associates from whatever Churches, by whose faith and moderation thou mayest direct, as the best arbiter, whatever necessity or cause shall have demanded, and mayest settle it as the chief mediator, as one chosen by us. The whole preparation of the documents in the cause [nt: So Holstenius, not, as Tillemont reads, *Achivorum*. That the discourse here is of archives, as of documents, both what precedes and what follows persuade. For it appears that the truth of the documents by which the office of vicar was delegated to the prelate of Thessalonica was then called into doubt by some. Wherefore Innocent, although he had said to Rufus just before, *as one chosen by us*, which sounds as though the power was conferred on him not so much by the prerogative of his see as by the gift of the one choosing him, yet does not deny that it was supported by custom: and therefore he urges the same man to review both his own former letter and others of his predecessors given on that account.] of the archives we have ordered to be made with the presbyter [nt: In the edition of the Councils, 11. The one which was 14 is now 25. By that very man, perhaps, who by Siricius, Epistle 9, n. 5, is said to have been given to Bassus, bishop, *into partnership in ruling the church*.] Senecio, a man very [0517A] mature. And so, from our former [nt: Written not, as it seems to me at least, to Rufus himself, but to his predecessor Anysius, and which holds the first place above. And hence again it is confirmed that the word *archivorum* was to be retained, and not changed.] letter, and from these documents, reviewing well what thou oughtest to do, take cognizance of it. For we have made this our will sufficiently manifest by letters, as was fitting, throughout each province. Given on the fifteenth day before the Kalends of July [n: (Junii 17 ann. 412)], Honorius for the ninth time and Theodosius for the fifth time, augusti, being consuls.

## EPISTLE [nt: In the Roman edition of the pontifical letters it is noted as copied *from Cresconius*. It would perhaps have been more fittingly noted *from Dionysius*. OF POPE INNOCENT, TO JULIANA, A NOBLE LADY, EXHORTATORY.

[0518B]That the amplitude of thy religion stands forth a singular member of the Church, and is most reverently honoured by us, is sufficiently manifest to all. For on the very summit of nobility thou bestowest a far nobler devotion upon the Church, and, rejoicing the more in the knowledge of Christ, thou obeyest his precepts, and exultest in faith rather than boastest of the flower of so great a lineage. It is of the highest virtue to have overcome the glory of the flesh: and it is of the great grace of Christ [0519A] [nt: Baronius observes that this was said not without cause, but in order that by it, as by an antidote, the poison lurking in these words of Pelagius to Demetrias might be shaken out, words which so greatly displeased Alypius and Augustine: *Spiritual riches no one besides thyself will be able to confer on himself: in these thou art rightly to be praised, in these deservedly to be preferred to others, which cannot be except from thee and in thee.* Nor is the close of the letter less opposed to them, in which Innocent does not say *that thou mayest render thyself more illustrious through the ages to him to whom thou hast shown thyself distinguished*, but refers the whole of this to the gift of God. Being, then, secure, most beloved, strive to make illustrious with eternal and divine offices whatever spans of this life there are: that he who made thee distinguished may render thee more illustrious to himself through the ages.
