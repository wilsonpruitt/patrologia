## 1.

All those letters Dionysius Exiguus has preserved for us. From his collection they flowed into the Spanish and the Isidorian. It is admitted that they were written on the occasion of the peace of the Antiochene church. Of which peace Theodoret, book V of the History, c. 35, to touch on his words summarily, committed these things to memory. [0539B] Alexander, excelling in the institute of a stricter life, in the pursuit of wisdom, in contempt of riches, in eloquence and in countless other gifts, when he had succeeded Porphyrius, joined to the body of the Church, by the gentle persuasion of his words and frequent exhortations, the Eustathians, whom formerly Paulinus, and after him Evagrius, had by no means permitted to be united with the rest; and for that cause he instituted a festival of such a kind as no mortal had ever seen. Having set these things first, the same writer adds concerning Alexander: « He first of all inscribed the name of the most excellent John in the ecclesiastical tablets. » And the dissension of the Antiochene church, as the same author recounts, book III, c. 5, « lasted eighty-five years, » up to Alexander. Whence the time also at which that peace was entered into, and the following letters were written, could be discerned and determined with certainty, if there were agreement among the learned about the beginning of the aforesaid dissension. And Baronius indeed, at the year 308, while he thinks it is to be reckoned either from the day on which Meletius professed the faith of the consubstantial, [0539C] or from that on which Lucifer ordained Paulinus, that is, from the year 361 or 362, is compelled to reject Theodoret's reckoning as false. But Tillemont, counting the beginning of the Antiochene dissension from the deposition of Eustathius, which he judges to have happened in the year 430 or 431, observes that those eighty-five years end in the year 413 or 414. Eutychius, although not accurate enough elsewhere, supports this computation, when he relates that Porphyrius, whom he calls Barchus, presided over the church of Antioch for ten years. For Palladius, in the dialogue on the Life of Chrysostom, p. 141, is the authority that he was put in place of Flavian about the beginning of the year 403. Besides this, Theodoret, book V, cap. 35, teaches that the reconciliation of the Antiochene church came about when Cyril, the successor of Theophilus, was bishop of the Alexandrian church. But Theophilus, as Socrates witnesses, book VII, c. 7, ceased to live in the year 412, on the 15th day of the month of October. From which it follows that this reconciliation cannot be assigned earlier than the year 413. But there is no inconvenience [0539D] if it is put off to the year 414 or even 415.

## 2.

The reconciliation of the dissenting parties in the Antiochene church was followed at no long interval by the embassy by which its bishop Alexander made haste to ask for the communion of the Roman [0540A] see. The father of the Annals judged that he did not obtain it at once; and therefore he devised a double embassy: one in which Alexander asked for Innocent's communion, and to which Innocent prescribed the conditions for granting peace; the other in which Alexander testified that the prescribed conditions had been satisfied by him. But no cause appears for this double embassy. For already from the death of John Chrysostom, that is, from the year 407, Innocent had made these conditions well enough known, when he denied his communion to the bishops of the East until they should have inscribed John's name in the sacred diptychs. On which matter Theodoret, book V, c. 34, speaks thus: « After the death of the great Doctor of the world, the Western bishops (without doubt by the consent and prescription of Innocent) did not admit the communion of the bishops of Egypt and of the East and of the Bosphorus and of Thrace before they had recorded the name of that divine man together with the rest of the bishops who had died. » Nor was Alexander so ignorant of affairs as not to know this; nor [0540B] so inconsiderate as knowingly to send a useless embassy. And indeed Innocent, in epist. 22, recalling that the conditions of peace had been fulfilled by Alexander, and demanding a like « profession of the fulfilment of all the conditions » from Atticus, does not say, which we prescribed when Alexander's embassy was sent back, but « which we declared beforehand at different times. »

## 3.

Nor can we assent either to the learned Tillemont, who judges that epistle 20 was sent first, and 19 given somewhat later. For we judge that both, with 21 to Acacius joined to them, were given together. And the note added at the foot shows that the former, that is 19, was synodal and common to Innocent with the twenty bishops who subscribed it. The other, that is 20, we judge private and familiar. Mention of the synodal acts, which were subjoined to the former, is made in both.

## 1.

By the grace of apostolic favour the great beauty of peace has shone out even to us: and it has shed so much light and joy upon the faithful that, speaking the greatest praises to God, we confessed that we owed greater. And we were the more gladdened by God because, all the sides [0540D] of the actions of thy fraternity having been examined, we learned that the whole had been so done with piety and patience that in all things we praised the Lord. And we see that success itself was granted [0541A] to thy labours for this reason: that, being a lover of peace with all thy strength, thou didst find it when sought, and didst keep it when found with the highest charity, both towards all, and especially concerning those who had once been counted under the name of the bishops Paulinus and Evagrius. [nt: (I have the sum of my wishes, in that the cleansing of the ancient blemish is bestowed in thy times and by thy merits.)] Of whom I judged that those also of the same name who received the merits of the clerical state in Italy should, for the good of quiet, retain the grace they had received. And because our fellow presbyter Cassianus said [nt: Merlin and Crabbe, with the Royal, Noyon and one Colbert manuscript: *duxit*. This Cassian is thought to be the writer of the *Conferences*, who, as Palladius relates in the Life of Chrysostom, p. 27, came to Rome while still a deacon as the year 404 was passing: but afterwards was honoured with the grade of the presbyterate. This whole passage, inasmuch as it is that of a mind speaking with the highest feeling, and caring not so much how it should order the words as how it should express its feeling, will perhaps seem intricate to some. To understand it, it must be recalled that the Antiochene church had been split into two parties, namely that of Paulinus, to whom Evagrius succeeded, and that of Flavian, in whose place Alexander was put after Porphyrius, Evagrius having already died. And because the West favoured Paulinus and then Evagrius, some who were attached to them betook themselves to Italy, and there had earned the dignity of the clerical state. Now the custom of ecclesiastical discipline required that, if the dignity conferred were judged to be kept for these men, they should be counted clerics of that city by whose bishops they had been ordained or promoted. But Innocent first judged that the grace of the clerical state was to be kept for the clerics of the parties of Paulinus or Evagrius ordained or promoted in Italy, and then, at Cassian's instance, decreed that the same *should be numbered among the other priests and ministers who are in the city* of Antioch. The same things are confirmed below too, in epist. 23, and stated a little more openly. Here it is to be observed in passing that by the word *sacerdotes*, contrary to the usual custom of the ancients, presbyters, not bishops, are signified. In the same sense too Jerome, using this word on chapter 48 of Ezekiel, rebukes the arrogance of the deacons, *who, ignorant of the humility of their order, swell beyond the priests, that is, the presbyters.* And in the epistle to Evangelus: *If a presbyter is ordained from a deacon, let him know that he is less in gains, greater in priesthood.* Innocent, in epist. 25, n. 6, writes that presbyters are *second priests*.] it would be pleasing to thy worthiness if, by my counsel, they were judged to hold the order of the clerical state in your city, I have decreed, on account of thy benevolence and the promises of the man mentioned, that they be numbered among the other priests and ministers who are in the city, dearest brother. Gladly [0541B] besides I learned concerning the bishops [nt: That those two bishops were shut up in their houses for three years for the cause of Chrysostom, Palladius relates, p. 195, in these words: *Elpidius, that great bishop of Laodicea in Syria, and Pappus, for the whole three years did not come down the stairs of their houses, giving themselves to prayers.*] Elpidius and Pappus that they have recovered their churches without dispute: and, as the acts subjoined [nt: In the printed editions, as in the Isidorian: *subscripta*. Better in the manuscripts of the collections of Dionysius, Hadrian and the Spanish: *subjecta*. Namely, Innocent did not confirm the peace before he had heard Alexander's legates in a public assembly, and had diligently questioned them about the things which had been done at Antioch. And since he had taken care that acts should be drawn up on that matter, he saw to it that they were subjoined to this letter.] testify, I inquired anxiously and much whether all the conditions had been satisfied in the cause of the blessed and truly God-worthy priest [0542A] John. And when the assertion of the legates acknowledged point by point that they had been fulfilled as wished, giving thanks to the Lord, I received the communion of your Church in such a way that I openly declare that the fellow disciples of the apostolic see were the first to give the others the way of peace, in which the kindness of the Lord Christ will so embrace and fortify you and us, established in it, that for the rest it may be struck by no [nt: The Colbert exemplar: *titulamento*: which would not unfitly be said for a title, or pretext, or colour. A little before, the Antiochenes are called *fellow disciples of the apostolic see*: because the Antiochene church was founded by the same Apostle as teacher as the Roman.] titillation, or by the slight shame of anyone's contention.

## 2.

## EPISTLE XX [nt: In the edition of the Councils, 15. And what was 20 is now 41. This letter too is marked in the Roman edition *ex Cresconio*. *INNOCENT TO ALEXANDER, BISHOP.*

How welcome to me, how pious, how necessary was the embassy sent to us by thy holiness, dearest brother, thou wilt learn from the unfolding of the acts themselves. For our fellow presbyter Cassianus wished that this little page of our friendship should be written by our fellow presbyter Paul, Nicholas the deacon, [0543B] and Peter the subdeacon, our sons, as it were the first fruits of our peace. I therefore greet both thy brotherhood to me in Christ and all that church which thinks so well with thee. And I ask that, as you do, you both address us more often by letters and gladden us more frequently about your welfare. For the Lord, as I trust, will grant us to make up the losses of all the past time by a most loving converse [nt: The editions: *recompensare*: the manuscripts opposing.] of letters.
