## 12.

To all these things, then, dearest brother, which thy love wished us to set forth, we have taken care to answer as far as we were able, so that thy Church may be able to preserve and guard the Roman custom, from which it draws its origin. The rest, which it was not right to write, we shall be able to declare when asked, once thou art present. And it will be for the power of the Lord to bring this about too: that thou instruct well both thy Church and our clerics who serve the divine offices under thy pontificate, and give to others a pattern which they ought to imitate. [0561B] Given on the fourteenth day before the Kalends of April [n: (Martii XIX, ann. 416)], Theodosius Augustus for the seventh time and Palladius [nt: The printed editions: *viris clarissimis*. More correctly the manuscripts: *v. c.*, that is, *viro clarissimo*: which applies to Palladius alone. But where the notaries wish to express *viris clarissimis*, they double the same abbreviation in this manner: *vv. cc.*, which is then understood to be predicated of two consuls.], a most illustrious man, being consuls.

## MONITUM ON THE FOLLOWING EPISTLES.



## 1.

The six following letters are presented by a great many old codices examined in the newest edition of Augustine. The same, together with the following 32, are presented by the exemplars not only of the collection of Isidore, but also of the Spanish collection, and also of the Codex published by Quesnel. Augustine himself, epistle formerly 106, now 186, num. 2, mentions them one by one in this manner: « So reports were sent on this matter from two councils, that of Carthage and that of Milevis, to the apostolic see . . . We also wrote to Pope Innocent of blessed memory, besides the reports of the councils, a familiar letter, in which we dealt with the cause itself at somewhat greater length. To all of it he wrote back to us in the manner which was right and fitting for the apostolic [0561C] see. » And expressly concerning the letters of Innocent he says this, book I against Julian, c. 4: « For what could that holy man answer to the African councils, except what the apostolic see and the Roman Church, with the others, has held perseveringly from of old? » Then, having copied out certain sentences which Innocent sets forth in those letters, he presses Julian thus: « Answer him, or rather the Lord himself, whose testimony that prelate used. » Likewise, book II against the Two Letters of the Pelagians, c. 4, n. 6, where he has brought forward some words of Epistle 29, he exclaims: « What is clearer than this judgment of the apostolic see? » And again, num. 7, having set out other words from Epistle 30, he adds: « What does the ungrateful man say to this? » So he calls Coelestius, whom there too, n. 5, he convicts of deceit, since he afterwards answered falsely before Zosimus that he agreed with these letters of Innocent. With so many and such evident testimonies Henry Noris, Hist. Pelag. p. 74, refutes the censure of Vossius, who after Erasmus calls into doubt « whether these letters are worthy of so great a prelate, » [0561D] and at the same time confutes the opinion of a certain more recent writer, who seems likewise to hesitate about their genuineness. We pass over in silence Prosper, Pope Celestine and the other [0562A] writers who ascribe those letters to Innocent in express words. Nor is there need to explain at greater length why he by no means discourses more fully on so abundant and so ample a matter, since he himself has given the reason for this.

## 2.

What the cause was for the Africans to write to Innocent, they themselves did not keep silent. They testify that they desire, in so grave a storm as Pelagius and Coelestius had stirred up, to be made more certain about the truth of the faith; and that their own sentence be approved by the judgment of Innocent; and that in this way the firm authority of the apostolic see be added to their statutes. Then at last they indicate that they hope that those who hold such perverse and pernicious opinions will more easily yield to the authority of the same see, as being « drawn from the authority of the holy Scriptures [n: (epist. 27, n. 3)] ».

## 3.

How much the Africans judge should be deferred to the apostolic see, they show clearly in their letters. From this Innocent too seized the occasion of extolling it. If it should seem to anyone that in this he exceeded measure, Augustine will refute him, [0562B] in whose judgment, as we saw above, « he wrote back to all of it in the manner which was right and fitting for a prelate of the apostolic see. » Or are Augustine and the other Africans to be thought to have approved only what Innocent judged concerning Pelagius, Coelestius and their associates, and not likewise what he says by way of preface, in Epistles 29 and 30, about the prerogative of his see? That these things too were approved by them is gathered from what their successors write in the opening of the letter which, sent by three councils of Africa to Pope Theodore, was afterwards read out in the Lateran council under Martin I. For there whatever Innocent, epist 29, maintains about the dignity of the Roman Church, they repeat in their own name in the same sentences, and all but entirely in the same words [n: (Lab. t. 6, p. 118)]. But nothing more illustrious can be wished for to commend the pre-eminence of the apostolic see than what Augustine, on receiving the letters of Innocent, openly proclaimed, serm. 131, n. 10: « For already two councils have been sent on this cause [0562C] to the apostolic see; rescripts too have come from there: the cause is finished; would that the error may at some time be finished! » That is, the Pelagian cause was diligently examined and judged in two African councils. And since the judgment of the apostolic see also agrees with them, the cause, decided by a threefold judgment of so great authority, is rightly judged to be finished.

## 4.

So Augustine preached, when he was still uncertain whether the Pelagians would abide by those rescripts or not; and so when they had recently arrived in Africa. Now it has been observed in the new edition of Augustine that that Sermon is headed in an old codex « preached on the ninth day before the Kalends of October, on the Lord's day », and that in the year 417 the ninth day before the Kalends of October fell on a Sunday. From which it also follows that those rescripts are not more recent than the month of September of the year 417. But their date remains certain, preserved in manuscripts of the best repute, in which they are dated « on the sixth day before the Kalends of February, after the consulship of Theodosius for the seventh time and Junius for the fourth, V. C., » that is, on the 27th day of the month of January of the year 417. Nor does this other date, which some codices present, make that date [0562D] doubtful, but rather confirms it: « on the sixth day before the Kalends of February, Honorius and Constantius, V. C., being consuls. » For [0563A] in fact Honorius and Constantius held the consulship in the year 417. And we shall judge, not rashly, that this change came about partly through the work of a copyist, who noted in the margin, in place of the consuls of the past year, the consuls of that very year, whom Innocent perhaps did not then know, and partly through the simplicity of a scribe, who, removing the original reading, carried that annotation into the text. Further, of the seven following letters, the first was written a little before 27 and 28, but was sent together with them by the same Julius, an African bishop, as the year 416 was running out. The four following, however, were dated on one day and were delivered by the same bishop Julius, who carried them. That these too were delivered to the Africans before the month of October of the year 417 is concluded from the note prefixed to Augustine's Sermon 131, as we pointed out before.

## 5.

Peter Marca, book VII On the Concord, c. 12, n. 5, and John Garnier, in the Dissertation on the synods held in the Pelagian cause, assert that Innocent wrote back to the Africans from some Roman synod: but Ant. Pagi, at the year 416, n. 19, denies this, and confirms his opinion [0563B] with the words of Augustine, book IV to Boniface. But that twofold opinion may be reconciled without much trouble. For if, when the Pelagians boasted that the dogmas of Pelagius and Coelestius had been condemned without the gathering of a synod, Augustine, book IV against the Two Letters of the Pelagians, c. 12, n. 31, does not deny this to them; the same Doctor, in the same work, book II, c. 3, answers the same heretics, who were accusing the Roman clerics of prevarication: « If, which God forbid, judgment had then been given in the Roman church concerning Coelestius or Pelagius in such a way that those dogmas of theirs, which Pope Innocent had condemned in them and with them, were pronounced worthy to be approved and held; from this rather would the brand of prevarication have to be burned upon the Roman clerics. » But there would be nothing for which these clerics could be blamed, if Innocent had written against Pelagius and Coelestius without their knowledge. That holy pope, therefore, composed these letters without the gathering of a synod of bishops summoned from different provinces, but not without a gathering of the Roman [0563C] clerics: and in this way opinions that seem to conflict are reconciled; and the perpetual custom [0564A] is confirmed by which, as we have seen hitherto, the Roman pontiffs, when consulted by the Churches, were accustomed to answer only when at least the assembly of their clerics had been gathered.
