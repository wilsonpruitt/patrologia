## 2.

For we have heard that there are in the city of Rome, where he *(Pelagius)* lived a long time, some who for various reasons [0572C] favour him: some, namely, because [nt: So Quesnel, from the Oxford manuscript, and from there the new edition of Augustine: with which agrees what Innocent writes back. But Merlin and Crabbe: *qui nobis talia persuasisse perhibentur*: where Crabbe added in the margin, *qui vos* and *quia vos*. From there the Roman edition and the other editions of the Councils: *quia vos talia persuasisse perhibent*. The manuscripts show similar readings, except that for *perhibent* most have *perhibentur*, and one Colb. and another from the Vatican *perhibetur*. In the latter there is read besides *quia vobis*. From which this reading could conveniently be put together: *quia vobis talia persuasisse perhibetur*. The Pelagians, namely, as Possidius testifies, c. 18, *tried through their own ambition to persuade the holy see of their perfidy*. And what they could not accomplish, they boasted falsely that they had accomplished. Among those, moreover, whom they boasted they had persuaded of their depraved dogmas at Rome, the chief was Xystus, then a presbyter and afterwards supreme pontiff, whom, after the calumny had been exposed, Augustine, epist. otherwise 101, now 191, n. 1, congratulates thus: *For what can be read or heard more welcome than so pure a defence of grace against its enemies, from the mouth of him who was boasted to be a patron of great weight to those same enemies?* See also his epistle 194, n. 1.] he is said to have persuaded them of such things; but more, who do not believe that he holds such things; especially because ecclesiastical proceedings are boasted to have been held in the East, where he lives, by which he is thought to have been cleared; where indeed, if the bishops pronounced him catholic, it must be believed to have been done for no other reason than that he said he confessed the grace of God, [0573A] and that man can live justly by his own labour and will in such a way that he did not deny that he is helped to this by the grace of God. For when these words were heard, the catholic prelates could understand no other grace of God than that which they were accustomed to read in the books of God and to preach to the peoples of God, that namely of which the Apostle says, *I do not make void the grace of God; for if justice is through the Law, then Christ died in vain [n: (Galat. II, 21)]*: without doubt the grace by which we are justified from iniquity, and by which we are saved from infirmity; not that by which we were created with our own will. For if those bishops had understood that he meant that grace which we have even in common with the impious, with whom we are men, but denied that by which we are Christians and sons of God: which of the catholic priests, we do not [0573B] say, would hear him patiently, but [nt: Two Colb. manuscripts: *tantum ante oculos*: with a certain greater emphasis. How Pelagius confessed the grace of God in the synod mentioned, Augustine sets out in the book On the Proceedings of Pelagius himself, c. 6 and 7, and presently, cap. 10, explains how he detected that he had confessed the grace of God there deceitfully.] would bear him before his eyes? Wherefore the judges are not to be blamed, because they heard the name of grace according to ecclesiastical custom, not knowing what men of this kind are accustomed to scatter either in the books of their teaching or in the ears of their own followers.

## 3.

It is not a question of Pelagius alone, who perhaps has already been corrected, and would that it were so! but of so many, by whom, as they contend talkatively, and drag weak and untaught souls as though bound together [nt: Merlin: *conjunctas*. The Roman edition and the more recent editions of the Councils, and Garnier: *convictas*. The new edition of Augustine: *vinctas*. We prefer, with Crabbe and the better manuscripts, *convinctas*, as though *bound together*. *devinctas* would not displease, because of what Innocent writes back in epist. 31, n. 2: *If then there are any whom so great a perversity has bound fast in its defence*. And indeed nearer to this word comes what we read in the copies of the Isidorian collection, *pervinctas*.], and weary the firm and those stable in the faith by the very contention, all things are now full everywhere. Either, then, he must be summoned to Rome by thy veneration, and diligently questioned what grace he means, by which he confesses, if indeed he now confesses it, that men are helped not to sin and to live justly: [0573C] or this very thing must be dealt with him by letter. And when he has been found to mean that grace which the ecclesiastical and apostolic truth teaches, then he is to be absolved without any scruple of the Church, without the hiding place of any ambiguity; then in truth we must rejoice over his clearing.

## 4.

For whether he has said that grace is free will, or that grace is the remission of sins, or that grace is the precept of the law; he says none of those things which belong, through the supply of the Holy Spirit, to conquering concupiscence and temptations, [0574A] the Spirit whom he who ascended into heaven, and, leading captivity captive, gave gifts to men, poured out most richly upon us [n: (Tit. III, 6)]. For hence we pray that we may be able to overcome the temptation of sins [n: (Ephes. IV, 8)], that the Spirit of God, of whom we have received the pledge, may help our infirmity [n: (Rom. VIII, 26)]. But he who prays and says, *Do not bring us into temptation [n: (Matth. VI, 13)]*, certainly does not pray for this, that he may be a man, which is nature; nor does he pray for this, that he may have free will, which he already received when nature itself was created; nor does he pray for the remission of sins, because this is said above, *Forgive us our debts [n: (Matth. VI, 12)]*; nor does he pray that he may receive the commandment; but plainly he prays that he may do the commandment. For if he has been brought into temptation, that is, has failed in temptation, he certainly commits a sin [0574B] which is against the commandment. He prays, therefore, that he may not sin, that is, that he may do nothing evil: which the Apostle prays for on behalf of the Corinthians, saying: *And we pray to the Lord that you do nothing evil [n: (II Cor. XIII, 7)]*. Whence it appears clearly enough that for not sinning, that is, for not doing evil, although it is not doubted that there is a choice of the will, [nt: In the Roman edition and that of the Councils *liberum* is added here, superfluously and against the faith of the manuscripts. What the bishops a little above call *liberum arbitrium*, without adding the word *voluntatis*, they now call *arbitrium voluntatis*, without prefixing the epithet *liberum*. Hence also at the beginning of num. 5 they say: *Nor would the law command, unless there were will; nor would grace help, if will were enough*, now making mention of will alone.] yet its power does not suffice unless infirmity is helped. Prayer itself, therefore, is the clearest testimony of grace. Let him confess this, and we shall rejoice in him whether he is upright or corrected.

## 5.

Law and grace are to be distinguished. The law knows how to command, grace how to help. Nor would the law command, unless there were will: nor would grace help, if will were enough. We are commanded to have understanding, where it is said, *Be not like the horse and the mule, which have no understanding (Psal. [0574C] XXXI, 9)*; and yet we pray that we may have understanding, where it is said, *Give me understanding, that I may learn thy commandments [n: (Psal. CXVIII, 125)]*. We are commanded to have wisdom, where it is said, *You fools, be wise at last [n: (Psal. XCIII, 8)]*; and yet it is prayed that we may have wisdom, where it is said, *But if any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask of God, who gives to all abundantly and does not upbraid; and it shall be given him [n: (Jacob. I, 5)]*. We are commanded to have continence, where it is said, [nt: So Merlin, Crabbe, Garnier and our manuscripts. Yet it is manifest that something is lacking here. Wherefore the Roman editor added this within brackets, in another type: ［*Something of this kind is lacking here: Let your loins be girded: and yet we pray that it be given us, where it is said.*］ Which was afterwards retained in certain other editions as well. In place of this addition Quesnel substituted: *Turn away from thy pleasures, and yet it is prayed that we may have continence, where it is said*. Neither supplement satisfies. But how this place is to be mended may be fished out from another like passage of Augustine. That Doctor, epist. 147, to Hilary, n. 9, treating the same argument, writes thus: *When the Law says, Thou shalt not covet, what else does it seem to command but continence from unlawful desires?... and yet it is prayed that we may have continence, where it is said, When I knew that no one can be continent*, etc. And presently he concludes: *God therefore commands continence, and gives continence*. According to the mind of Augustine, then, our passage will be made whole if we restore: *We are commanded to have continence, where it is said, Thou shalt not covet: and yet it is prayed that we may have continence, where it is said, When I knew*, etc. The close repetition of the same words, a fruitful crop of lacunae, gave occasion to this.] *When I knew that no one can be continent unless God grant it, and [0575A] this very thing was a part of wisdom, to know whose gift this was: I went to the Lord, and besought him [n: (Sap. VIII, 21)]*. Finally, lest it be too long to run through everything, we are commanded not to do evil, where it is said, *Turn away from evil [n: (Psal. XXXVI, 27)]*; and yet it is prayed that we may not do evil where it is said: *And we pray to the Lord that you do nothing evil [n: (II Cor. XIII, 7)]*. We are commanded to do good, where it is said, *Turn away from evil and do good [n: (Psal. XXXVI, 27)]*; and yet it is prayed that we may do good, where it is said, *We do not cease praying and asking for you [n: (Coloss. I, 9)]*, and among the other things which he prays for them he says: *That you may walk worthily of God, unto all pleasing, in every good work and word [n: (Ibid., 10)]*. As, then, we acknowledge the will when these things are commanded; so let him too acknowledge grace [0575B] when they are asked for.

## 6.

We have sent to thy reverence a book which certain religious and honourable young men, servants of God, gave us, whose names too we do not keep silent; for they are called Timasius and Jacobus. Who, as we have heard, and as thou too deignest to know, at the exhortation of Pelagius himself abandoned the hope which they had in the world, and now serve God in continence. When at length, through our efforts such as they were, by the Lord's inspiration, they had been freed from that same error, they brought forward the same book, saying that it was Pelagius's, and earnestly asked that it be answered. It was done: the answer itself was written back to them ( *Augustine's book On Nature and Grace* ); they wrote back giving thanks [n: (Epist. apud Augustin. nunc 168)]. We have sent both, [0575C] both that to which the answer was made and the answer that was made. And lest we should be too burdensome, we have made marks at those places which we ask thee not to be loath to look at, how, when the question was put to him that he denied the grace of God, he answered in such a way as to say that it was nothing but the nature in which God created us.

## 7.

But if he denies that this book is his, or those same places in the book, we do not contend: let him anathematize them, and confess most openly that grace which Christian doctrine demonstrates and preaches to be proper to Christians, which is not nature, but that by which nature is saved: [nt: So this passage was restored in the newest edition of Augustine from a Vatican manuscript, which two Colb. also favour. But in the other books, Merlin's edition excepted, *quae naturam... fovet* is lacking. Of those three words Quesnel kept only the first. Then in the same books, and also in Merlin's edition, *sicut plantatur quodammodo et irrigatur*, except that Quesnel struck out the particle *sicut*.] which fosters nature not by teaching sounding in the ears, or by some visible aid, as a planter, in a certain manner, and a waterer from without, but by the supply of the Spirit and by hidden mercy, [0575D] as he does who gives the increase, God [n: (I Cor. III, 7)]. For although, by a reckoning not to be disapproved, that is called the grace of God by which we were created, so that [0576A] we should not be nothing, nor be something in such a way as a corpse which does not live, or a tree which does not feel, or a beast which does not understand; but men, who should both be, and live, and feel, and understand; and might be able [nt: The Roman edition: *agere debeamus*: it is corrected from the other books.] to give thanks to our creator for so great a benefit; whence this too can rightly be called grace, because it was given not for the merits of any preceding works, but by the gratuitous goodness of God: yet there is another, by which, predestined, we are called, justified, glorified, so that we can say: *If God is for us, who is against us? He who spared not his own son, but delivered him up for us all [n: (Rom. VIII, 3)]*.
