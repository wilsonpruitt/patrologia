## 8.

It was about this grace that the question turned, when it was said to Pelagius by those whom he was gravely offending and troubling, that he was attacking it in his disputations, [0576B] in which he asserted that human nature is sufficient to itself through free choice, not only for doing but even for perfecting the divine commandments. It is this grace that the apostolic doctrine [nt: In Merlin, Crabbe and the edition of Augustine, *non immerito* is added here.] calls by that name, by which we are saved and justified by the faith of Christ. Of this it is written: *I do not make void the grace of God. For if justice is through the Law, then Christ died in vain [n: (Galat. II, 21)]*. Of this it is written, *You are made void of Christ, you who are justified in the Law; you are fallen from grace [n: (Galat. V, 4)]*. Of this it is written, *But if by grace, it is not now of works; otherwise grace is no more grace [n: (Rom. XI, 6)]*. Of this it is written, *Now to him that works, the reward is not reckoned according to grace, but according to debt: but to him that works not, yet believes in him who justifies the ungodly, [0576C] his faith is reckoned unto justice [n: (Rom. IV, 4)]*. And many other things, which thou thyself canst better remember, and understand more wisely, and preach more illustriously. But that grace by which we were created men, even though we understand, not undeservedly, that it may be so called, it is nevertheless a wonder if we read it so called in any writings of the Law, the Prophets, the Gospels or the Apostles.

## 9.

When therefore the question was put to him concerning this grace, best known to faithful and catholic Christians, that he should cease to attack it, what is it that, when he himself in his own book had put this objection to himself as from an opposing person, so that he might clear himself by answering, he answered nothing else than that the nature [nt: Quesnel alone: *naturam creati referri ad gratiam creatoris*. This *referre* means to display and represent in itself. Augustine, in the book *On Nature and Grace*, c. 51, copies out and reports Pelagius's very words in this manner: *The very possibility of not sinning lies not so much in the power of choice as in the necessity of nature. Whatever is placed in the necessity of nature is without doubt held to belong to the author of nature, namely God. How then is that thought to be said to be without the grace of God which is shown to belong properly to God?* There, says Augustine at once, *the opinion that lay hidden was expressed. He attributes the possibility of not sinning to the grace of God for this reason, that God is the author of that nature in which he says the possibility of not sinning is inseparably implanted.*] of created man displays the grace of the creator; and that he thus says that justice can be fulfilled without sin through free choice [0576D] with the help of divine grace, because God gave this to man by the very possibility of nature?

## 10.

To whom it is deservedly answered: *Then is made void [0577A] the stumbling-block of the cross [n: (Galat. V, 11)]*. *Then Christ died in vain [n: (Rom. IV, 25)]*. [nt: The Roman edition and the Councils: *Non enim*. Next, Merlin and Crabbe: *nec ascendisset in altum et captivans*; the Roman edition and Labbe: *nec ascendens in altum, captivans*.] For if he had not died for our sins and risen again for our justification, nor ascended on high and, leading captivity captive, given gifts to men, would that possibility of nature which he defends not be in men [n: (Eph. IV, 8; Psal. LXVII, 19)]? Or was the commandment of God perhaps lacking, and was it for that reason that Christ died? Nay, this too already existed, holy and just and good [n: (Rom. VII, 12)]. It had already been said, *Thou shalt not covet [n: (Exod. XX, 17)]*; it had already been said, *Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself [n: (Levit. XIX, 18)]*. In which saying the Apostle says that the whole law is fulfilled [n: (Rom. XIII, 9)]. And since no one loves himself unless he loves God, therefore the Lord says that on these two commandments [0577B] hang the whole law and the Prophets [n: (Matth. XXII, 40)]. These two commandments had already been divinely given to men. Or had the eternal reward of justice not yet been promised? He himself does not say this, who has set down in his writings that the kingdom of heaven was promised even in the Old Testament. If then there already existed, for doing and perfecting justice, the possibility of nature through free choice, there already existed the holy and just and good commandment of the law of God, there already existed the everlasting reward promised: then Christ died in vain.

## 11.

Therefore justice is neither through the law, nor through the possibility of nature; but from faith and the gift of God through Jesus Christ our Lord, the one mediator of God and men. Who, had he not in the fullness of time [0577C] died for our sins, and risen again for our justification [n: (Rom. IV, 25)], assuredly both the faith of the ancients would be made void, and ours. And faith being made void, what justice would remain to man, since the just man lives by faith [n: (Heb. X, 38; Rom. I, 17)]? For since by one man sin entered into the world, and by sin death, and so passed upon all men, in whom all have sinned [n: (Rom. V, 12)]: beyond doubt, from the body of this death, where another law fights against the law of the mind [n: (Rom. VII, 21 et 24)], no one has been delivered or is delivered by [nt: In some manuscripts, faultily: *suae possibilitas*. Hence occasion was given to a copyist, in a second revision, in the Navarre manuscript and perhaps in others, to add *naturae*. Whence in the printed editions too, except the Augustine edition, *suae possibilitas naturae* prevailed. Below, in the 29th epistle of Innocent, n. 3, we shall read *quasi ex nostra in totum simus possibilitate* (the word *naturae* not being added) *perfecti*. See below, epist. 30, n. 4.] his own possibility, which, being lost, needs a redeemer; being wounded, a saviour: but by the grace of God through faith in the one mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus [n: (I Tim. II, 5)]; who, since he was also God, made man, and remaining [0577D] God, having been made man, himself remade what he made.

## 12.

But I think it escapes him that the faith of Christ, which afterwards came into revelation, was hidden in the times of our fathers; through which nevertheless they too were delivered by the grace of God, whoever in all the times of the human race could be delivered, by the hidden judgment of God, yet not a blameworthy one. Whence the Apostle says: *But having the same spirit [0578A] of faith* (the same, that is, as they also had) *according as it is written [n: (II Cor. IV, 13)]*, *I believed, for which cause I have spoken; we also believe, for which cause we speak also [n: (Psal. CXV, 1)]*. Thence it is that the mediator himself says: *Abraham desired to see my day; and he saw it, and was glad [var: Vulg. "exsultavit ut videret," rejoiced that he might see] [n: (Joan. VIII, 56)]*. Thence Melchisedech, bringing forth the sacrament of the Lord's table, knew how to figure his eternal priesthood [n: (Gen. XIV, 18)]. But now, when the Law had been given in writing, which the Apostle says entered in that sin might abound [n: (Rom. V, 20)], and of which he says: *For if the inheritance be of the Law, it is no more of promise. But God gave it to Abraham by promise. Why then the Law? It was set for the sake of [nt: In Quesnel and the Augustine edition, as in the Pithou manuscript: *proposita est*.] transgression, until the seed should come to whom the promise was made, being ordained by Angels in the hand of a mediator. Now a mediator [0578B] is not of one, but God is one. Was the Law then against the promises of God? God forbid. For if there had been a Law given which could give life, justice would altogether be of the Law. But the Scripture has shut up all things under sin, that the promise by the faith of Jesus Christ might be given to them that believe [n: (Galat. III, 18, seqq.)]*. Is it not sufficiently shown that this was done through the Law, that sin might be recognized, and be increased by transgression? *For where there is no law, neither is there transgression [n: (Rom. IV, 15)]*; and that so, against the victory of sin, refuge might be taken in the divine grace which is in the promises: and thus the Law would not be against the promises of God, since the knowledge of sin comes through it for this purpose, and from the transgression of the Law the abundance of sin, that for deliverance the promises of God may be sought, which is the grace of God; and that there may begin to be in man a justice [0578C] not his own, but God's, that is, given by the gift of God.

## 13.

Which even now certain men, *not knowing the justice of God,* as was then said of the Jews also, *and seeking to establish their own, have not submitted themselves to the justice of God [n: (Rom. X, 3)]*. For they too think themselves justified through the Law, free choice sufficing them for keeping it, that is, by their own justice brought forth from human nature, not given from divine grace, on account of which it is called the justice of God. Whence likewise it is written: *For by the Law is the knowledge of sin. But now without the Law the justice of God is made manifest, being witnessed by the Law and the Prophets [n: (Rom. III, 21)]*. When he says *is made manifest,* he shows that it existed then too, but like that rain which Gedeon obtained, then hidden as in the fleece, but now manifest as on the threshing-floor *(Judic. [0578D] VI, 37)*. Since therefore the Law without grace could have been, not the death of sin, but its strength; for thus it is said, *The sting of death is sin, and the strength of sin is the Law [n: (I Cor. XV, 56)]*: as many now take refuge from the face of reigning sin in grace, lying open as on the threshing-floor; so few took refuge in it when it then lay hidden as in the fleece. But this distribution of the times is referred to the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God, of [0579A] which it is said, *How incomprehensible are his judgments, and his ways unsearchable [n: (Rom. XI, 33)]*!
