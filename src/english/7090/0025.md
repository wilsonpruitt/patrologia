## 14.

Wherefore, if [nt: Quesnel expunged altogether the particle *si*, for which Garnier substituted *sic*.] both before the time of the Law and in the very time of the Law it was not the possibility of a nature weak and [nt: The Roman edition and the Councils and several manuscripts: *indignae*: which arose from *indiguae*; for so the ancients liked to write the word *indigae*.] needy and vitiated and sold under sin, but the grace of God through faith, that justified the just fathers who lived by faith, and now the same grace, coming at last into the open, revealed, justifies: let Pelagius then anathematize his writings, where he disputes against it, if not through obstinacy, yet through ignorance, by defending the possibility of nature for overcoming sins and fulfilling the commandments. Or if he denies that they are his, or says that the writings which he denies are his were foisted among his writings by his enemies, let him nevertheless anathematize and condemn them, at the fatherly exhortation and by the authority of thy holiness. If then he is willing, let him learn to remove the scandal, burdensome [0579B] to himself and pernicious to the [nt: The Pithou manuscript: *Ecclesia*: faultily. Quesnel published this whole passage thus: *si non vult; onerosum sibi et perniciosum esse discat Ecclesiae scandalum afferre, quod auditores*; against the credit of all the codices and the mind of Innocent. But the reading which he judged less correct coheres excellently with what goes before and what follows. Namely, the five bishops counsel Pelagius to learn by what means he may remove the scandal both burdensome to himself and pernicious to the Church. Nor do they doubt that it will be removed as soon as his disciples learn that the book published under his name has been condemned by himself.] Church: a scandal which his hearers, and those who love him in a perverse way, do not cease to spread everywhere. For if they learn that the same book, which they either think or know to be his, has been anathematized and condemned by him himself, on the authority of catholic bishops, and above all of thy holiness, which we by no means doubt carries greater weight with him; we do not think they will dare any longer, by speaking against the grace of God which has been revealed through the passion and resurrection of Christ, to trouble faithful and simply Christian hearts: but rather, with the help of the Lord's mercy, and thy prayers, burning with charity and piety, contending together with us, that they will trust, not only for being blessed for ever, but also for being just and holy [0579C], [nt: In Quesnel the word *conari* is added here: which is more rightly absent from the other books.] not in their own strength, but in that same grace. Wherefore we have thought that the letter written to him himself by one of us, to whom he sent certain writings, as it were of his [nt: Nor was there need here for Quesnel to add the word *causa*. This passage is illustrated by that other one of Augustine's book *On the Proceedings of Pelagius*, c. 32: *In the little paper of his defence, which he sent me by a certain Charus, our fellow citizen of Hippo but a deacon of the East, he did something which stands otherwise than is contained in the episcopal proceedings. But what the proceedings contain is far better and firmer and clearer for the catholic truth against the plague of that heresy.* From which it is manifest that Pelagius called *writings of his clearing*, or proceedings (or, as Augustine says, epist. formerly 252, now 179, n. 7, *a certain defence, as it were, composed by himself, in which he said he had answered the objections of the Gauls*), the adulterated proceedings of the synod of Diospolis which he had sent to Augustine. Of these again Augustine, in the same book *On the Proceedings of Pelagius*, c. 29, speaks thus: *Whatever proceedings of his clearing he may have brought forward.* Nor is it hence less plain that by the five bishops Augustine is to be understood under the name *one of us*; whose letter indeed, sent at that time, has perished and has not come down to us.] clearing, by a certain deacon of the East but a citizen of Hippo, should rather be directed to thy blessedness [sic: *beattiudini*], judging it better and [0580A] asking that thou thyself deign to [nt: Quesnel, with one Colbert manuscript and the Pithou: *ut eam emittere*. The reading of the other books is sounder.] send it to him. For thus he will not disdain rather to read it, attending in it more to him who sent it than to him who wrote it.

## 15.

But as for what they say, that man can be without sin and easily keep the commandments of God if he will: although, when this is said to be through the help of grace, which nevertheless was revealed and bestowed through the incarnation of his Only-begotten, it may seem more tolerable to say; yet, since it can, not undeservedly, [nt: Merlin and Crabbe: *quoniam merito quaeri potest*. Quesnel kept *quaeri potest*: which we have found in no manuscript. For *quaeri*, Crabbe noted *debere* in the margin, which the Pithou manuscript also has in the text.] move [us], where and when it is brought about in us through that same grace that we be altogether without any sin, whether in this life, when the flesh lusts against the spirit; or rather in that one, when the saying shall come to pass which is written [n: (Galat. V, 17)]: *O death, where is thy victory? O death, where is thy sting? for the sting of death is sin* [0580B] [n: (I Cor. XV, 55)]; it must be handled more diligently on account of certain others, who have held, and have committed to memory in their writings, that even in this life man can be without sin, not from the beginning of his birth, but by conversion from sins to justice, and from a [nt: In Garnier and the Augustine edition: *a vita*. Pelagius at one time came over to the opinion of those of whom there is question here. For Augustine, epist. formerly 252, now 179, n. 7, relates that he wrote this to him: *We did not say that anyone is found who from infancy to old age has never sinned; but that, having been converted from sin by his own labour and helped by the grace of God, he can be without sin.* But that he did not always stand by this opinion the same Doctor proves in the same epistle, n. 8.] reprobate way to a good life [n: (S. Ambros. in I Lucae 6)]. For so they understood what is written of Zachary and Elizabeth, that they walked *in all the justifications of the Lord without blame (Lucae I, 6)*. This that is said, *without blame,* they took as said for "without sin," not indeed denying, nay rather, as is found in other places in their writings, piously confessing the help of the grace of our Lord, not through the natural spirit of man, but through the principal Spirit of God. Who seem to have considered too little that Zachary himself [0580C] was certainly a priest. And all priests at that time were bound by the law of God to offer sacrifice first for their own sins, then for those of the people [n: (Levit. IX, 7; Hebr. VII, 27)]. As then now [nt: In one of the Colbert manuscripts: *per orationes sacrificiorum*.] we are convicted, through the sacrifice of prayer, of not being without sin, since we are commanded to say, *Forgive [0581A] us our debts [n: (Matth. VI, 12)]*: so then too the priests were convicted, through the sacrifices of animal victims, of not being without sin, since they were commanded to offer for their own sins. But if the matter stands thus, that through the grace of the Saviour we make progress indeed in this life, as desire decreases and charity grows; but are made perfect in that life, when desire is extinguished and charity completed: assuredly that which is written, [nt: The same manuscript: *Qui ex Deo est*. The other Colbert, with the Pithou: *Ex Deo*, the words *Qui natus est* being omitted.] *He who is born of God sins not [n: (I Joan. III, 9)]*, is said according to charity itself, which alone does not sin. For to the birth which is of God belongs charity, which is to be increased and perfected, not desire, which is to be diminished and consumed: which nevertheless, so long as it is in our members, by a certain law of its own fights against the law of the mind [n: (Rom. VII, 20)]. But he who is born of [0581B] God, neither obeying its desires, nor yielding his members as weapons of iniquity to sin [n: (Rom. VI, 13)], can say, *Now it is no more I that do it, but the sin that dwells in me [n: (Rom. VII, 20)]*.

## 16.

But however that question stands, since, even if a man is not found without sin in this life, it is nevertheless said that this can come about through the help of grace and of the Spirit of God, and that in order that it come about it must be striven for and prayed for: anyone is tolerably mistaken in this; nor is it diabolical impiety, but human error, to affirm what is to be laboured for and wished for, even if he cannot show what he affirms. For he believes that that can come about which it is certainly praiseworthy to will. But it suffices us that no one of the faithful is found in the Church of God, in any [nt: The Roman edition and the Councils: *profectu excellenti atque justitia*. Garnier and the Isidorian manuscripts: *profectu, excellentia atque justitia*: they are corrected from Quesnel, the Augustine edition and the better manuscripts.] advancement and excellence of justice, who would dare to say to himself [0581C] that the petition of the Lord's prayer, *Forgive us our debts [n: (Matth. VI, 12)]*, is not necessary; and to say that he has no sin, lest he deceive himself, and the truth be not in him, although he already lives without blame [n: (I Joan. I)]. For it is not [nt: Merlin and Quesnel, with one Colbert manuscript and the Pithou: *qualescumque etiam tentationes*. Truer is the reading of the other codices, by which we are taught that those who are proclaimed to live without blame are to be understood as immune from grave sins indeed, but not likewise from light ones, which we contract through temptation or human frailty.] any sin whatever of human temptation, but grave sin, that comes to blame. [0582A]

## 17.

The other things which were objected to him, thy blessedness will without doubt judge of them, just as thou shalt have seen them defended in the proceedings. The most gentle sweetness of thy heart will surely grant us pardon for having sent to thy holiness a letter perhaps longer than thou wouldst wish. For we do not pour back our rivulet into thy large fountain to increase it; but in this [nt: In two Colbert manuscripts: *non cum parva*. In the rest: *non comparva*. In the Augustine edition: *non tamen parva*. But Merlin and Crabbe, after the word *refundimus* above, immediately subjoin *verum etiam no ter licet exiguus*, etc., the intermediate words being omitted.] not small temptation of the time, from which may he deliver us to whom we say, *Bring us not into temptation,* we wish this to be approved by thee, whether ours too, though small, flows from the same head of streams as thine abundant one also, and to be consoled by thy rescript concerning the common participation of the one grace.

## 1.

Thus then they begin: *That a great and unfailing fountain, overflowing with streams for all Christians, stands at the Apostolic see, no one could doubt; from which rivulets come forth abundantly, most largely watering the whole world of Christians; to which also, in honour of the most blessed Peter, the decrees of the fathers have decreed all special reverence IN SEEKING OUT THE THINGS OF GOD, which ought altogether and carefully, but most of all and justly, to be examined by the very apostolic summit of prelates, whose ancient solicitude it is as much to condemn evil things as to approve things worthy of praise.
