## 2.

I rejoice therefore, dearest brethren, that you have sent a letter to us by our brother and fellow bishop Julius: and that, while you take care of the churches over which you preside, you show your solicitude for the benefit of all: and that you ask that something be decreed which may profit all alike throughout all the churches of the whole world: so that the Church, established by her own rules [0583C], [nt: The Roman edition and the more recent Councils have *ex hoc*, and then, with the older ones, *quod illos caveat pronuntiationis justae firmata decretalibus* (Merlin: *petere*; Crabbe: *patere*; the Roman edition and those following: *pavere*) *non possit*. Garnier, cutting out *hoc quod illos caveat*, kept *et pronuntiationis justae firmata decretalibus pavere non possit*. Our reading is that of Quesnel, the Augustine edition and the better manuscripts, except that in Quesnel *hoc* has been struck out, and in the Augustine edition, as in the Corbie and Saint-Germain manuscripts, *parete* stands in place of *patere*. It is then to be understood thus, as if Innocent said: You ask that it be decreed by me that the Church, already established by her ancient rules, being now also strengthened by this new decree justly pronounced, by which she is bidden to beware of those heretics mentioned, may not henceforth lie open to such men, but may remain shut off from them for ever.] strengthened also by this decree of just pronouncement, by which she may beware of them, may not lie open to such men as, instructed, nay rather destroyed, by perverse subtleties [0584A] of words, disputing under the appearance of the catholic faith, as if breathing out a pestilential poison, so as to corrupt towards the worse part the hearts of men who think rightly, seek to overturn the whole discipline of true doctrine. It must therefore be healed the more speedily, lest an accursed [nt: One of the Colbert manuscripts: *exsecrandis animorum moribus inserpat*. The other: *exsecrandos animis morbos inserpat*. The Roman edition and the Councils, with some other manuscripts, keep the word *inserpat*. In its place the Saint-Germain and Corbie manuscripts better show *inrepat*.] disease creep further into souls: just as a physician, when he sees some sickness in this earthly body, reckons it a great proof of his art if someone despaired of quickly escapes through his intervention; or when he has looked upon a putrid wound, applies poultices, or the other things by which the wound that had arisen may be closed over; and if, remaining, it cannot be healed, lest it corrupt the rest of the body with its decay, he cuts off with the knife what was doing harm, so as to keep the rest whole and untouched. That must therefore be cut off [0584B] which has crept upon the body, all too pure and sound, like a wound; lest, when it is cleansed too slowly, a bilge of this evil that cannot afterwards be drained should settle almost in the very vitals.

## 3.

For what are we henceforth to reckon right in their minds concerning these men, who think that they owe it to themselves that they are good, and do not consider him whose grace they obtain daily? But now these men, who are such, obtain no grace of God; [nt: Crabbe, and after him the editions of the Councils and the Roman: *quia sine illo*.] who trust that they can attain without him as much as those who ask of him scarcely [nt: The Roman edition and the Councils, with the Pithou and Isidorian manuscripts: *et accipiunt*. One Colbert codex: *ut accipiunt*; whence Quesnel preferred *ut accipiant*. In the Augustine edition, as in the Corbie manuscript: *accipere*, the particle *et* not being put before it, which is kept with the Saint-Germain and others. The sentence will be clear if arranged in this way: *quantum vix illi etiam, qui postulant, accipere promerentur*.] also merit to receive. For what can be so unjust, so barbarous, so ignorant of all religion, so hostile to Christian minds, as to deny that thou owest whatever thou obtainest in daily grace to him to whom thou thyself confessest that thou [0584C] owest that thou wast born? Wilt thou then be more excellent for thyself in providing, than [nt: So the manuscripts. But the Roman edition and the Councils: *quam potest is esse*.] can be in him who brought it about that thou shouldst be? [nt: Garnier: *Si huic putes debere*. Other printed editions: *Et cum te putes ei debere*. Pelagius is here refuted without being named: who, distinguishing to be able, to will and to be, placed being able in nature, willing in choice, and being in effect. Then he so granted that being able belongs properly to God, the author of nature, as to contend that willing and being, or acting, are to be referred to man. He taught that it is of God alone that man is able to will and to accomplish the good; but that he wills to accomplish it, and actually accomplishes it, and so too that he wills to be good and perfect, or even is so, he proclaimed to be of man himself. He did not deny that man is always helped by the aid of grace: but he placed that aid in this, that God never fails to give the possibility of willing and of working. See his very words in Augustine, book *On the Grace of Christ*, cap. 4.] And to whom thou thinkest thou owest that thou livest, [0585A] how dost thou not think thou owest to him that, by obtaining his daily grace, thou livest thus? And thou who deniest that we need the divine help, as if we [nt: In the Roman edition and the Councils *simus* is missing.] were entirely perfect from our own possibility, how do we not call forth his help [nt: The word *ejus* is recalled from the Saint-Germain manuscript and the Augustine edition. Garnier alone presents this passage thus: *Dic quomodo vere adjutorium ejus in nos, cum tales a nobis etiam esse possimus, provocemus. Eum qui adjutorium Dei negat, rursus vellem interrogare cur dicat nos id non promereri?*] upon us, when we can be such even of ourselves?

## 4.

For I should like to ask him who denies the help of God what he says: that we do not deserve it, or that he cannot bestow it, or that there is nothing on account of which each one ought [nt: Quesnel, with one Colbert manuscript: *praestolari*.] to ask for it? That God is able to do this, his works themselves testify. And that we need daily help, we cannot deny. For whether we live well, [nt: So the manuscripts, and also the old editions of the Councils. But the Roman edition and the more recent Councils, with Garnier: *precamur*.] we call it forth, that we may live better and more holily; or whether, thinking wrongly, we are turned away from good things, we need his aid the more, that we may return to the right way. [0585B] For what should seem so deadly, so headlong towards a fall, so exposed to all dangers, as if, thinking that this alone can suffice us, that we received free choice when we were born, we should seek nothing further from the Lord, that is, forgetful of our author, should abjure his power in order to show ourselves free; as though he who made thee free at thy birth had nothing more that he could give: not knowing that unless grace, implored with great prayers, descends [nt: Garnier: *nequidquam*; and below, *David inclamat*, for *Nam si ille clamat*.] upon us, we should by no means strive to overcome the errors of earthly defilement and of the worldly body, since not free choice, but the aid of God alone, can make us equal to resisting?

## 5.

For if he cries out that he has need of the divine help, who would not worthily have sought it, if free [0585C] choice were of more profit to anyone; since, although a blessed man and already chosen by the Lord, he lacked nothing, yet he so beseeches God, asking: *Be thou my helper, forsake me not, neither despise me, O God my saviour [n: (Psal. XXVI, 9)]*: we ask free choice for ourselves, he [0586A] asks God for himself as helper; we say that what we were born with can suffice us, he prays God that he be not forsaken. [nt: The Roman edition: *Nos ergo dicimus manifeste*, etc. Garnier: *Nos dicimus manifeste, ut quid oremus Deum, ille*: and below, *David ergo*, for *David enim*.] Do we not, I ask, learn plainly what we should pray for, when that man, so greatly blessed, as we said above, longs not to be despised? For those who confirm the one must needs refute the other. For let David be accused of being ignorant of prayer and unaware of his own nature, who, though he knows that so much is present in [nt: The Roman edition and the Councils: *in sua natura esse*. Next, Garnier: *adjutorem invocat, nec illi sufficit assiduum invocare*: against the other books, from which it is clear that the word *adjutorem* is governed by the verb *exoptat*.] his nature, longs for God as his helper, and a constant helper, nor does constant suffice him, but, bowed down in prayers, longs that he may never despise him; and throughout the whole body of the psalter he both proclaims and cries this. If then he, so great [nt: Some manuscripts with Quesnel, and also the Roman edition and the Councils: *ita magnum scit, ut assidue diceret*. In the Augustine edition: *ita magnus sicut assidue dicere*. Garnier: *hoc illi tam magnum visum sit, ut assidue diceret, tam necessarium confessum sit*. We keep what the more numerous and older manuscripts present. We should prefer, however, *ita magnus scivit assidue dicere, et ita*.], knows so to say this continually, so confessed it necessary as to teach it: how do Pelagius and Coelestius, setting aside every answer [0586B] of the psalms and renouncing such teaching, trust that they will persuade some that we neither ought to seek the help of God nor need it; when all the saints testify that they can do nothing without it?

## 6.

For that man once, having undergone free [nt: Merlin, and from him Quesnel: *Libero enim arbitrio olim ille perversus*; against all the manuscripts and the other printed editions, not only here, but also in book II of Augustine to Boniface, cap. 4, n. 6, in Prosper's book against the Collator, c. 5, n. 15, and in the epistle of Celestine 21, n. 5, where these same words are cited. Certainly the sense of Innocent calls for *permissus* rather than *perversus*.] choice, while he used his goods too unadvisedly, falling, was sunk into the depths of transgression, and found no way by which he could rise from there; and, deceived for ever by his own liberty, he would have lain under the crushing of this ruin [nt: In the manuscripts, as in the editions of Merlin and the Roman: *ruinae latuisset oppressus* or *oppressum*, except that in the Pithou *oppressu* stands, and in the Corbie *jacuisset* has been restored by an ancient hand: an emendation proved right both by Augustine's book II to Boniface against the two letters of the Pelagians, n. 6, and by the epistle 21 of Pope Celestine, cap. 4, where these words are cited. Although in those two places too manuscripts are not lacking which present *latuisset oppressu*. In Crabbe, and thence in the editions of the Councils, one may read here *huic ruinae suae jacuisset oppressus*; in Garnier and Quesnel, *hujus ruina subjacuisset oppressus*.], had not the coming of Christ afterwards [nt: So the manuscripts with great agreement, together with the Augustine edition. But in the printed editions: *per suam gratiam*.] raised him up for his grace: who through the purification of the new regeneration cleansed all past fault by the laver of his baptism; and, strengthening his state, so that he might proceed more rightly and more steadily, yet did not deny his grace for the time to come. For although he had redeemed [0586C] man from past sins, yet knowing that he could sin again, he kept in reserve for himself many things for his restoration, by which he could correct him even after these things: he supplies daily remedies, and unless we strive relying and trusting on them, we shall by no means be able to overcome [0587A] human errors. For it is necessary that, as we conquer when he helps, so, when he does not help, we be conquered again.

## 7.

But I could say more, were it not evident that you have said everything. Whoever therefore is seen to assent to this opinion, by which he would say that we have no need of the divine help, professes himself an enemy of the catholic faith and ungrateful for the benefits of God. [nt: The conjunction *Nam* is recalled from the editions of Merlin, Crabbe, the Augustine edition and most manuscripts. Then in the Isidorian manuscripts *nostra* is missing. Next the Roman edition and the Councils, with some manuscripts: *Sic enim*, where with the other books we prefer *Ipsi enim*. But Garnier, judging this passage mutilated, advises by a note written in the margin that it could be repaired thus: *Pelagium igitur et Coelestium, quos auctores nefarii prorsus et omnibus anathematizandi erroris advertistis, justa sententia damnastis: nec illi nostra*, etc. But these words of theirs at the end of the preceding epistle 26 do not sound like a sentence pronounced by name against Pelagius and Coelestius by the fathers of the council of Carthage: *If Pelagius and Coelestius have been corrected, or say that they never held these things, and deny that whatever writings have been brought forward against them are theirs, and there is no way in which they may be convicted of lying, yet let whoever shall have been found a champion of the doctrines whose authors they are thought to be be anathema in general terms.* Nothing appears in this passage of Innocent either, from which it should be judged *matilus*.] For neither are they worthy of our communion, which they have polluted by preaching in such a way. For they themselves of their own accord, while they follow those things which they say, have fled further from the true religion. For since the whole of this consists in our profession, and in our daily prayers we do nothing but seek how we may obtain the mercy of God; how can we bear these men boasting? What error, [0587C] I ask, so great blinds their hearts, that if they themselves feel no grace of God, because they are neither worthy nor deserve it, they do not even consider concerning others what divine grace bestows daily on each? They are indeed [nt: So in Garnier and the Augustine edition, as in the better manuscripts. But in other books: *omnes*; and below, *Negantes enim Dei adjutorium*.] most worthy of all blindness, who have not left even this to themselves, that they should believe that they can be recalled from their errors by the divine aid. For denying help, they have taken this away utterly not from others but from themselves: they are to be torn further away and removed far from the vitals of the Church; lest [nt: The printed editions, except the Augustine edition: *ne diu multa*. More fittingly in the Augustine edition, as in the Corbie and Saint-Germain manuscripts: *ne diu inultus multa*. Hence there is aptly joined on: *Si enim diu fuerint sub hac IMPUNITATE*.] the error, long unpunished, seizing on many things, should afterwards grow incurable. For if they have long lived under this impunity, they must needs lead many into this depravity of their mind, and deceive the innocent, or rather the unwary, who follow the catholic [nt: This reading of Garnier, which is also that of the Corbie and Saint-Germain manuscripts, Quesnel approves, although he himself keeps the common one in the Roman edition and the Councils, namely *non sequuntur*. But here in the Augustine edition the particle *nunc* has dropped out by the copyist's drowsiness.] faith now. For they will think that those think rightly [0587C] whom they see still remaining in the Church. Let the unsound wound then be separated from the sound body, and, the breath of the raging disease being removed, let the things that are sincere endure more safely, and let the purer flock be cleansed from this contagion of the evil beast. Let there be an unimpaired perfection of the whole body, which we have learned that you follow and hold by this pronouncement against them, and which we preserve together with you with equal assent. [0588A]

## 8.

Yet if they call forth in themselves some [nt: In the Roman edition and the Councils: *aliquando in se*.] help of God, which up to now they have denied, and recognize that they need his aid, so that they may be freed from this stain into which they had fallen by the crookedness of their heart; and, as if drawn into the light out of a foul darkness, all things being removed and renounced by which their whole sight, lest it should behold [nt: In Quesnel and the Augustine edition: *aspicerent*.] the truth, was befouled and darkened, let them condemn these things which up to now they have held; and at length, applying their mind to right disputations, corrected in some small measure from this stain, they give and submit themselves to be healed [nt: In Garnier: *conciliis*. The preceding word, *sanandos*, which Quesnel removed, all our manuscripts preserve.] by true counsels: if they do this, it will be in the power of the bishops to come to their aid in some degree, and to afford such wounds some care, which the Church is not wont to deny to the lapsed when they have come to their senses: so that, [nt: The word *revocati* is missing in Merlin. In its place the Roman edition and the later Councils have substituted *remoti*.] recalled [0588C] from their precipices, they may be brought back within the sheepfold of the Lord: lest, set outside, and shut out from so great a protection [nt: Quesnel: *praesidio aede munitionis*. Garnier: *praesidio fidae munitionis*. This last reading displeases less.] of the defence of faith, they be exposed to all dangers, to be devoured and harried by the teeth of wolves, whom they could not resist with this perversity of doctrine by which they had provoked them against themselves.
