## 1.

The letters of your fraternity, full of faith and strengthened with all the vigour of the catholic religion, sent from two councils through our brother and fellow bishop Julius, we have received with a most grateful mind, because their tenor and whole contexture [nt: Garnier here has *contertio est*, and below *integra relatio ita consistit* for *integra ratione consistit*. Before that, for *correctione*, the Corbie manuscript has *correptione*.] rests with sound reason upon the consideration of the daily grace of God and upon the correction of those who think the contrary: so that it may both take away [nt: In Quesnel, as in the Pithou manuscript, *pos int*. The other books more correctly have *possit*, that is, the contexture of the letters. Next, the Roman edition and that of the Councils have *idoneo dato cuiris*, except that in the older editions of the Councils *quovis* remains. In Garnier: *idoneum cuivis, dato legis*.] all error from them, and, given any example whatever of our law, may offer them a worthy and fit teacher whom they ought to follow. But of these things, as I think, we have already said enough above, when, answering your reports, we wrote back what we thought either of their faithlessness or of your judgment. But [0594B] thereupon there comes to hand and is supplied against them what may be said: nor can there ever [nt: In the editions of Merlin, Crabbe, Garnier and Augustine, as in the Vatican manuscripts, *deesse*. It seems better to prefer *esse* with the other books, as if it were said: Nor can it ever come about that that doctrine should prevail which is so wretched and so impious that it is more fully overcome by the strength of our faith and by the truth itself.] be what may prevail, since that is so wretched and impious which is more fully overcome by the strength of our faith and by the truth itself. For he who has spurned and despised every hope of life, confounding his heart with a hostile and damnable disputation, since he believes that there is nothing which he may receive from God, nor anything left which he may ask for his healing; he who has taken this from himself, what has he left [nt: The Vatican manuscripts: *quid requirit*.] further?

## 2.

If therefore there are some whom so great a perversity [nt: Merlin: *defixit*; Garnier: *devinxerit*. Lower down Crabbe and Labbe have *oberrans*; and the Roman edition *aberrans* for *abhorrens*.] has bound to its defence, who give themselves up to and join themselves with this doctrine, hoping that that belongs to catholic teaching which, shrinking far from it, is proved to be thoroughly contrary, [nt: The editions: *infecti*; the Vatican manuscript: *infectis*; they are corrected from an old Colbert exemplar. These answer to the words of the five *e, i-covorum* no. 14: *which scandal its hearers and, perversely, ITS LOVERS do not cease to spread everywhere. For if they come to know*, etc.] led on by their affection and by their admonitions and words to slip, they will make haste to return to [0594C] the right track of the way, lest error, besieging the mind any longer [nt: Garnier: *nec diutius.... invadet*.] and as it were fed upon their senses, [0595A] should invade it. For if Pelagius, in whatever place he has stayed, has deceived by this assertion the minds of those who would easily or simply believe him as he disputed, [nt: One Colbert codex and the Pithou, with the Corbie in the first hand: *seu hic illi*, etc. The other, as in Quesnel: *seu hic aliqui decepti sint, quos nescientes*.] whether those are in this city, which, not knowing it, we can neither make manifest nor deny, since even if they are, they lie hidden, nor would they ever dare either to defend him when he preaches these things, or to throw out such things when any one [nt: In one manuscript: *quolibet nostrorum*.] of ours is present, and in so great a multitude of people someone could easily be detected, [nt: In Garnier: *nec ubi lateat possit*. Manuscripts of lower note: *nec deprehendi aliquis facile vel alicubi*.] nor be recognized anywhere; or whether they dwell in any place of the earth whatever, we believe by the mercy and grace of our God that they will easily be corrected on hearing the condemnation of him who has been found the obstinate and resisting author of this doctrine. Nor does it matter where these have been, so long as, wherever they can be found, they are to be healed. [0595B]

## 3.

We, however, [nt: In place of the particle *nec*, Garnier substituted *vix*.] cannot be persuaded that he has been cleared, although acts have been brought to us [nt: Garnier recalled here from the early editions of Augustine: *a nostris quibusdam laicis*. The acts of the synod of Diospolis are meant here.] by I know not what laymen, in which he would believe himself both heard and acquitted. Whether these are true we doubt, because they came with no proceeding of that council, [nt: Merlin and Crabbe: *executione*; then Garnier: *innotuerint*: the other books opposing.] nor have we received any letters on this matter from those before whom this man pleaded the causes of this affair. But if he had been able to be confident of his clearing, we believe that he would rather have done this, which could have been much more truly done, namely to compel them by his letters to indicate [nt: In Quesnel: *quid judicaverint*, which the Navarre codex also has from a second correction; which indeed, with the other exemplars of Isidore, showed *quid judicaverunt*. The later editions of the Councils: *qui judicarerunt*. In Augustine: *qui dijudicaverunt*. In the Roman edition, as in the Colbert, Pithou and Corbie manuscripts: *quid judicaverant*. Nearer to these comes what we recall from Merlin, Crabbe, etc.: *qui dijudicaverant*. But Garnier's *quod judicaverant more ecclesiastico indicare* smacks of a gloss.] what they had judged. But since there are some things set down in the acts themselves which, when objected, he partly suppressed by avoiding them, partly confounded in total obscurity by twisting many words back upon himself, some things he cleared by false arguments rather than by true reason, [nt: One of the Colbert manuscripts: *quod ad tempus*. Garnier presents this whole passage thus: *quam vera ratione purgavit negando, alia falsa interpretando, ne ad tempus quidem videri potuerit absolutus*, words taken away or added or moved from their place against the credit of the other books. From this account of Innocent one may suspect that the acts of the synod of Diospolis sent to him were not genuine, but those which Pelagius had adulterated and which he called the paper of his defence, such as the same heretic had also sent to Augustine. Certainly Augustine too suspects, in the book On the Proceedings of Pelagius, no. 57, that he had taken pains that those adulterated acts should run about everywhere, where he says: *Who would not believe that this was contrived, so that that paper might run about everywhere as though it were an abridgement of those acts?* Nor should we think without reason that those unknown men who brought the writing mentioned were devoted to Pelagius, and delivered to Innocent what, out of favour to their master, they held as established. Rightly therefore does Innocent say above that he doubts of those acts, whether they are true, and repeats it below.] as he could seem for the time, [0595C] denying some things, turning others by a false interpretation.

## 4.

[0596A]But would that, what is rather to be wished, he may now turn himself from that error of his own track to the true way of the catholic faith, so that he may desire and wish to be cleared, considering the daily grace of God and acknowledging his help, so that he may be seen [nt: In Augustine, as in the manuscripts: *verum*. Shortly after, Labbe, following the Roman edition and Merlin: *gestorum judicio*: wrongly.] truly and be approved by all as corrected by manifest reason, not by the showing of acts, but with his heart turned to the catholic faith! Wherefore we can neither approve nor blame the judgment of those men, since we do not know whether the acts are true; or, if they are true, whether it is certain that he rather slipped out than cleared himself with the whole truth. If he is confident, and knows that what he says is not worthy of our condemnation, [nt: Quesnel, having put before this *Qui si se confidit*, here adds *ut confirmet verum esse quod dicat, aut etiam hoc totum*, etc., a mere gloss, which we have not found even in the old exemplars of the Codex he himself published. In the Roman edition the conjunction *aut* is lacking before *jam hoc totum*. By Garnier, and thence in the new edition of Augustine, this passage was published thus: *Quod si confidit novitque nostra dignum se esse damnatione, quod dicat jam totum hoc refutare* (in Augustine, *refutasse*) *quod dixerat*. Our reading is the better in that it is not only drawn out according to the credit of the old codices, but is also fuller and more complete than the others. For Innocent means this: that if Pelagius is confident that what he even now says and teaches is not worthy of condemnation, or that he has already refuted, that is, retracted and repudiated, what he had formerly said and taught, he himself, sure of his acquittal, ought to hasten to Rome.] or that he has already refuted all this which he had said, he ought not to be summoned by us, but rather himself to hasten, so that he can be acquitted. For if he still thinks in such a way, when, summoned by any letters whatever, [nt: The editions: *acceptis*; the Pithou manuscript: *acceptus*. More fittingly the Corbie: *accitus*. A little before, for *accersiri*, the Vatican manuscript has *acciri*.] [0596B] will he commit himself to our judgment, since he knows that he is to be condemned? But if he were to be summoned, it would better be done by those who are nearer and are seen not to be separated by a long space of lands. But care will not be lacking if he offers matter for the medicine. For he can condemn what he had thought, and by letters sent, as befits a return to us, ask pardon for his error, dearest brethren.

## 5.

The book indeed which was said to be his, sent to us by your charity, we have unrolled: in which we read that many things were written against the grace of God, many blasphemous things, nothing that pleased, nothing almost [nt: This *pene*, and the words above, *multa contra Dei gratiam legimus esse conscripta*, have been restored from Augustine's edition and the Vatican and Corbie manuscripts, since they were lacking in the others.] that did not wholly displease, to be condemned and trodden under foot by anyone, whose like no other would take into his mind and think except him who had written these things. [0596C] For we did not [0597A] think it necessary in this place to dispute more broadly about the law, as though Pelagius were placed before us and resisting; since we are conversing with you who know the whole [nt: The Roman edition and that of the Councils: *totam rem*. The Corbie manuscript: *totum*. More truly the other printed and written books: *totam*, namely the law. For, as Quesnel noted, Innocent alludes to that saying of the Apostle, Rom. VII, 1: *For I speak to them that know the law*. So too afterwards Celestine, addressing the synod of Ephesus in Epistle 17, no. 4, says: *Encouraged briefly by all these things, since, as the Apostle says, I speak to them that know the law*.] of it, and who rejoice with us in equal assent. For these examples are better set down when we deal with those whom it is plain to be ignorant of these matters. [nt: In three manuscripts, as in the edition of the Councils: *prudentes*. The other books more correctly: *imprudentes*, that is, unskilled and rude men who are less versed in the law. Therefore the nearest *melius* is the same as *more opportunely* and *more fittingly*.] For concerning the possibility of nature, concerning free will, and concerning all the grace of God, and daily grace, to whom that thinks rightly would it not be most fruitful to dispute? Let him therefore anathematize what he has thought: so that those who had fallen through his discourses and precepts may know at length what the true faith holds. For they will be able to be recalled more easily when they perceive that these things are condemned by their own author. But if he wishes obstinately to persist in this impiety, it must be done [nt: Garnier: *qualiter vel iis possit subveniri, quos non scelus huc, sed magis error induxit*: corruptly. Here the author of the error is distinguished from those whom he led astray.] so that help may at least be given to those whom not their own error but rather his has led on; lest this medicine perish for them too, whose cure this man neither admits nor asks for. ( *And [0597B] in another hand:* ) May God keep you safe, dearest brethren. Given on the sixth day before the Kalends of February [nt: In Quesnel, as in the Roman edition and that of the Councils and most manuscripts, what follows is lacking. In its place Garnier substituted *Honorio XI et Constantio II coss.* He who wrote out the Corbie codex, unless the fault is older, having the genuine reading before his eyes, set it out thus corrupted through unskilfulness: *Pridie kalendas Februarias gloriosissimi Theodosii qui et Junii quarti Palladii v. c. conss.* That is, as was observed in the newest edition of Augustine, moving the sign *p. c.* from its place and joining it with *VI kalendas*, he made *pridie kalendas* out of it; and in place of *Theodosii VII aug.* he read *Theodosii qui*. By comparison with Epistle 29, as each slip becomes manifest, so it is also repaired. The same subscription is given thus in the Vatican manuscript: *Data kal. Februarii post. cons. Theodosii aug. et Junii Quarti Palladii*, the two numeral signs, namely, being omitted.] [n: (Jan. 27, ann. 417)] after the consulship of the most glorious Theodosius Augustus for the seventh time and of Junius Quartus Palladius, V. C.

## EPISTLE [nt: In the edition of the Councils, 27; the one which was 32 is now 35. It is found in the old exemplars of the Codex published by Quesnel and of the collection of Isidore, and with great agreement it is entitled a familiar letter. It was written at the same time as the three above and sent by the same bishop Julius.] XXXII. *Innocent greets Aurelius again in familiar terms.*

*INNOCENT TO AURELIUS, bishop of Carthage.*

In familiar writings true love consists. For the offices of charity buy a firmer right better [nt: The Roman edition and that of the Councils: *melius se orsa mercantur*; they are corrected from the manuscripts. Just after, *extrinsecus missae*, that is, written separately.] apart. Wherefore, through our brother Julius, I was eager to answer [nt: An old exemplar of Quesnel's Codex: *respondere maluimus, ne apud nos forsitan*, and just after *frater charissime* omitted.] a letter sent separately, lest perchance there should remain with me the offence of a particular greeting [0597C] denied, dearest brother. I therefore restore the aforesaid brother of ours to thy love, with the judgments of the apostolic see [nt: In the early editions of the Councils and the Roman, the word *dignum* is wrongly added here, against the credit of the manuscripts. Further, Innocent calls *judicata* the answers which show the judgment of the apostolic see on matters referred to it. We shall see in their place that these two words, *judicatum* and *constitutum*, are synonyms for Pope Vigilius and for Gregory.] upon the report of the twofold synod. It remains that the Lord, entreated by us, may deign to grant that every stain of his Church may be wiped away by our continual labours. May God keep thee [0598A] safe, [sic: *fratrer*] dearest brother. Given on the sixth day before the Kalends of February [nt: The consular note, omitted here, is to be supplied from the preceding letters.].

## NOTICE ON THE THREE FOLLOWING LETTERS.



## 1.

The three following letters were first published at Rome among the pontifical letters from an old book of the Vatican library, and afterwards reprinted by Baronius too, at the year 416, after a collation of them had been made with another codex. Whence their first editor thought occasion was given for writing them he explains thus: « When John, bishop of Jerusalem, followed the errors of Origen, Jerome, living at Bethlehem, and very many others abstained from his communion. For which reason, through the work of John, they were afflicted with many evils. » But we have learned the truer cause of them from what Augustine relates at the close of the book On the Proceedings of Pelagius, in this manner: « But concerning those things which after this judgment (held at Diospolis in Palestine) are said to have been perpetrated there with incredible audacity by I know not what band of abandoned men, who are said to favour Pelagius in a very [0598B] perverse way, so that the servants and handmaids of God belonging to the care of holy Jerome were afflicted with most wicked slaughter, a deacon was killed, the buildings of the monasteries were burned, and he himself was scarcely protected from this onset and incursion of the impious, in the mercy of God, by a more fortified tower, I see that we must rather be silent, and wait to see what our brethren the bishops there think should be done about these great evils, which who would believe they could pass over? » For it is clear that the evils described in the letters subjoined were suffered by Jerome not at all from the Origenists but from the Pelagians.
