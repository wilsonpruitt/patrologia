## 2.

From the same account we learn that the irruption of these heretics into the monastery committed to Jerome's care happened after the synod of Diospolis. Whence it follows that it by no means took place at the time when Jerome was abstaining from John's communion. For in the year 415, as the month of December was running out, the synod of Palestine, or of Diospolis, [0598C] was held. But from the year 397 the learned prove that John had been reconciled with Jerome through the work of Theophilus of Alexandria. [n: (V. Tillem. tom. XII, p. 199.)] From the same place it is also manifest that the following letters were by no means written before the year 416. But neither does the death of John, recorded at the year 417, allow them to be much later than this year. By Baronius they are referred to the end [0599A] of the year 416. But he ought to have gathered from Epistle 33 itself that they are to be put off to the year 417. For Aurelius, to whom this letter is inscribed, and through whom Jerome wished his letters to be delivered to Innocent, and in turn Innocent wished those in which he wrote back to Jerome to be delivered to him, he judges to be none other than the bishop of [sic: *Cathaginensi*] Carthage; nor does anyone oppose him in this matter. But in truth, when this Aurelius with his colleagues wrote at the beginning of the year 417 to Innocent against Pelagius and Coelestius, he did not know that Jerome had suffered anything from the satellites of these heretics. At that time it was only noised abroad in Africa that Pelagius, established at Jerusalem, was deceiving many there, but that many more, of whom in the letter of the synod of Milevis Jerome alone is named, « were contending against him for the grace of Christ and the truth of the catholic faith. » Certainly this place called for the addition also of what the new defender of the grace of Christ had borne from the impious, if knowledge of so atrocious a crime had reached Africa. It is not to be doubted, however, that [0599B] that most keen conflict of Jerome against Pelagius was the true cause why he was dealt with so cruelly. Although, therefore, Eustochium and Paula had kept silent about the cause and the author of the violence brought upon their monastery, yet it was not difficult for Innocent, forewarned of what Pelagius was busy doing at Jerusalem, to scent this out.

## 3.

This pope rebuked John of Jerusalem with letters so threatening, not only because it was his part either to restrain or to punish such great crimes, but also because perhaps a suspicion was not lacking that he had favoured them. The old enmities between him and Jerome on the cause of Origen, although long since laid to rest, were not hidden. He had also heard, as report announced, how greatly Pelagius was loved by John. Wherefore Augustine, epist. 179, no. 1, addresses John himself thus: « But Pelagius our brother, thy son, whom I hear that thou lovest much; » and in no. 5: « If you love Pelagius, let him also love you, nay rather himself, [0599C] and not deceive you. »

## 4.

Lastly we observe that the letter of Innocent to this John savours of Greek phrasing: and therefore that this pope either wrote in Greek, or at least took care that what he had written in Greek should be turned into Latin, and that only the Latin interpretation of this Greek text, very tenacious of the letter, has come down to us. Certainly one or another place which at first sight appears obscure or even maimed, when brought to the Greek phrasing, at once comes out clear and whole.

*INNOCENT TO HIS MOST BELOVED BROTHER AURELIUS.*

Our fellow presbyter Jerome did well to believe in thy most pious affection for coming [nt: Some persuade themselves from this, following Baronius, that the letters of Jerome, as also of Eustochium and Paula, to Innocent were carried by Aurelius himself. ## EPISTLE [nt: In the edition of the Councils, 32; the one which was 34 is now 33.] XXXIV. *Innocent consoles Jerome for what he has suffered, and declares what he has thought should be done for the present, and has already done.*

*INNOCENT TO HIS MOST BELOVED SON JEROME, PRESBYTER.* [0600B]

The Apostle testifies that contention has never done any good in the Church; and therefore he bids that the rebukes of heretics be made first rather [nt: So we had thought it should be corrected, because it had been published thus with no sense, nay with a sense contrary to Innocent's mind, as *magisque diuturna*, when our conjecture was confirmed by a re-examination of the Vatican archetype itself, and we learned that the fault had arisen from the mere unskilfulness of a copyist in reading old books. Innocent looks to that saying of Paul, Tit. III, 10: *A man that is a heretic after the first and second rebuke avoid*. And that pope teaches this chiefly of the obstinate and deceitful heretic, such as Pelagius was, who was more eager to deceive and to cover his heresy than to seek the truth.] than that they be drawn out in lengthy conference. While this rule is carelessly regarded, [nt: Baronius, having thrown *aspicitur* out to the margin of the book (which here is the same as *consideratur*), substituted *accipitur* in the text.] the evil which is to be guarded against is not avoided, but increased. Yet since thy grief and groaning so shake our bowels that [nt: In the Vatican manuscript *ratio* is lacking. Some other word is wanted for the completeness of the sentence. It will be more complete if one reads *ut nulla ratio*.] there is reason for not treating and consulting: first I address the faith of thy constancy. That everyone, for the truth, is struck with injury or, as thou sayest, with danger, [nt: In the edition of the Councils, after Baronius: *qui exspectat*. More truly the Roman, with the Vatican manuscript: *quia exspectet*. This reading will become clearer if construed thus: *Quisque* (as *quisquis*) *injuria aut periculo pro veritate percelletur, quia is beatitudinem exspectet, ipsi multis saepe narrastis*, that is, *exposuistis*. From which it follows that a man of this sort, since he is blessed in hope, is the less to be mourned.] because he awaits blessedness, you have often related in many words, and we remind [nt: The Roman edition: *et tu averte praedicationum*; the others: *et tuae verbis praedicationis*: they are corrected by the help of the Vatican manuscript, in which *tuarum te praedicationum* stands.] thee, well mindful of thy own preachings. And so, roused by so great a scene of evils, we hastened to take up the authority of the apostolic see for putting down all wickedness; [0600C] but against whom we should rise up we read neither named by name nor taxed with any account of the crime. What therefore we can, we grieve with thee. But if thou layest an open and manifest accusation against any men, I will either assign competent judges, or, if anything more urgent and careful can be done by us, I will not delay it, most beloved son. Yet I have written to my brother bishop John that he act more circumspectly: lest [nt: This place will be more complete if it is read thus: *ne si circa ecclesiam sibi creditam adhuc tale aliquid fiat, quale providere eum oportuit ne accideret, vel ne accidat*. Innocent here mentions what he writes to John at the beginning of the following letter: *It behoved thy brotherhood to keep guard, and to provide more carefully for his own flock, lest anything of this kind should arise*, and also the other things with which he closes that same letter. With what Innocent now writes are to be compared these words of Augustine, in the book On the Proceedings of Pelagius, no. 66, about the same evils: *For the impious doctrines of men of this kind are to be refuted by any catholics whatever, even those who are far away from those lands, lest they be able to do harm wherever they have been able to reach. But impious deeds, whose restraint belongs to episcopal discipline, are to be punished chiefly where they are committed, by those present or placed nearby, with pastoral diligence and godly severity. We therefore, placed so far away, ought to wish that such an end be given to these causes there that there be no need for them to be judged any further anywhere.* Where that doctor, distinguishing impious doctrines from impious deeds, teaches that impious doctrines can be refuted by any catholic and anywhere, but not likewise that impious deeds can be restrained by anyone. But Innocent, thinking otherwise of the power of his see, did not doubt that he had the right of punishing impious deeds committed in a territory no less distant from him than from Augustine, either by assigning competent judges, or even by some other means. Although he comes near to Augustine's opinion in this, that he teaches that competent judges are to be assigned by him. For by this name he may not undeservedly seem to understand bishops placed either in the same province or in the nearest one.] anything of the kind [0601A] should still happen with regard to the church entrusted to him, such as to provide against and drive off, lest it should happen, or lest it happen, would be most troublesome even to himself, and afterwards.

See, brother, the snares of the ancient enemy, and watch with the spirit of a good ruler, so that these things which have been brought to us more by report than by manifest accusation may either be corrected or checked: lest the ecclesiastical law concerning things overthrown compel him who has not defended to answer for [nt: The editions: *causis*, for which word we had already conjectured that *causam*, that is, account, should be restored, when we learned that *causas* is read in the archetype. ## 2.

Thou hast written, then, that by the fervour of faith in which thou art strong, and by love of the holy people, thou hast either repaired the churches of God or built certain new ones: but that in these thou hast no clerics whom thou mightest appoint, and that some are mutilated, some are digamists. At which we were astonished, that a prudent man should have wished to consult about things which are known to all by certain reason. We therefore say this not as to men ignorant; but we say that you, occupied perhaps with other things, have forgotten this.

## 3.

Whoever, then, of his own will has cut off part of any finger [0603C] from himself, him the canons do not admit to the clergy [n: (Dist. 55, c. 6; Concil. Tribur. c. 33, Nicaen. c. 1)] But whomsoever it has befallen [nt: The Royal manuscript: *labsu aliquo*.] by some chance, while he is either bestowing care on rustic work, or, doing something, has struck himself not of his own accord, these the canons direct both to be made clerics and, if they have been found in the clergy, not to be cast out. For in the former the will [nt: In the same codex: *voluntas est quae sibi ausa fuerit ferrum incidere*, *judicata* being omitted; which word is the same as *damnata*, inasmuch as *veniam meruit* is opposed to it just after. And the reason of this judgment is subjoined, that such a one would not hesitate to commit against others, that is, would commit without any scruple, what he had committed against himself. Hence *dubitari*, which then stands in the printed texts, does not please. Then in the Royal manuscript: *possint*; nothing prevents that word from being admitted, referred to those whose will is charged, not to the will itself.] has been judged, which has dared to lay the iron upon itself, because, namely, it could not hesitate to do [0604A] this to others as well: but in the latter the chance has earned pardon.
