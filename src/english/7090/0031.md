## 4.

Likewise he challenged his adversaries to the reading of the books which he had written earlier, in these words [0609B] [n: (Ibid., c. 35)]: « Let them read that letter [nt: To these Augustine again subjoins: "This I have read; and I found that through almost the whole of it he dwells on nothing but the faculty and possibility of nature, and places the grace of God almost there alone. But Christian grace he touches with such brevity that he seems to have done nothing else than to have feared to be silent about it."], which we wrote to the holy man Paulinus, bishop, almost twelve years ago, which in perhaps three hundred lines confesses nothing else than the grace and help of God, and that we can do nothing at all of good without God. Let them read also the letter [nt: Augustine testifies that he could not find this. "But if, he says, it is not unlike the others, it too has not what we are looking for."] to the holy bishop Constantius [n: (Ibid., c. 36)], where briefly indeed, but plainly, I joined the grace and help of God to the free will of man. Let them read also the one which [nt: Of this the same Doctor speaks thus: "This indeed I have read, and it had almost persuaded me that he confesses that grace which is in question. But when other things too had come into my hands, which he wrote later and more at large, I saw how he could have named grace even there, hiding what he thought under an ambiguous generality, yet breaking the odium and declining offence by the word grace." And Augustine laid bare the poison lurking in this work of Pelagius in the epistle formerly 143, now 188, to Juliana, at the end of which letter he marks, not obscurely, this letter of Pelagius to Innocent, nay this very passage, where he says: "But in a certain letter of his, where the same Pelagius both puts his own name most openly and does not keep silent the name of the sacred virgin, he says that he wrote to her, and tries to prove by the testimony of that same work of his that he most openly confesses the grace of God, which he is asserted either to keep silent or to deny."] we wrote in the East to Demetrias, the sacred virgin of Christ [n: (Ibid., c. 37)]: and they will find that we so praise the nature of man that we always add the help of the grace of God. Let them read also my recent [nt: Of this little work this is Augustine's judgment: "There are four books of this work, and I have read them... But in these four books also, whatever he seems to say in favour of the grace by which we are helped to turn aside from evil and to do good, he so says that he in no way departs from verbal ambiguity, which he can so explain to his disciples that they believe in no help of grace by which the possibility of nature is aided, except in the law and teaching." And after a few things left out: "But this help of law and teaching he judges to have existed in the times of the prophets too, while the help of grace, which is properly called grace, he judges to consist in the example of Christ; which you perceive nonetheless belongs to teaching, which is preached to us as the gospel."] little work [n: (Ibid., c. 41)], which we were lately compelled to publish on behalf of free will: and they will recognize how unjustly they have been eager to defame us with the denial [0609C] of grace, we who through almost the whole text of that very work perfectly and entirely confess both free will and grace. » [0610A]

## 5.

But from this letter too he wishes the reader to pass over to the book of his faith, which he had subjoined to it [n: (Ibid., c. 32)]. This book of faith, moreover, is inserted whole in the Sermon formerly of Augustine *de Tempore* 191, now appendix 236, n. 2 and following, and besides has been published separately in the appendix of vol. X of the new edition of the same saint, page 96, as also among the works of Jerome, to whom it had once been ascribed.

## 6.

Moreover in that letter, « which, » says Augustine [n: (lib. de Peccato origin., n. 19, 20 et 21)] , « he sent to Rome to Pope Innocent of blessed memory, and since they did not find him in the body, it was given to holy Pope Zosimus, and directed to me, that is, in the same letter, he complained » that he was defamed [nt: To this Augustine, in the place cited: "These things are not objected to them as he has put it. For they neither deny to little ones the sacrament of baptism, nor promise the kingdom of heaven to some without the redemption of Christ... But it is objected to them that they will not confess that unbaptized little ones are liable to the condemnation of the first man."] by men, as though he denied to little ones the sacrament of baptism, and promised to some the kingdom [0610B] of heaven without the redemption of Christ. » And when he had said « that he had never heard even any impious heretic who would say this of little ones, » he went on: « For who is so ignorant of the reading of the Gospel as not only to attempt to affirm this, but who could even lightly say it or even think it? Next, who so impious as to wish little ones to be excluded from the kingdom of heaven, while he forbids [nt: About to bring forward these words of Pelagius, Augustine puts before them: "See him preparing hiding-places of ambiguity as refuges for falsehood, pouring darkness over the truth; so that even we, when we first read these things, almost rejoiced at them as right or corrected. But his broader disputations in his books, he says, where, however much he may cover himself, he is mostly compelled to uncover himself, made even these things suspect to us, so that, looking into them more attentively, we found them ambiguous."] them to be baptized and reborn in Christ, and forbids [nt: Having explained how much Augustine laboured over the understanding of these words, he adds: "They are so ambiguous that they could afford hiding-places for their doctrine, from which the heretical sense might leap out to lie in wait."] him who was born to an uncertain life to be reborn to a perpetual and certain one. »

## 7.

Together with the aforesaid letter of Pelagius and his little book of faith there was sent to Innocent, and together delivered to Innocent's successor Zosimus, the letter of Praylius, bishop of Jerusalem, of which we learn this one thing from Zosimus, that in it Praylius, John's successor, intervened as a very earnest supporter of the cause [0610C] of Pelagius [n: (Epist. 3, n. 2)].

## 8.

But whoever reads the aforesaid [0611A] fragments of the letter of Pelagius, and weighs also the observations subjoined by Augustine, who knew well all the arts and subterfuges of this crafty heretic, and hears at the same time that the same man was approved and praised by the commendation of the prelate of Jerusalem, will surely wonder the less that Zosimus, receiving his words simply as they sounded, could be deceived by him.

## NOTICE ON THE FRAGMENT FOLLOWING.[n: (Spicileg. Maii, tom. III.)]

To the letters of Innocent I above, published often, we subjoin a larger part of his unpublished letter to Severian, bishop of Gabala. Indeed in the Arabic codex *Haiala* is written, not *Gabala*, a reading which receives some force from the fact that *Aila* is an episcopal city of Palestine in Le Quien, Or. ch. vol. III, p. 758; but yet among the very few bishops of that city whom [0611B] Le Quien knew, Severian is not counted. I therefore thought at once of Severian, bishop of Gabala, likewise a Syrian city, who was a contemporary of Innocent; and, what matters more, we learn in the notice of the lost letters of Innocent in Coustant, p. 921, that letters passed back and forth between Severian and Innocent. Besides, a little below in the Arabic codex a fragment likewise of Severian is read, in which place his episcopal see is called not *Haiala* but *Gabala*. The fault of the Arabic codex therefore arose from the likeness of a small letter. Let us then gratefully accept this fragment of a letter of Innocent, preserved for us by an Arab.

## EPISTLE XLIII. OR FRAGMENT OF THE LETTER WHICH INNOCENT, ONE OF THE FIRST BISHOPS OF ROME, WROTE TO BISHOP SEVERIAN.

[0611C]When the divine Word came from heaven, and dwelt in the womb of the holy Virgin Mary, from whom he took flesh, he by no means brought his body [0612A] with him from heaven, nor did he acquire his divinity on earth; but he himself was God, he himself formed his body in the Virgin's womb; nor did he have any partner in making his body, but he alone made it. And we confess that in all things which were done by the humanity the divinity was its companion, which was never separated from the humanity by any moment of time, not even the least. We confess also that at the very time when our Saviour came from heaven and descended into the womb of the Virgin Mary, he joined the divinity with the humanity, which were never divided in any thing or action, since they were indivisible. And just as his divinity lacks end, so also his humanity remains for ever after the resurrection. He sucked milk from a woman; nonetheless the divinity [0612B] and the humanity constituted one ( *Christ* ). Nor let anyone think that at the time when the divine Word on earth came to receive baptism from John, the beginning of his divinity was then, when, namely, John heard the voice of the Father coming out of heaven. It is by no means so; but at the very moment of time at which he descended into the Virgin's womb, one indivisible thing was made with the body and the divinity; and the divinity was the consort of the body, and in a certain immutable and inseparable manner a single unity was made. The divinity is not separated from the humanity. When Christ was on the cross, the divinity did not withdraw from his humanity. He ascended into heaven with the body which he took from the Virgin Mary, and sat at the right hand of his most good Father. This is our [0612C] faith. But those who do not so believe, the catholic Church expels them, nay its author, God, excommunicates them. Praise be to God for ever! Amen.
