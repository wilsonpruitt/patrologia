## THE FIRST COMMONITORY OF ST. VINCENT OF LÉRINS.[n: (Ex editione Baluziana.)]

[0637C]HERE BEGINS THE TREATISE *of Peregrinus, for the antiquity and universality of the Catholic faith against the profane novelties of all heretics.*

## I.

Since Scripture says and admonishes, *Ask thy fathers, and they will tell thee; thy elders, and they will declare to thee, Deut.* 32, and again, *Incline thine ear to the words of the wise, Eccli.* 6, and again, *My son, forget not these words, and let thy heart keep my words, Prov.* 3, it seems to me, Peregrinus, the least of all the servants of God, [0638C] that a matter of no small usefulness will result, the Lord helping, if I set down in writing those things which I have faithfully received from the holy fathers — a thing surely most necessary to my own weakness, since I would then have ready at hand that by which the frailty of my memory might be repaired by constant reading. To which task not only the fruit of the work, but also the consideration of the time and the opportunity of the place, urges me on. The time: because, since all human things are snatched away by it, we too ought in turn to snatch from it something that may profit unto [0639] life eternal; especially since a certain terrible expectation of the approaching divine Judgment demands that the zeal of Religion be increased, and the fraudulence of the new heretics gives sign of much need of care and attention. The place, moreover: because, shunning the throng and crowds of the cities, we dwell in the retreat of a rather remote little farmstead, and in it the secluded lodging of a monastery, where, without great distraction, that may come to pass which is sung in the psalm: *Be still,* he says, *and see that I am the Lord, Psal.* 45. And the plan of our purpose accords with this too; for we, who for some while were tossed about in the various and sorrowful whirlwinds of secular soldiery, at last, Christ breathing upon us, found harbor in the port of Religion, ever most faithful to all; so that there, laying aside the blasts of vanity and pride, and appeasing God by the sacrifice of Christian humility, we might be able to escape not only the shipwrecks of the present life, but also the fires of the age to come. But now, in the name of the Lord, I will approach what is at hand, namely, to set down the things handed down by our elders and deposited with us, with the fidelity of a reporter rather than the presumption of an author; keeping, however, this law of writing: that I touch by no means upon all things, but only upon such as are necessary, and that not in an ornate and polished but in an easy and common speech, so that most things may seem indicated rather than expounded. Let those write elegantly and carefully who are led to this office either by confidence in their talent or by the reckoning of their duty. But for me it will be enough to have prepared a Commonitory for myself, for the sake of relieving my recollection, or rather my forgetfulness — which nevertheless I shall try, little by little, by recalling what I have learned, to amend and fill out daily, the Lord granting it. And I have given this very warning beforehand for this reason: that if perchance it should slip from us and come into the hands of holy men, they may not rashly find fault in it with anything which they may yet see is to be filed down by the promised amendment.

## II.

Often, therefore, inquiring with great zeal and the utmost attention from very many men eminent in holiness and learning, in what way I might discern, by some sure and, as it were, general and regular path, the truth of the Catholic faith from the falsehood of heretical depravity, I brought back from nearly all of them almost always an answer of this kind: that whether I or anyone else wished to detect the frauds of the heretics as they arose, and to avoid their snares, and to remain sound and whole in a sound faith, he ought to fortify his faith in a twofold manner, the Lord helping: [0640] first, namely, by the authority of the divine law, and then, secondly, by the tradition of the Catholic Church. Here perhaps someone may ask: Since the Canon of the Scriptures is perfect, and abundantly and more than sufficient of itself for all things, what need is there that the authority of ecclesiastical understanding be joined to it? Because, evidently, owing to its very depth, not all men receive Holy Scripture in one and the same sense, but one interprets its utterances one way and another another, so that almost as many opinions can seem to be drawn out from it as there are men. For Novatian expounds it one way, Sabellius another, Donatus another; Arius, Eunomius, Macedonius another; Photinus, Apollinaris, Priscillian another; Jovinian, Pelagius, Caelestius another; and lastly Nestorius another. And therefore it is very necessary, on account of so many windings of such varied error, that the line of prophetic and apostolic interpretation be directed according to the standard of the ecclesiastical and Catholic sense. In the Catholic Church itself, moreover, great care must be taken that we hold that which has been believed everywhere, always, and by all. For this is truly and properly Catholic, as the very force and reason of the name declares, which comprehends nearly all things universally. But this will at length come to pass, if we follow universality, antiquity, consent. And we shall follow universality in this way, if we confess that one faith to be true which the whole Church throughout the world confesses; antiquity, moreover, thus, if we in no wise depart from those senses which it is manifest our holy elders and fathers celebrated; and consent likewise, if, in that very antiquity, we follow the definitions and opinions of all, or certainly of almost all, the priests and teachers alike.

## III.

What, then, will a Catholic Christian do, if some little portion of the Church has cut itself off from the communion of the universal faith? What indeed, but prefer the soundness of the whole body to the pestilent and corrupt member? What if some new contagion attempts to defile not merely a little portion, but the whole Church alike? [0641] Then likewise he will provide to cleave to antiquity, which now can in no way be seduced by any fraud of novelty. What if in that very antiquity an error be detected of two or three men, or certainly of one city or even of some province? Then he will altogether take care to prefer the decrees of the universal Church, established of old universally, if there be any, to the rashness or ignorance of a few. What if some such thing should emerge where nothing of this kind is found? Then he will give his effort to consult and question the opinions of the elders, compared among themselves — of those, at least, who, though in diverse times and places, yet remaining in the communion and faith of the one Catholic Church, stood forth as approved teachers; and whatever he shall have found that not one or two only, but all alike, with one and the same consent, openly, frequently, perseveringly held, wrote, and taught, let him understand that this too is to be believed by himself without any doubt.

## IV.

But that what we say may become plainer, they must be illustrated one by one with examples and amplified a little more fully; lest, by a zeal for excessive brevity, the weight of the matters be carried off by the speed of the discourse. In the time of Donatus, from whom the Donatists are named, when a great part of Africa was hurling itself into the frenzies of its [0642] error, and, forgetful of its name, its religion, its profession, was preferring the sacrilegious rashness of one man to the Church of Christ, then whoever throughout Africa, detesting the profane schism, were associated with all the Churches of the world — these alone out of all those were able to be saved within the sanctuary of the Catholic faith; leaving indeed to posterity an excellent pattern, namely, how thenceforth, by a good custom, the soundness of all should be preferred to the madness of one, or certainly of a few. Likewise, when the poison of the Arians had contaminated not now some little portion, but almost the whole world, so that, nearly all the bishops of the Latin tongue being deceived partly by force, partly by fraud, a certain fog was poured over their minds as to what above all was to be followed in so great a confusion of things, then whoever stood forth a true lover and worshiper of Christ, preferring the ancient faith to the new perfidy, was stained by no plague of that contagion. And by the peril of that time it was more than sufficiently shown how great a calamity is brought in by the introduction of a new doctrine. For by it not only small things, but even the greatest, were shaken to their foundations. For not only affinities, kinships, friendships, households, but even cities, peoples, provinces, nations, and finally the whole Roman Empire were utterly shaken and moved from their place. For when that profane novelty of the Arians, like some Bellona or fury, having first captured the Emperor of all, then subjected all the heights of the palace to new laws, it never thereafter ceased to confound and vex all things, private and public, sacred and profane alike, to make no distinction of the good and the true, but to strike down whomsoever it pleased, as from a higher place. Then were wives dishonored, [0643] widows despoiled, virgins profaned, monasteries demolished, clerics thrown into confusion, deacons beaten, priests driven into exile, workhouses, prisons, and mines filled with saints: the greatest part of whom, the cities being forbidden them, thrust out and banished, among deserts, caves, wild beasts, and rocks, afflicted with nakedness, hunger, and thirst, were worn down and wasted away. And all these things — for what other cause, save indeed while human superstitions are brought in for the sake of a heavenly doctrine, while well-founded antiquity is undermined by wicked novelty, while the institutions of our forebears are violated, while the decrees of the fathers are rescinded, while the definitions of the elders are torn up, while the lust of profane and new curiosity does not contain itself within the most chaste bounds of a hallowed and incorrupt antiquity?
