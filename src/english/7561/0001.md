## V.

But perhaps we invent these things out of hatred of novelty and love of antiquity. Whoever thinks this, let him at least believe blessed Ambrose, who, in his second book to the Emperor Gratian, himself lamenting the bitterness of the time, says: « But now, almighty God, we have sufficiently washed away, by our exile and our blood, the slayings of Confessors, the exiles of Priests, and the abomination of so great an impiety. It has become sufficiently clear that those who have violated the faith cannot be safe. » *Ambrose, On the Faith, Book 2, ch. 4, otherwise* 16. Likewise in the third book of the same work: « Let us therefore keep, he says, the precepts of our forefathers, and not, with rude rashness, dare to violate the seals of our inheritance. That sealed prophetic book neither Elders, nor Powers, nor Angels, nor Archangels dared to open: to Christ alone was reserved the prerogative of expounding it. Who of us would dare to unseal the priestly book, sealed by Confessors, and already consecrated by the martyrdom of many? Those who were afterward compelled to unseal it [0644] nevertheless sealed it again, the fraud being condemned; those who did not dare to violate it stood forth as Confessors and martyrs. How can we deny the faith of those whose victory we proclaim? » *Ibid., Book 3, ch. 7, otherwise* 15. We proclaim it plainly, I say, O venerable Ambrose! We proclaim it plainly, and, praising them, we marvel. For who is so out of his mind as not to long, even if he cannot avail to attain them, to follow those whom no force drove back from the defense of the faith of our forefathers — not threats, not blandishments, not life, not death, not the palace, not the guardsmen, not the Emperor, not the empire, not men, not demons? — whom, I say, for their tenacity in a devout antiquity, the Lord judged worthy of so great a gift, that through them he restored the prostrate Churches, gave life again to the spiritual peoples that had been extinguished, set back in place the cast-down crowns of the priests, and — a fountain of the tears of the faithful being poured from heaven upon the bishops — blotted out those nefarious things of the new impiety, not writings but blottings, and at last called back nearly the whole world, stricken by the savage tempest of a sudden heresy, from a new perfidy to the ancient faith, from the madness of novelty to the ancient soundness, from the blindness of novelty to the ancient light. But in this certain divine virtue of the Confessors there is also this above all to be considered by us, that then, in the very antiquity of the Church, the defense undertaken by them was not that of some party, but of the whole. For it was not right [0645] that men so great and of such a kind should assert, with so great an effort, the wandering suspicions of one or two men, contrary even to themselves, or truly should contend, by some rash conspiracy, for the sake of some *provincio lae*, but, following the decrees and definitions of all the priests of the holy Church, the heirs of apostolic and Catholic truth, they chose rather to betray themselves than the faith of the ancient universality. Whence also they merited to arrive at so great a glory, that they are rightly and deservedly held to be not only Confessors, but even the chief of Confessors.

## VI.

Great, therefore, and plainly divine is this example of those same blessed men, and to be recalled by all true Catholics with unwearied meditation, who, like the seven-branched candlestick shining with the sevenfold light of the Holy Spirit, showed forth to posterity a most clear pattern of how thereafter, through each and every empty talk of errors, the audacity of profane novelty may be crushed by the authority of hallowed antiquity. Nor is this indeed anything new. For this custom has always flourished in the Church, that the more devout each one was, the more readily he opposed himself to new inventions. All things are full of such examples. But, lest it grow long, let us take some one, and this above all from the Apostolic See; that all may see, clearer than light, with how great force, with how great zeal, with how great contention the blessed succession of the blessed Apostles has always defended the integrity of the Religion once received. Once, then, Agrippinus of venerable memory, bishop of Carthage, first of all mortals, against the divine Canon, against the rule of the universal Church, against the judgment of all his fellow priests, against the custom and institutions of our forefathers, held that rebaptism ought to be practiced. Which presumption brought in so much evil, that it furnished not only to all heretics a pattern of sacrilege, but even to certain Catholics an occasion of error. When, therefore, all cried out on every side against the novelty of the thing, and all the priests everywhere held back, each according to his own zeal, then Stephen of blessed memory, Pope, prelate of the Apostolic See, together [0646] indeed with his other colleagues, but yet more than the rest, resisted, judging it worthy, as I suppose, that he should surpass all the rest as much by devotion to the faith as he excelled them by the authority of his place. In short, in the Epistle which was then sent to Africa, he decreed in these words: LET NOTHING BE INNOVATED SAVE WHAT HAS BEEN HANDED DOWN. For the holy and prudent man understood that the reckoning of piety admits of nothing else than that all things which had been received from the fathers with the same faith should be consigned to the sons; and that it behooves us to follow Religion, not to lead it whither we would, but rather whither it leads; and that it is proper to Christian modesty and gravity, not to hand down our own things to posterity, but to keep the things received from our elders. What, then, was the outcome of the whole affair? What indeed, but the usual and accustomed one? Antiquity, namely, was kept, and novelty was hissed off. But perhaps at that time patronage was wanting to the new invention? Nay rather, there was present so great a force of talent, so great rivers of eloquence, so great a number of maintainers, so great a semblance of truth, so many oracles of the divine law — but plainly understood in a new and evil fashion — that to me all that conspiracy seems in no way to have been able to be destroyed, unless the very undertaking, the very defended and praised profession of novelty which was the cause of so great an effort had itself failed. Finally, what force had that same African Council or decree? By God's gift, none; but all things, as though dreams, as though fables, as though superfluous, were abolished, made obsolete, trodden underfoot. And, O wondrous turning of events! The authors of that same opinion are judged Catholics, but the followers heretics; the masters are absolved, the disciples condemned; the writers of the books shall be sons of the kingdom, but hell shall receive the maintainers. For who is so out of his mind as to doubt that that most blessed light of all the saints and bishops and martyrs, Cyprian, with the rest of his colleagues, shall reign forever with Christ? Or who is so sacrilegious to the contrary as to deny that the Donatists and the other plagues, who by the authority of that Council [0647] boast that they rebaptize themselves, shall burn forever with the devil?
