## X.

But someone will say: Why, then, are certain eminent persons set in the Church very often divinely permitted to announce new things to Catholics? A right question, and worthy to be treated more diligently and more fully: to which, however, satisfaction is to be given not by one's own wit, but by the authority of the divine law, by the teaching of the ecclesiastical magisterium. Let us hear, then, holy Moses; and let him himself teach us why learned men, and those who on account of the grace of knowledge are even called prophets by the Apostle, are sometimes permitted to put forward new doctrines, which the Old Testament is wont in allegorical speech to call strange gods, namely because the heretics observe their own opinions just as the Gentiles do their gods. The blessed Moses, then, writes in Deuteronomy: *If there arise,* he says, *in the midst of thee a prophet, or one who says that he has seen a dream, Deut.* 13, that is, a teacher set in the Church, whom his disciples or hearers think to teach from some revelation. What next? *and shall have foretold,* he says, *a sign and a wonder, and there shall have come to pass what he spoke.* Assuredly some great teacher, I know not who, is signified, and of such great knowledge that to his own followers he may seem able not only to know the things that are human, but even to foreknow the things that are above man; such as their disciples boast that Valentinus, Donatus, Photinus, Apollinaris, and the rest of that kind were. What afterward? *and shall have said,* he says, *to thee: Let us go, and follow strange gods whom thou knowest not, and let us serve them.* Who are the strange gods, but foreign errors which thou didst not know, that is, new and unheard of? *And [0651] let us serve them,* that is, let us believe them, let us follow them. What at the last? *thou shalt not hear,* he says, *the words of that prophet or dreamer.* And why, I pray thee, is it not forbidden by God to be taught that which by God is forbidden to be heard? *Because,* he says, *the Lord your God tries you, that it may be made manifest whether you love him or not, with all your heart and with all your soul.* Clearer than light the cause is laid open why divine Providence sometimes suffers certain teachers of the Churches to preach certain new doctrines. *That the Lord your God may try you,* he says. And assuredly it is a great temptation when he whom thou thinkest a prophet, a disciple of the prophets, a teacher and maintainer of the truth, whom thou hast embraced with the utmost veneration and love, suddenly and secretly brings in harmful errors, which thou canst neither quickly detect, being led by the prejudgment of the ancient teaching, nor easily deem it right to condemn, being hindered by affection for the old master.

## XI.

Here perhaps someone may demand that the things which have been asserted in the words of holy Moses be demonstrated by some ecclesiastical examples. A fair demand, and not to be long deferred. For, to begin from things near and manifest, of what kind do we suppose the temptation lately was, when that unhappy Nestorius, suddenly turned from a sheep into a wolf, had begun to tear the flock of Christ, while those very men who were being gnawed still for the most part believed him a sheep, and therefore lay the more open to his bites? For who would easily think him to err whom he saw chosen by so great a judgment of the Empire, attended by so great a zeal of the priests; who, while he was celebrated with great love of the saints and the highest favor of the people, daily handled openly the divine utterances, and refuted every harmful error of the Jews and Gentiles? In what way at last would this man not have made anyone believe that he taught rightly, preached rightly, held rightly, who, that he might open a hearing for his one heresy, assailed the blasphemies of all the heresies? But this was that which Moses said: *The Lord your God tries you, whether you love him or not.* And, to pass over Nestorius — in whom there was always more of admiration than of usefulness, more of fame than of experience, whom for some while human [0652] favor rather than divine had made great in the opinion of the crowd — let us rather call to mind those who, endowed with many advances and much industry, stood forth as no small temptation to Catholic men. As in Pannonia, within the memory of our elders, Photinus is recorded to have tempted the Church of Sirmium: where, when he had been taken into the priesthood with the great favor of all, and for some while administered it as a Catholic, suddenly, like that evil prophet or dreamer whom Moses signifies, he began to persuade the people of God entrusted to him to follow strange gods, that is, foreign errors, which before they knew not. But this is usual. That, however, is pernicious, that for so great a wickedness he used no mean supports. For he was both strong in the powers of talent and excellent in the riches of learning and most mighty in eloquence [0653]; inasmuch as he disputed and wrote copiously and gravely in either tongue: which is made manifest by the monuments of his books, which he composed partly in the Greek, partly in the Latin tongue. But well it is that the sheep of Christ committed to him, much watchful and wary for the Catholic faith, quickly looked back to the words of the forewarning Moses, and, though they admired the eloquence of their prophet and shepherd, yet did not fail to recognize the temptation. For him whom before they followed as though the ram of the flock, the same they thereafter began to flee as though a wolf. And not only by the example of Photinus, but also of Apollinaris, do we learn the peril of this ecclesiastical temptation, and are at the same time admonished to the more diligent keeping of the guard of the faith. For he too generated great heats and great straits for his hearers; inasmuch as, on this side, the authority of the Church drew them, on that side the custom of the master drew them back; and, wavering and tossing between the two, they could not make out what they ought rather to choose for themselves. But perhaps he was a man of such a kind as to be worthy of being easily despised. Nay rather, he was so great and of such a kind that in very many things he was too quickly believed. For what was more excellent than he in acuteness, in exercise, in learning? How many heresies he crushed in many volumes, how many enemy errors of the faith he refuted, that work of no fewer than thirty books is witness, most noble and greatest, in which he confounded the mad calumnies of Porphyry with a great mass of proofs. It is long to recount all his works; by which assuredly he might have been equal to the highest builders of the Church, had he not, by that profane lust of heretical curiosity, invented I know not what new thing whereby he both befouled all his labors as though by the admixture of a certain leprosy, and brought it about that his doctrine should be called not so much an edification as rather an ecclesiastical temptation.
