## XII.

Here perhaps it may be demanded of me that I set forth the heresies of those whom I have mentioned above, namely of Nestorius, Apollinaris, and Photinus. This indeed does not pertain to the matter of which we now treat. For our purpose is not to pursue the errors of each, but to bring forth the examples of a few, by which may be evidently and clearly demonstrated that which Moses says: namely, that if ever some ecclesiastical teacher, himself also a prophet in interpreting the mysteries of the prophets, attempt to bring some new thing into the Church of God, divine Providence suffers this to be done for our temptation. It will therefore be useful, in a digression, briefly to set forth what the above-mentioned heretics hold, that is, Photinus, Apollinaris, Nestorius. The sect of Photinus, then, is this. He says that God is single and solitary, and is to be confessed after the Jewish manner. He denies the fullness of the Trinity, and thinks there is no person of the Word of God nor any of the Holy Spirit. But Christ he asserts to be merely a solitary man, to whom he ascribes a beginning from Mary: and this he teaches in every way, that we ought to worship only the person of God the Father and only Christ as man. This, then, is Photinus. Apollinaris, indeed, boasts that he in a manner consents to the unity of the Trinity, and this itself not with the full soundness of faith; but in the incarnation of the Lord he blasphemes by open profession. For he says that in the very flesh of our Savior either there was no human soul at all, or certainly it was such as had no mind and reason. And he said that the very flesh of the Lord was not taken from the flesh of the holy Virgin Mary, but had descended from heaven into the Virgin; and, ever wavering and doubtful, he preached it now coeternal with God the Word, now made out of the divinity of the Word. For he would not have there be in Christ two substances, one divine, the other human, one from the Father, the other from the mother; but he thought the very nature of the Word to be cleft, as though one part of it remained in God, but another had been turned into flesh; so that, whereas the truth says that Christ is one out of two substances, he, contrary to the truth, asserts that out of the one divinity of Christ two substances were made. This, then, is Apollinaris. But Nestorius, with a disease contrary to Apollinaris, while he feigns to distinguish two substances in Christ, suddenly introduces two persons, and by an unheard-of wickedness will have there be two [0655] sons of God, two Christs, one God, the other man, one who is from the Father, the other who is begotten from the mother. And therefore he asserts that holy Mary is to be called not *Theotocon,* but *Christotocon:* namely because from her was born not that Christ who is God, but he who was man. But if anyone thinks that in his writings he says one Christ and preaches one person of Christ, let him not rashly believe it. For either he has contrived this by the art of deceiving, that through good things he might the more easily persuade evil ones as well, as the Apostle says: *Through that which is good it wrought death in me,* Rom. 7. Either, then, as we have said, for the sake of fraud, in certain places of his writings he boasts that he believes one Christ and one person of Christ, or certainly, after the Virgin's childbearing, he alleges that two persons so came together into one Christ, that nevertheless at the time of the Virgin's conception or childbearing, and for some while after, he contends that there were two Christs; so that, namely, Christ was first born a common and solitary man, and not yet joined to the Word of God by unity of person, and afterward the person of the assuming Word descended into him; and, although now he who was assumed abides in the glory of God, yet for some while there seems to have been no difference between him and other men.

## XIII.

These things, then, do Nestorius, *Apollmaris*, Photinus, rabid dogs, bark against the Catholic faith: Photinus, by not confessing the Trinity; Apollinaris, by calling the nature of the Word convertible, and by not confessing two substances in Christ, and by denying either the whole soul of Christ or certainly the mind and reason in the soul, and by asserting that the Word of God was in place of the sense of the mind; Nestorius, by affirming that there are two Christs always, or that there were two for some while. But the Catholic Church, holding right views both of God and of our Savior, blasphemes neither in the mystery of the Trinity nor in the incarnation of Christ. For it venerates both one divinity in the fullness of the Trinity and the equality of the Trinity in one and the same majesty, and confesses one Christ Jesus, not two, and the same both God and man alike. One person indeed in him, but two substances; two substances, but it believes there is one person: two substances, because the Word of God is not mutable, that it should be turned into flesh; one person, lest, by professing two sons, it should seem to worship a quaternity, not a Trinity. But it is worth the labor that we unfold this very thing again and again more distinctly and more expressly. In God one substance, but three persons; in Christ two substances, but one person. In the Trinity, one person and another, not one thing and another; in the Savior, one thing and another, not one person and another. How, in the Trinity, one person and another, not one thing and another? Because, namely, the person of the Father is one, that of the Son another, that of the Holy Spirit another; but nevertheless of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit there is not one nature and another, but one and the same nature. How, in the Savior, one thing and another, not one person and another? Because, evidently, one substance is of the divinity, the other of the humanity; but nevertheless the deity and the humanity are not one person and another, but one and the same Christ, one and the same Son of God, and of the one and the same Christ and Son of God there is one and the same person; just as in man the flesh is one thing, and the soul another, but one and the same man is soul and flesh. In Peter and Paul the soul is one thing, [0656] the flesh another; nor yet are there two Peters, flesh and soul; nor one Paul the soul, and another Paul the flesh; but one and the same Peter, one and the same Paul, subsisting from the twofold and diverse nature of soul and body. So, therefore, in one and the same Christ there are two substances; but one divine, the other human; one from God the Father, the other from the Virgin mother; one coeternal and equal to the Father, the other in time and less than the Father; one consubstantial with the Father, the other consubstantial with the mother; yet one and the same Christ in either substance. Not, therefore, one Christ God, another man; not one uncreated, another created; not one impassible, another passible; not one equal to the Father, another less than the Father; not one from the Father, another from the mother; but one and the same Christ, God and man, the same uncreated and created, the same unchangeable and impassible, the same changed and suffering, the same both equal to the Father and less, the same begotten of the Father before the ages, the same generated in the age from the mother, perfect God, perfect man. In God the highest divinity, in man full humanity. Full humanity, I say: inasmuch as it has at once a soul and flesh, but true flesh, ours, maternal, and a soul endowed with intellect, strong in mind and reason. There is, therefore, in Christ word, soul, flesh; but this whole is one Christ, one Son of God, and one our Savior and Redeemer. One, however, not by some corruptible confusion of divinity and humanity, but by a certain whole and singular unity of person. For that conjunction did not convert and change the one into the other (which is the error proper to the Arians), but rather so compacted both into one, that, the singularity of one and the same person ever remaining in Christ, [0657] the property of each nature also abides forever; so that, namely, God may never begin to be body, nor may the body ever cease to be body. Which is also demonstrated by the example of the human condition. For not only in the present, but also in the future, each of men will consist of soul and body, nor yet will either the body ever be turned into the soul or the soul into the body; but, since each of men will live without end, in each of men the difference of both substances will of necessity remain without end. So in Christ too the property of each substance, proper to each, is to be retained forever, the unity of the person nevertheless being preserved.
