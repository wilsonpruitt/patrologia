## XIV.

But since we often speak of a person, and say that God was made man through a person, we must beware most earnestly lest we seem to say that God the Word took upon himself the things that are ours by a mere imitation of action, and that whatever belongs to human life he performed as one shadowed forth, not as a true man — as is wont to happen in the theaters, where one actor suddenly counterfeits several persons, of whom he himself is none. For as often as any imitation of another's action is undertaken, the offices or works of others are so performed that nevertheless those who play them are not themselves those whom they play. For neither (to use, for example's sake, the cases of the secular stage and of the Manichaeans), when a tragic actor represents a Priest or a king, is he a priest or a king. For when the acting ceases, the person which he had taken up ceases along with it. Far be from us this wicked and impious mockery. Let that madness belong to the Manichaeans, who, preachers of a phantom, say that the Son of God is God, and that the person of a man existed not in substance but was feigned by a certain putative action and manner of life. But the Catholic faith declares that the Word of God was so made man that he took upon himself what is ours not deceitfully and in shadow, but truly and expressly, and that he did not imitate what was human as though it were another's, but rather bore it as his own; and that altogether what he did, this also he was. Just as we ourselves too, in that we speak, know, live, and subsist, do not imitate men, but are men. For neither Peter nor John — to name them above all — were men by imitating, but by subsisting. Nor did Paul feign an Apostle, or counterfeit Paul; but he was an Apostle, and subsisted as Paul. So too God the Word, by assuming and possessing flesh, by speaking, acting, and suffering through the flesh, yet without any corruption of his own nature, deigned wholly to bring this to pass: that he did not imitate or counterfeit a perfect man, but exhibited one; that he was not seen or reckoned to be a true man, but was such and subsisted as such. Therefore, just as the soul joined to the flesh, and yet not turned into flesh, does not imitate a man but is a man — and a man not by pretense but by substance — so also the Word, God, without any conversion of himself, [0658] by uniting himself to man, not by confounding, not by imitating, was made man, but by subsisting. Let there be cast off, then, entirely and to the depths, that understanding of a person which is taken up by imitative feigning, where there is always one man and another thing is counterfeited, where he who plays is never the one whom he plays. For far be it that God the Word be believed to have taken the person of a man in this deceitful manner; but rather in this way — that, his own unchangeable substance remaining, and by taking up in himself the nature of a perfect man, he himself was flesh, he himself man, he himself the person of a man, not a feigned but a true person, not an imitative but a substantial one, not one, finally, that would cease with the action, but one that would wholly abide in substance.

## XV.

This unity of person in Christ, then, was by no means compacted and perfected after the Virgin's childbearing, but in the very womb of the Virgin. For we must most earnestly take heed to confess Christ not merely as one, but also as always one: for it is an intolerable blasphemy that, even if you now grant him to be one, you should nevertheless contend that at some time he was not one but two — one, that is, after the time of his baptism, but two at the time of his nativity. And this immense sacrilege we shall assuredly not be able to avoid in any other way than by confessing that man was united to God — but by a unity of person — not from the ascension, or the resurrection, or the baptism, but already in the mother, already in the womb, already, in short, in the very virginal conception: on account of which unity of person the things proper to God are ascribed to the man indifferently and interchangeably, and the things proper to the flesh are ascribed to God. For hence it is that it is written by God's own word that both the Son of man descended from heaven, and the Lord of majesty was crucified upon earth, John 3, 1 Cor. 2. Hence also it is that, the flesh of the Lord being made, the flesh of the Lord being created, the Word of God itself is said to be made, the very wisdom of God to be filled, knowledge to be created; just as in prophecy his hands and his feet are reported to have been pierced. Through this unity of person, I say, that too was accomplished by a like reckoning of mystery, that, the flesh of the Word being born of an undefiled mother, God the Word himself is most catholically believed, and most impiously denied, to be born of the Virgin. And since these things are so, far be it that anyone should attempt to defraud holy Mary of the privileges of divine grace and of her special glory. For she is, by a certain singular gift of our Lord and God, who is however her own Son, most truly and most blessedly to be confessed *theotocos*; yet not *theotocos* in the way that a certain impious heresy imagines, which asserts that she is to be called mother of God by mere appellation, because, that is, she bore him as man who afterward was made God — just as we speak of a Presbyter's mother, or a Bishop's mother, not because she bore a Presbyter or a Bishop, but because she brought forth as man him who afterward was made a Presbyter or a Bishop. Not so, I say, is holy Mary *theotocos,* but rather for this reason: because, as was said above, already in her consecrated womb that most sacred mystery was wrought, whereby, on account of a certain singular and unique unity of person, as the Word in the flesh is flesh, so the man in God is God.

## XVI.

But now let us, for the sake of renewing the memory, more briefly and closely repeat those things which were said above in brief concerning the aforementioned heresies [0659] or concerning the Catholic faith; that so they may both be understood more fully by repetition, and be held more firmly by being driven home. Anathema, therefore, to Photinus, who does not receive the fullness of the Trinity, and who preaches Christ as only a solitary man. Anathema to Apollinaris, who asserts in Christ a corruption of the divinity turned into flesh, and who takes away the property of a perfect humanity. Anathema to Nestorius, who denies that God was born of the Virgin, who asserts two Christs, and who, the faith of the Trinity being exploded, brings in upon us a quaternity. But blessed is the Catholic Church, which venerates one God in the fullness of the Trinity, and likewise the equality of the Trinity in one Divinity; so that neither may the singularity of substance confound the property of the persons, nor again may the distinction of the Trinity separate the unity of the Deity. Blessed, I say, is the Church, which believes in Christ two true and perfect substances, but one person of Christ, so that neither may the distinction of natures divide the unity of the person, nor again may the unity of the person confound the difference of the substances. Blessed, I say, is the Church, which, that it may confess Christ to be and to have been always one, confesses that man was united to God not after the childbearing, but already in the very womb of the mother. Blessed, I say, is the Church, which understands that God was made man not by a conversion of nature, but by reason of the person — a person, moreover, not feigned and transient, but substantial and abiding. Blessed, I say, is the Church, which preaches that this unity of person has such power that, on account of it, by a wondrous and unspeakable mystery, it ascribes both the divine things to the man and the human things to God. For on account of it, it does not deny that the man, according to God, descended from heaven, and it believes that God, according to the man, was made on earth, suffered, and was crucified. On account of it, finally, it confesses both the man to be the Son of God, and God to be the Son of the Virgin. Blessed, therefore, and to be venerated, blessed and most holy, and altogether to be compared to that heavenly praising of the angels, is the confession which glorifies the one Lord God with a threefold sanctification. For it is for this very reason above all that it preaches the unity of Christ: lest it exceed the mystery of the Trinity. Let these things be said in passing, to be treated and set forth more fully at another time, [0660] if it shall please God. Now let us return to our purpose.
