## XVII.

We were saying, then, in the earlier passages, that in the Church of God there is a testing of the people, an error of the teacher, and a testing all the greater the more learned is he who errs. Which we were teaching first by the authority of Scripture, then by ecclesiastical examples — namely, by the recalling of those who, though for some while they were held to be of sound faith, yet in the end either fell away into another sect, or themselves founded their own heresy. A great matter, assuredly, and useful for learning, and necessary for pondering; which we ought again and again to illustrate and drive home with masses of examples, so that all who are truly Catholic may know that they ought to receive teachers together with the Church, not to desert the faith of the Church together with the teachers. But I judge thus: that although we are able to bring forward many in this kind of testing, there is almost no one who can be compared to the testing of Origen; in whom things so many and so illustrious, so singular, so wondrous, existed, that anyone would easily have judged that faith was to be placed in all his assertions from the very outset. For if a life makes authority, great was his industry, great his chastity, patience, endurance. If birth or [0661] learning, what more noble than he, who was first born in that house which was made illustrious by martyrdom, then, for Christ's sake, deprived not only of his father but of all his substance as well, made such progress amid the straits of holy poverty that, for the name of the Lord's confession, he was, as they report, often afflicted? Nor indeed were these the only things in him which would afterward all become matter of testing, but also so great a force of intellect, so profound, so keen, so refined, that he far and greatly surpassed almost all; so great a magnificence of learning and of all erudition that there were few points of divine philosophy, and perhaps almost none of human, that he did not thoroughly master: and, the Greek disciplines yielding to his knowledge, the Hebrew too were elaborated by him. But why should I mention his eloquence, [0662] whose speech was so charming, so milk-sweet, so pleasant, that to me not so much words as certain honey seems to have flowed from his mouth? What things difficult of persuasion did he not clarify by the powers of disputation? What things hard in the doing did he not make to seem most easy? But perhaps he wove his assertions together only by the connections of arguments? Nay, plainly there was never any of the teachers who used more examples of the divine law. But, I suppose, he wrote little? No mortal wrote more; so that to me all his works seem not only never to have been read through, but not even able to be found: to whom, lest anything at all be lacking for the instruments of knowledge, even the fullness of years abounded. But perhaps he was little happy in his disciples? Who was ever happier? Indeed from his bosom there came forth teachers innumerable, priests innumerable, confessors and martyrs. Now truly, how great was the admiration of him among all, how great his glory, how great his favor, who could recount? Who did not, being somewhat religious, fly to him from the farthest parts of the world? Who among Christians did not venerate him almost as a prophet, who among philosophers not as a master? And how reverend he was not only to private station but to the empire itself, the histories declare, which report that he was summoned by the mother of the Emperor Alexander, by the merit surely of his heavenly wisdom, with the grace and love of which she too burned. And they bear witness also to the testimony of his Epistles, which, to the Emperor Philip, who was the first of the Roman Princes to be a Christian, [0663] he wrote with the authority of a Christian teacher. And if anyone, when we report of his certain incredible knowledge, does not accept the testimony of a Christian, let him at least receive the confession of a Gentile from the testifying philosophers. For that impious Porphyry says that, roused by his fame, he went as almost a boy to Alexandria, and there saw him already an old man, but plainly such and so great a one as had founded the citadel of all knowledge. The day would fail me sooner than I could touch, even in its smallest part, upon those things which existed illustrious in that man: all of which, however, pertained not only to the glory of Religion, but also to the magnitude of the testing. For how few would either easily set aside a man of such intellect, of such learning, of such favor, and not rather use that saying — that he would rather err with Origen than think truly with others? And why say more? To this the matter came: that a testing — not any human one, but, as the outcome taught, an exceedingly perilous one — of so great a person, so great a teacher, so great a prophet, led very many away from the integrity of the faith. Wherefore this Origen, so great and so notable, while he abuses the grace of God too insolently, while he indulges his own genius too much, and trusts himself enough, while he reckons of little worth the ancient simplicity of the Christian Religion, while he presumes to be wiser than all, while, despising the ecclesiastical traditions and the teachings of the ancients, he interprets certain chapters of the Scriptures after a new manner, he deserved that of him too it should be said to the Church of God [0664]: *If there arise within your measure a prophet, Deut.* 13. And a little after: *You shall not hear,* he says, *the words of that prophet.* And again: *Because the Lord your God tests you,* he says, *whether you love him or not.* Truly not only a testing, but even a great testing, suddenly, little by little, and by degrees, to lead the Church — devoted to him and hanging upon him in admiration of his intellect, knowledge, eloquence, manner of life, and favor, suspecting nothing about him, fearing nothing — from the ancient Religion into a new profanity. But someone will say that Origen's books have been corrupted. I do not resist; nay rather I even prefer it. For this has both been handed down and written by certain persons, not only Catholics, but also Heretics. But this is what we must now observe: that even if not he himself, yet books published under his name are of great testing; which, teeming with many wounds of blasphemies, are read and loved not as another's but as his own; so that, although Origen's mind was not in conceiving the error, yet for the persuading of the error Origen's authority seems to have force.

## XVIII.

But of Tertullian too the same reckoning holds. For as the former is among the Greeks, so this man among the Latins is easily to be judged the chief of all our own. For what is more learned than this man, what more practiced in things divine and human? Indeed he embraced all philosophy and all the sects of the philosophers, the authors and defenders of the sects, and all their disciplines, all the variety of histories and studies, with a certain wondrous capacity of mind. And did he not excel in a genius so weighty and vehement that he proposed to himself almost nothing to overthrow which he did not either break through by acuteness or crush by weight? Now further, who could recount the praises of his speech, which is knit together with such — I know not what — necessity of reasonings that it drives to agreement with itself those whom it could not persuade; of whom there are almost as many judgments as words, as many victories as meanings? This the Marcions, the Apelleses, the Praxeases, the Hermogeneses, the Jews, the Gentiles, the Gnostics, and the rest know; whose blasphemies he overthrew with the many and mighty masses of his volumes, as with certain thunderbolts. And yet this man too, after all these things — this Tertullian, I say — holding too little firmly to the catholic doctrine, that is, to the universal and ancient faith, and much more eloquent than fortunate, having afterward changed his opinion, did at the last what the blessed confessor Hilary writes of him in a certain place: *By a following error,* he says, *he detracted authority from his approvable writings,* Hilary on Matthew, ch. 5. And he too was a great testing in the Church. But of this I do not wish to say more. This only will I recall: that, rising up in the Church against the precept of Moses, and asserting to be true prophecies the newfangled ravings of Montanus and those insane dreams of insane women concerning the novel doctrine, he deserved that of him too and of his writings it should be said [0665]: *If there arise in the midst of you a prophet.* And soon: *You shall not hear the words of that prophet.* Why? *Because,* he says, *the Lord your God tests you, whether you love him or not.*

## XIX.

By these, then, so many and so great masses of ecclesiastical examples, and by the rest of the same kind, we ought evidently to perceive, and, according to the laws of Deuteronomy, to understand more clearly than light, that if ever some ecclesiastical teacher has strayed from the faith, divine Providence suffers it to happen for our testing, whether we love God or not with all our heart and with all our soul.
