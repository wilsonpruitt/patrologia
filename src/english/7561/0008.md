## XXIII.

But perhaps someone says: Shall there then be held to be no progress of Religion in the Church of Christ? Let there be, plainly, and the greatest. For who is he so envious of men, so hateful to God, who would attempt to forbid this? But in such a way, nevertheless, that it be truly a progress of the faith, [0668] not a change. Since indeed it pertains to progress that each single thing be enlarged within itself; but to change, that something be turned from one thing into another. It behooves, therefore, that there grow and greatly and vigorously advance, of individuals as of all, of one man as of the whole Church, according to the stages of ages and centuries, the understanding, the knowledge, the wisdom — but only within its own kind, that is, in the same doctrine, the same meaning, and the same judgment. Let the religion of souls imitate the reckoning of bodies: which, although with the advance of years they unfold and unroll their numbers, yet remain the same that they were. There is much difference between the flower of boyhood and the maturity of old age; yet the very same become old men who had been youths; so that, although the state and condition of one and the same man be changed, nevertheless there is one and the same nature, one and the same person. The small limbs of nurslings, the large of young men, are yet the same. As many are the members of little ones as of grown men; and if there are any which are brought forth at the age of a riper time, they were already put forth in the reckoning of the seed; so that nothing new is afterward produced in old men which did not already lie hidden beforehand in children. Whence there is no doubt that this is the lawful and right rule of advancing, this the sure and most beautiful order of growing: if the number of the ages ever unfolds in the greater those parts and forms which the wisdom of the Creator had preformed in the little ones. But if the human shape be afterward turned into some figure not of its own kind, or if certainly something be added to the number of the members or taken away, it must needs be that the whole body either perish, or become monstrous, or certainly be weakened: so also it befits the doctrine of the Christian Religion to follow these laws of progress, that it be consolidated by years, dilated by time, exalted by age, yet remain uncorrupted and unimpaired, and be full and perfect in all the measures of its parts and in all its members, as it were, and its own senses, admitting thereafter no change, sustaining no loss of its property, no variety of its definition. For example: our forefathers of old sowed in this ecclesiastical field the seeds of the wheaten faith; it is exceedingly unjust and unfitting that we, their posterity, should gather, in place of the genuine truth of the grain, the counterfeit error of tares. Nay rather this is right and consequent, that, the first and the last not being at variance with themselves, from the increases of the wheaten planting we reap also the fruit of the wheaten doctrine; so that when something from those first beginnings of the seeds is unfolded in the course of time, and is now made glad and cultivated, yet nothing be changed of the property of the shoot: let the appearance, the form, the distinction, be added, yet let the same nature of each kind remain. For far be it that those rosy nursery-plants of the catholic understanding be turned into thistles and thorns. Far be it, I say, that in that spiritual paradise, from the shoots of cinnamon and balsam, darnel and aconite should suddenly spring up. Whatever, then, has been sown in this husbandry of the Church of God by the faith of the fathers, this same it befits should be cultivated and observed by the industry of the sons, this same should flourish and ripen, this same should advance and be perfected. For it is lawful that those ancient doctrines of the heavenly [0669] philosophy be, in the course of time, refined, filed, polished; but it is unlawful that they be changed, unlawful that they be lopped off, that they be mutilated. Let them receive, indeed, clarity, light, distinction; but they must needs retain their fullness, integrity, and property. For if once this license of impious fraud be admitted, I shudder to say how great a peril of Religion to be cut down and abolished follows. For, any part of the catholic doctrine being renounced, another too, and again another, and thereafter another and another, will be renounced, as it were now by custom and by leave. Furthermore, the parts being repudiated one by one, what else will follow at the last, but that the whole likewise be repudiated? But on the contrary, if things newfangled begin to be mingled with the old, foreign things with the domestic, and profane with the sacred, this custom must needs creep on into everything, so that thereafter nothing be left among the Church untouched, nothing unimpaired, nothing whole, nothing unspotted, but there be there thenceforth a brothel of impious and base errors where before was the sanctuary of chaste and incorrupt truth. But may the divine mercy avert this wickedness from the minds of her own, and let this rather be the madness of the impious. But the Church of Christ, a careful and cautious keeper of the doctrines deposited with her, never changes anything in them, diminishes nothing, adds nothing; she does not cut off what is necessary, does not append what is superfluous, does not lose her own, does not usurp what is another's; but with all industry she is intent on this one thing: that, by treating the old faithfully and wisely, if there be any things anciently shaped and begun, she may perfect and polish them; if any things already expressed and unfolded, she may consolidate and strengthen them; if any things already confirmed and defined, she may guard them; in short, what else has she ever striven for by the decrees of the Councils, but that what before was believed simply, this same might afterward be believed more diligently; what before was preached more slackly, this same might afterward be preached more urgently; what before was cultivated more securely, this same might afterward be cultivated more solicitously? This, I say, and nothing else, has the Catholic Church, roused by the novelties of the Heretics, ever accomplished by the decrees of her Councils, but that what she had first received from her Elders by tradition alone, she should afterward consign also to posterity by the handwriting of Scripture, comprehending a great sum of matters in few letters, and, for the most part, for the sake of clearness of understanding, marking a not-new meaning of the faith by the property of a new appellation.
