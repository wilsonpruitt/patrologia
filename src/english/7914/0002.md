## CHAPTER ONE. (cont.)

Verse 9. *Thy cheeks are beautiful as the turtle-dove's.* It is the nature of the turtle-dove that, if by chance it has lost its mate, [1059D] it seeks no other thereafter. To the turtle-dove therefore is the Church likened: because from the time that Christ left the world in bodily presence and sought the heavens, the Church perseveres most tenaciously in his love; nor does she receive any adulterous lover: because she despises the world and all its concupiscences, and contemplates in mind the beauty of her bridegroom alone. And he compares the cheeks of the Church to the cheeks of the turtle-dove: because in the cheeks modesty chiefly appears, and by this the shame and modesty of the Church is shown: because she blushes at everything foul, and dreads to commit what may displease her bridegroom. And the sense is: fear not that thou wilt wander among the flocks of thy companions, because with so great a modesty of love for me, and with so great a shamefastness, have I endowed and fortified thee, that I am certain thou canst not forsake me and turn aside to strangers.

*Thy neck as necklaces.* By the *neck* of the Church the holy teachers are designated; for through the neck food is supplied for the refreshing of the body. The words also by which the secrets of minds are made known go forth [1060A] through the pipe of the neck. So the holy teachers both show us the food of saving doctrine, and also make plain to us the hidden things of the Scriptures. This *neck* of the Church, therefore, is as it were a necklace, because the holy teachers are adorned with the gems of chaste doctrine and with the ornaments of the virtues. For a *necklace* is an ornament of maidens or of matrons.

Verse 10. *We will make thee murenulae of gold, vermiculated with silver. Murenulae,* according to the letter, are an ornament of a virgin's and a girl's neck, namely little rods of gold interwoven, with the finest threads of silver sometimes intermingled in a beautiful variety; and this is what he says: *Vermiculated with silver,* that is, marked out and joined to one another in the manner of the earthworms which they call *lumbrici.* And *murenulae* are so called from their likeness to the sea-fish which is called *murena.* Mystically, the *murenulae* are the interwoven doctrines of the Scriptures, joined together out of the diverse opinions of the holy Fathers. For gold signifies the brightness of the spiritual sense: silver indeed denotes the lustre of eloquence. The bridegroom therefore makes golden *murenulae* for his bride, [1060B] when Christ instructs his Church with the teachings of the holy Fathers, shining in sense and in eloquence, and kindles her to imitate their faith and virtue. It is to be noted that he says in the plural, *we will make thee;* for Christ as it were joins himself to the persons of the teachers, through whom those very *murenulae* are linked together, that the Church may be adorned with them.

Verse 11. *While the king was at his reclining.* The voice of the bride calls her bridegroom Christ king, who by his divinity governs all things, and who also according to his humanity was born of the royal stock of David. The *reclining of the king* was his incarnation. For to recline is to be laid back or to sit down at meat. And then Christ reclined, when for our redemption he bowed himself down even to the taking up of our nature. And then

*My nard gave forth its odour,* when the virtue of the Church shone forth the more. For when she considers God made man for her sake, she is the more kindled to the pursuit of the spiritual virtues and to the love of her Creator. [1060C] For *nard* denotes the fragrance of the spiritual virtues. There were certain saints of God before the incarnation, but (as has been said) holiness increased the more from the time of the incarnation, and then in a manner the *nard* of the Church, that is, the odour of the virtues, gave forth its odour more strongly. According to the letter, *nard* is an aromatic herb, with a thick and brittle root, and a small and dense leaf, whose tips spread themselves out into spikes. With this ointment Mary Magdalene is read in the Gospel to have anointed the feet and the head of the Lord in the house of Simon [n: (Matth. XXVI, 7; Marc. XIV, 3; Luc. VII, 38; Joan. XII, 3)].

Verse 12. *A bundle of myrrh is my beloved to me.* Myrrh is an aromatic species of exceeding bitterness, with which the bodies of the dead are embalmed. In this place the passion and burial of Christ are designated: for the body of the Lord, when taken down from the cross by Nicodemus and Joseph, was embalmed with myrrh and aloes, and wrapped in linen cloths with spices, and given over to burial [n: (Matth. XXVII, 59; Marc. XV, 46; Luc. XXIII, 53; Joan. XIX, 40)]. The Church therefore says to her bridegroom: *A bundle of myrrh [1060D] hath my beloved* been made *to me:* because for my sake he died and was buried.

*He shall abide between my breasts.* No one doubts that the place of the heart is between the breasts. She says therefore: *He shall abide between my breasts,* that is, he shall be held eternally in the memory of my heart, and I shall never forget his so great benefits; but whether I be in prosperity or in adversity, yet shall I remember him who loved me so greatly, and died for me.

Verse 13. *A cluster of Cyprus is my beloved to me, in the vineyards of Engaddi. Cyprus* is an island where noble vines are reported to be, bringing forth the largest clusters. *Engaddi* likewise is reported to be a place having little trees that sweat out the liquor of balsam. The bride therefore speaks of the resurrection of Christ her bridegroom: *A cluster of Cyprus is my beloved to me;* as if she said: My bridegroom, who tasted the bitterness of death for me, *508* and was to me as it were a bundle of myrrh, in his resurrection was made [1061A] *to me a cluster of Cyprus,* when he gladdened me with the joy of his resurrection. For *wine maketh glad the heart of man [n: (Psal. CIII, 15)]*; and this *in the vineyards of Engaddi:* because by his resurrection he gave forth his fragrance throughout the whole world as with the sweetest odour of balsam, and spread abroad far and wide the fragrance of faith in him. And *Engaddi* is interpreted the fountain of the kid, by which baptism is signified, and the sacrifice in which all sins are washed away. For the kid signifies sinners. Baptism therefore is the fountain of the kid; because it receives sinners, but renders them unstained and cleansed from all sins.

Verse 14. *Behold thou art beautiful, my friend: behold thou art beautiful.* The voice of the bridegroom Christ to his bride the Church. Twice he repeats that she is beautiful, on account of the perfection of her work and the purity of her thought. *Behold thou art beautiful,* that is, thou art perfect in good works, living holily and piously and justly according to the Apostle [n: (Tit. II, 12)]. *Behold thou art beautiful,* having a clean and single intention of heart: because not for earthly advantages, nor for the glory of the world, but that thou mayest please the eyes of God alone, [1061B] dost thou work good things.

*Thy eyes are of doves.* The dove is a most simple bird, and by it the simplicity and innocence of the Church is figured. For she has as it were the *eyes of doves,* because she keeps herself free from all feigning and dissimulation. Or also she has the *eyes of doves,* because she understands the knowledge of the divine Scriptures with a chaste and simple gaze, seeking in it the spiritual and divine senses. For since the Holy Spirit appeared in the likeness of a dove [n: (Matth. III, 16)], rightly is the heavenly doctrine understood in the eyes of the dove.

Verse 15. *Behold thou art beautiful, my beloved, and seemly.* As if she said: Thou indeed sayest that I am beautiful in work and in thought; but I believe and know that I have all this beauty from thee. For *thou art beautiful and seemly,* thou art the fountain of every good, and whatever good I have, I have by thy grace. And he is called *beautiful and seemly:* because he is wonderful both in the perpetuity of his divinity and also in the dignity of the humanity he took up.

[1061C] *Our little bed is flowering.* The little bed of the Church is whatever tranquillity or rest there is in the present life. For the Church is as it were in war, while for Christ she endures diverse conflicts: but she rests as it were in a little bed when she enjoys some small measure of peace granted her. But this *little bed is flowering,* that is, the rest of the Church is picked out with the variety of the virtues as with blossoming flowers. For then she applies herself more freely to divine contemplation; then she carries out more freely fastings, vigils, and the other exercises of good works.

Verse 16. *The beams of our houses are of cedar, our panelled ceilings of cypress.* The *houses* of the Church are the assemblies of the faithful scattered throughout the whole world. But the *beams* and the *panelled ceilings* are the very persons of the faithful, of whom the Church consists. But it is to be observed that the *beams* are for the strengthening of the house, the *panelled ceilings* for its adornment. And therefore by the *beams* are designated the teachers, by whose teaching the Church is propped and sustained; but by the panelled ceilings the saints who rejoice in simplicity, who by their [1061D] virtues adorn the Church, but do not fortify it by teaching. And this is to be observed, that the panelled ceilings are fastened to the beams: so too the life of the faithful in the Church hangs upon the teachers, because by their teaching it is instructed and strengthened. And well are they called *beams of cedar* and *panelled ceilings of cypress;* for both trees are of an incorruptible nature, designating the saints who burn with unfading desire in the love of their Creator, and, despising things transitory, hasten to things eternal. The cedar also puts serpents to flight by its odour: so the holy teachers drive off demons or heretics by the fragrance of their teaching.
