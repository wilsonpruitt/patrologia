## CHAPTER V.

Verse 1. *Let my beloved come into his garden, that he may eat the fruit of his apple trees.* The Church, hearing persecution called for, as though preparing herself against adversities, says: *Let my beloved come into his garden*, [1080D] that is, let Christ my bridegroom come, whom I love with my whole heart, to his Church, *that he may eat the fruit of his apple trees*, that is, let him be delighted and fed by the good works of his faithful. For the food of the Lord is our good works, as he himself, beholding the faith of the Samaritans or of the Gentiles, said to his disciples: *I have meat to eat which you know not [n: (Joan. IV, 32)]*.

*I am come into my garden, my sister, my bride.* To the Church desiring that he come to her the bridegroom answers that he has already done this. I (he says) have already come. For *veni*, here, is not the word of one commanding, but the indicative of the past tense. O my sister, my bride, *520* I have already come into my garden, I have already visited my Church; but I also visit it daily, and I am fed with the apples of her virtues and enjoy her odor. I come to her, that I may correct the erring, strengthen the infirm, confirm the doubting, and endow all the perfect with heavenly rewards.

[1081A] *I have reaped my myrrh with my [sic: aromalibus].* By *myrrh* are designated those who have ended their life by martyrdom, or also who have crucified their flesh with its vices and concupiscences [n: (Gal. V, 24)]. But the aromatical spices are the works of the holy virtues, fragrant with the odors of good working. The Lord therefore *reaps* his *myrrh with* his *aromatical spices*, when he cuts off from this life with the sickle of death the holy martyrs, or the rest of the elect who are marked out by good works, and, when they have been brought to the ripeness of good works, stores them up in the granaries of the country above.

*I have eaten my honeycomb with my honey, I have drunk my wine with my milk.* By the *honeycomb* and the *wine* the holy preachers are figured: but by the *honey* and the *milk*, the good hearers. For the preachers are the *honeycomb*, who bring forth the hidden and innermost mysteries of the Scriptures as honey from the combs, when by preaching they make them manifest to others. But the *honey* is the good hearers: because they strive to receive attentively the sweetness of the word of God, and to be fed upon it with delight. The preachers themselves are the *wine*, who are also the *honeycomb*: because they announce the strong sacred mysteries [1081B] of the Scriptures. But the *milk* is all the weak hearers, for whom open mysteries are suited as milk. Since, therefore, the life of all these is passed in different pursuits, and by the good intention of all of them the Redeemer is fed and delighted; and thus he as it were *eats the honeycomb with the honey, and drinks the wine with the milk.* We can also refer this to the death of the elect, whom the Lord eats, when through death he calls them to eternal life, and joins them to his body, that is, to the fellowship of the elect now rejoicing in that heavenly happiness. But if we understand this of the death of the saints, we ought to understand in the *wine* those whose souls now glory in heavenly happiness; but in the *honeycomb*, those who now rejoice in body and soul in that blessedness, such as are those who rose again with the Lord [n: (Matth. XXVII, 52)].

*Eat, O friends, and drink, and be inebriated, O dearly beloved.* Not only does the Lord rejoice in the advancement of the saints, but he also exhorts us to rejoice in their virtues [1081C] and to imitate them: *Eat* (he says), *O friends*, that is, my faithful, who are friends by doing what I command; you are my *dearly beloved* by loving me with a perfect charity of soul. *Eat*, therefore (he says), *and drink*, that is, rejoice together in the good acts of the saints, and set them before yourselves for imitation. But it is to be noted that he says, *drink and be inebriated.* Inebriation is sometimes put in the Scriptures for fullness, as it is said of the brethren of Joseph: *They drank and were inebriated with him [n: (Gen. XLIII, 34)]*; and the Psalmist: *Thou hast visited the earth, and hast inebriated it [n: (Psal. LXIV, 16)]*, that is, thou hast satisfied and filled it. He exhorts us therefore not only to eat and to drink, but also to be filled: because there are some who eat and are not filled, because, namely, they hear the divine preaching with delight, but negligently forget it, and do not bring what they have heard to the effect of good work. But those eat and are filled who lay up in their inward senses the words of life which they hear, and labor to fulfill them in work; [1081D] of whom the Psalmist says: *The poor shall eat and shall be filled [n: (Psal. XXI, 27)]*. But if (as has been said above) we understand this of the death of the saints, the *friends* and the *dearly beloved* are the angelic spirits, whom the Lord bids rejoice together with his elect, when they are carried over from this life to the rest of eternal blessedness, according to what is read in the evangelical parable: *Rejoice with me, because I have found my sheep that was lost [n: (Luc. XV, 6)]*.

Verse 2. *I sleep, and my heart watcheth.* The voice of the Church, *I sleep*: because, my bridegroom granting some little peace, I am at rest, nor do I suffer such oppressions as the primitive Church, and therefore *my heart watcheth*, because, peace being granted me, I cleave more securely to the love of my bridegroom, and direct toward him the eyes of my heart. But because this time is not one of rest, but rather of labor and of contest, again the bridegroom rouses the Church to labors, and exhorts her to the contest of preaching.

[1082A] *The voice of my beloved knocking.* The beloved *knocks*, when Christ exhorts his faithful to the advancement of the virtues; or he *knocks*, when he admonishes them to gain their neighbors by preaching. And there is a third knocking, with which the Lord knocks upon his elect, when through the trouble of sickness he signifies that death is near; of which knocking it is said in the Gospel: *That when he cometh and knocketh, they may open to him immediately [n: (Luc. XII, 36)]*. But in this place he is said *to knock*, that is, to urge the Church on to the work of preaching.

*Open to me, my sister, my friend, my dove, my undefiled. Open to me, my sister. Sister*, because thou hast been made coheir of my kingdom; *my friend*, because, delivered from the yoke of servitude, thou hast come to know the secrets of my truth; *my dove*, because thou hast been sanctified by the dowry of my Spirit; *my undefiled*, because by the pouring out of my blood thou hast been cleansed from every spot of sins. *Open to me*, that is, go forth from the quiet and leisure of sweetness and of contemplation to the work of preaching.

[1082B] *For my head is full of dew, and my locks of the drops of the nights. The head* of the Church (as the Apostle says) is Christ [n: (Ephes. V, 23)]; but the *locks* are the innermost thoughts of the saints, which do not flow loose and unbound, but are gathered together by the bond of the fear and the love of God. But the *dew* and the *drops of the nights* in this place designate minds that are dark and full of unbelief and cold. The *head*, therefore, of the bridegroom *is full of dew*, when all worldly men grow torpid toward the charity of God, and according to what the Lord says: *When sin aboundeth, charity groweth cold [n: (Matth. XXIV, 12)]*. And when such men persecute and hate the saints of God who meditate upon heavenly things, then as it were the *locks* of the bridegroom *are full of the drops of the nights.* When, therefore, such men are multiplied and burden the Church, then the bridegroom admonishes his bride that she rise and press on to the work of preaching.

Verse 3. *I have put off my garment, how shall I put it on?* The Church, being called on by her bridegroom to the office of preaching, answers: *I have put off my garment: how shall I put it on?* I have stripped myself of the cares and occupations [1082C] of this world, without which the office of preaching is carried on either scarcely or not at all, and how can it come about that I should return again to those things which I have forsaken? For he who fits himself to take up the office of preaching ought also to provide temporal things for his subjects, which cannot be managed without great solicitude. But that the garment signifies the solicitudes and cares of the world, the Lord shows in the Gospel: *And he that is on the housetop, let him not come down to take his garment [n: (Matth. XXIV, 18)]*; which is plainly to say: He who stands on the lofty citadel of contemplation, let him not come down to take his garment *521*, that is, let him not come down to take up the cares of the world; that is, lest he be again entangled in the occupations of the world. The Church, therefore, or every holy soul, fears to be stripped of this garment, and, resting in the contemplation of her Maker, to be clothed again and taken up with worldly business.

*I have washed my feet, how shall I defile them? I have washed my feet*, that is, the actions with which I was never accustomed to touch the dust, [1082D] that is, to do earthly things, I have washed with the worthy weeping of penance, so much so that now nothing pleases me to think upon but divine things. *How shall I defile them?* that is, how can it come about that I should return again to thinking upon perishable and earthly things, I who now, in an occupied and free heart, enjoy the divine gaze?

Verse 4. *My beloved put his hand through the hole, and my belly trembled at his touch. The beloved puts his hand through the hole* and touches the belly, when the Maker visits the heart with inward inspiration, and kindles it to the advancement of the virtues: or also when he calls us back to the memory of his works, that we may consider: that, when he was God, he deigned to become man for us; that, taking on our earthly things, he might make us heavenly, and by dying for us might bestow eternal life upon us. And when this comes to pass, *the belly trembles*: because, when we begin to think upon such things, the secret places of our heart are shaken, while we begin to be amazed with how great a condescension the Maker deigned to do these things for us [1083A] For that the belly signifies the heart, the prophet shows, saying: *My belly, my belly is in pain [n: (Jer. IV, 19)]*; and he shows what this was, saying: *The senses of my heart are troubled.*

Verse 5. *I arose up to open to my beloved.* We open to the beloved not only when we receive his coming, but also when we preach to others; and those who through malice had closed their breast, converted by our preaching, we bring it about that they too open the door of their heart to Christ. And well is it first said, *I arose up;* then, *to open to my beloved.* For he who by preaching wishes to open the hearts of others to Christ ought first to rise up, that is, to be lifted up to the zeal of good works, and to fulfill in work what he preaches: *lest perhaps, preaching to others, he himself become reprobate [n: (I Cor. IX, 27)]*; hence also Luke says of the Lord: *Which Jesus began to do and to teach [n: (Act. I, 1)]*; first he says to do, then to teach.

*My hands dropped with myrrh, and my fingers [1083B] were full of the choicest myrrh.* By the hands with which we work is designated the working itself; but by the fingers, the discernment of the working: because in no member are the distinctions of the joints so many as in the fingers. The *hands* of the Church *drop myrrh*, when her preachers give their labor to continence and to the mortification of their flesh, saying with the Apostle: *I chastise my body, and bring it into subjection [n: (II Cor. IX, 27)]*. Or when they are ready to die for Christ; as John says: *If Christ laid down his life for us, we also ought to lay down our lives for the brethren [n: (I Joan. III, 16)]*. But the fingers are full of the choicest myrrh, when we subtly discern the work of mortification itself, whether it be done for the sake of the reward above, or for the praises of the world. For he who fasts for human favors, or presses on with works of abstinence or of continence, has indeed myrrh in his hands, but has it not in his fingers: because he does not discern why he does this; of whom the Lord says: *Amen I say to you, they have received their reward [n: (Matth. VI, 2)]*. Or the hands of the bride drop [1083C] myrrh, when they fit themselves to the works of the holy Church, of chastity or of holy continence.
