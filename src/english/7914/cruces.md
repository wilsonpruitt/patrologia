## The Song of Songs rule, and how it governed this work

Wilson's ruling, 2026-07-30: **each commentary stands on its own; a lemma is never
harmonized with another commentary.** Sixteen PL commentaries expound this book, and
imposing one English across them would be editorializing Migne. So every choice below
was derived from THIS plate. Where the result differs from another Song commentary on
the site, that is correct and must not be swept.

**The rule's second case — "where a gloss depends on the word, the Latin stands" — did
most of the work here, and the agents applied it four times to words nobody pre-set:**

- ***murenulae*** (pre-set) — *Murenulae autem dicuntur a similitudine piscis marini,
  qui murena vocatur.* Kept in italic Latin.
- ***vermiculatas argento*** → "vermiculated with silver", because the next clause
  glosses it *in modum vermium terrenorum*; an English word without the *verm-* root
  breaks the gloss exactly as translating *murenulae* would.
- ***elatae palmarum*** — glossed through the Greek ἐλάτη at 1085C. Italic Latin.
- ***malum granatum* / *malum punicum*** — glossed etymologically at 1101A (*granorum*
  … *Punica enim ipsa est Africa*), so both stay Latin **in that passage only**;
  "pomegranate" everywhere else, matching the 12 instances in the peer chunks.

**⚠ Site furniture is NOT a lemma.** `Vers. N.` was unified to **"Verse N."** across the
work (Migne prints the abbreviation uniformly; the English was split 76/40). That is
apparatus, like the project's existing `CAPUT` → `CHAPTER`, and site-wide consistency
there is right. **The ruling governs the words of Scripture, not the furniture around
them** — do not read this sweep as licence to harmonize a lemma.

## Work-wide conventions (reconciled at merge)

- ***ferculum*** (Song 3:9, 1070D) → **"litter"**, reached independently by two agents.
  This work does NOT gloss it etymologically, only allegorizes it (*designatur per

### *ferculum* (Song 3:9) — DECISION: **"litter"**

*ferculum* does not occur in this batch (it enters at 0007, col. 1070D), but the decision is
recorded here for propagation, per the batch brief.

**Rendering: "litter."** Reasoning, from this work's own framing and nothing else:
1. The work does **not** gloss the word etymologically. It allegorizes it — *Quod superius per
   lectulum Salomonis, sic nunc designatur per ferculum, sancta videlicet Ecclesia, in qua velut
   in lecto requiescunt sancti Dei, et velut in lecto discumbunt ad epulas.* So Pattern-9 case 2
   (gloss depends on the word) does **not** apply, and the Latin need not stand.
2. The two things the exposition needs the object to do are **be reclined on as a bed**
   (*velut in lecto requiescunt*) and **be reclined on at a banquet** (*discumbunt ad epulas*).
   "Litter" — a portable couch — carries both, and carries them at once, which "palanquin"
   (conveyance only) and "bed" (already spent on *lectus/lectulus*) do not.
3. *lectulus* is heavily used in this work (Song 1:15, 3:1, 3:7) and is rendered **"little bed"**;
   *lectus* is **"bed."** The exposition at 1070D turns on *ferculum* being the *same thing under
   a different name* as the *lectulus Salomonis* — so *ferculum* must be a word that is clearly
   distinct from those two and still recline-able. "Litter" is.
4. Downstream at 3:10 the work reads *media hujus ferculi constrata sunt* — "the middle parts of
   this litter were spread/paved" — which "litter" supports without strain.

**Merge note:** if a later agent has already shipped a different word, this is a WITHIN-work
consistency question (the boundary in `translation-style.md` §Song of Songs), so it must be
reconciled to one term, not left to stand.

## Readings rendered literally, no marker (Pattern 7 / 12 boundary)

These are **real words that are merely wrong or odd**. Per Pattern 12 they are rendered as
ordinary English — the word on our page is ours — and the conjecture lives here.

### 4. `Itera` for *Iterat* — 0006, col. 1068B
Printed: *Itera autem difficultatem inventionis, et dicit: quaesivi, et non inveni.*
`Itera` is a well-formed imperative ("repeat!"), but the sentence is third-person narration
about the author's technique (*et dicit*), so *Iterat* ("he repeats") is near-certainly meant.
Rendered as printed: "Repeat, however, the difficulty of the finding, and he says…". The
English is as odd as the Latin, deliberately. **Not marked** — it is a real word, and marking it
would blame the plate for a rendering that is ours.

### 5. Missing first `nec` — 0006, col. 1069B ⚠ NEGATION
Printed: *Quanta laude et admiratione digna, quae circumcisione mundata, nec adhuc baptismo
sanctificata jam currit ad Deum?*
The subject is *Ecclesia de gentibus* — the Church **of the Gentiles**, which by this work's own
argument was neither circumcised nor yet baptized. Sense requires *nec circumcisione mundata,
nec adhuc baptismo sanctificata*; the printed text has only the second `nec`, so it asserts that
she **was** cleansed by circumcision.
**Rendered exactly as printed** — "who, cleansed by circumcision, and not yet sanctified by
baptism, already runs to God?" The printed `nec` is carried; no `nec` is supplied. This is a
Pattern 7a case of the purest kind: the smooth reading would require inventing a negation Migne
does not print, and the contradiction with the surrounding argument is precisely the evidence
the edition exists to expose.

### 6. `labores aeternos` — 0003, col. 1062B
Printed: *Videns sponsam suam ad requiem festinare, et labores aeternos fugere, ac per quietem
praesentis temporis ad aeternam beatitudinem tendere.*
"Fleeing **eternal** labours" contradicts the sentence's own destination (*ad aeternam
beatitudinem tendere*) and the moral that follows (*per pressuras et labores hujus saeculi illuc
pervenire*). Conjecture: *externos* ("outward"), i.e. the labours of public action she is being
told she cannot skip — which is exactly this chapter's theme (cf. 1065A, *de secreto quietis
egredere ad publicam actionem*). Rendered as printed: "flee eternal labours." No emendation.

### 7. `Inter omnes virtute primatum tenet charitas` — 0003, col. 1063A
Printed *virtute* (ablative singular) where *virtutes* ("Among all the virtues") is expected and
gives the standard sentence. Rendered as printed: "Among all, charity holds the primacy in
virtue." An ablative of respect is defensible Latin, so this may not be a defect at all; logged
for the record, no marker.

### ***ferculum*** (Song 3:9, col. 1070D) — **RULED: "litter"**
The prompt flagged this as the known crux. **This work does not gloss *ferculum*
etymologically**; it allegorizes it: *sic nunc designatur per ferculum, sancta videlicet

with silver pillars, a golden reclining-place and a purple ascent — one climbs *up* into

(the commonest lexical sense of *ferculum*) is impossible: nothing is served on it.
"Palanquin" and "portable throne" carry the conveyance but not the couch. **"Litter"**
carries both — an English litter is a couch that is carried and ascended — and it keeps
the later *media hujus ferculi constrata sunt* ("the midst of this litter is paved")
construable. Used consistently: 0007 ×4 (the lemma, the *sic nunc designatur*, the
*media…constrata*, the *hujus ferculi*). Since the gloss is allegorical and not
etymological, keeping the Latin was not required (Song-of-Songs rule, case 2 does not
fire).

in the same paragraph — the lemma of Song 8:14 at 1105C prints *assimilare capreae
**hinnulo**que cervorum*, and the re-quotation a few lines above prints *Similis esto
capreae **hinnulo**que cervorum*. Conjecture *hinnuli*, "young harts".

## Rendered as printed — no marker

### 1097A — *Statura … assimilantur*, singular subject with plural verb
Migne prints *Statura ergo Ecclesiae palmae **assimilantur***. The subject *statura* is
singular; the verb is plural. Unlike a gender mismatch (Pattern 9), English **does** have
an exponent for number, so smoothing it to "is likened" would be a silent repair of
exactly the class Pattern 7a forbids. The English carries the plural — "The stature
therefore of the Church **are** likened to the palm" — and the reader is told here that
the disagreement is the plate's. Conjecture: *assimilatur*, or *staturae*; the sentence
construes either way and neither is preferable on internal evidence, so nothing is
proposed in the text. This will look like our typo on the reading page and is not one.

### 1103A — *nullius mundi substantiae*
*nam **nullius mundi substantiae**, nullae opes comparatione dilectionis aliquid sunt.*
The genitive singular *nullius mundi* against nominative plural *substantiae* is odd;
rendered as printed ("for the substances of no world, no riches, are anything in
comparison of love") rather than normalized to "no substance of the world". Real words,
so no marker (Pattern 12 boundary). The argument does not turn on it.

### 1105C–1106A — the two *pacifici* constructions
Song 8:12 *Mille tui pacifici* is grammatically ambiguous (predicate adjective vs.
vocative "O peaceable one"), and the work reads it as a predicate: *mille argentei
pacifici tui sunt, id est, manet te certa illa remuneratio*. The lemma is therefore
rendered "A thousand are thy peaceable ones", following the exposition rather than the
commoner vocative reading. Recorded because a reader who knows the verse will expect the
other construction, and because the choice is the work's, not ours.

## Punctuation carried per Pattern 8

### 1102C–D — Rom. 8:35 punctuated as one clause
Migne prints *Quis nos separabit a charitate Christi, tribulatio, an angustia, an
persecutio, an fames, an nuditas, an periculum, an gladius?* — a single comma-run with

Christ, tribulation, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or danger, or

as printed.

### 1102C — the inline citation *(Aggaei II, 24)*

`[n: …]` note, unlike every other citation in the batch. Reproduced verbatim in place,
inside the italics, untranslated. Not tagged `[f: …]`: this is a lemma-and-gloss
commentary, not a florilegium, and Pattern 4's trigger is a citing *practice*, not a
stray instance. Flagged so the indexer's operator knows the reference exists and will not
be harvested from a `[n:]`.

Marked sparingly per Pattern 14. Each of the following was judged not to change the sense

  prints II for IV; Migne's note, left verbatim, no correction proposed.

  Aegyptiorum … ut incederetis erecti*), not Exod. 6:8, and *ergastulum* ("workhouse")
  and *recte* are both the plate's. Rendered as printed; the note is Migne's and stands.

- **1097D, Rom. 9:5.** Printed *Quorum patres, et ex quibus Christus*, an abbreviation of

- **1099D, Luc. 24:44.** Printed *Necesse est impleri omnia quae scripta sunt in Lege, et

## Construction notes

### 1100A — *Tu qui modo es in sinu Patris*
The relative *qui* is printed inside the italic run while *Tu* is outside it
(*Tu* **qui** *modo* **es in sinu Patris**), so the italics fall across a clause boundary
rather than round a quotation. Reproduced as printed: the English sets *who* italic and
*art now in the bosom of the Father* italic, with *Thou* roman. This looks like a
typesetting slip and is carried, not tidied.

### 1100D — *Marc. IX* with no verse
The note attached to *saepe etiam volentes interrogare apostolos Dominus praeveniebat*
gives a chapter only. Left verbatim; no verse supplied.

### 1101B — *S. Gregorius, homil. 30 in Evang.* and 1101C — *S. Greg. in cap. II Cant. cant.*
Two notes citing Gregory in two different abbreviation styles within four columns. Both
left verbatim per the `[n: …]` rule; the variance is evidence of Migne's citing practice
and is not normalized (CLAUDE.md rule 9).

### 1100B–C — the Synagogue called the Church
*Nec mirum videri debet, si nunc Ecclesiam Synagogam dicimus; una est enim Ecclesia in
praecedentibus et in sequentibus partibus.* The work uses *Ecclesia* and *Synagoga* of
the same subject throughout chapter VIII, and says so. Both words kept distinct in the
English wherever the Latin distinguishes them; no clause re-bound to make the referent
easier.

### 1104C — *In ea quae habet populos*
The lemma is printed without the *quae* clause's antecedent (Song 8:11 *in ea quae habet
populos*), and the gloss supplies it (*Ecclesia vero habet populos*). Rendered as printed
— "In that which hath peoples" — rather than expanded to "in the vineyard which…".

### 1105A — the doubled hundred
*Sicut per millenarium plenitudo perfectionis, sic et per centenarium summa perfectio
designatur: quia centenarius de laeva in dexteram transfertur.* The remark about the
hundred being "carried over from the left hand to the right" is the ancient
finger-reckoning convention (a hundred is the first number counted on the right hand).
Rendered literally; no explanatory word added to the text.

### 1096B — *quas Graece βάτια vocant*
The relative *quas* is feminine while its antecedent *canales* is masculine. Pattern 9:
Latin gender has no English exponent, nothing to emend, nothing rendered differently.
Logged only so a later sweep does not churn it.

# Blind polarity read (step 4a) — 2026-07-30

Four Opus units, blind, 23/23 chunks, no sampling. **14 sites: 7 source-side, 7 ours.**
Units in `data/sweeps/polarity-7914/`. Unit boundaries deliberately STRADDLED the
translation batches, so every batch seam fell inside some reader's range.

## The finding that matters beyond this work

**All seven ours-side defects were lemma conformations (5) or mis-bound references (2).
Not one was a dropped negation.** The same day's read of 11065, a homily collection,
gave the opposite profile. **The genre predicts the failure mode.** In a verse-by-verse
commentary the risk is not losing a `non`; it is the familiar English of a known verse
arriving before the printed Latin is read. Written up as `translation-style.md` **7a″**,
with these cases as the worked examples.

## Fixed (ours)

- **@1079C — Heb 12:14.** Migne prints *et **castimoniam**, sine qua nemo videbit Deum*;
  we gave the Vulgate's "holiness" (*sanctimoniam*). Nine words earlier our own English
  renders *castimonia* as "chastity", and the text is adduced **precisely for chastity
  of the flesh** — the argument's keyword had vanished from its own proof-text. → "and
  chastity".
- **@1094A — Heb 11:10.** Printed *Exspectabat enim **manentem** civitatem*; we gave "a
  city that hath **foundations**", importing a word the printed text does not contain.
  → "an abiding city".
- **@1103A — Matt 7:25.** Printed *fuit enim **aedificata** supra petram*; we gave
  "**founded**", six words after rendering *aedificavit* as "built". → "built".
- **@1071D — Heb 2:9.** Printed perfect *Vidimus*; we restored the Vulgate present
  "We see". → "We saw".
- **@1079B — Gen 18:27.** Printed *cinis et pulvis*; we gave Douay's "dust and ashes",
  reversing an order the gloss turns on (cinnamon's *cinericeum colorem*). → "ashes
  and dust".
- **@1102A — speaker.** *Et bene arborem mali **dicit*** rendered "does **she** say".
  The speaker of the lemma *Sub arbore malo suscitavi te* is the bridegroom (*ipsius
  sponsus et dilectus*), and our own next paragraph renders the following *dicit* as
  "he means". The subject does shift to the bride at *comparavit* — *dilectus **meus***
  is hers — but that shift is the author's and belongs to the second verb only. → "does
  he say".
- **@1095D — *contra* smoothed.** *Contra Damascum ergo est haec turris: quia … semper
  … resistit* rendered "is **toward** Damascus", so our own "because … always resists"
  no longer followed. → "is **against** Damascus". **The lemma at 7:4 keeps "which looks
  toward Damascus"** — *respicit contra* is idiomatic facing; the author's gloss exploits
  the adversative sense, and flattening the two would destroy the point he is making.
