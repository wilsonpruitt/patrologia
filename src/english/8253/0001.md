## 6.

Besides the son raised from death by the prayers of Saint Arbogast, Dagobert had several daughters; among whom the chief is Saint Irmina, virgin, first abbess of the monastery of Oeren at Trier, inscribed also in the calendars of the Roman Martyrology at 24 December. She was buried in the monastery of Wissembourg, endowed by the munificence of her father Dagobert, by whom several monasteries were founded or endowed, as we show elsewhere. Another daughter of Dagobert, and granddaughter of Saint Sigebert, is Adela, who after her husband's death, kindled by the example of her sister Irmina, likewise took up the monastic habit, passed her life in the greatest holiness, and presided as abbess over the monastery built at Pfalzel on the bank of the river Moselle; and she named its church heir [0315D] of her goods, the testament being signed on the Kalends of April in the 12th year of King Theuderic — that is, the last — the year of Christ 731. In that testament mention is made of her sister Regentrudis; and, omitting her, others set down Rathildis, or Rothildis, as a third daughter of Dagobert, and would have her married to Ludericus Bucanus, founder of Insulense Castrum in Gallo-Flanders. But this we have not yet found proved by ancient records. This is certain, that Alberic, the son of the said Adela, begot several children, from among whom Saint Gregory was the inseparable companion of Saint Boniface until his death, and afterwards, as a presbyter, presided over the see of Utrecht in the bishop's stead; whose life Saint Ludger, his disciple, wrote. Whether from one of Saint Gregory's brothers was descended Saint Alberic, afterwards bishop of Utrecht and so named from his grandfather's name, we inquire elsewhere. Saint Gregory is venerated on 25 August, and Saint Alberic on 14 November. Whether he who is venerated as Saint Dagobert the martyr among the people of Stenay in Lorraine was king of the Franks and [0316A] the son of Saint Sigebert, we do not thus far, in our judgment, dare to determine.

## § II. *Monasteries built and endowed.*



## 1.

The author in the Acts of Saint Sigebert reports twelve monasteries built; which Gelenius attributes to the Ardennes alone; twenty built in all, with Gelenius, Molanus, in the *Auctarium* to Usuard, Galesinius, and Saussay would have it — whose words we have given above. Brouwer writes thus of them in his *Metropolis*, not yet published: « In the year 651, Sigebert, king of the Austrasians, at Metz in his royal seat, flourished by the singular innocence of his life and by outstanding liberality toward every kind of religious, with twenty monasteries founded and built on no common scale and enriched with perpetual revenues here and there, among which were Malmundariu II and Stavelot within the bounds of the Ardennes, and Saint Martin's in the suburb of Metz. » So Brouwer; but the remaining monasteries neither he nor others name. Of the one at Metz, founded and [0316B] destroyed, we shall treat below. At the Life of Saint Amandus, 6 February, we shall relate that among the Austrasians, while Saint Sigebert the king was set over them, many monasteries were built in the Belgic and Germanic provinces, which we here omit to enumerate. Before Stavelot and Malmedy, another was erected at Casaeconguduni in the duchy of Luxembourg on the river Sesmarus, or Sesomiris, commonly *Sesmoy* or *Semoy*, between the towns of Chiny and Bouillon: of which in later ages there existed a priory in the district of Cougnon. Of it we shall treat more fully on 3 February, at the Life of Saint Hadelin. We give below [n: (Col. 319, dipl. I)] a diploma of Saint Sigebert communicated to us by that most courteous man Jean Antoine de Gournay, presbyter of the Congregation of the Oratory, from which it is established that Saint Remaclus was appointed abbot when he was not yet bishop of Maastricht, while Godo — by others Dodo, or Godeo — bishop of Metz, was still surviving, whom Saint Chlodulf, son of Saint Arnulf, succeeded, to which bishop [0316C] Remaclus in the first year of his episcopate sent Saint Trudo. Hence Bouchet is excellently refuted, who, in part I of the *Origins of the Royal Family of the Franks*, chapter 6, reckons the period of Godo's episcopate from the year 659 to 667, and judges that Saint Chlodulf, put in his place, lived until the year 707. In the diploma there is named, besides the Sesomiris, the river Alsnia, which rises above Luxembourg, washes the city, and, mingled with the Atarto, discharges its water into the Sûre, and thence glides down into the Moselle. And the wood Uriacuise would have reached to the village of Ures below Bouillon; or rather Viracuise is to be read, and then it would be referred to the neighboring river Vire. These things having been set out beforehand, the diploma will be more easily understood.

## 2.

Notger, bishop of Liège, in the Life of Saint Remaclus, bishop of Maastricht, 3 September, makes mention of another Life composed by an older and almost contemporary author, concerning which he writes thus in the prologue to Abbot Werenfrid: « You have offered a little book on the Life of the special patron both of ours and of yours, that is, of the lord [0316D] Remaclus, complaining that, owing to the negligence of your predecessors, it had been treated more briefly than the matter required for the greatness of his deeds; and at the same time you seemed — that I may not say to entreat, but rather to exhort — that I should bid it be not merely copied out, but polished somewhat more elegantly. » In this earlier little book, or Life of Saint Remaclus, we read thus concerning the building of the monasteries of Stavelot and Malmedy: « It came to pass, by God's favor, that it was ordered by the pious princes of the kingdom of the Franks, King Sigebert and Duke Grimoald, by the will of God and the counsel of their magnates, that monasteries should be built within the forest, situated in the district which is called the Ardennes, named Stavelot and Malmedy, in which religious monks should dwell together, who might there spiritually serve Christ; and might beseech the almighty Lord for the state of the whole kingdom, and for the safety of the king and of his sons, or of those exercising the care of the kingdom. Then they began to build more earnestly, and, as is wont in such a work, the divine power [0317A] followed after, and with prosperous success they were swiftly completed. And when the time was at hand that the churches, adorned with new material, should be dedicated, the aforesaid king, sending to the blessed bishop Remaclus — because one of them, that is, Stavelot, belonged to his diocese, but the other, that is, Malmedy, since it looked to the metropolitan see, with the consent of Cunibert, who was metropolitan of Cologne — summoned him, that, as befitted his ministry [ *Al.,* mysterio], he might dedicate them. And that most holy man, obeying at once, with great diligence completed the work enjoined upon him, which had long since been carried out by his own arrangement; because, since by reason of his very great sanctity he was close to the king and to the chief men of the palace and beloved by them, nothing of importance was done without his counsel. Whence it came about, since he was held so dear by them, that when the solemnities of the Masses had been performed, the aforesaid mayor of the palace Grimoald handed over to him the said places, that all things should be ordered under his jurisdiction, and that the institutes of the monastic life should be kept there. But after no long time he at last obtained from the king that, leaving behind [0317B] a successor after him in the pontifical see, as he had long desired, he might go to this place of the desert, and there, removed from men, be free for God alone . . . . And so remaining with unsleeping care, without any reproach, in the monastic disciplines, he converted the minds of many noble men, so that they became imitators of that life whose teaching was [0318A] perfect: because what he taught with his mouth, he took care first to fulfill in deed. Whence it came about that, as religion grew, the revenue of that same church also grew, for the uses of the servants of God serving there. For the aforesaid most pious princes, hearing of the practice of religion of this kind, summoning the blessed man, handed over to him from that same forest twelve leagues in length, and likewise in breadth, within which space there should be no gainsayer of his jurisdiction or of those succeeding him, so that they might quietly serve God as soldiers. Moreover they confirmed it by a testament with the imperial seals, that it might endure stable for all time: which the most holy Father received gladly and held reverently. But not long after he took pains to shorten that same space, that it might be without hindrance to those who followed, and that the rulers of the place might be able to keep it without any obstacle — which down to our own time appears to endure altogether unshaken. » So far that. Now the monasteries of Stavelot and Malmedy lie about o to hours' leagues distant from Liège, in the direction in which one goes eastward and toward Trier. The building [0318B] of both was begun about the year of Christ 655, which was the sixth of the episcopate of Saint Remaclus; but the diplomas were granted toward the end of the life of the same King Sigebert.
