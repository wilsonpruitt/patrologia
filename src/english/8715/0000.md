## I LETTER TO POPE LEO III.

[1161A]

To the excellent and orthodox great man, crowned by God, the gem of priests, to Leo the bishop, happily governing the highest and golden Roman see with glory and all honor: that most worthless servant of all the servants of God — we beg salvation and glory for the unfading crown of your glory and blessedness, and humbly, as though before your most holy feet [ *Forte,* humi prostratus], we pray for your glory and favor, and with insistent entreaty we beg your most holy service; because, so far as the chosen Son of God has deigned to grant us strength, we desire to remain in your service, as is worthy and just toward so truly angelic a servant of God. For we, your most humble [1161B] little servants, have resolved, with the Lord's help, to journey to Rome for the sake of prayer, and to greet his doors with devout kisses [ *F.,* pio osculo . . . ostia]; and to set kisses with warm cheeks upon the pavement before most holy *Peter*, and to ask pardon and indulgence for our [past] offenses. But now your kindliness knows that, on account of the great distance of our land and the hindrance of the pagans, we have by no means been able to come here on the day on which we had resolved [habemus] to attend, as the great necessity of our frailty demanded [ *Cod. Sal.,* expetit] of us. Therefore we beseech your holy clemency to be willing to ask pardon for us at his most blessed confession [ *Cod. Sal.,* rogare delictis], so that through your [1162A] intercession the merciful Lord may deign to grant us some little release from the chains of our offenses. But we are not worthy to render you service in any matter. We know that from God and from Saint Peter you will obtain a recompense both in this world and, in the world to come, a wage and glory. May the kindly Trinity and inseparable Unity guard the lord Leo, the highest priest, now and always and unto eternity, happily and with the glory of God.

## II. *449* ANONYMOUS POEM FOR THE POET'S FRIENDS.

Little letter, go quickly, in your course across the level sea;
with the winds behind you, make for the strong mouths of the fish-rich Rhine,
entering there where it rolls into the deep with rushing waves;
[1162B]then let your prow be drawn by a very long towrope,
and let the ship not be swept swiftly backward by the river.
If my Albric should come and meet you upon the stream,
say to him in haste: hail, cattle-rich prelate.
For prior Hadda serves you, for no more than a single night
at Traject., honey, and porridge, and butter,
inasmuch as Frisia pours out neither oil nor wine.
From here lift your sails; in your flight leave Dorsdrada behind;
perhaps black Hrotberct prepares no hospitable roof for you;
see, the greedy merchant has no love for your song;
but turn aside, you, to the shores of my poet Jonah:
for there is sure rest for the weary who come thither.
Here herbs abound for guests, here fish, here bread.
The city of Cologne kindly opens her roofs to you:
[1163A]here greet Father Ricwulf with a humble voice;
say: Your praise, beloved, will remain with me always.
Hence you will make for the forts, your ship running through the waves,
until you reach the calm waters of the longed-for Moselle.
After you have furrowed the river's stretch with rowing,
then halt your craft here, and let the stern take the sand,
and seek on foot the holy places of Father Willibrord;
and search out the roof of Samuel the priest;
remember to knock at the doors with a Castalian quill,
and steadily say to the boy who serves, in a Pythian voice:
Publius Albinus sent me from the British world,
to bring a sweet greeting to a most sweet Father.
If the chance of a word in his presence be granted you,
poured out upon the ground as a suppliant, kiss his consecrated feet,
and say: Fare well, father Samuel; say: Live long, priest.
[1163B]Uncover your lap now, and bring forth the honorable fathers
Priscian and Phocas — and he delights in such a gift,
if only Neptune has not drowned them in the sea.
If perchance he should wish to lead you to the king's hall,
here run through and greet the nobles, the fathers, the brothers.
Before the king's feet spread out all your Muses;
say many times over: Hail, best of kings, hail:
be present to me as protector, guardian, defender,
that no envious tongue may have power to tear at me with its harm —
of Paulinus, of Peter, of Albric, of Samuel, of Jonah,
or of whoever may wish to gnaw my vitals with his bite:
let him flee far off at your terrifying, let him depart with nothing.
Say with a hushed murmur: Fare well, Peter my teacher —
that fierce one strikes with a Herculean club: beware.
Rejoicing, embrace the gifts of master Paulinus,
[1163C]and ten times take coaxing kisses from his honey-bearing lips.
Duly greet Ricwulf, Raefgot, and Rado,
and touch their ears little by little with your singing.
Say: Companions, brothers, joyful ones, hail and fare well.
Perhaps you will come to the distinguished city of Mainz,
and there say an everlasting farewell to Lull the teacher:
he is held the very model of the Church, the splendor of wisdom,
in character and in life fully worthy of so great an honor.
O Bassinus, glory of the good people of Speyer,
[1164A]commend me, I beg, kindly Father, to Paul my patron,
whose kindly house had made us both brothers.
Who, devout Fulcrad, will dare to touch you with the lyric quill?
By your merits you surpass the songs of the Muses.
*450* Yet now allow this ivy to creep about your consecrated [ *F.,* sacra] brows,
most high Father, and out of your accustomed goodness [bonitate sueta]
permit me at least once to say hail to you.
Come, on with it, O paper [charta], board the ready ship at once;
let the curved keel swim beneath you the Ocean and the Rhine.
Let no tawny stuff of gold, I beg, hold you back,
which the weary settler brings forth from the bowels of the earth;
let no forts, houses, cities, nor flowering fields
[1164B]detain you, dazed, for the space of even a single hour;
but flee, break off all delay, hasten, run your course in flight.
Safe, sound, rejoicing and thriving,
may you find our friends, and gladly.
May almighty God grant them salvation through the ages,
and afterward lead them, glad, into the heavenly hall.
When all this is done, return sure of your way to your homeland,
and remember to tell what each one says to you;
and when in spring the red buds . . . . . out of the bark
burst forth, may they see you at play beneath our roof,
and bringing us new songs once again.
Then I will fashion for you golden garlands of fresh flowers,
and you, joined with me, will rest in pleasant meadows.

## IV. *456* VERSES ON THE CELL OF CORMERY.

O my cell, dwelling sweet and beloved to me,
forever and unto eternity, O my cell, farewell!
[1165A]On every side the trees encircle you with resounding boughs,
a little wood ever laden with flower-bearing leaves.
All the meadows too will bloom with health-bringing herbs,
which the physician's right hand seeks for the aid [ *Ed.,* ore] of health.
Rivers encircle you with flowering banks on every side,
where the fisherman exulting spreads his nets.
Your cloisters are fragrant with fruit-laden boughs throughout the gardens,
white lilies mingled with little red roses.
Every kind of bird sounds out its morning songs,
and praises God the Creator with its voice.
In you there once resounded the kindly voice of the master,
which with holy mouth handed on the books of wisdom.
In you, at appointed seasons, the holy praise of the Thunderer
sounded with peaceful [ *Ed.* pacificos] voices and minds.
[1165B]You, my cell, I now bewail with tearful songs,
and groaning I lament your downfall from my heart.
For you have suddenly fled the songs of poets [ *Edit.,* fatum; *Mabill.,* fato],
and now a wholly unknown hand holds you.
Now neither Flaccus nor Homer the bard [ *Ed.,* fatis] will have you,
nor do boys sing the Muses beneath your roof.
For thus all the glory of the age is turned about of a sudden;
all things are changed in varied successions.
Nothing remains eternal, nothing is truly unchangeable;
shadowy night darkens the holy day.
Cold winter suddenly strikes down the beautiful flowers,
and a sadder wind troubles the peaceful sea.
[1165C]The holy youth that used to drive the stags across the fields
now leans upon a staff, an old man and weary.
Wretched are we! Why do we love you, O world, you fugitive?
You flee from us, ever rushing headlong everywhere.
Flee then, you that flee; let us love Christ always,
let the love of God hold our hearts always.
May he in his mercy defend his servant [ *F.,* pios famulos] from the dread foe,
snatching up to heaven our hearts, which are his own;
and let us with our whole heart alike praise him and love him:
he in his mercy is our glory, our life, our salvation.
