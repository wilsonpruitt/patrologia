## HERE BEGINS THE CANTICLE OF HABAKKUK THE PROPHET. (cont.)

« Sprinkling waters in his journeys, the deep gave its voice from the height of its imagination. » [1124C] The same doctors who above are figured by the name of rivers are figured also by the name of the deep. But they are rivers because of the force of a stronger invective, wherewith they cut through the hardness of the earthly mind to do penance for its errors. But they are rightly called a deep from the height of the knowledge wherewith they themselves are filled within, Solomon bearing witness, who says: « The words of a man's mouth are deep water [n: (Prov. XVIII)]. » Sprinkling waters, therefore, in the journeys, the deep gives its voice, when holy preachers, filled within in heart with the deep knowledge of truth, set forth the ministry of the word outwardly to their hearers, bringing it out little by little and in parts, according to the capacity of the weak, which they themselves inwardly take in both many and ample at once. And by "in the journeys" he means "in the works," either of the doctors themselves, namely, or of their hearers. For they sprinkle waters [1124D] in their own journeys, when wherever they go they always show to those who look on examples of right living together with the voice of preaching. And they sprinkle waters also in the journeys of those who look on, when by teaching and by living alike they show them beforehand by what ways of action they ought to walk. And well, when he had said that the deep gave its voice, did he add "from the height of its imagination"; because that which the holy preachers utter marvellously without proceeds assuredly from that fountain of wisdom wherewith they themselves within are more marvellously enlightened by the contemplation of heavenly joys. Or did not the great deep give its voice from the height of its imagination, when Paul the apostle said: « Our mouth is open to you, O ye Corinthians, our heart is enlarged; you are not straitened in [1125A] us, but you are straitened in your own bowels; I speak as to my children: be you also enlarged. But whence, or in what order, it came to pass that the world should be sprinkled with saving waters, and that the deep of heavenly wisdom should thunder upon the lands, is shown in what follows, when it is said:

« The sun was lifted up, and the moon stood in its order. » For the Lord — the sun of justice, namely — was lifted up into heaven after his passion and resurrection, and, the Spirit having been sent by the Father, he enlightened the Church and spread her abroad through the whole world, that she in her own order — that is, after the secret of the Lord's incarnation had been fulfilled — rising from the long languor of unbelief, might stand in faith, act manfully, and be strengthened in his charity. And because he had compared Christ to the sun and the Church to the moon, [1125B] fitly, turning to that same sun, he straightway subjoined:

« In the light thy darts shall go, in the brightness of the flashing of thy arms. » Now the darts of Christ are his words, wherewith the hearts of men are pricked, so that, a saving wound being inflicted, the faithful soul may say, I am wounded with charity: which darts, namely, go in the light, because through the ministry of the doctors the words of truth became openly known to the world, according to that which the Truth itself commanded them, saying: « The things which I tell you in the dark, speak ye in the light; and that which you hear in the ear, preach ye upon the housetops [n: (Luc. XII)]; » and since the brightness of miracles also followed the utterances of light, he added, in the brightness of the flashing of thy arms; for with darts men at war strike their adversaries, [1125C] with arms, by fortifying themselves, they descend from a wound. Whence by darts are fittingly intimated the sayings of preachers, wherewith they overcome the depravity of unbelievers, and by arms the miracles wherewith they confirm the truth of their preaching. The darts of Christ, therefore, go in the light; they go in the brightness of the flashing of his arms: because the mighty works which he did, the secrets which he opened, the commandments which he set forth, the rewards which he promised, have now become known to the whole world through the evangelical writings more clearly than the sun. And the holy doctors also, because they are sons of light, whatsoever by his gift they do or speak shines out bright with light and brightness.

« In thy threatening thou wilt make the earth less, and in thy fury. » Thou wilt drag down the nations by threatening the strictness of the judgment whereby the impious are condemned; thou wilt wholesomely humble [1125D] those who were wont to set earthly things before heavenly, so that, earthly desires being little by little made less, they may begin to relish and to seek the things that are above; and by bringing in [sic: *fororem*] thou wilt condemn for ever those who, for the sake of their eternal exaltation, scorned to be humbled for a time.

« Thou wentest forth for the salvation of thy people, that thou mightest save thy christs. » The mediator of God and men went forth from the Father and came into the world, not that he might judge the world, but that the world might be saved by him. And by christs he means all the elect, because from the unction of spiritual grace they are most rightly named by this word. Whence is that word of the Psalmist concerning those who, wishing to hurt the saints, were restrained [1126A] by a divine prohibition. And he reproved kings for their sakes: « Touch ye not my christs [n: (Psal. CIV)]. » And he saved his christs — not those whom he found christs, but those whom, by going forth from the Father and appearing in the flesh, he made christs, that is, his anointed ones, through the Spirit of adoption. Of which unction John the apostle, admonishing his hearers, says: « And as for you, let the unction which you have received from him abide in you [n: (I Joan. II)]. »

« Thou hast sent death upon the heads of thine enemies; thou hast stirred up the bonds even unto the necks. » For the Lord slays sin, sending death upon the heads of his enemies, that he may give life unto justice. I will say something more boldly: Christ came into the world for this, that he might send death upon the heads of his enemies, and that, as he himself died once to sin, so they too might die to iniquity, and that they who [1126B] had been made partakers of death might be made partakers also of this life. The Lord raised up the bonds of charity, that, the former burden being laid down and the heaviest yoke wherewith they were pressed being cast off, we might take up the light yoke of Christ, so that, set with them in the chariot, we might carry the best of charioteers. Which can also be understood otherwise: for he who offered the joy of salvation to his elect, whom he had called christs through the Prophet, himself sent eternal death upon those who neglected to be anointed with his grace; which is agreed to have been fulfilled even bodily in that very nation of the Jews, which persecuted the Lord appearing in the flesh even unto death: for after it had crucified him, not many years intervening, when the Roman army pressed on — only those excepted who had separated themselves unto the faith of the evangelical grace — [1126C] it was condemned with a monstrous slaughter, and was moreover itself deprived of kingdom and country. And this is what he says: « Thou hast stirred up the bonds even unto the neck » — the neck, namely, of the kingdom, wherewith they had been raised up against the Lord; of which the most blessed protomartyr Stephen says to those same men, raging even against himself: « Stiffnecked and uncircumcised in hearts and ears, you always resist the Holy Spirit [n: (Act. V)]. » But even unto this neck did the Lord stir up the bonds, when he sent a hostile army for the overthrow of that proud nation. Whence it is written: « The just Lord shall cut the necks of sinners [n: (Psal. CXXVIII)]. »

« Thou hast cursed his sceptres, the heads of his warriors, coming as a whirlwind to scatter me. Their exultation is as of him that devoureth the poor man in secret. Thou hast made a way in the sea for thy horses, in the [1126D] mire of many waters. I have heard, and my belly was troubled; at the voice my lips trembled. Let rottenness enter into my bones, and swarm beneath me, that I may rest in the day of my tribulation, that I may go up to our girded people. » And now I have set down the Hebrew only, that we may discuss the edition of the LXX separately; for the LXX disagrees much with the translation of all the rest. Thou hast cursed the sceptres, that is, his kingdoms — there is no doubt but that they are the impious one's, of whom he had said above, thou hast struck the head from the house of the impious, thou hast laid bare the foundation even to the neck. But we understand the impious one to be either Nebuchadnezzar or every adversary of the people of God; and not only his sceptres, but also the heads of the warriors [1127A] whom he had struck, who came, he says, as a whirlwind, that they might scatter me — that is, that they might overturn Israel and lead it captive into divers places — so that they exulted, devouring the poor man and Israel subjected to them, as though they did this in secret, and devoured us unawares. Thou camest therefore to battle for thy people, and, sending thy four-horse cars into the waters, that is, into many nations, thou madest them a way in the mire of many waters, that is, that thou mightest trample them down and grind them like mire with the hoof and the wheel of thy horses and thy chariot. But as to what follows, « I have heard, and my belly was troubled at the voice, » it is to be understood thus: my lips trembled; let rottenness enter into my bones and let them swarm beneath me, that I may rest in the day of tribulation, that I may go up to our girded [1127B] people. This is the sense: now we all willingly suffer straits, and at thy threatening we tremble with all our bowels; now my lips tremble, and the dread of a trembling mind is marked upon my mouth; and not only this, but of my own accord I seek and of my own accord I desire yet another thing: let rottenness enter into my bones and swarm above me — that is, I willingly suffer what Job suffered; I desire that not only my flesh but the marrow of my bones also should waste away, and that my bed should swarm with the rottenness of the body and with worms without number, so that, after I shall have borne these things here for my sins, I may rest in the bitter day, in the day of tribulation, in the day of need and of straitness; and that I may go up to our girded people, that is, to a strong warrior and a fighter. And beautifully, "I shall go up," [1127C] for one always goes up to a girded people; and elegantly, "our," for he who has been afflicted and has willingly borne oppressions, and has weighed the rewards to come against present evils, boldly says "our", so that, like Abraham and Isaac and Jacob, he too may sleep in a good old age, full of days, and be laid unto his fathers. Another edition:

« Thou hast cut off in alienation the heads of the mighty; the nations shall be moved therein. » For "alienation" it is written in the Greek « in ecstasy, » which some have interpreted « in stupor, » others « in an excess of mind »: but whether it be called stupor, or alienation, or excess of mind, it signifies one and the same thing — when a man, troubled and stupefied by a sudden marvel, is rendered a stranger to the sense of his own mind. And that this befell the Jews [1127D] the frequent history of the Gospel declares, saying that they were stupefied and wondered at the doctrine and the mighty works of Jesus, saying: « Whence hath this man all these things, whose father and mother we know [n: (Joan. VI)]? » And in the Acts of the apostles, when the lame man at the gate of the temple had been healed by Peter and John: « They were filled, he says, with stupor and ecstasy [n: (Act. III)]; » in which ecstasy, that is, in wonder or alienation of mind, many of the people were provoked to believe in the Lord; but the heads of the mighty, that is, the chief priests and the elders, were cut off from the lot of the faithful by not believing. The nations also were moved therein, when, having heard or seen the mighty works of the Lord and of his apostles, they were so stupefied and amazed that, anathematizing [1128A] and casting away those whom they had worshipped as gods, they received with devout mind the new faith of Christ.
