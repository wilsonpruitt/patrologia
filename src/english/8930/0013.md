## HERE BEGINS THE CANTICLE OF HABAKKUK THE PROPHET. (cont.)

« They shall open wide their mouths as a poor man eating in secret. » For as a poor man who has remained fasting for some while, if perchance he finds food anywhere, busies himself to refresh himself with it at once in secret, and will not bring it forth in public, lest perhaps it be snatched away by another and he himself perish of hunger: so assuredly, so did the peoples of the nations, when the bread of the word was offered them through the apostles — from which they had continued fasting a longer while — straightway open the mouth of their heart, and begin to taste it with all avidity. And in what order the poverty of the nations came to receive the banquets of the word is intimated, when it is straightway added:

« Thou hast sent into the sea horses troubling many waters. » [1128B] For by the sea he means the world; by the horses of God, the holy preachers; by the many waters, the peoples of the nations. And when the horses of God were sent into the sea, many waters were troubled, because when the heralds of the word were scattered into the world the hearts of the nations were troubled — some to believe and to receive the sacrament of faith, but others to gainsay, or even to persecute the heralds of that same faith. And well is it said above of these horses, namely of the holy preachers, Because ascending thou wilt ascend upon thy horses; and now of the same it is added, Thou hast sent thy horses into the sea; so that from both sentences it may be gathered that the Lord so sent preachers into the world that he himself is never absent from those same preachers, but, as a charioteer over his horses, so is he always set over their minds to govern them.

[1128C] « My belly kept watch and was terrified at the voice of the prayer of their lips. » By his belly, after the manner customary to the prophets, he means his own mind; because as by the belly food is received whereby the strength and life of the body is restored, so pious thoughts are received in the holy mind whereby the life of the inner man, lest it should fail, is sustained and held together. I kept watch, he says, over myself the more carefully with trembling mind, lest perchance I should sin in heart, and lest, preaching to others, I myself should be made a reprobate. And it is to be noted that he says he was terrified at the voice of the prayer of his own lips, whereas he seems to have prayed nothing at all in this whole song, but only, fearing and quaking, to describe the sacraments of Christ and of the Church that were to come. Nor yet is he deceived who calls his song a prayer, who also set over it [1128D] such a title, The prayer of Habakkuk the prophet for ignorances; because whatsoever a holy man speaks, all this assuredly is a prayer to God: everything that you do, whose sincere intention is to please the Lord, this intercedes for him with the Lord, and commends him to the Lord.

« And trembling entered into my bones. » Just as [sic: *Sicu*] by the name of flesh Scripture sometimes designates our carnal actions, so by the word of bones it is wont to designate strong and spiritual deeds. My heart therefore was terrified, he says, at these things which I foresee as coming into the world; and whatsoever I thought there was of spiritual strength in me, all this trembled as a frail thing, while I look upon the greater strengths and passions of Christ and of the blessed apostles — [1129A] which very thing is more manifestly explained by the following word, when it is said:

« And beneath me my strength was troubled. » And well does he say that his strength was troubled not in himself but beneath himself, because the prophet, caught up to the contemplation of heavenly secrets, saw himself in a manner lifted above himself; and the loftier he is made by the light of contemplation, the more does he behold his own imperfection: for being raised up to gaze upon things above, he is rightly troubled concerning the things which he had done among the lowest. And the strength of the prophet was troubled, his bones trembled, his belly was terrified, not only because he knew himself less perfect in action, but also because he had learned that all who would live godly in Christ should suffer persecutions. And he saw that Christ himself, who should enter into the world without sin, [1129B] should not go forth from the world without the punishment of sin.

« I shall rest in the day of my tribulation, that I may go up to the people of my transmigration. » For he rests in the day not only of retribution but also of tribulation, because he doubts not that through temporal afflictions he shall increase his eternal joys, according to that word of the Apostle: « For by hope we are saved [n: (Rom. VIII)]. » And again: « Rejoicing in hope, patient in tribulation [n: (Rom. X)]. » And this is the rest of the elect in this life: that, the appetite of the lowest things being left behind, with the whole intention of the mind and with the daily steps of good works they may strive to ascend and to pass over to the fellowship of those who had gone before them in Christ. But even before that last and general judgment the saints rest in the day of tribulation, ascending [1129C] to the people of their transmigration, when after good works, being translated from the world, they are joined in the heavens to the joys of the just who went before, while their persecutors, likewise taken away from this life, undergo the torments of gehenna, to be afflicted perpetually. And the day of tribulation can be understood also in this life, when, the penury of temporal goods growing heavy, they who had loved such things grieve exceedingly, as though surrounded with miseries; but every elect man, though bearing those same discomforts bodily, with the hope of his mind fixed in the Lord, has rest, knowing that the more heavily he is pressed down among the lowest things, the more loftily after his oppressions he ascends to the eternal fellowships of the citizens above. To which sense that which follows fitly agrees:

« For the fig tree shall not blossom, and there shall be no bud in [1129D] the vines; the work of the olive shall lie, and the fields shall bring no food; the flock shall be cut off from the fold, and there shall be no herd in the stalls. » LXX: « Because the fig tree bringeth not forth fruit, and there shall be no generation in the vines; the work of the olive shall lie, and the fields make no meats; the sheep have failed from meat, and there shall be no oxen in the stall. » According to the Hebrew, in which we said above, let rottenness enter into my bones and swarm beneath me, that I may rest in the day of tribulation, that I may go up to my girded people, thus will the things that went before be joined to those that follow: therefore have I willed to bear tribulation for the present, and afterwards to go up to the strong people, because the day of tribulation and of need shall come; and while the rest are set in straitness, I shall rejoice in thy [1130A] majesty: the fig tree shall not blossom, and there shall be no bud in the vines; the work of the olive shall lie, and the fields shall bring no food, and the rest — which, because they differ not much from the LXX, let us discuss together with their interpretation. When the day of tribulation shall have come, and I shall have gone up to the people which once went on pilgrimage with me — or assuredly, when the day of the overthrow of the Jewish nation and of the former people shall have come, and the daughter of Sion shall have been left as a tabernacle in a vineyard, and as a lodge in a cucumber field, and as a city that is stormed — while that one indeed perishes I have been chosen for the people of which it is said: « And unless the Lord had left us seed, we had been as Sodom, and we should have been like unto Gomorrha [n: (Rom. IX)]. » I will join myself to the disciples of Christ, whom, because he teaches them on the mountain, the crowds and the feeble [1130B] being left below, I too will climb up to the mountains. But these verses, if anyone ask what they figuratively signify, can be expounded thus: the fig tree, the vine and the olive were the synagogue of the Jews, when, devoted to God, it brought forth the sweetness of good working, the blazing of fervent love, the fatness of a merciful mind. Sheep and oxen were typical in that same people: sheep, namely, in those who humbly heard the voice of the chief shepherd; but oxen in those who, diligently bearing the yoke of the law to bring forth the fruits of good works, prepared the hearts of their hearers by sedulously teaching and chastising them, as by ploughing the land of the Lord; and to these, living spiritually, the most broad fields of the divine Scriptures made spiritual meats, in whose pasture he delighted who had been made as a beast of burden before God [1130C] and always clung to him, saying: « The Lord ruleth me, and I shall want nothing: in a place of pasture, there hath he set me [n: (Psal. XXII)]. » But this fig tree, when the Lord came to it a third time — that is, in the giving of the law through Moses, in sedulous rebuke and exhortation through the prophets, in the offering of grace through himself — neglected to bear the fruit of virtue; for which cause, at his curse, it was condemned with everlasting dryness. The generation failed in the vines that were once the Lord's, that is, the fruit of charity in the multitudes of the Jews; for which cause they offered him vinegar for wine when he thirsted — that is, to him who sought in them the sweetness of the virtues they brought forth the sharpness of vices. The work of the olive lied, when that same people fattened the heads of wretched men with the oil of flattery, and re-echoed with a false mouth the truthful things drawn from the prophet, saying: « But I, [1130D] as a fruitful olive tree in the house of God, have hoped in the mercy of God [n: (Psal. LI)]. For which cause, at the time of the last retribution, being about to bring lamps extinguished, it is to be shut out with its own darkness from the entrance of the heavenly country. The fields make no meats, when that same people, though the pages of the divine letters lie open, is not able, by understanding rightly, to find the pastures of truth. The sheep fail from meat, because for those from whom the refreshment of inward sweetness is absent, there is nothing whence the innocence of a simple life may come forth. And thus it is said, the sheep have failed from meat, that is, because meat was lacking — as also the Prophet in the psalm: « And my flesh, he says, is changed for oil [n: (Psal. CVIII)], » that is, because there was no oil wherewith I might be refreshed or anointed. Finally certain codices [1131A] have it thus, they failed, because the sheep did not eat: there are no oxen in the stall, since indeed among the Jews the stalls of the heavenly letters abound, but because they do not relish in them the fodder of heavenly understanding, those who would bear the sweet yoke of the Gospel are wanting. All which things the Prophet, considering that they should come upon the faithless part of his own nation, straightway shows what he himself would do together with the faithful of that same nation — nay rather, together with the fellowship of the whole Church, which was to be gathered throughout the world in Christ, or had been gathered — saying:

« But I will rejoice in the Lord, and I will exult in God my Jesus. » Another edition: « But I will glory in the Lord, I will rejoice in God my Jesus. » But I, he says, will glory not in my own justice, but in the faith of the divine protection; I will rejoice in God my Jesus, [1131B] I who weigh that salvation is not mine in me, but is in him. And, as though we should ask of him why he gloried in the Lord and in God Jesus — whom by the grace of a great love he called his own — and why he rejoiced, straightway, intimating as it were the most just cause of that same joy, he thus ended his song.
