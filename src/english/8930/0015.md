## HERE BEGINS THE CANTICLE OF THE BOOK OF DEUTERONOMY.

It is read in Deuteronomy that Moses, after he had written out the words of the law in a volume and had finished it, [1132C] spoke, in the hearing of the whole assembly of Israel, the words of this song, and completed it even to the end. Whence it is given us to infer that this song, which Moses sang for a testimony to the children of Israel, does not sound a simple history only, but is most full of spiritual understanding. For in part, according to the history, it reproaches the treachery of the Israelites in return for the benefits most richly conferred upon them by the Lord; in part, through allegory, it instructs the holy Church of God concerning the heavenly mysteries; in part, moreover, it calls forth every one of the faithful to uprightness of morals through tropology. Whence it befits a sober reader that in the history he keep the truth of Scripture; but in the allegory let him consider the reason of the heavenly mysteries, and in the tropology let him learn thoroughly the usefulness of good disciplines, so that, [1132D] taught by this threefold distinction, he may be carried forward to that heavenly theoria — that is, to the inspection of hidden things and to the sublimity of divine contemplation. And this too must be diligently looked into, that Moses is said to speak the words of the song and to have completed it even to the end — whether perhaps this admonishes us that he who is zealous to praise God with his mouth should not contradict it by his works: for praise is not comely in the mouth of a sinner. Therefore the Lord himself says in the Gospel - « He that shall break one of these least commandments and shall so teach men, shall be called least in the kingdom of heaven; but he that shall do and teach, he shall be called great in the kingdom of heaven [n: (Matth. XIX)]. » Now the beginning of this song is as follows:

[1133A] « Hear, O heavens, the things that I shall speak; let the earth hear the words of my mouth. » According to the opening of the prophet Isaiah it is thus written: « Hear, O heaven, and give ear, O earth, for the Lord hath spoken [n: (Isa. I)]. Above, therefore, in the same book, it has been shown by the title who the Prophet is, whose son, who [prophesied] against Juda and Jerusalem, or through Juda and Jerusalem, and at what time he saw; now he calls heaven and earth to hear, signifying by heaven the supernal and angelic powers, by earth the race of mortals, showing [sic: *metonymic ω s*] from those things which contain the things which are contained. For since through Moses the Lord had called heaven and earth as witnesses when he gave his law to the people of Israel, and had said: Give heed, O heaven, and I will speak; let the earth hear the words of my mouth, » after the people's transgression he calls the same again to testimony, [1133B] that all the elements may know that God was justly stirred to wrath in the avenging of his commandments.

« Let my doctrine gather thick into rain, let my speech flow as the dew, as a shower upon the herb and as drops upon the grasses, for I will call upon the name of the Lord. » Another edition: « Let my speech be looked for as the rain, and let my words come down as the dew, as a shower upon the sprout, and as snow upon the hay, for I have called upon the name of the Lord. » He compares the speech of the law of God to rain and to dew, because it seeks from the hearts of the faithful the sprouts of spiritual fruits; for these are the vineyard of the Lord, from which he seeks a fruit lovely to himself, that is, the fruit of good works. A comparison like to this we find in the prophet Isaiah: « As, he says, the shower and the snow come down from heaven and return no more [1133C] thither, but water the earth and drench it, and make it to spring, and give seed to him that beareth [var: Vulg. *serenti*, "to him that soweth"] and bread to him that eateth: so shall my word be, which shall go forth out of my mouth; it shall not return to me void, but shall do whatsoever I have willed, and shall prosper in those things for which I sent it [n: (Isa. LV)]. » But because Moses said that his doctrine gathers thick into rain, in order that he might plainly show what this rain was, he subjoined, saying, « for I will call upon the name of the Lord. » To call upon the name of the Lord, therefore, is to preach the Gospel of Christ; and he who hears it and does it will without doubt possess in the heavens the fruit of a good work.

« Give magnificence to our God; the works of God are perfect, and all his ways are judgments. » Another edition: « Give greatness to our God. God — true are his works, [1133D] and all his ways are judgments. » To magnify the Lord is to preach the majesty of the Saviour with a believing heart and a devout mouth, together with good deeds. Whence Mary the mother of the Lord says in the canticle of the Gospel: « My soul doth magnify the Lord, and my spirit hath rejoiced in God my Saviour [n: (Luc. I)]. » « The works, he says, of God are perfect. » Which then are these? Hear Moses saying in Genesis: « So then the heavens and the earth were finished, and all the furniture of them; and God completed on the seventh day his work which he had made, and he rested on the seventh day from all his work which he had accomplished [n: (Gen. II)]. » The work of God, therefore, is the redemption of the human race; and when he had perfected it, resting in the sepulchre he showed us the spiritual Sabbath. « All, he says, [1134A] his ways are judgments, » because all his commandments are true. Whence the Psalmist says: « The law of the Lord is blameless, converting souls; the testimony of the Lord is faithful, giving wisdom to little ones [n: (Psal. XVIII)]. » And likewise: « The judgments, he says, of God are true, justified in themselves; more to be desired than gold and much precious stone, and sweeter than honey and the honeycomb [n: (Ibid.)]. » And elsewhere: « With my lips, he says, I have pronounced all the judgments of thy mouth [n: (Psal. CXVIII)]. »

« God is faithful and without any iniquity, just and right. » Another edition: « God is faithful and there is no iniquity in him; just and holy is the Lord. » He is faithful, because what he promised he has beyond doubt fulfilled. Without any iniquity, because the highest equity in no way admits fellowship with iniquity. Just, because to those who sin [1134B] and do not repent he will repay punishment for their evils. Right, because to those who work worthily and deserve well he will render everlasting life. Holy, because in all his judgments and acts he is most approved: for *sanctus* signifies both the merciful and the patient, and the bestower of all good things, since whatever is wrought by him is filled full with the praise of virtues.

« They have sinned against him, not his sons, in filth. » Another edition: « They have sinned not against him, sons defiled. » How are they not sons of God, save because they are not of faith: « But as many as received him, he gave them power to be made the sons of God, to them that believe in his name [n: (Joan. I)]. » Whence the Lord says to the Jews, who would not receive faith in him and who spurned his teaching: « You are of your father the devil, and the desires of your father you will [1134C] do; he was a murderer from the beginning, and a robber. » And Stephen says to the same Jews: « Which of the prophets have your fathers not persecuted? And they killed them who foretold of the coming of the Just One, of whom you have now been the betrayers and murderers — you who received the law by the disposition of angels and have not kept it. » He therefore who sins incorrigibly proves himself not to be a son of God. But that edition which the seventy interpreters published has it thus: « They have sinned not against him, sons deserving of blame. » Which some have rendered out of the Greek as "sons defiled," some as "sons idle." Whence there is no great question here, nay rather none at all. But this rightly moves one to inquiry: if it has been said generally, they have sinned not against him, the sons — since he who sins does not sin against God, that is, does not harm God, but himself — how then is it to be understood [1134D] that is read in the psalm: « To thee only have I sinned [n: (Psal. L)]. » And in Jeremiah: « To thee have we sinned, O patience of Israel, O Lord [n: (Jerem. XIV)]. » And again in the psalm: « Heal my soul, for I have sinned against thee [n: (Psal. XL)]. » And whether it is the same thing to sin *to* God as to sin *against* God — whence also Heli the priest says: « If a man shall sin against God, who shall pray for him [n: (I Reg. II)]. To sin against God is to sin in those things which pertain to the worship of God; for God is read to have said this to Abimelech concerning Sara: « Therefore I spared thee, that thou shouldst not sin against me [n: (Gen. XX)]. » But to sin to the Lord, or rather to have sinned to the Lord — unless perhaps somewhere a written text should occur which resists this sense — those men seem to me to be so called not undeservedly who do not perform worthy penance [1135A] for their sin, so that God may be glorified in pardoning; for David, rendering the reason why he had said: « To thee only have I sinned and done evil before thee [n: (Psal. L)], » subjoined and says: « That thou mayest be justified in thy words, and mayest overcome when thou art judged, » because to all who think rightly it plainly appears that the Lord is just in all his ways and holy in all his works: « Nor does he will the death of the sinner, but that he be converted and live [n: (Psal. XL)]. » Likewise also as to what was said by David: « Heal my soul, for I have sinned against thee. » This same thing is at work, that God may be glorified in pardoning, because great is his mercy toward those who confess to him and return to him. But in this Canticle the Prophet foresaw that there would be certain men who were so to sin, offending God [1135B] by their great iniquities, that they would not even be willing to do penance and to return to God, that they might be healed; of whom also it is said: « Since they are flesh, and a spirit going and not returning. » Therefore he said that they were not sons of God, because they proved incorrigible.

« A depraved and perverse generation, is it these things thou renderest to the Lord? » Another edition: « For, a depraved and perverse generation, these things hast thou repaid to the Lord. »

With a like description did the Saviour brand their treachery in the Gospel, when he answered those who tempted him and sought a sign from him, saying: « A depraved and perverse generation seeketh a sign, and a sign shall not be given it, but the sign of Jonas [n: (Matth. XII)]. » So too John the Baptist says to the crowds of the Jews who came to him: « Ye brood of vipers, who hath shewed you to flee [1135C] from the wrath to come? Bring forth therefore fruits worthy of penance, and do not choose to say, We have Abraham for our father. For God is able of these stones to raise up children to Abraham. » For the generation of the Jews was ever depraved and perverse, which showed itself ungrateful for the benefits of God, and repaid him for this no worthy praises — nay, on the contrary, ever various transgressions, the filth of idols, and provocations.
