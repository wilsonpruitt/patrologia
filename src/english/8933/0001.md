## CHAPTER VI.

All his acquaintances too were standing at a distance, and beside the cross his mother, and many other women: and the more his enemies were and the fewer his friends, the more they put him to confusion. But how great was the grief of his heart, to bear in himself so long so great a bitterness of death, when the grief of the friends standing by, and most of all of his mother, pressed him no less? How could such a mother of such a Son stand, seeing him die thus? Did not a mother's heart have bowels of tenderness? What was the mother of mercy thinking, who felt all the things she saw her Son endure? How could she bear the weight of that death, when many, after so many years, cannot bear even [1428C] the memory of the passion? Where is the mother who could see and endure her son hanging on a gibbet, even if the son had deserved it? Do not mothers so love their sons that they cannot bear to hear even a hard word against them? How then did the mother of the Lord stand, and not rather fall down a hundred times stricken or dead? What was she doing? how could she stand, and be silent, where she heard them all speaking to one another, and her Son himself speaking so many times? How did she not run to the cross crying and wailing, and tearing her Son away from them, or at least demanding with tears that he be given back to her? How did she not, by her outcry and her tears, drive her sisters, and Mary Magdalene, and the others to weeping and lamentation?

If Mary Magdalene, on the third day after, stood outside at [1428D] the tomb weeping for one so long dead, so that she could not be consoled even by angels, how would she not have wept to see him die thus — him who had so kindly forgiven her her sins, who a little before had raised her brother Lazarus, and for love of whom she had left all things? Far more strongly and more bitterly ought the sisters of the Lord's mother to have wept and lamented, both for the passion of their nephew and out of compassion for their sister. Who then doubts that the mother grieved infinitely more for her Son?

## CHAPTER VII.

But whatever was to her a cause of grief and sorrow, by the power of God she held it all most tightly within herself, and inwardly she was tormented in body: and the less [1429A] she dared to cry out — lest she lose her patience and, dying with him, burden her dying Son the more — the sharper were the rendings of her inward parts that she felt in her heart.

But when she saw that he, so ill treated, [was praying] to God the Father for them, and even excused them because they did not know what they were doing, how did she not say to him: Most beloved and most sweet Son, why dost thou say this? They crucify thee, mock thee, and curse thee, and thou on the contrary blessest them? How do they not know what they do, to whom thou hast done no evil? And what could she say, when, dying, he entrusted her to a boy to be kept? What consolation was this for a most sorrowing mother at the death of a most beloved son: Receive the son of Zebedee in place of the Son of God? What could a mother say to her son, and a son to his mother, if they could then have spoken long with one another? And how [1429B] at last could she see the sword coming, which for so many years, from Simeon's prophecy, she had awaited day by day, and which then, through her Son's side, pierced a mother's inward parts? How could John then hold her up, that she should not fall, when he himself was not without the greatest grief?

The Lord also saw, to the increase of his own grief, such blood poured out so cheaply and so uselessly, since he labored thus in vain, that he then scarcely gained one robber, and lost one apostle. He saw again, then, in those present and those to come, the general ingratitude — that even by Christians he was to be given over to forgetfulness like a dead man: because very few are they who give him thanks for so great a benefit, even while they behold him crucified, saying: *Glory, praise, and honor be to thee, Christ the King, Redeemer.* The Lord saw [1429C] also the most wicked recompense of those present and to come, from those who hate him without cause and repay evil for good: because, if he could suffer, he would suffer even worse things from bad Christians than he did then from the Jews: for adding transgression upon the pain of his wounds, they daily renew his blows, and crucify him again, and hold him up to scorn.

## CHAPTER VIII.

He could grieve much, foreseeing the men of our own day, whom the Apostle also wept over — enemies of the cross of Christ, ungrateful for such benefits, taken up with the delights of the flesh, glorying in vain things, gaping after worldly things: for whom it is not enough that they go to destruction, but by persecuting the good they mock, revile, and despise them — nay, they even plunder [1429D] and kill them, as the king's soldiers did to the Lord, and the clerks of the law, and the people consenting with them; of whom it is said in Malachi: *You crucify me, O nation, all the day long.*

Nor do the many wicked displease less, who have done many evils, and inflict evils, and blaspheme God, and [1430A] murmur in adversities, like the robber hanging on the left. But few bear evils patiently for their sins and are saved, like the robber hanging on the right. And fewest of all truly suffer with the passion of Christ after the manner of the holy women.

And yet the impious were not so satisfied with his punishments, but raging with unheard-of madness against the dead man they pierced his side with a lance. And when darkness came on, and the veil of the temple fell, when the rocks were split and the earth trembled, when the tombs lay open, and the robber confessed, and the centurion glorified the just Son of God, the faithless Jews were willing neither to believe nor to suffer with him, but called him a deceiver, and guarded him buried, to blot out his name and memory, and declared that he had been carried off by theft by his disciples.

## CHAPTER IX

[1430B]

O soul, weigh and think over these things: how narrow and rough is the way that leads to life, and how much it behooved him to suffer that he might enter into his glory. If he bought his own glory thus, who shall have it altogether for nothing? Finally, if these things do not move you, consider that he is your heavenly Father, and your brother too according to the flesh, and a faithful friend, beneficent and helpful — so much so that even for you, condemned as you were, before he died thus, he is thus punished in all his members for your great and manifold guilt, that he might be merciful to all your iniquities: thus he endures many blows, that he may heal all your infirmities: thus he redeemed from destruction your life — mean, useless, abominable, damnable — by a redemption [1430C] so precious, so abundant, so gracious, by a Godhead so glorious. Thus he destroys the guilt of sin and of hell through such a destruction of his own body. Thus he crowned his head with thorns, that he might crown you in mercy and compassions: so was his soul filled with evils, and he had no good of the world, that he might fill your desire with good things. Thus his flesh dies on the cross, that your youth may be renewed like the eagle's.

And you, in all these things unfeeling, hardened, and ungrateful, see these things and laugh, you worthless and degenerate servant, who without shame and without grief see before you the gibbet, and your Father hanging upon it. Nor do you consider, wretched and pitiable man, of what judgment and punishment you deserve to be condemned, who have pierced the body of him thus hanging on the gibbet with arrow or lance as many times [1430D] as you have sinned mortally: nor was it enough for you not to render your own soul to your Redeemer, but you have taken others from him besides. Pray therefore that God may be merciful and favorable to you, and turn to him with your whole heart: who lives forever and ever
