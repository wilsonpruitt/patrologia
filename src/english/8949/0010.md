## CHAPTER VIII.

VERS. 2-6.-- *Behold, I will strike all thy borders with frogs,* etc. [n: (STRAB.)] There are three kinds of frogs: one of the river and vocal; another of that which is called the calamite, of which it is reported that if it be thrown into the mouth of a barking [0205D] dog, the dog at once falls dumb; a third of that which is called the bramble-frog, because it lives among briars.

[n: (ORIG.)] Frogs signify the songs of the poets, which by an empty and inflated modulation, like the sounds and singing of frogs, have brought the fables of deception into the world; for that creature is good for nothing, save that it gives back the sound of its voice in shameless and importunate clamours.

[n: (RAB.)] The second precept: *Thou shalt not take the name of thy God in vain.* [var: Vulg. Ex 20:7 *Non assumes nomen Domini Dei tui in vanum*, "Thou shalt not take the name of the Lord thy God in vain"] The name of God is truth, for he himself says: *I am the truth.* He who speaks truth, speaks of God; for *he who* *speaks a lie, speaks of his own.* For to speak truth is to speak rationally; to speak vanity is to make a din. The second [0206A] precept, therefore, is concerning the love of truth, to which the love of iniquity is contrary. Truth speaks, vanity clamours. Contrary to this is the second plague, the abundance of frogs, whose loquacity signifies vanity. The lovers of truth do not take the name of God in vain, because they do not withdraw from truth into vanity. But those who contradict the truth, and, deceived by vanity, deceive others, are frogs, bringing weariness to the ears, not food to minds. Hear men speaking rationally, psalm XVIII: *There are no speeches nor languages whose voices are not heard.* Hear the frogs: *They have spoken vain things, every one to his neighbour,* etc.

VERS. 7-9.-- *And the magicians did so,* etc. [n: (AUG., quaest. 23.)] It is asked whence they did it, if it had already [0206B] been done everywhere. But there is a like question: whence did they turn water into blood, if all the water of Egypt had already been turned into blood? But it is to be understood that the region where the children of Israel dwelt had not been struck with such plagues, and thence the enchanters were able to draw water which they might turn into blood, or to bring forth frogs for the display of magical power; although they were also able to do it after those things had been checked. But Scripture in narrating joined together what could have been done afterwards.

VERS. 10-14.-- *He* answered: *To-morrow.* [n: (THEOD.)] Pharaoh, wishing to know whether the prayers of Moses and Aaron had any power with God (for he supposed that they rested through the night), said: *To-morrow,* that he himself, having gone out to the river, might see whether the frogs had ceased. [0206C] Wherefore God, that Moses might be believed, not only made the frogs cease at that very time which Moses had appointed, but also commanded those which were upon the land to perish, lest they should think that the same had returned.

VERS. 15.-- *But Pharaoh seeing,* etc. [n: (AUG. Quaest. 24.)] Here it appears that Pharaoh was not hardened only because the enchanters did the like, etc., *as far as* he did not say gifts, or rewards, but thy judgments.

VERS. 16, 17.-- *Stretch forth thy rod,* etc. *Mystically.* Moses chastising Egypt with the rod signifies the law, which rebukes the world with the ten plagues of the commandments. The rod also, by which Egypt is corrected and Pharaoh overcome, is the cross of Christ, by which the world is conquered and the devil overcome, or triumphed over. The rod cast down becomes a serpent, which signifies wisdom, according to [0206D] that of Matthew X: *Be ye wise as serpents,* etc. The rod devoured the serpents of the magicians, and the cross of Christ, whose preaching seems foolishness, overcomes all the wisdom of the world. Whence: *For he wrote of me [n: (Joan. V)]*. The rod cast upon the earth was turned into a serpent, because when the mystery of the cross came to the faith of men, it made the wisdom of the world foolish.

*And let there be sciniphs,* etc. [n: (ORIG.)] This creature is upheld by wings, flitting through the air, but so fine and minute that it escapes the sight of the eye, unless of one who sees keenly. Yet when it has settled upon the body, it bores with a most bitter sting, so that he who is not able to see it flying feels it stinging. This creature therefore is compared to the art of dialectic, [0207A] which bores souls with minute and subtle stings of words, and encompasses them with such craft that the man deceived neither sees nor understands whence he is deceived.

[n: (RAB.)] *Morally.* The third precept: *Remember to sanctify the day of the sabbath.* [var: Vulg. Ex 20:8 *Memento ut diem sabbati sanctifices*, "Remember that thou keep holy the sabbath day"] By which is intimated the leisure and tranquillity of mind which a good conscience makes. There is sanctification, because there is the Spirit of God. Whence: *Upon whom shall my Spirit rest, save upon the humble and the quiet?* etc. [n: (Isa. LXVI)]. [var: Vulg. Is 66:2 *ad quem autem respiciam, nisi ad pauperculum, et contritum spiritu, et trementem sermones meos*, "but to whom shall I have respect, but to him that is poor and little, and of a contrite spirit, and that trembleth at my words"] The restless, therefore, spring back from the Holy Spirit, provokers of quarrels, lovers of calumnies, nor do they admit the rest of the spiritual sabbath. Be thou therefore meek; let there be no tumult in thy heart, with phantasms flitting through corruption and stinging thee. Hear God saying in psalm XLV: *Be still and [0207B] see that I am God.* Thou through restlessness wilt not be still, and, blinded, thou demandest to see what thou canst not. For mark the contrary plague: the sciniphs were born in the land of Egypt out of the slime, flies namely most minute and most restless, flying without order, rushing into the eyes, and denying rest to men; while they are driven off, they return: so are the phantasms of the restless. Hold the precept, beware the plague.

VERS. 18.-- *And there were sciniphs.* Blood is the doctrine of the philosophers; frogs, the fictions of the poets; sciniphs, the sophisms of the dialecticians: all which have deceived the world.

VERS. 19.-- *This is the finger of God,* etc. [n: (ISID. ex Aug. ep. 119.)] At the third sign the magicians fail, saying: *It is the finger of God:* who had the type of heretics and their animosity. [0207C] Whence: *As Jamnes and Mambres resisted Moses, so these also resist the truth, men corrupted in mind, reprobate concerning the faith,* etc. [n: (II Tim. III)]. But these, who through corruption of mind were restless, failed at the third sign, confessing that the Holy Spirit, who was in Moses, was against them. For the Spirit is set in the third place, who is the finger of God: and he, as when appeased he affords rest to the meek and humble of heart, so when angered he harries the unmeek and the proud with restlessness, which the most grievous flies signified, under which the magicians failed, saying: *This is the finger of God,* that they might not understand whence they were deceived.

[n: (STRAB.)] It is to be noted that the magicians were overcome at the third sign, because all perfidy and worldly wisdom, or philosophy, is overcome by faith in the Trinity. For although [0207D] they discovered philosophy and disputed concerning the fabric of the whole world, they were not able to attain to the knowledge of the Trinity. By the finger the Holy Spirit is understood, not that he is less than the Father and the Son, but because in a finger there are very many divisions, and through the Holy Spirit the division of graces is given.

[n: (AUG., quaest. 25 in Exod., tom. III.)] What the magicians said to Pharaoh, etc., *as far as,* although they were not silent concerning the Son.

VERS. 20.-- *Thus saith the Lord,* etc. [n: (RAB.)] The fourth precept: *Honour thy father and thy mother.* Contrary to this is the fourth plague, namely the *cynomyia*, that is, the dog-fly: it is doglike not to acknowledge one's parents; whence their whelps are born blind.

[0208A]VERS. 21.-- *Every kind of flies.* [n: (ISID.)] *Mystically.* In the fourth place Egypt is struck with flies. Now the fly is an insolent and restless creature, in which are figured the insolent cares of carnal desires. But Egypt is struck with flies, because the hearts of those who love the world are smitten with the restlessnesses of desires. The Seventy set down *cynomyia*, that is, the dog-fly: by which doglike manners are signified, in which the pleasure of the human mind and the lust of the flesh are reproved. The forensic eloquence of men also can be signified by the dog-fly, men who like dogs tear one another.

[n: (PROCOP.)] God does not use great beasts in the storming of Egypt, but summons the ministry of the most contemptible little creatures and insects: namely, lest [0208B] the effect should be ascribed to the power of those beasts. To those abject and feeble little creatures he granted unconquerable strength, so far that Egypt despaired of being able to resist those little creatures, and cried out: *This is the finger of God:* For the whole world massed together will not bear the hand of God.

[n: (ORIG.)] The dog-fly is the sect of the heretics [ *Al.,* Cynicorum], who, upon the remaining shamelessnesses of their deception, preach pleasure and lust as the highest good. Since therefore the world was first deceived by each of these, deservedly the word and law of God, at its coming, reproves with rebukes of this kind, that from the quality of the punishments they may know the qualities of their own error.

VERS. 22, 23.-- *And I will make wonderful,* etc. [n: (AUG., quaest. 26.)] This is to be understood to have been [0208C] done in the earlier signs also, that the land in which the people of God dwelt was vexed by no such plagues. But it was opportune that this should be set down openly there; whence now begin the signs which the magicians did not attempt to do the like of. For it is certain that the sciniphs had been everywhere in the kingdom of Pharaoh, but had not been in the land of Gessen or Gesen: there the magicians attempted to do the like, and were not able: until therefore they should fail, nothing was said concerning the separation of that land, but from the time when those things began to be done which they would not even dare to attempt.

VERS. 24, 25.-- *And there came a most grievous fly,* etc. [n: (AUG., ibid.)] The fly is put for the multitude of flies. For I know not how it is that, to the senses of men steeped in the custom of speaking, often more seems to be said by what is said in the singular than by what is said in the plural; [0208D] for example: There is a soldier there, than There are soldiers there; There is a fish there signifies more than There are fishes there.

VERS. 26-31.-- *Abominations.* The perverse Egyptians abominate good things, that is, the citizens of this world abominate sheep, that is, the simplicity of the good. For as the good abominate evils, so the evil abominate goods. Whence the good abominate the ungodly man, and the ungodly those who are in the right way.

[n: (AUG. Quaest. 28.)] Moses said that he wished to go into the desert where the Egyptians would not see their abominations. But this is to be understood as mystically signifying what we have said of the shepherds, who were abominable to the Egyptians; and therefore the Israelites [0209A] received a separate land in Egypt. For thus the sacrifices of the Israelites are abominations to the Egyptians, as the life of the just is to the wicked.

*[n: (STRAB.)] Historically.* The Egyptians abominate sheep and adore them because of Jupiter Ammon, who is painted with a ram's head. Likewise: the Egyptians worship the ox, which is consecrated to Apis, as the cow to Isis. For Apis was the greatest deity of the Egyptians, who once in the year at a fixed time appeared to their priests. And they, attending him with hymns and praises, prophesied like men frenzied.

[n: (GREG., lib. XVIII Moral., c. 28, tom. II.)] Egypt is struck with flies, etc., *as far as Be still and see, that I am God.*

VERS. 32.-- *And it was made heavy,* etc. *(AUG., quaest. [0209B] 29.)* When the locust had been taken away, it was said of Pharaoh: *Pharaoh made his own heart heavy at this time also, and would not let the people go.* Certainly it is not said now, The heart of Pharaoh was made heavy, but *Pharaoh made his own heart heavy.* So assuredly in all the plagues. From the will of man is the origin of vices. But the hearts of men are moved by causes, some in this way, others in that, even by causes not diverse, often in a diverse manner, according to their own qualities, which come from their wills.
