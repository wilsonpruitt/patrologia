## CHAPTER IX.

VERS. 1-6.-- *And the Lord said to Moses,* etc. [n: (PROCOP.)] Among the beasts horses are reckoned first, which are deceitful or false unto salvation. Then the unclean [0209C] beasts, which neither divide the hoof nor chew the cud, yet are yoked to the chariot. Next camels, which although on account of their chewing the cud they are reckoned as it were clean, yet, because their hoof is not cloven, are judged unclean. Nevertheless camels are declared to be partly of clean, partly of unclean condition. But since this plague was believed to be but little harmful, because of the vileness of the unclean animals, it passes on to oxen, and thence to the gentlest of animals, the sheep.

[n: (ORIG., hom. 4.)] In the fifth place Egypt is scourged with the slaughter of animals, or of cattle. In which the senselessness or folly of mortals is reproved, who like irrational cattle laid the worship and the name of God upon figures, not only of men but of beasts, stamped upon wood and stones: worshipping Ammon Jupiter in a ram, [0209D] Anubis in a dog, Apis in a bull, and the other monstrous gods which Egypt admires; so that in those to which they believed divine worship to belong, they might behold a pitiable punishment.

[n: (RAB. in Exod., tom. II.)] The fifth precept is: *Thou shalt not commit adultery,* etc., *as far as* mark the plague: if thou art a beast, at least fear to die.

VERS. 7.-- *And it was made heavy,* etc. [n: (AUG., quaest. 30.)] If the cattle of the Israelites had died, there would seem a fitting cause why his heart should be weighed down to the contempt of God, as though his magicians had slain the cattle of the Israelites; but from that whence he ought to have been moved to fear or to believe, it was made heavy, and that heaviness has gone forward even to this point.

[0210A]VERS. 8.-- *Take handfuls,* etc. [n: (AUG., quaest. 31.)] Another reading: *Take to you handfuls of ashes out of the furnace, and let Moses sprinkle it,* etc. The earlier signs were done by the rod, which not Moses but Aaron stretched over the water, or struck upon the ground. But now, two signs having been interposed, namely of the dog-fly and the deaths of the cattle, where neither Aaron nor Moses work anything with the hand, it is said that Moses should sprinkle ashes toward heaven; both are bidden to take, but he to sprinkle, not upon the earth but toward heaven: as though Aaron (who had been given to the people) ought to strike the earth, or to stretch out his hand upon the water or upon the earth; but Moses, who was in those things which pertain to the Lord, is bidden to sprinkle the ashes. What of those two earlier signs, where neither Moses nor Aaron work anything [0210B] with the hand? What does the diversity itself mean? For neither is it nothing.

[n: (RAB.)] The sixth precept: *Thou shalt not kill;* the sixth plague, pustules upon the body, blisters boiling and swarming, the burnings of wounds and the ashes of the furnace. For murderers burn with anger, with madness. If thou couldst see the souls of murderers, thou wouldst bewail them more than the putrefying bodies of the ulcerated.

VERS. 9-12.-- *[n: (ORIG., ibid.)] Wounds and blisters* with burning are brought forth at the sixth stroke. In the ulcers a crafty and purulent malice is reproved; in the blisters a swelling and inflated pride; in the burning, anger and the madness of fury.

VERS. 13-15.-- *And the Lord hardened the heart,* etc. [n: (ORIG., ibid.)] Note that in the sixth plague it is not said, [0210C] The heart of Pharaoh was hardened, but something more terrible is added; for it is written *that the Lord hardened the heart of Pharaoh,* etc. Again in the seventh, when hail and fire lay Egypt waste, the heart of Pharaoh is indeed hardened, but not by the Lord. But in the eighth, where the locust is brought forth, the Lord is said to harden the heart of Pharaoh. And in the ninth, when the darkness becomes palpable; and at last, when, the firstborn being destroyed, the Israelites had departed, it is thus said: *And the Lord hardened the heart of Pharaoh king of Egypt and of his servants, and he pursued them.* And also, when Moses is sent from the land of Madian into Egypt and is commanded to do wonders, it is added: *Thou shalt do them in the sight of Pharaoh: but I will harden the heart of Pharaoh,* etc. Hence first [0210D] it was said by the Lord: *I will harden the heart of Pharaoh,* etc. A second time also, the princes having been enumerated, it is said to Israel: *I will harden the heart of Pharaoh,* etc. Not in vain was that variety made, that now the Lord is said to have hardened the heart of Pharaoh, now that it was hardened of its own accord. I see Paul, as one understanding what difference there is between the heart of Pharaoh's having been hardened and the Lord's having hardened the heart of Pharaoh, saying in Romans II: *Or despisest thou the riches of his goodness, and of patience, and of longsuffering? Knowest thou not that the benignity of God leadeth thee to penance,* etc. Here without doubt he blames him who was hardened of his own accord. But elsewhere, as it were proposing a question upon this, he says: *Therefore on whom he will he hath mercy, and whom he will he hardeneth,* etc. He adds also to these: *O man, thou who art, who repliest against God [n: (Rom. IX)]*. [var: Vulg. Rom 9:20 *O homo, tu quis es, qui respondeas Deo?*, "O man, who art thou that repliest against God?"; Migne prints an unquestioning relative in place of the interrogative, and sets the same verse correctly eleven lines below at 0211A] By which, concerning [0211A] him who is said to have been hardened by the Lord, he answers not so much by the solution of the question as by apostolic authority, because of the incapacity of his hearers, as he says elsewhere [n: (II Cor. XII)]: *And he heard secret words, which it is not granted to man to utter.* Whence in what follows he deters the man who, not so much by the merit of his studies as by the lust of knowing, plunges himself too curiously [curiosus] into the more secret questions, saying in Romans IX: *O man, who art thou that repliest against God.*

[n: (RAB.)] The seventh precept: *Thou shalt not steal;* [var: Vulg. Ex 20:15 *Non furtum facies*, "Thou shalt not commit theft"; Migne's *Non furaberis* is the wording of Rom 13:9, not that of the Decalogue in Exodus] the seventh plague, hail upon the fruits. For no one has unjust gain without just loss. While thou stealest, thou gainest a garment, thou losest faith: gain visible, loss invisible: gain out of thy blindness, loss out of the Lord's cloud. For those who steal outwardly by evil desire [0211B] are hailed upon inwardly out of judgment, and the field of their heart is laid waste.

VERS. 16, 17.-- *And therefore have I set thee up,* etc. [n: (AUG., quaest. 32.)] The Apostle set down these words, etc., *as far as* Pharaoh therefore was preserved for their profit.

VERS. 18.-- *Behold I will cause it to rain to-morrow,* etc. [n: (ORIG.)] Thus far the punishments are tempered to the world through the figures of its own errors: after these come stripes from on high.

VERS. 19-21.-- *Send therefore now,* etc. [n: (AUG., quaest. 35 in Exod., tom. III.)] God seems to forewarn of this not so much in indignation as in mercy, etc., *as far as* since God had threatened the death of the cattle, although Scripture has been silent of it.

[n: (PROCOP.)] Moreover it was brought about by divine providence [0211C] that the beasts should not all perish. For there was need of beasts to draw the chariots with which Pharaoh was afterwards to pursue the fugitives, that he might slaughter them in the wilderness. But divine providence governed the whole business otherwise.

VERS. 22.-- *Stretch forth thy hand,* etc. [n: (PROCOP.)] The reader is to be advised in this place that Moses is not bidden to do all the wonders with the rod, but only certain of them; certain also to foretell, as the gnats and the destruction of the beasts; certain to accomplish by the ministry of the hands, as when he takes soot from the furnace and stirs up the hail. But what is the cause of these things? Lest the common people should suppose that the rod had been made by magical superstition, and should assign to it those wondrous effects.

VERS. 23-29.-- *And Moses stretched forth the rod,* etc. [0211D] [n: (ORIG., ubi supra. )] This also is to be noted, that Aaron is said to inflict certain stripes upon Pharaoh or upon Egypt, Moses certain, and certain the Lord himself. For in the first plague, where he turns the waters into blood, Aaron lifts up the rod and strikes the water. In the second he strikes the waters and brings forth frogs. In the third he stretches out his hand and strikes the dust of the earth with the rod, and there are made sciniphs. In the fourth the Lord is said to have done it, that the dog-fly should come. In the fifth also, when the cattle are destroyed, he is said to have done this thing. But in the sixth Moses sprinkles the ashes, and there are made ulcers and blisters. In the seventh he lifts his hand toward heaven and there are made thunders and hail, namely, etc. In the eighth likewise he stretches his hands toward heaven, and the Lord brings on a wind, and brings in [0212A] the locusts. And in the ninth also he stretches his hand toward heaven, and there are made darkness and gloom. But in the tenth the end and completion of the whole work is administered by the Lord. For thus it is said: *And the Lord struck every firstborn in the land of Egypt.*

[n: (IDEM.)] There is another difference to be observed: for in the first plague, when the water is turned into blood, it is not yet said to Moses that he should go in to Pharaoh, but, *go forth to meet him,* etc. But in the second, the first having been ministered constantly and faithfully, it is said to him: *Go in to Pharaoh,* etc. But in the third the sciniphs are brought in, the magicians yield, confessing that *this is the finger of God.* But in the fourth Moses is bidden to watch in the morning, and to stand before Pharaoh as he goes forth to the waters, when the houses of the Egyptians are filled with the dog-fly. [0212B] In the fifth, when the cattle are destroyed, he is bidden to go in to Pharaoh. In the sixth Pharaoh is despised, nor is it said that Moses and Aaron went in to him, because ulcers and blisters with burning were made even upon the magicians, and they could not resist Moses. In the seventh he is bidden to watch very early, and to stand before Pharaoh, when he brings forth hail and fire and thunders. In the eighth he is commanded to go in to him when the locust is brought forth. In the ninth again Pharaoh is despised, and Moses [Moses] is commanded to stretch his hands toward heaven, that palpable darkness may be made: nor does he go in, but he is called to Pharaoh. Likewise in the tenth, when the firstborn are destroyed, and he is compelled to go out of Egypt with haste.

*And the Lord gave thunders,* etc. [n: (ID., homil., 4.)] [0212C] See the moderation of the divine correction: he does not scourge in silence, but gives voices, and sends doctrine from heaven, by which the chastised world may be able to acknowledge its own fault. He gives hail, by which the tender things of the vices, still being born, are laid waste. He gives fire also, knowing that there are thorns and thistles which had to be grazed down; of which the Lord says in Luke XII: *I came to send fire in the earth [n: (Luc. XII)]*. By this fire the incitements of pleasure and of lust are consumed.

[n: (IDEM.)] There are many other things to be observed, in which the divine wisdom is shown. For at the first Pharaoh is not bent, when the water is turned into blood. At the second he seems a little softened: *For he called Moses and Aaron, saying: Pray to the Lord for me,* etc. At the third [0212D] the magicians yield, saying: *This is the finger of God,* etc. At the fourth, scourged by the dog-fly, he says: *Go and sacrifice to the Lord your God,* etc. At the fifth, where the slaughters of the cattle are said to be, he does not yield, but hardened himself the more. Likewise at the sixth, over the plague of ulcers. But at the seventh, when he is laid waste with hail and with fires, *he called Moses and Aaron, saying: I have sinned this time also; the Lord is just, but I and my peoples are wicked,* etc. At the eighth, when he was pressed with locusts, he hastily called Moses and Aaron, saying: *I have sinned before the Lord your God, and against you. Take up my sin, and even now pray to the Lord your God.* At the ninth, when he is overspread with darkness, he calls Moses and Aaron, saying: *Go and serve the Lord your God.* At the tenth, when the firstborn are destroyed, he calls them by night and says: [0213A] *Arise, go out from my people, you and the children of Israel,* etc. And he adds: *And bless me also.* These things cannot be explained save by the same Spirit by which they were done, because, according to the Apostle [n: (I Cor. XIV)], the spirits of the prophets are subject to the prophets; therefore the sayings of the prophets are said to be subject, not to any whomsoever for the explaining of them, but to prophets.
