## CHAPTER IX. (cont.)

VERS. 30-35.-- *But I know that,* etc. [n: (AUG., quaest. 35 in Exod.)] Pharaoh had been terrified by the crash of heaven and by the hail. Whence he begged Moses to pray for him, confessing his own iniquity and that of his people. But Moses was seeking another fear. For it is easy to fear punishment; but this is not to fear God, with that fear of piety, namely, of which Jacob says: *Unless the God of my father Abraham, and the fear of Isaac had been present with [0213B] me, thou wouldst now have sent me away empty [n: (Gen. XXXI)]*.

## CHAPTER X.

VERS. 1-3-- . . . *For I have hardened his heart,* etc. [n: (AUG., quaest. 36 in Exod.)] He did not say this as though God had need of anyone's malice, but it is thus to be understood, as if he should say: I have been patient over him and his servants, that I might not take them away, so that my signs might come upon them in order; for because the evil mind was becoming the more obstinate through the patience of God, for this, that I was patient over him, it is said, *I have made his heart heavy.* The plagues of the Egyptians are the instruction of the children of Israel, lest they be hardened in like manner, and suffer like things. When the pestilent man is punished, the little one shall be the wiser.

[n: (ORIG.)] The ten plagues with which Egypt is scourged through [0213C] the Israelites can be compared to the times of the Roman empire, because these things were done in a figure of us. The Christian and the Israelite people are of one God; the cause of both was one and made subject, the Synagogue to the Egyptians, the Church to the Romans. The Egyptians persecuted, and the Romans also persecuted. There Moses is contradicted ten times, here ten edicts against Christ. There diverse plagues of the Egyptians, here diverse calamities of the Romans. There first blood flows from the wells and rivers; here the first plague under Nero, that everywhere there was the blood of the dying, either corrupted by diseases in the City, or poured out by wars throughout the world. There the second plague, frogs in the inner chambers; famine almost destroyed the inhabitants: here under Domitian unbridled henchmen, at the command of a bloody [0213D] prince, and want, dispersed almost all the Roman citizens. There the third plague, sciniphs, most savage little flies, which in midsummer fly in swarms through squalid places, and are borne with a burning bite into the hair of men and the bristles of cattle: here the third plague stirred up the Jews under Trajan, who, whereas before, being scattered, they were quiet as though they were not, being moved by a sudden heat, went about ravaging. Besides, many cities in those same times fell down by an earthquake. There the fourth plague, dog-flies, nurslings of rottenness, mothers of worms; here under Marcus Antoninus a pestilence poured into many provinces gave over to rottenness and to worms Italy and the city of Rome, and the army of the Romans scattered through diverse winter quarters and dissolved by death. There the fifth plague, of cattle and of beasts of burden: here under Severus the persecutor, by civil [0214A] wars, the very bowels and supports of the commonwealth, that is, the commons of the provinces and the legions of soldiers, were broken in pieces. There the sixth plague, swelling blisters and running ulcers: here the plague which followed the persecution of Maximinus, who had commanded that especially the bishops and clerics, the popular crowd being passed over, the chief men of the Churches, should be tortured; and, swelling frequently with anger and perfidy, it was exalted not through the slaughter of the common people, but through the wounds of dying princes and men of power. There the seventh plague, hail gathered in the air, which was destruction to men and to beasts: here under Gallus and Volusianus, who succeeded Decius the persecutor, a pestilence was poured through all the spaces of the Roman empire, and gave nearly the whole human race to death. There the eighth correction, locusts stirred up on every side: here nations stirred up on every side [0214B] laid waste the Roman world with slaughters and burnings. There the ninth plague, darkness that might be handled, threatening more of peril than working it: here, while Aurelian the persecutor was decreeing, amid dreadful whirlwinds a terrible thunderbolt fell at his very feet, showing what so great an avenger would do, were he not patient; although within six months three emperors were slain, Aurelian, Tacitus and Florianus. There the tenth and last plague, the slaying of the firstborn: here the destruction of all the idols, which, being made first, they loved among the first. There the king proved the power of God, and felt it, and feared, and let the people of God go free: here also he did all these things, and even believed.

[n: (IDEM.)] There the people of God was not drawn back to servitude, nor here afterwards compelled to idolatry. There [0214C] the precious vessels of the Egyptians were handed over to the Hebrews: here the precious temples of the pagans passed over to the Church of the Christians.

[n: (IDEM.)] Upon the Egyptians, pursuing the Hebrews who had been let go after the ten plagues, the destruction of the sea rushed in: upon us also, as we journey, the persecution of the Gentiles remains, until we pass through the Red Sea, that is, the fire of judgment, with Christ as leader and as judge. But those who persecute Christians shall be drowned, with their king Antichrist, in the pool of eternal fire.

VERS. 4-12.-- *Behold I will bring in the locust to-morrow,* etc. [n: (RAB.)] The eighth precept: *Thou shalt not bear false witness:* [var: Vulg. Ex 20:16 *Non loqueris contra proximum tuum falsum testimonium*, "Thou shalt not bear false witness against thy neighbour"] the eighth plague, the locust, a creature namely harmful with the tooth. For a false witness harms by biting and consumes by lying. Whence: *If you bite [0214D] and consume one another, take heed lest you be consumed one of another [n: (Gal. V)]*. [var: Vulg. Gal 5:15 *comeditis*, "devour"; the gloss's own verb two lines above is *consumit*, which follows Migne's reading and not the Vulgate's]

*[n: (ORIG.)] hom. 4 in Exod.)* I think that by this kind of plague the inconstancy of the human race, ever at variance and discordant with itself, is refuted. For the locust, although it has no king, according to the Scripture, leads an ordered army: but men, although they were made rational, were neither able to govern themselves in order, nor did they patiently bear the guidance of God who governs.

[n: (STRAB.)] By the locust, which has no king, is signified the rashness of those who, although punished with diverse penalties, always remain in their own hardness, as though they had no ruler.

[0215A] *The locust.* [n: (ISID.)] Locusts are taken for the mobility of levity, as souls wandering and leaping after the pleasure of the world.

VERS. 13-18.-- *And Moses stretched forth the rod.* [n: (RAB.)] Certain wonders are done through Moses, certain through Aaron, and certain through the Lord himself; that we may know that in some things we are to be cleansed through the sacrifices of the priests and the supplications of the pontiffs, whom the person of Aaron designates; in some to be amended through the knowledge of the law, which the office of Moses designates. But in the more difficult things we have need of the power of the Lord himself.

[n: (GREG., lib. XXXI, Moral., cap. 20)]. The plagues of Egypt shown forth from heaven, etc., *as far as* when with empty praises they unnerve the works of certain men who are now as it were strong.

[0215B]VERS. 19, 20.-- *There remained not so much as one,* etc. [n: (AUG.)] Scripture certainly recalled a benefit of God, in that he took away the locusts, and going on it said that the Lord had hardened the heart of Pharaoh, namely by his own benefit and patience, by which that man was becoming the more obstinate while he was being spared: as all evil hearts of men grow hard by ill using the patience of God.

VERS. 21.-- *Stretch forth thy hand,* etc. [n: (AUG.)] A third time it is said to Moses: *Stretch forth thy hand toward heaven,* that the plague of darkness also might be made. But never was it said to Aaron that he should stretch his hands toward heaven. What therefore was said to Moses: *Stretch forth thy hand over the land of Egypt, and let the locust come up upon the land,* I believe this to be signified, that he who can do the greater can do the less also; but not at once that he to whom the lesser things are granted [0215C] can do the greater.

[n: (ORIG.)] The ninth plague is darkness, that the blindness of their mind may be reproved, or that they may understand that the reasons of the divine dispensation and providence are most obscure. For God *hath put* *darkness his covert [n: (Psal. XVII)]*: and they, boldly and rashly searching it out, and asserting one thing from another, have been rolled down into the thick and palpable darkness of errors.

[n: (STRAB.)] By the darkness their folly [Judaeorum] is shown, who were afflicted and did not know to what end their punishments would come. Or also by the darkness is signified the secret of the divine judgment, by which he mercifully saves the just and justly condemns the ungodly.

[0215D]VERS. 24-29.-- *Only the sheep,* etc. [n: (AUG. in Locutionibus in Exod., l. 52.)] The Greek has, *besides sheep and oxen leave ye,* and it is an unusual manner of speech, unless after a pause there be inserted, *leave ye;* and there be understood, these things, so that the sense may be: Go, besides sheep and oxen, and leave these things. For such an ellipsis is wont to be made often in the manners of speech of the Scriptures.

[n: (RAB.)] The ninth precept: *Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's wife:* [var: Vulg. Ex 20:17 *Non concupisces domum proximi tui, nec desiderabis uxorem ejus*, "Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's house, neither shalt thou desire his wife"; Migne's wording here is that of Dt 5:21, not that of the Decalogue in Exodus] the ninth plague, thick darkness. For there is a certain adultery even in desiring another man's wife, when his own does not suffice a man, and he attempts another's also. Truly the darkness is thick; for nothing so grieves the sufferer, nothing does the doer so refuse to suffer: he who does this is truly blinded with a horrible fury.
