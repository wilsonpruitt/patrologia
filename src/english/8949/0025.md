## CHAPTER XX.

VERS. 2.-- *I am the Lord thy God. [n: (ORIG., hom. 8 in Exod.)]* Of him who despises the present world, that is to say Egypt, and, being translated by the word of God, is not found, because he hastens to the world to come, God says: *I am the Lord thy God,* etc. Not to those only who set out from Egypt, but to those who serve the Egyptians no longer, brought out of Egypt, out of the house of bondage [n: (Psal. LXXX)]. The businesses of the world, the acts of the flesh, are the house of bondage: just as to leave the things of the world, and to live according to God, is the house of liberty. Whence John VIII: *If you shall continue in my word, you shall know the truth, and [0249D] the truth shall make you free.* Egypt, therefore, is the house of bondage; Judea and Jerusalem, the house of liberality. As, therefore, earthly Egypt is called for the children of Israel a house of bondage in comparison with Judea and Jerusalem, which for them is a house of liberty: so, in comparison with the heavenly Jerusalem, which is the mother of liberty, the whole world and the things that are in it are called a house of bondage: and because from the paradise of liberty men came, through sin, to the bondage of the world, the first word of the decalogue is concerning liberty: *I am the Lord thy God who brought thee out of the land of Egypt,* etc.; this voice thou wilt not be able to hear in Egypt. Even if it be laid upon thee to keep the pasch, to gird thy loins, to take sandals [0250A] upon thy feet, to hold a rod in thy hand, and to eat unleavened bread with bitterness. But neither in the first station, nor in the second, nor in the third, nor when thou shalt have passed the Red Sea, even if thou come to the sea and the bitterness be turned for thee into sweetness, nor in Elim, where were twelve fountains of waters and seventy palm trees, even if thou pass Raphidim by and climb the remaining stages, art thou yet judged fit, but only when thou shalt have come to Mount Sinai. Scarcely, therefore, wilt thou at some time deserve to receive the precepts of liberty, and to hear: *I am the Lord thy God, who brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage.* This, however, is not the word of a commandment, but it shows who he is that commands. The first commandment, therefore, is: *There shall not be to thee other gods beside me.* [var: Vulg. Ex 20:3 *Non habebis deos alienos coram me*, "Thou shalt not have strange gods before me"; Migne's own lemma at VERS. 3 below prints the Vulgate form, and the wording used throughout this gloss is the Septuagint's]

[0250B] [n: (AUG. quaest. in Exod.)] It is asked: how are the ten precepts of the law to be divided? etc., *as far as* unless to that which was said: *There shall not be to thee other gods beside me?*

[n: (ISID.)] God gives to Moses the law of our innocence and of the knowledge of himself, which consists in ten words, written on tables of stone by the finger of God. There are three which pertain to the love and the knowledge of the Trinity, seven to the love of one's neighbour, by which human society is not injured. The first pertains to God the Father: *The Lord thy God is one God,* that, hearing this, thou mayest worship one God the Father, and mayest not pour out fornication upon many feigned gods. The second to the Son: *Thou shalt not take the name of the Lord thy God in vain;* that is, do not think that Christ the Son of God is a creature, because every creature is made subject to vanity, but equal to the Father, God of God, [0250C] light of light.

VERS. 3.-- *Thou shalt not have strange gods,* etc. [n: (ORIG.)] The first commandment is: *There shall not be to thee other gods beside me;* and there follows: *Thou shalt not make to thyself an idol, nor any likeness,* etc. [var: Vulg. Ex 20:4 *Non facies tibi sculptile, neque omnem similitudinem*, "Thou shalt not make to thyself a graven thing, nor the likeness of any thing"; the distinction between idol and likeness drawn below rests on the word Migne prints] Some think all these together to be one commandment: but if it be so reckoned, the number of ten commandments will not be made up. The first commandment, therefore, is: *There shall not be to thee other gods beside me,* etc. The second, *Thou shalt not make to thyself an idol, nor any likeness,* etc. Let us begin, therefore, from the first commandment: *There shall not be to thee other gods beside me.* If he had said, They are not, the statement would seem more absolute; but because he says, *There shall not be,* he did not deny that they are, but forbade that they should be to him to whom these precepts are given. Hence I think Paul took it, I Cor. VIII: *If indeed [0250D] there are those who are called gods, whether in heaven or on earth.* And in other places thou wilt find gods named, as there: *A great king above all gods,* and *God hath spoken to God,* etc. [var: Vulg. Ps 49:1 *Deus deorum Dominus locutus est*, "The God of gods, the Lord, hath spoken"; the verse is adduced to show that Scripture names gods in the plural] Of lords the same Apostle says: *Whether thrones, or dominations,* etc. [n: (Col. I.)] But dominations are nothing else than a certain order and multitude of lords. In which the Apostle seems to open the sense of the law; as if he should say: Though there be many lords, who rule over other nations, and many gods who are worshipped by others: yet nevertheless there is one God to us, and one Lord. Whence Deut. XXXII: *When the Most High divided the nations and separated the sons of Adam, he appointed the bounds of the peoples according to the number of the angels of God.* [var: Vulg. Dt 32:8 *juxta numerum filiorum Israel*, "according to the number of the children of Israel"; the reading Migne prints is the Septuagint's, and the argument that follows stands on it] The angels, therefore, to whom the Most High committed the nations, Scripture calls lords [0251A] or gods: gods as being given by God, and lords as having obtained power from the Lord. Whence it is said to them in psalm LXXXI: *I have said: You are gods, and all of you the sons of the Most High. But you like men shall die, and shall fall like one of the princes;* imitating, that is, the devil, who was made the prince of all unto ruin. Whence it is plain that transgression, not nature, made them accursed. To thee, therefore, O Israel, who art the portion of God and his cord, *there shall not be,* he says, *to thee other gods beside me:* because he who is truly God is one God, and he who is truly Lord is one Lord; but upon the rest, created by himself, he conferred this name not by nature, but by grace. Nor are these things said only to Israel according to the flesh, but rather to thee who hast been made Israel by seeing God in mind, and circumcised in heart, not [0251B] in flesh; if only thou so act that thou be worthy to be the portion of God, and to be measured in the cord of his inheritance: otherwise let those be an example to thee who had been called to this, that they should be the portion of God, and, their sins requiring it, were scattered through all nations, and, having been brought out of the house of bondage, now serve not the Egyptians alone, but all nations. *For he who sins is the servant of sin [n: (Joan. V)]*.

VERS. 4.-- *Thou shalt not make,* etc. [n: (ORIG.)] *Thou shalt not make to thyself an idol, nor any likeness,* etc. Idols are one thing, gods another. For of gods the Apostle says, I Cor. VIII: *If indeed there are many gods, and many lords,* etc. But of idols, *that an idol is nothing in the world;* of likenesses, however, he did not add that they are nothing. But here it is said: *Thou shalt not make to thyself an idol, nor any likeness whatsoever.* It is one thing, therefore, to make [0251C] an idol, another to make a likeness. For example, if a man in gold and silver, in wood or in stone, makes the figure of a fourfooted beast, or of a serpent, or of a bird, and sets it up to be adored, he makes not an idol but a likeness. But he who makes what is not, that is, a figure which the eye has not seen but the mind feigns to itself; as if a man should form a dog's head or a ram's upon human limbs, or two faces in the one shape of a man, or the hinder parts of a horse or of a fish upon a human breast; he who does this makes not a likeness, but an idol: for he makes what is not, nor has it anything like itself. Therefore the Apostle says *that an idol is nothing in the world [n: (I Cor. VIII)]*. For no figure is taken from things that subsist, but what an idle and curious mind has invented. But a likeness is when something is formed out of those things which are either in heaven, or on [0251D] earth, or in the waters: but as it is easy to pronounce concerning the likenesses of those things which are on earth or in the sea, it is not so concerning the heavenly ones; unless one should say that this can be understood of the sun, and the moon, and the stars, whose forms the heathen represent. But Moses, learned in all the wisdom of the Egyptians, desired to forbid also those things which among them were in secret, such as Hecate and the other forms of demons, which the Apostle calls *the spirits of wickedness in the high places*, Ephes. VI; of which perhaps it is said: *My sword is inebriated in heaven [n: (Isa. XXXIV)]*. For by these forms and likenesses they used to invoke demons for the repelling or the avoiding of evils. Yet it must be known that when thou hast resolved to keep this commandment, and to have one God and Lord only, [0252A] this is to declare war upon all the others, whom thou didst renounce in baptism, confessing God alone, Father and Son and Holy Spirit. But confessing this, unless we love the Lord our God with the whole heart, cleaving to him with the whole soul, with the whole strength, we are not made the portion of the Lord, but, set on the border, we suffer those whom we flee to be offended, and him to whom we flee we do not make favourable. To whom the prophet says: *Woe to the double soul [n: (Eccli. II)]*. And again: *How long do you halt upon both feet? [n: (III Reg. XVIII.)]* [var: Vulg. 3 Kg 18:21 *Usquequo claudicatis in duas partes?*, "How long do you halt between two sides?"] From those, then, we are cut down as fugitives, and by our Lord we are not defended as unstable, whence Jer. IV: *Thy lovers are made thine enemies.* [var: Vulg. Jer 4:30 *contempserunt te amatores tui; animam tuam quaerent*, "thy lovers have despised thee, they will seek thy life"; the sentence Migne prints stands nearest Lam 1:2] Many are the lovers of the soul, taken with her beauty, with whom she has played the harlot, saying: *I will go after my lovers who give [0252B] me my wine,* etc. [n: (Ose. II)]: these she offends when she returns to her former husband. Unless, therefore, thou remain in whole faith with thy husband, thou art suspect for the crimes which thou hast committed; he marks all thy motions, thy looks and thy goings; he can see nothing further in thee that is wanton, nothing dissolute or prodigal. If, therefore, thou neglect the grace received, and entangle thyself in worldly businesses, straightway the unclean spirit returns with seven others more wicked [n: (Matth. XII)], that he may not be cast out again, and the last things become worse than the first. There is, then, no fellowship of the temple of God with idols. If we are God's, we ought to be such in whom God may dwell and walk; whence Isa. LII: *You that carry the vessels of the Lord, go out and touch not the unclean thing, and I will receive you,* etc. He says, therefore: *There shall not be to thee other gods beside me.*

[0252C]VERS. 5-11.-- *Thou shalt not adore,* etc. It is one thing to worship, another to adore. A man can adore unwillingly, either flattering idolatrous kings, or overcome by torments, when he knows that an idol is nothing. But to worship is to be made over wholly to these with affection and zeal. He cuts off both, therefore, that thou mayest neither worship with affection, nor adore in outward show.

*I am the Lord,* etc. See the kindness of God: that he may teach us and make us perfect, he does not refuse the frailty of human affections; he does and suffers all things for our sake, and speaks to us by affections known and familiar to us.
