## CHAPTER XX. (cont.)

VERS. 25, 26.-- *Of hewn stones.* [n: (ISID.)] Hewn [0256C] stones are they who cut the unity asunder, and divide themselves from the fellowship of the brethren: these Christ does not receive into his body, of which body the building of the altar signifies the figure. The stones not hewn, of which the altar is commanded to be built, are they who through faith are made firm in unity; of whom it is said, I Peter II: *You are living stones, built together into spiritual houses.* [var: Vulg. 1 Pt 2:5 *et ipsi tamquam lapides vivi superaedificamini, domus spiritualis*, "be you also as living stones built up, a spiritual house"] Upon these no iron has been laid, because they are uncorrupted, and have not received the fiery darts of the evil one: all make one altar in the unity of faith and the concord of charity. [nt: In numbering the precepts of the decalogue, Origen and Augustine, in the testimonies of the Gloss cited at the head of this chapter, seem to follow contrary opinions. For Origen holds that the ten commandments cannot be reckoned unless it stand thus: Thou shalt not make to thyself an idol, nor any likeness, etc. Augustine, however, though he does not reject that enumeration of Origen's, yet judges another, a different one, more fitting, so that he reckons the prohibition of making idols and graven images for the sake of worship to be the explication of the first precept: and then he sets down two precepts for restraining forbidden desires, one whereby the coveting of another's wife, the other whereby the coveting of all other things that are one's neighbour's, is to be restrained. Origen is followed by Athanasius in the Synopsis of Scripture; Gregory Nazianzen in the poem in which he numbers the ten precepts; Chrysostom in his second exposition of Matthew, homily 49; Jerome and Ambrose, chapter six of the Epistle to the Ephesians. Augustine's numbering, in that sense, is followed by the scholastic doctors, who, even as Augustine himself, prefer it to the former, yet without holding the former to be unfitting. And indeed the contrariety which a perverse disposition finds in subduing those two covetings would seem rather to have wanted two distinct precepts, since there are those who covet another's wife, and yet disdain to covet another's wealth or servants, and the reverse: but no true worshipper of God finds it difficult to spurn idols. Yet this diversity of numbering is, in the thing itself, nothing at all: for no precept is laid down by Augustine that is denied by Origen, or the reverse; but the one joins precepts by a certain ordering of subordination, which the other separates by reason of a verbal connection, and conversely. For Augustine joins the precept of not making idols to the precept of worshipping no God but one, because the former is subordinate to the latter and is the fuller explication of it, extending also to the forbidding of the ceremonies of the Gentiles: but Origen divides that same matter into two precepts, because they seem to be distinguished by the very structure of the sentence, with no mark of connection, just as the rest that follow: Thou shalt not have strange gods; Thou shalt not make to thyself a graven thing; Thou shalt not take, etc. And again, those two prohibitions of the twofold coveting Origen joins into one precept, on account of the sequence of the context, which seem to belong to one and the same sentence.]
