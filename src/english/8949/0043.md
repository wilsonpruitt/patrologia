## CHAPTER XL.

VERS. 1.-- *And the Lord spoke to Moses, saying: In the first month,* etc. [n: (STRAB.)] The first month of the year is Nisan itself, which is also April, in which the Jews [0293D] celebrated the pasch, and the tabernacle was set up. Allegorically, however, the first month signifies the beginning of grace, when the tabernacle of God, that is, the Church, was set up, when Christ, namely, being crucified, brought forth out of his side the mysteries of blood and water, by which the Church is dedicated and consecrated. And it is to be noted that this was the first month of the second year: for the first year was before the law, the second year under the grace of the Gospel, which the Lord blessed. Whence: *Thou shalt bless the crown of the year of thy goodness [n: (Psal. LXIV)]*. For it was the year of goodness, in which the salvation of the human race appeared through the humanity of Christ: in this year therefore the tabernacle of God was set up, that is, the Church founded and exalted.

[0294A] [n: (RAB. in Exod., tom. II.)] The first year is the whole time of the law, etc., *as far as* that the teachers of the Gospel may use these things in the confirmation of the faith.

VERS. 9.-- *Thou shalt anoint the tabernacle,* etc. [n: (STRAB.)] The tabernacle with its vessels is consecrated with the oil of unction; because whatsoever holy Church does with her virtues she ought to dedicate and consecrate to the grace of the Holy Spirit. For the oil of unction is compared to spiritual grace, by which the works of all the virtues are consecrated to the Lord.

VERS. 12.-- *And thou shalt bring Aaron,* etc. [n: (STRAB.)] The doors of the tabernacle, that is, of holy Church, are the apostles, through whom one must enter the court of heaven: to these are Aaron and his sons to be brought, that is, the whole company of the saints and chiefly of those set over them, that they may [0294B] imitate them and follow their footsteps.

VERS. 16.-- *And the bars.* The bar not only binds the walls together, but also strengthens the pillars of the entrance, that they fall not down, nor bend away from their upright standing; because the Lord has both joined either people together in one faith of love, and strengthens their teachers, that they may persevere in faith and in action and in preaching. Nor is it wonderful if by the heads themselves, and by the bar which holds those heads together, Christ is understood; because he is the head of every elect person by ruling him, whence: *The head of the man is Christ [n: (II Cor. XI)]* [var: Vulg. 1Cor 11:3 *omnis viri caput, Christus est*, "the head of every man is Christ"; these are the words Migne prints, reordered, but his own citation names II Corinthians]: and because he not only raises up single men to seek after heavenly things, but also holds all together, and, that they may not be bent away from their upright standing nor be parted from one another by discord, confirms them with the bond of peace. To which is like that the Lord is called both lamb, and shepherd, and door, [0294C] since the shepherd feeds the lamb, and both enter by the door; whereas properly he is the Word in the beginning [n: (Joan. I, X)]: but of these none is proper to him, but figurative. Shepherd, because he leads to the pastures of life; door, because it is not except through him that one comes to the Father; Lamb, because he saves from Egypt. So here also he is figured by the heads, on account of the rule by which he is set over individuals, and by the bar, on account of the general providence, because he confirms all [n: (Num. IX; III Reg. VIII)].

[n: (BEDA.)] So the soul obtains diverse names for its diverse actions. While it gives life to the body, it is called soul; while it wills, it is mind; while it knows, it is understanding; while it recalls, it is memory; while it judges, it is reason; while it breathes, it is spirit; while it perceives, it is sense.

VERS. 33.-- *Nor could Moses,* etc. [n: (STRAB.)] [0294D] Moses therefore in this place, as in very many others, holds the figure of the Jewish people. Moses, therefore, the cloud standing in the way, could not enter into the tabernacle, because the Jewish people was never able to penetrate the sacraments of Scripture. For the cloud of the Lord brooded over the tabernacle by day, and fire by night: in this place the fire signifies the knowledge of the divine mysteries. For Holy Scripture is light to the holy, and to those confessing their own ignorance; but to the wicked, and to those boasting of knowledge, it is a cloud, because it blinds them.

[n: (AUG., quaest. 176 in Exod.)] A thing much to be wondered at is to be noted, etc., *as far as* a cloud by day, a flame by night.

Now in the tabernacle of the Lord there were boards, in whose [0295A] rings were fixed bars of sethim wood, by which they could be lifted and carried from station to station, all which things are full of mysteries. By the tabernacle we understand the present Church, in which there are boards, namely the saints full of humility, to whom five bars are fastened, that is, the spiritual knowledge of the five books of Moses, by which they may raise up themselves and others to the brightness of eternal blessedness. By the bars, therefore, as we have said, we understand the five books of Moses, which are Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, the book of Numbers, and Deuteronomy: in which are contained the beginning of the world and its middle and its consummation. In Genesis is described the creation of the world, and in Exodus its re-creation; in Leviticus the administration of the sacraments of the Church; in the book of Numbers the evangelical [0295B] preaching; in Deuteronomy the last blessing. Whence also it is ended in blessings. And so there is in Genesis the order of creation; in Exodus the mystery of re-creation; in Leviticus the holiness of the Church's administration; in the book of Numbers the consolation of evangelical preaching; in Deuteronomy the consummation of eternal blessing. Now among these five books of Moses Exodus excels, which we now have in hand. For in it are contained both the things which befell the children of Israel in a figure, and fully the mysteries of Christ and of the Church. Here, however, it treats first of the actual going out of the carnal children of Israel, of the historical Egypt, in which is signified the spiritual going out of the spiritual Israelites from the true Egypt, that is, from the darkness of the vices. It treats also of the passage of the Red Sea [0295C] and of the drowning of Pharao and his army, and of the deliverance of the people of God: in which is figured the baptism consecrated by the blood of Christ, in which the vices of the devil are drowned, and the souls of the faithful are delivered. It treats also of the battle of the Israelites with Amalec, in which is figured the victory of Christ and of his own, and the confounding of the devil. Then, when the list of the stations has been described, it treats of the law given on mount Sina; in which it is intimated to us that, being raised up from earthly things, we may learn to be made subject to heavenly disciplines. It treats again of the construction of the tabernacle and of its vessels, of the vestments of the priests and of the unction; in which is understood the present Church, and the diverse persons of the faithful appointed to diverse offices, just as the vessels were appointed to diverse uses, or offices, and the most sweet perfumes of the sacraments and of the consecrated prayers. Thus far of these things; now it is to be said that according to the order of history [0296A] and according to allegory, this book is fittingly the second in the Pentateuch: for after he had treated in Genesis of the creation of heaven and earth, and of the entering in of the children of Israel into Egypt, it was fitting that he should consequently treat of their going out. Behold what is fitting according to the order of history: according to allegory also it was not unfittingly done. For after he had spoken of the carnal generation, by which through the sin of the first man we are born sons of the devil, it was fitting that he should straightway add concerning the spiritual regeneration, by which through the justification of the second man, that is, of Christ, we are born again sons of God. Now this book is called ἔξοδος in Greek, ואלה שמות veelle semoth in Hebrew, going out or departure in Latin: for this reason, because it treats of the going out of the children of Israel from Egypt. But it may be asked why this book [0296B] is called Exodus, because it treats of their going out, whereas in the beginning it treats not of the going out, but of their entering in. To which it may be said that although it purposes to treat especially of their going out, yet because much time flowed by between the going out, of which it here treats, and the entering in with which the first book is ended or had been ended, in order that it may more suitably join its narrative together briefly, it first repeats concerning the entering in, and so descends the better to the going out. So we also in our discourses, when a long interposition has been made, being about to say other things, repeat what went before, that we may the better continue what has been said. These things being considered, the matter is to be looked at, which is the bodily going out of the children of Israel from Egypt, and the spiritual going out, holy, of the saints from the world. By its intention it admonishes us to go out spiritually from the conformity [0296C] of the world, as they did who went out bodily from Egypt, that as we have been conformed to the world, so we may be conformed to God. The manner is such: touching briefly upon the entering in of the children of Israel into Egypt, it treats of their multiplication; then of the new king, who knew not Joseph and began to afflict the people; then of the plagues of Egypt, and at length of their going out and of the passage of the Red Sea and of the drowning of the Egyptians. At length it comes to mount Sina, in which the law was given, and it treats of the tabernacle, and of its vessels, and of the vestments of the priests and of the unction, and so it is ended.

Note that although the history is one and undivided which Moses set before himself to describe, yet they distinguish it by chapters and parts, or by books, that weariness may be taken away from readers, just as a traveller divides the road, which is undivided, into days' journeys, that the labour may be lessened and weariness be absent.
