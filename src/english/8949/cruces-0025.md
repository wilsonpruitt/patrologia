## 1. ⭐ THE HEADLINE — the Decalogue's FIRST precept, and it is now marked

`8949-ADJUDICATIONS.md` §2 records the first precept as diverging from Exodus 20 and carrying no
marker, because its only occurrence then in play stood at 0205B, inside a peer's chunk. **It falls
again inside my range, six times in chunk 0025, and it is now marked** — once, at its first

| col | Migne prints | Ex 20 | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0250A | *Non erunt tibi dii alii praeter me* | 20:3 *Non habebis deos alienos coram me* | `[var:]` fired here |
| 0250B, 0250C ×3, 0251A, 0252B | the same, five more times | — | recorded, not re-marked |

⭐ **And Migne himself supplies the control, which is why this is a finding and not a suspicion.**
Eleven lines below the marker, at **0250C**, his own verse lemma prints the Vulgate:

> VERS. 3.-- *Non habebis deos alienos,* etc. [n: (ORIG.)] Primum mandatum est: *Non erunt tibi
> dii alii praeter me;*

**The Vulgate wording and the Septuagint wording stand in one sentence, four words apart, on a
column I read at the plate (f125, corners 249/250, right column, band C).** So the divergence is
not the compositor and not our transcription: Migne sets the Vulgate as the lemma and lets Origen
expound the Old Latin/Septuagint form his argument actually needs — and it does need it, because
the whole gloss turns on the *tense*:

> Si dixisset, Non sunt, absolutior sermo videretur; sed quia dicit, *Non erunt,* non negavit quia
> sint, sed ne illi sint, cui haec praecepta dantur, inhibuit.

*Non habebis* has no future *esse* in it at all. Conform the lemma and the paragraph loses its
subject. This is Pattern 7a″'s founding shape, and it is why the marker sits on the gloss's
wording rather than on Migne's lemma.

⚑ **The marker fires ONCE.** Six markers on six occurrences of one three-word phrase inside 1,400
words would be apparatus, not annotation. The note at 0250A says the wording recurs; the crux says
where.

## 7. Renderings decided, and why

- ⭐ **`praeceptum` = "precept", `mandatum` = "commandment", held apart throughout** — the
  provisional convention of ADJUDICATIONS §1, and **my range gives it a reason the 0010–0014 stint
  did not have.** See §8.
- **`colere` = "to worship", `adorare` = "to adore", never interchanged.** At 0252C the gloss
  exists only to separate them: *Aliud est colere, aliud adorare … Utrumque ergo resecat, ut nec
  affectu colas, nec specie adores.* Collapsing them costs the paragraph its subject.
- **`zelans` = "jealous", `zelus` = "jealousy", `zelotypia` = "jealous rivalry".** The 0252D–0253B
  passage is a sustained marriage figure and uses all three within a few lines
  (*nec zelotypia movetur* … *excitatur super eam zelus ejus* … *Deus zelans*). Three Latin words,
  three English ones; *zelus* is not rendered "zeal" anywhere here, because in this passage it is
  the husband's jealousy, not eagerness.
- **`sermo` in the Decalogue frame = "word" / "saying", not "sermon" or "discourse."** Isidore says
  the law *in decem verbis consistit*, so *primus sermo decalogi* (0249D) is "the first word of the
  decalogue" and *non est sermo mandati* (0250A) "not the word of a commandment". At 0250C
  *absolutior sermo videretur* is "the statement would seem more absolute" — the same word doing
  ordinary work, and rendered ordinarily.
- **`damnum` = "loss" throughout 0259A–C**, never varied to "detriment" or "harm", because the
  whole exposition is a definition of the word (see §2b). *Detrimentum* appears only inside the
  `[var:]` notes, which is where the Vulgate's word belongs.
- **`similitudo` = "likeness", `idolum` = "idol", `sculptile` only inside a `[var:]`.** Same
  reasoning as `damnum`: Origen is defining the terms.
- **`subreptio` (0254C) = "stealthy deceit."** Isidore's ten-item list pairs each commandment with
  the vice it kills, and the first commandment is against false gods; the word carries the sense of
  a thing got in by stealth rather than "theft" (his seventh item is *rapacitas*, his eighth
  *falsitas*, so the vocabulary is already crowded and each has to stay distinct).
- **Pattern 17 applied throughout.** *thou/thee/thy* for every singular address — 0249D–0252B is a
  long second-person-singular exhortation (*audire non poteris*, *si tibi injungatur*, *mereberis*,
  *Tibi ergo, o Israel*, *quibus in baptismo renuntiasti*, *Nisi ergo tota fide permaneas cum viro
  tuo*), and *you* only where the Latin is plural (0249C *veritas liberabit **vos***, 0252B Isa
  52:11 *exite … et ego recipiam **vos***, 0253C *Vos de patre diabolo estis*).
- **0252C anchor placement.** Our twin sets `[0252C]` immediately before `VERS. 5-11.` with no
  space; reproduced exactly so, in place.
- **0257A** — our twin sets a bare `[0257A]` on its own line between the head and the first lemma;
  reproduced as its own line, since moving it into the paragraph would change anchor position.
- **0262A** — the italic run `*(AUG., quaest, [0262B] 86 in Exod.)*` is carried **verbatim and
  untranslated**, anchor inside, because it is one of Migne's citations that the TEI failed to mark
  as a note (patches file). Translating it would have made our page assert a note structure the
  Latin twin does not have.
- **Question marks are exact against the twin** in all five chunks (0025 3/3, 0026 4/4, 0027 0/0,
  0028 5/5, 0029 0/0). None supplied, none deleted.

## 8. ⭐ FOR THE MERGE — new evidence on *praeceptum* / *mandatum*, and it cuts one way

ADJUDICATIONS §1 leaves this open, with the 0010–0014 stint's argument (*mandatum* stands eleven
lines from *praeceptum* and needs a different English) against chunks 0002 and 0009, which render
both "commandment". **My range settles the empirical half of it, and the answer is not close.**

**Migne uses both words for the Decalogue, in the same glosses, and the two enumerated series use
different words.**

- **Origen's series is `mandatum`.** 0250A–0250C: *Primum **mandatum** ergo est* … *Est ergo primum
  **mandatum*** … *Secundum* … *a primo **mandato*** … *non complebitur numerus decem
  **mandatorum***. Nine `mandat-` forms against three `praecept-` in chunk 0025.
- **Rabanus's series, in the peer's chunks 0010–0014, is `praeceptum`** — *secundum, tertium,
  quartum … praeceptum*, fourteen of them.
- ⭐ **And Isidore uses BOTH, four lines apart, at 0254C — plate-confirmed on f127 c2 band C:**

  > Primum **mandatum** prohibet subreptionem; secundum, errorem; … decimum, adulterii
  > cogitationem. Et notandum quod sicut decem plagis percutiuntur Aegyptii, sic decem
  > **praeceptis** scribuntur tabulae … vel quia sunt **praecepta** duo dilectionis Dei et
  > proximi … In una enim tria **praecepta**, quae ad dilectionem Dei pertinent.

**Under "commandment for both," that paragraph reads as one word repeated seven times and the
reader cannot see that Migne wrote two.** Under the split it reads as what it is: the ten
*mandata* enumerated, then the two tables written with ten *praecepta*, then the two *praecepta* of
love. The distinction is doing work on the page, not in a translator's ear.

**A second consequence, and it is the one that matters for the merged book.** Because Origen's
enumerated series is *mandatum* and Rabanus's is *praeceptum*, the split convention **keeps the two
series apart in English** — a reader meeting "the first commandment" in CAPUT XX and "the second
precept" in CAPUT VIII can tell that these are two different expositions of the Decalogue by two
different authors. Collapse them and the book appears to contain one enumeration that keeps
restarting. That is a stronger argument than the eleven-lines one, and it is available only once
both ranges are in view — which is what the merge is for.

**Counts, so the sweep can be sized:** 28 `mandat-` and 49 `praecept-` forms across the 44 Latin
chunks. I have applied the split uniformly in 0025–0029 (11 "commandment", 10 "precept", each
tracking its Latin exactly). Chunks 0002 and 0009 are the known non-conforming sites.

▶ **Recommendation to the merge: adopt `praeceptum` = precept, `mandatum` = commandment, sweep all
44 chunks, and record it in the conventions header.** I have not edited a peer's chunk.
