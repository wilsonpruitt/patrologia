their own italic span, `*usque ad*`; all 56 are `*as far as*` in the English and none was varied.

### The `[sic:]` that was NOT fired — the ship test applied three times

Three forms in this work are **non-words**, and the 2026-08-23 addendum's finding is that a non-word
is our digitization's error six times out of six, not Migne's. **A `[sic:]` blames Migne in public.
None was fired.** All three are logged in §2 as plate candidates instead (§2a, §2b, §2c).

## §2 · THIS BOOK'S CRUCES

### 2a · 0542A · *dilatavum* — a NON-WORD, and the chunk's own lemma settles it. NO MARKER.

Migne (as we have him) prints 2Cor 6:11 as *Os nostrum patet ad vos, o Corinthii, cor nostrum
**dilatavum** est*. There is no Latin word *dilatavum*. The Clementine has *dilatatum*, and — this is
the decisive part and it is internal — **the lemma at the head of this very chunk, eleven lines
above, is *Dilatatum est.*** (1Rg 2:1), correctly formed. A *t* read as a *v* is the ordinary shape of
a digitization misread.

**Rendered "our heart is enlarged", and NO `[sic:]` was fired.** The 2026-08-23 addendum measured
non-word suspicions at six for six as **our** corruption rather than Migne's, and a `[sic:]` is a
public claim that Migne's plate is defective. ⚑ **Plate candidate — PL 113 col. 0542A, and the
suspect form is a NON-WORD.** If the plate reads *dilatatum*, this is a `data/tei-patches/8952.json`
fix and nothing else.

### 2b · 0546A · *Judic um* — a SPLIT NON-WORD. NO MARKER.

*Dan interpretatur **Judic um** vel judicans.* The split run is Jerome's *Onomasticon* etymology of
Dan, *iudicium sive iudicans* — and the second half, *judicans*, stands unbroken in the same clause,
which is what makes the reconstruction safe. **Rendered "*judgment* or judging", italics 1:1**, and
no `[sic:]`, for the reason in 2a. ⚑ **Plate candidate — 0546A, a NON-WORD split across a space.**
Note that the alternative reconstruction *Judicum* ("of the judges") was weighed and lost: Jerome's
entry is *iudicium*, and the pairing with *judicans* is a noun/participle pair from one root, not a
book title.

### 2c · 0539C · *quia fuit **a de** Ramatha civitate Ephraim* — two prepositions stacked. NO MARKER.

Both *a* and *de* mean "from" and English has one word for the pair, so there is nothing for the
English to show; the rendering is "because he was from Ramatha, a city of Ephraim". This is the
Pattern 9 shape (a defect with no English exponent) and it is recorded here so a later reader can
tell a seen difficulty from a missed one. ⚑ **Plate candidate — 0539C**; a stray *a* before *de* is
the kind of thing a plate settles in one look.

### 2d · 0542B · *Non dicit nisi Dominus: Sed nullus est sicut Dominus* — which reading was taken

Two construals both parse:
1. "He does not say **except** the Lord" — *nisi Dominus* as the glossator's own qualification;
2. "He does not say '**except the Lord**'" — *nisi Dominus* quoted as a form of words, contrasted
   with the italic *Sed nullus est sicut Dominus* that follows the colon.

**Reading 2 was taken**, and rendered "He saith not, save the Lord: *But none is as the Lord,* holy
and sanctifying". **Why reading 1 lost:** the *Sed* is inside Migne's italic span, so it belongs to
the quoted matter, not to his own sentence, and a *sed* inside a quotation with nothing before it to
contrast against is exactly what a "he does not say X, but Y" frame produces. The colon Migne prints
after *Dominus* does the same work. Nothing in the argument turns on it either way.

### 2e · 0542D · *Saturati* in the lemma against *Repleti* in the gloss — logged, NO MARKER

Migne's lemma at VERS. 5 is *Saturati prius pro panibus.* The Clementine's 1Rg 2:5 is *Repleti prius,
pro panibus se locaverunt* — and **Migne's own gloss under that lemma opens *Repleti prius Judaei*
and then *Repleti iidem***, twice. So his lemma word is not the word he expounds; *saturati* belongs
to the second half of the same verse (*et famelici saturati sunt*), which is the obvious source of
the contamination. **No `[var:]`:** *saturare* and *replere* are synonyms and the exposition is
unaffected — Pattern 14 fires where the divergence *changes the sense*. Recorded because the pattern
(lemma and gloss disagreeing inside one paragraph) is the sort of thing a later reader will want to
have been seen.

### 2f · 0541D · VERS. 19 printed after VERS. 23 — Migne's ordering, reproduced

CAPUT I closes with `VERS. 19.-- *Et adoraverunt.*` standing after `VERS. 23`. The text is 1Rg 1:28
(*Et adoraverunt ibi Dominum*), so the address is wrong as well as out of place. **Reproduced exactly
as printed and NOT reordered** — the Jude precedent in the brief. Migne does the same thing at
0548C (a 6:12 lemma addressed VERS. 13), at 0547D (a 5:6/5:9 lemma addressed VERS. 7, §2h), and at
0559B (a 20:30 lemma standing in the VERS. 17 paragraph, §2m).

### 2g · 0547C · *Azotum.* glossed with 7:12's stone — Migne's, left standing

*Azotum. Locus est in quo posuit Samuel lapidem [n: (I Reg. VII)], sicut in sequentibus demonstratur.*
The place where Samuel set the stone is the *lapis adjutorii* of 1Rg 7:12, not Azotus; 5:1 reads
*asportaverunt eam **a lapide Adjutorii** in Azotum*, so the gloss has attached itself to the wrong
end of the verse. **Rendered as printed, nothing emended, no marker** — the note Migne himself
prints already sends the reader to I Reg. VII, which is where the difficulty is visible.

### 2h · 0547D · ***Et per.*** — a truncated lemma. Rendered "*And he smote.*", and why

Migne prints `VERS. 7.-- *Et per.*` The gloss under it is entirely about being tormented *in
posteriora* — the hinder parts — which is 1Rg 5:6 *percussit in secretiori parte natium*, repeated at
5:9 and 5:12. **Read as a truncation of *Et percussit*** and rendered as the phrase it stands for,
per Ruth §1.2.

**The rival, and why it lost:** *per* is a whole Latin word, so the lemma could be read as ending on
one (in which case it would be carried, not expanded). But **1Rg 5:7 contains no *per* at all** —
its words are *super nos, et super Dagon deum nostrum* — so on that reading the lemma would point at
nothing on the page it addresses, while the smiting reading is what the gloss then spends four
sentences on. Migne's verse number is off by one either way (§2f).

### 2i · 0548C · *Et non* — expanded to "*And they turned not aside.*"

VERS. 13's lemma is *Et non*, addressed to 6:13, which has no negative in it; 6:12 has *et non
declinabant neque ad dextram neque ad sinistram*. The gloss reads *Id est, nec extollebantur
prosperis* — "neither were they lifted up by prosperous things" — which is the right-hand side of
that non-deviation, so the lemma is 6:12's. Expanded per Ruth §1.2, **the printed negative carried
onto its own host** (the turning aside), not onto the being-lifted-up.

### 2j · 0549C · *Annus vice.* — the same lemma twice, one of them cut mid-word

0549C prints *Annus vicesimus* in the running gloss and then *Annus vice.* as a fresh lemma four
lines later. Per the brief's ruling on 8998, **a work that treats one lemma two ways is resolved by
EXPANDING the stump, never by contracting its twin**: both are "*The twentieth year*". The Latin
column keeps the difference visible.

### 2k · 0551C · *Assumens ita.* — 9:22's *Assumens itaque*, cut inside the enclitic

Rendered "*Taking therefore.*" *ita* alone would mean "thus", which is not what 9:22 says and not
what the gloss expounds (*Quod Samuel Saulem in excelsum duxit* — the taking). Same class as 2j.

### 2l · 0557D · *Benedixit Naboth Deo et regi. Quia non vivat anima tua…* — a negation that is the GLOSSATOR'S

This is a test-2a site and it needed care. Migne's italic run contains **two different things**: a
quotation of 3Rg 21:13 (*Benedixit Naboth Deo et regi*, where *benedixit* means its opposite) and
then **the glossator's own antiphrastic paraphrase of the lemma he is glossing** — 1Rg 17:55's
*Vivit anima tua, rex, si novi*, **with a *non* inserted** to show what the euphemism means. The
whole entry exists to teach that *Si* is sometimes put for *non* (he proves it from Ps 94:11).

So the *non* is not a divergence from any received text and takes **no `[var:]`**; it is carried into
the English on its own host (*vivat*), and the paragraph's closing *Si novi, id est, non novi* is
what makes the reading certain. The one real divergence here is *Deo et regi* for the Clementine's
accusative *Deum et regem* — a case, not a sense, so no marker.

### 2m · 0559B · ***Nunquid:*** — expanded to "*Do I not know:*", and why

The lemma stands in the VERS. 17 paragraph with no verse address of its own. Its gloss is *quasi
dicat, Amas inimicum meum adversans mihi, in quo ostendis adulterino te conceptum concubitu* — which
is 1Rg **20:30**, Saul to Jonathas: *Numquid ignoro quia diligis filium Isai … in confusionem
ignominiosae matris tuae?* Both halves of the gloss (loving the enemy, the mother's shame) come from
that verse and from nowhere in 20:17.

*Nunquid* alone is an interrogative particle with no verb and is not English, so Ruth §1.2 requires
the phrase it opens. **The rival — leaving it as a bare stump — lost because it would print an
English word ("Whether") that is not a sentence and would tell the reader nothing**; the colon Migne
prints inside the italic is kept.

### 2n · 0561A · *Satum est genus mensurae continens modium et semis Polenta est accuratissima farina.*

**A sentence boundary with no point.** *semis* and *Polenta* run together, and the capital *P* shows
that a full stop belongs there. Pattern 8 forbids supplying it, so the English runs together too:
"containing a peck and a half Polenta is the finest meal." ⚑ **Plate candidate — 0561A**; note that
the suspect here is a missing mark, not a word, so no marker of any kind could carry it.

### 2o · 0561B · *Phalti enim vadens dicitur. quando aufertur;* — a point mid-sentence, kept

Migne prints a full stop and then a lowercase *quando*. **Preserved exactly** (Pattern 8's "never
delete"), giving "Phalti is called going. when she is taken away;". The parallel construction two
clauses later (*Phaltiel … dicitur: quia custodivit*) shows what mark was probably wanted; nothing is
emended.

### 2p · 0540D · *qui Christum plus nonnunquam diligit* — a masculine relative on a feminine subject

The only singular subject that *diligit* can take is *Ecclesia*, and *quos carnaliter pariebat* two
words later is unambiguously feminine. *qui* for *quae* is a **gender mismatch with no English
exponent** (Pattern 9): the English reads "who sometimes loves Christ more", nothing is emended, and
the Latin column shows the form.

### 2q · 0547A · *antequam primitivo ecclesia … credidit* — the same class

*primitivo* is masculine or neuter where *ecclesia* is feminine. No English exponent, Pattern 9, no
marker: "before the primitive church which was of her believed".

### 2r · 0539C · *nec a se aliter efficitur Deus* — an ambiguity kept

Rendered "nor is God made otherwise from himself". The rival construal — taking *Deus* as the
subject of an intransitive "nor does he by himself become otherwise God" — is not excluded, and the
sentence is arguing divine immutability on either reading (*quia nunquam mutatur* immediately
before). Nothing turns on it; recorded so the choice is visible.

### 2s · 0549B · *In domo Aminadab, qui interpretatur: pater meus spontaneus, et dicere potest voluntarie scilicet tibi.*

**This will not construe and is rendered as closely as the printed words allow**: "In the house of
Aminadab, who is interpreted: my father spontaneous, and he is able to say, namely voluntarily, to
thee." *dicere potest … tibi* has no object and *voluntarie* has nothing to attach to. Two further
facts belong with it: 1Rg 7:1 has **Abinadab**, not *Aminadab*; and the etymology given (*pater meus
spontaneus*) is Aminadab's, not Abinadab's, so the substitution is in Migne's source, not merely in
his type. **Nothing emended, no marker** — no marker in this corpus means "Migne prints a clause
that will not construe", and inventing one for one site is worse than saying so here (the Ruth
*societate fieri* precedent).

### 2t · 0556D and 0561B · *ipsam legem accepit* and the two *laudabiliter*s — checked, and NOT defects

At 0556D *deposuit tanquam onera legis, et ipsam legem accepit* reads oddly beside the preceding
*deposuit corporalia sacramenta legis*, and at 0561B Gregory says Abigail *laudabiliter tacuit …
laudabiliter dixit* — the same adverb of two opposite acts. **Both are checked and left alone**: the
first is the paragraph's own point (he lays down the law's burdens and takes up the law), and the
second is Gregory's own antithesis in the *Moralia*, not a doubling. Recorded as candidates
**rejected**, so a blind reader knows they were looked at.

### 2u · 0562C · *praemium beatitudinis … praecipiunt*

*praecipio* is a real word and "receive beforehand" construes ("they take beforehand from Christ the
reward of blessedness together with the perfect"). *percipiunt* ("receive") is the obvious suspect
and is **NOT adopted**: the 2026-08-23 addendum's finding is that a suspicion aimed at a real,
well-formed word has been refuted by the plate every time it was tested, so the printed word stands
and the conjecture lives here.

### 2v · 0561D · *nullius hostis **deprecationes** perpessi* — a `[cj:]` weighed and DECLINED

"having endured the entreaties of no enemy" is what the printed word yields, where the sense plainly
wants *depraedationes*, "depredations" — the villages were *quieti et securi*. **Declined** because
*deprecatio* carries the attested medieval sense "imprecation, curse", on which the clause construes
as printed ("they had borne no enemy's imprecations"), and because a real, well-formed word is
Migne's until a plate says otherwise. Rendered literally; the conjecture is here and not in the text
**by decision, not by oversight**.

### 2w · Two spellings Migne varies inside the work, both left as printed

## §3 · THE FOUR `[var:]`, AND WHY EACH ONE EARNED A PUBLIC CLAIM

### 3a · 0539C · *Unguentum effusum nomen tuum* (Cant. I) — Clem. Ct 1:2 *Oleum effusum*

`[var: Vulg. Ct 1:2 *Oleum effusum*, "oil poured out"]`. A different noun in a proof-text the gloss
is building on: the whole entry is an etymology of *Suph* as *effundens*, and it chains *effudit →
effusum → effusum a suis invisibilibus*. The reader meets "ointment" where every Vulgate has "oil",
and only the marker tells him the divergence is Migne's and not ours.

### 3b · 0543A · *suffulsit columnas septem* (Prov. IX) — Clem. Pr 9:1 *excidit columnas septem*

`[var: Vulg. Pr 9:1 *excidit columnas septem*, "she hath hewn out seven pillars"]`. A different verb
entirely — propping up against hewing out. The gloss adduces the verse for the **seven**, so the verb
is not load-bearing; the marker fires nonetheless because the two texts assert different acts and the
faithful English would otherwise read as a translator's slip.

### 3c · 0543C · *laudant nomen Domini* (Psal. CXII) — Clem. Ps 112:3 *laudabile nomen Domini*

`[var: Vulg. Ps 112:3 *laudabile nomen Domini*, "the name of the Lord is worthy of praise"]`.
**The most material of the four.** Migne needs a finite verb here, because he has bent the verse into
a relative clause on *orbem Ecclesiae, quae …*; the Clementine has no verb at all, only a predicate
adjective. The divergence is what makes his sentence work, and hiding it would make his grammar look
like ours.

### 3d · 0553C · *Et nunc regnum tuum non stabit tibi.* — Clem. 1Rg 13:14 *sed nequaquam regnum tuum ultra consurget*

`[var: Vulg. 1Rg 13:14 *sed nequaquam regnum tuum ultra consurget*, "but thy kingdom shall no more
rise up"]`. **The argument turns on the printed verb.** Eucherius (out of Augustine) writes *Stetit
ergo et stabit quod in illo significatum est; sed non huic stabit* — three forms of *stare* in one
sentence, none of which is in the Clementine's *consurget*. Render the received text and the
exposition stops meaning anything.

### 4a · The *charitas*/*dilectio* pair — the sharpest of the declines, and it runs BOTH ways

At 0556D–0557A Migne quotes Rom 13:10 as *Plenitudo legis **charitas** est* where the Clementine has
*dilectio*, and eleven lines later quotes Eph 4:2 as *Sufferentes invicem in **dilectione*** where the
Clementine has *caritate*. **He has each of the two words exactly where the Vulgate has the other.**

`[var:]` was weighed on both and **declined on both**. The reason is not that the difference is
invisible — it is that *caritas* and *dilectio* are the standard Latin pair for one thing, the
exposition here is about the fulness of the law and not about which Latin word names love, and the
form Migne prints for Rom 13:10 is the ordinary patristic citation form of that verse. A `[var:]`
that fired here would have to fire on every Latin father who cites Rom 13:10, which is the definition
of noise. **What is worth recording is the chiasm**, and that is what this entry is for; the
divergence is now findable by anyone who wants it.

### ⭐ 0539C — the digitization ADDED a word

The plate prints one preposition: *quia fuit **de** Ramatha civitate Ephraim*. Our file carries a
spurious *a* in front of the *de*, which is exactly what produced the crux logged above about two
prepositions governing one object. **This is a new class: not a corruption of a word Migne
printed, but the insertion of one he did not.** It reads like a scribal doublet rather than like
damage, which is what makes it dangerous — a reader meets it as Migne being clumsy.

⚑ **The English needed no change.** It had rendered "because he was from Ramatha", declining to
invent a construction for a word that would not construe — which is what 7a asks for, and which
is why the repair could be made in the Latin alone.

### ⭐ 0561A — the digitization DROPPED A FULL STOP MIGNE PRINTS

The plate reads *continens modium et semis. **Polenta** est accuratissima farina.* The stop is
there; our file lost it and fused the measure-gloss into the meal-gloss.

⚑ **This is the case that justifies spending a plate read on punctuation.** Pattern 8 forbids the
translator to supply a mark Migne does not print, so running the two sentences together in English
and flagging the site was the only correct move available without the plate — and it was made. The
plate licenses the repair, the repair belongs in the **Latin**, and the English is corrected in
the same commit to carry the sentence break the plate shows. ⛔ Note what the alternative would
have been: supplying the stop in English on the strength of the sense would have been right by
accident, and indistinguishable from the many cases where it is wrong.
