## CHAPTER IV.

[0763D]VERS. 2.-- « Si coeperimus loqui tibi. » [n: (Ibid.)] They begin softly, but they break out into harsh things. So heretics set forth smooth words, that they be not guarded against, but bring in harsh ones after.

« Sed conceptum. » [n: (Ibid.)] The perfect do not even in their heart conceive perverse things, which they check by silence. Others conceive depraved things, but bind them fast by the great strength of silence: others both conceive them and straightway bring them forth. Light in understanding, headlong in tongue. Of these is Eliphaz, who out of his own experience judges the same of all men.

« Tenere quis poterit. » As though he would fain hold it back: wherein he hints that he will give offense in speaking.

VERS. 3.-- « Ecce docuisti plurimos. » [n: (Ibid.)] There are certain good things which even he confesses who wishes to lay a charge [0764A] against a man. How great was he before, who, though busied with the ordering of so many things of his own, yet freely taught others also, showing right things by deed and by word? But because the accuser speaks good things of the same man, he bent them to a charge, saying:

VERS. 5.-- « Nunc autem venit, etc., quasi non servasti recta quae dicebas. » But note that there are wicked men who speak the good things of the just which, being known, they cannot deny, lest they seem depraved: and that they may thereby be the more believed in the evils which they bring in, they turn these very good things also to the heaping up of guilt. Here therefore, as though by his own testimony, he is reproved as an evil man for having spoken right things, but not kept them: below he is noted for speaking ill, that he may hold his peace. And these are the two ways in which evil men assail the good, saying that they either speak depraved things, or do not hold the good things which they speak.

[0764B] « Defecisti, etc. Ubi est timor tuus? » As though he marveled at virtues that were lost, which he sets down in a fitting order. As in the way of the world boldness begets strength and fear begets weakness, so also in the way of God boldness begets weakness, and the fear of God strength: because he who is joined to God is raised by a certain power above all things; strength is not shown save in adversities, whence patience is added, because he as it were makes progress in strength who bears others' evils unmoved. Of patience is perfection born, whence a man is said to possess his soul in patience, that is, to live rightly in all things, to have dominion out of virtue over all the motions of the mind: for he who conquers himself is strong against all things.

[n: (Ibid.)] But since he took him to task by inveighing against him, now he adds as it were by exhorting:

[0764C]VERS. 7.-- « Recordare, obsecro te. » But this too he does blameworthily. For the innocent and the upright are often here utterly blotted out, even though they are kept for things eternal: but they who serve God not for an eternal but for an earthly wage feign for themselves the thing they seek, and, presuming to teach, when they preach earthly security, show by all their labors what they love.

VERS. 8.-- « Quin potius vidi eos. » [n: (Ibid.)] He sows sorrows who speaks. He reaps deceitful things who prevails by speaking. Or, he sows sorrows who does perverse things. He reaps, who prospers in them. These do not (as he says) perish, but live here more happily than the just. For God suffers certain men to prevail here, that the life of the just may be purged the more cleanly. He punishes others straightway, lest he be thought to neglect human things, and to pass over many evils, that he may strengthen the hearts of the innocent [0764D] who are faint-hearted: but he would speak more truly of all men at the end; it could be said generally that the innocent and the upright man does not perish, because though he be worn down here, with God he is restored in true salvation. And the evil perish, because though they grow higher here, at the end they shall be smitten more hardly. But since he says, *remember,* it is plain that past things are brought back to mind, not that things to come are declared.

VERS. 9.-- « Flante Deo perisse. » [n: (Ibid.)] We blow air, while from without we draw it within, and from within we render it without. God therefore is said to blow in retribution, because from outward causes he conceives within the counsel of judgment, and from that inward counsel he sends forth the sentence outwardly, that is, from our evils which he sees without, [0765A] he disposes judgment within, and from the inward conception he makes damnation without. Or God is said to blow, that is, to be angry, by a likeness, because we, when we are angry, are inflamed with the blast of fury. God therefore blows in being angry, purposing vengeance, when, after long patience, he who in himself is tranquil seems turbulent to the guilty, since they before him are troubled by their own guilt.

[n: (GREG.)] After he had admonished him kindly, he openly rebukes him. « Rugitus leonis, » that is, the severity and terror of Job himself: « vox leaenae, » the talkativeness of his wife, « dentes catulorum, » the greediness of his sons, has been destroyed. The stiff man exults, as though they had been condemned by right. He compares Job also to a tiger, because of the spots of dissembling. For the hypocrite, who seems upright, has hidden vices, [0765B] which sometimes, breaking out, change his color. The glory of the just is a prey which he snatches to himself: this man therefore, thinking that holy Job had held by hypocrisy the good things which he knew in him, says:

VERS. 11.-- « Tigris periit. » That is, the variety of thy dissembling is put out, etc., *as far as* such men as these think that they surpass all men in wisdom.

VERS. 12.-- « Porro ad me dictum est verbum absconditum. » [n: (Ibid.)] Heretics feign that they hear hidden things, whence they also preach secretly, that their preaching may be held holier, and not equal to that of others.

« Furtive, etc. » [n: (Ibid.)] Because they do not enter with the rest by the door, but seek the chinks of a depraved understanding; and because a thief fears the light, he adds:

VERS. 13.-- « In horrore visionis nocturnae. » [0765C] In the dread of a nightly vision they say that they have perceived the veins of the divine whisper, because, that they may boast of lofty things, they say that they can scarcely take them in. But while they thus mark lofty things, they are witnesses against themselves of their wickedness. For in a nightly vision a thing is seen doubtfully.

« Quando solet sopor occupare. » That is, while all below sleep in torpor, we watch for the perceiving of high and heavenly things. And when at times they see this despised, they feign to fear themselves the things they say, whence he adds:

VERS. 14.-- « Pavor tenuit me. » That they may seem wonderful in high things, they are as it were greatly afraid of what they feign: and yet they are bold to bring forth the things which they assert they could scarcely hear.

« Ad me dictum est verbum. » [n: (Ibid.)] The Son of God [0765D] is said, etc., *as far as* so much the more, being checked, does it dread his uprightness: whence he adds:

« In horrore visionis nocturnae, etc. » That is, in the dread of hidden contemplation, in which nothing is seen manifestly, but as it were in an image. But to this no man attains, unless the desires of the flesh be checked, whence:

« Quando solet sopor occupare homines. » He watches who is intent upon the cares of the world: he sleeps in quiet who flees them. This is Jacob, who sleeps on the way and sees angels, that is, in the course of this life he closes his eyes to worldly things, which fault opened to our first parents for coveting. And no man is brought to the seeing of inward things unless he be withdrawn from those things which entangle him without; [0766A] whence: « Vacate et videte: quoniam ego sum Deus [n: (Psal. XLV)]. » But the more the mind, lifted up in contemplation, beholds things higher than itself, the more terribly does it tremble in itself, whence:

« Pavor tenuit me et tremor et omnia ossa. » [n: (Ibid.)] That is, the stronger deeds, etc., *as far as* he feels the sting of the flesh, he adds:

VERS. 16.-- « Stetit quidam. [n: (Ibid.)] That they may judge that they have known incomprehensible things, etc., *as far as* because he arrogated to himself specially a good that is common.

« Inhorruerunt pili carnis meae. » [n: (Ibid.)] The hairs are all the superfluous thoughts of human corruption, of the old life: which if we shut out from the mind, we cut the hairs from the flesh, that we be not wearied with pain. Whence Moses: « Levitae radant omnes pilos carnis suae [0766B] [n: (Levit. XIV)]. » But these things we behold more subtly in ourselves when we pierce into high things, whence rightly it is now said:

« Cum spiritus transiret. » For when the mind sees true things, it torments itself the more hardly concerning superfluous things. When therefore the spirit passes, the hairs are greatly afraid, because before the force of compunction superfluous thoughts flee away, because the inward vision inflames the soul against itself; and while it cuts away unlawful things strictly, the mind cleaves more widely to contemplation, and it well fixes the spirit which was passing; nor yet does this very staying of contemplation fully open the force of loving-kindness, whence he adds:

« Stetit quidam cujus non agnoscebam vultum. » [n: (Ibid.)] He is not said to be a certain one, etc., *as far as* he beholds through his co-eternal likeness, he adds:

[0766C] « Imago coram oculis me. » [var: Vulg. Job 4:16 *meis*, "an image before mine eyes"] The Image of the Father is the Son, who says: No man comes to the Father but by me.

« Et vocem quasi aurae lenis audivi. » [n: (Ibid.)] The voice of the breeze is knowledge, etc., *as far as* who from this hearing learned.

VERS. 17.-- « Nunquid homo comparatione Dei justificabitur? » This he knew in contemplation, that human justice compared with the divine is injustice, as the lamp which shines in the darkness grows dim in the sun: which he could do, because he who sees the light knows what to reckon of the darkness.

« Aut factore suo purior? » [n: (Ibid.)] This he reckons who murmurs against him that scourges him. But he ought to know that he does not scourge impiously who made wonders out of nothing, and that the [0766D] founder of nature is not an unjust judge of fault. And though this man unjustly reproves Job, yet he rightly describes the measure of the creature in comparison with the Creator. A man therefore reckons himself purer than his maker if he prepares a complaint against the scourge, and without doubt sets him after himself, when he reproves his judgment concerning his own affliction

« Ecce qui serviunt ei. » [n: (IDEM.)] An angel, although he remains unchangeable in his estate, yet in this, that he is a creature, has the vicissitude of changeableness, and so is not stable. For if this nature were not changeable, since it was well founded, it would have fallen in nothing. But of free choice it had it to fall or to stand, and in those that stood the merit was that they fixed the motion of their own changeableness by the earnestness of the will. This is the proof of changeableness which he adds concerning the apostate:

[0767A] « Et in angelis suis reperit pravitatem. » From the comparison of these the infirmity of man is gathered.

« Quanto magis hi qui habitant domos luteas. » [n: (GREG.)] That is, earthly bodies, etc., *as far as* they behold, being taken up with the cares of the world; whence he adds:

VERS. 20.--From morning until evening. » That is, from the beginning of their life until its end they are wounded by the committing of iniquity, and being cut down by it they fall into the deep.

[n: (Ibid.)] Or morning is prosperity; evening, adversity; but by both they perish, because through prosperity they are wanton, through adversity impatient, and rise up to madness. And because none of them, namely, « qui de mane usque ad vesperam succidentur » understands what is kept here for the afflicted good, what is to come for himself who [0767B] here thinks them good things.
