⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ IN THE NAME OF THE HOLY TRINITY, HERE BEGINS THE PROLOGUE of the Venerable Father, Brother Nicholas of Lyra, of the seraphic order of Saint Francis, on the commendation of Holy Scripture in general.

## Prologue on the Commendation of Holy Scripture

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0025B] « All these things are the book of life, » etc. [n: (Eccli. XXIII)]. ⟦s2+3⟧ According to what blessed Gregory says in Homily 35 on the Gospels: « Temporal life, compared with eternal life, [0025C] ought rather to be called death than life. » Now the sciences handed down by the philosophers are ordered to an end to be attained in the present life, because the practical sciences handed down by them are ordered to political happiness, speaking of the politics of the present life. ⟦s4⟧ Likewise the speculative sciences are ordered to contemplative happiness, speaking of the contemplation that can be had in the present life, and by way of nature, which depends upon the phantasm. ⟦s5⟧ Hence it is said in the third book *On the Soul* that « when we understand, we must at the same time contemplate phantasms. » And of such contemplation it is said in the same place that « understanding is corrupted in us when something within is corrupted. » But Holy Scripture is ordered to the happiness of the life to come, which the philosophers did not know, according to what blessed Jerome says in the epistle to Paulinus on all the books of Holy Scripture: « This the learned Plato did not know, this the eloquent Demosthenes was ignorant of. » From which it is concluded that the books written by the philosophers, containing [0025D] sciences ordered to an end to be attained only naturally in this temporal life, if they are compared with the books of Holy Scripture, which are ordered to the end of eternal life, ought rather to be called books of death than of life. ⟦s6⟧ But the book containing Holy Scripture (which, although it is divided into many partial books, is nevertheless contained under one book), which by a general name is called the Bible, is properly named the book of life, according to what is said in the word taken up at the start: *All these things,* etc.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ In which Holy Scripture is described in a fourfold way, according to four excellences by which it excels every other writing. ⟦s2⟧ For first it is described as of singular eminence, which the singular pronoun marks, when it is said, *haec* ("this"). ⟦s3⟧ Second, it is described as of general comprehensiveness, which the universal sign shows, when it is said, *all things.* ⟦s4⟧ Third, as of mirroring understanding, which the condition of a book denotes, when it is said, *book.* ⟦s5⟧ Fourth, as of saving efficacy, which the attainment of the intended end shows, when it is said, *of life.* ⟦s6⟧ Concerning the first, [0026B] it must be known that one science is more eminent or more honourable than another for a twofold cause, as is had in the first book *On the Soul.* ⟦s7⟧ One is, because it is about a nobler subject; [0026C] the other, because it proceeds in a more certain mode. ⟦s8⟧ And on account of both, Holy Scripture, which is properly called theology, since it alone is the text of this science, surpasses all sciences. ⟦s9⟧ First, because it has God for its subject, who is at the summit of all nobility: on account of which it is named theology, as it were discourse about God; second, because it proceeds in a more certain mode; for the other sciences, discovered in a human way, proceed through the investigation of human reason. ⟦s10⟧ In which [sic: *processis*] process, although there is no error as regards the knowledge of first principles, which are known through themselves [n: (secundum quod dicitur secundo Metaphys.: « In foribus quis delinquet » )], yet in the deduction of conclusions from the principles there can be error, most of all as regards conclusions far distant from the first principles. ⟦s11⟧ Hence also in such a process all the philosophers, relying only on human investigation, are found to have erred; and therefore of the singular eminence of this science it is said in the fourth chapter of Deuteronomy: « This is your wisdom, and understanding [0026D] before the peoples. » For that science is properly called wisdom which considers the highest causes, as is had in the first book of the *Metaphysics.* ⟦s12⟧ But Holy Scripture has God for its subject, as has been said, who is simply the first cause of all things, and therefore it is properly called Wisdom. ⟦s13⟧ But *your* is fittingly added, to distinguish the wisdom of the saints, or of catholics, which is Holy Scripture itself, from the wisdom of the philosophers; and this chiefly in two things, namely in the properties known of God, and in the end of the knowledge. ⟦s14⟧ For although the philosophers had knowledge of God, this was nevertheless only as regards the properties that can be concluded about him by an investigation of reason proceeding from creatures, as the Philosopher in the twelfth book of the *Metaphysics* proves his unity, and in the eighth of the *Physics* the infinity of his power, and so of like things. ⟦s15⟧ But the holy prophets and apostles, who handed this Scripture down to us by the revelation of the Holy Spirit, had knowledge of divine properties that transcend the investigation of reason, [0027A] as of the plurality of the persons, their distinction in the unity of the essence, and the like. ⟦s16⟧ Again, the philosophers did not order the knowledge they had of God to any other end, but sought it for the sake of contemplating only, inasmuch as it is the perfection of the one who knows. ⟦s17⟧ But the knowledge of God that is handed down in Holy Scripture is sought principally for this, that through it the one who contemplates or beholds may be borne into love of the very object known, namely God, loving him above himself and above all things. ⟦s18⟧ And for this reason the Saviour says in the twenty-second chapter of Matthew that on « the two precepts of charity the whole law hangs, and the prophets: » and the Apostle, in the first to Timothy, I: « The end of the precept is charity from a pure heart, and a good conscience, and faith unfeigned. » And because the philosophers, relying on their own sense, did not attend to this end, therefore they erred; for which reason their wisdom is named *foolishness*, in Romans I: [0027B] « Who, when they had known God, did not glorify him as God, or give thanks; but became vain in their thoughts, and their foolish heart was darkened: for professing themselves to be wise, they became fools. » But since Holy Scripture excels the others not only inasmuch as it is about a nobler subject, but also inasmuch as it proceeds in a more certain mode, namely through divine revelation, under which there can be nothing false: therefore there is added in the authority set down above: « And understanding before the peoples. » For understanding is properly called the habit of principles, about which there is no error, as has already been said: on account of which what is concluded directly from them is judged to be simply true, and what is found repugnant to them is judged to be simply false. ⟦s19⟧ So likewise whatever is manifestly concluded from Holy Scripture is judged true.; but what is repugnant to it is reckoned simply false: and therefore Holy Scripture is called not only wisdom, but also, together with this, understanding; [0027C] whence it is said in the fifteenth chapter of Ecclesiasticus: « He shall fill him with the spirit of wisdom and of understanding. » And in the first of Colossians: « That you may be filled with the knowledge of his will in all wisdom and understanding, » etc. ⟦s20⟧ And so the first is plain.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Concerning the second, it must be considered that under the consideration of any science there falls not only the subject, but also those things that have attribution to it, as is had in the fourth book of the *Metaphysics.* ⟦s2⟧ Just as under the consideration of the physician there falls not only health, or the body that can be healed, but also urine, inasmuch as it is indicative of health, and the potion, inasmuch as it is productive of it, and diet, inasmuch as it is preservative of it, and so of the others. ⟦s3⟧ Now all created things have attribution to God himself, who is the subject of this science; on account of which this science is in a certain way considerative of all things. ⟦s4⟧ In which appears its general comprehensiveness, which is marked in the word first taken up at the start, [0027D] when it is said, *all things;* of which may be expounded what is said in the first chapter of Wisdom: « That which contains all things has knowledge of the voice; » so that the sense is: That, namely the volume of Holy Scripture (which, although they are divided into several partial books, are nevertheless gathered into one volume which is called the Bible), contains all things, because this science is in a certain way considerative of all things. ⟦s5⟧ « Has knowledge of the voice: » it is a property of the voice that it be significative of a word, and it is proper to Holy Scripture that it be expressive of the divine Word, according to what is manifestly had in the first chapter of John: « In the beginning was the Word, » etc. ⟦s6⟧ And because all things were made through the Word, as is added in the same place, therefore all things are contained under the consideration of this science: not, however, under the proper aspects of each, because this belongs to the particular sciences discovered in a human way, but inasmuch as from all created things we are led by the hand into the knowledge and love of God through true faith formed by charity, to which the consideration of this science is ordered, as was said before. ⟦s7⟧ And this is what Augustine says in the fourteenth book *On the Trinity,* chapter one: « Not [0028A] indeed attributing to this science whatever can be known by man in human affairs, where there is a great deal of superfluous vanity and harmful curiosity, but only that by which the most wholesome faith, which leads to true beatitude, is begotten, nourished, defended, and strengthened. » Hence also in the sixteenth chapter of John the Saviour, speaking of the Holy Spirit, by whose revelation this Scripture was handed down, says: « He will teach you all truth; » the Gloss: « necessary for salvation. » And so the second is plain.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Concerning the third, which is mirroring understanding, which is marked when it is said, *book,* it must be considered that a book has a likeness to a mirror. ⟦s2⟧ For just as sensible forms appear in a mirror, so intelligible truths shine back in a book. ⟦s3⟧ Hence also the divine foreknowledge, in which all truths shine back, is called a *book:* in which book are read the holy apostles and prophets who handed down this science [cj: *leguntur*, "are read"; read *legunt*, "read"]. ⟦s4⟧ And this is what [0028B] the gloss says on that passage of the thirty-eighth chapter of Isaiah: « set thine house in order, » etc. ⟦s5⟧ The Gloss: « The prophets can read in the very book of God's foreknowledge, in which all things are written. » Which, however, is not to be understood as though the prophets saw the very essence of God, which is the same as his foreknowledge, because prophetic knowledge, since it has an enigma, is done away in such a vision, according to what the Apostle says in I Corinthians thirteen. ⟦s6⟧ But the prophets are said to have read in the book of God's foreknowledge to this extent, that through species divinely impressed on the minds of the prophets, and through a prophetic light proportioned to them, they saw in their own way the truth derived to them from God's knowledge by revelation. ⟦s7⟧ Just as the possible intellect, by virtue of the light of the agent intellect, beholds in the species received from the phantasms truths proportionable to itself. ⟦s8⟧ But we, who do not have such knowledge, who have not been illumined by prophetic light, [0028C] cannot read in the said book, but in the book of Holy Scripture handed down to us by the prophets.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ This book, however, has this special property, that one letter contains several senses. ⟦s2⟧ The reason for which is that the principal author of this book is God himself: in whose power it is not only to use words to signify something (which men also can do and do), but he also uses the things signified by the words to signify other things: and therefore it is common to all books that words signify something, but special indeed to this book that the things signified by the words signify something else. ⟦s3⟧ According, then, to the first signification, which is through words, is taken the literal or historical sense: but according to the other signification, which is through the things themselves, is taken the mystical or spiritual sense, which is threefold in general; for if the things signified by the words are referred to signifying what is to be believed in the new law, so is taken the allegorical sense; but if they are referred to [0028D] signifying what is to be done by us, so it is the moral or tropological sense; but if they are referred to signifying what is to be hoped for in the future beatitude, so it is the anagogical sense. ⟦s4⟧ And it is named from ἀνάγω, which is, I lift upward. ⟦s5⟧ Whence the verses: The letter teaches deeds; what you should believe, allegory;
⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ The moral, what you should do; where you should tend, anagogy.
