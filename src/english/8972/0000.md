SECOND PROLOGUE. On the intention of the author and the manner of proceeding.

## Prologue on the Intention of the Author

[0029B] « I saw in the right hand of him that sat upon the throne a book written within and without. » [n: (Apoc. V.)] As was said in the preceding prologue, this book is Holy Scripture, which is said to be written without, as regards the literal sense, and within, as regards the mystical and spiritual sense. Which, although it is divided threefold [0029C] in general, as was said before; and although under any member a multiplication of mystical expositions can be made in particular; all of them nevertheless presuppose the literal sense as a foundation. Hence, just as a building that leans away from its foundation is disposed to ruin, so a mystical exposition that departs from the literal sense is to be reckoned unbecoming and unfit, or at least, other things being equal, less becoming and less fit; and therefore for those wishing to make progress in the study of Holy Scripture it is necessary to begin from the understanding of the literal sense: most of all since from the literal sense alone, and not from the mystical, can an argument be made for the proof or declaration of any doubtful point, according to what Augustine says in the epistle against Vincent the Donatist.

It must further be considered that the literal sense, from which one must begin, as has been said, seems much obscured in modern days: partly by the fault of scribes, [0029D] who on account of the likeness of letters have in many places written otherwise than the truth of the text has; partly by the unskilfulness of certain correctors, who in many places have made points where they ought not to be made; and have begun or ended verses where they ought not to be begun and ended: and by this the meaning of the letter is altered, as will appear in its places in proceeding below, the Lord granting; partly from the manner of our translation, which in many places has otherwise than the Hebrew books, according to what Jerome declares in the book: on Hebrew, questions, and in many other places: and other expositors of Holy Scripture, reading or writing, say the same; and yet according to Jerome in the second prologue on Genesis, and in many other places, for having the truth of the letter in the scripture of the Old Testament recourse must be had to the codices of the Hebrews. In this, however, great caution must be used as regards the places of the Scripture of the Old Testament that speak of the deity of Christ and of what follows upon it: some of which the Jews [0030B] have corrupted in defence of their error, as I have partly declared in a certain question on the divinity of Christ, and shall declare more fully when such places occur, the Lord granting. But in those in which it is not likely that they have changed anything, [0030C] since neither did they have a cause for doing this, there seems to be no danger, but rather it is safer, according to the saying of blessed Jerome, in doubtful cases to have recourse to the Hebrew text, as to the original, for declaring the truth of the text.

It must also be known that the literal sense is much overshadowed on account of the manner of expounding commonly handed down by others: who, although they have said many good things, yet have touched little on the literal sense, and have so multiplied the mystical senses that the literal sense, intercepted among so many mystical expositions, is partly smothered. Again, they have divided the text into so many little parts, and brought in so many concordances to their purpose, that they partly confound the understanding and the memory, drawing the mind away from the understanding of the literal sense. Proposing therefore to avoid these and similar things, with the help of God I intend to insist upon the literal sense: and sometimes to insert very few and brief mystical expositions, [0030D] though rarely. Likewise I intend to bring in, for the declaration of the literal sense, the sayings not only of the catholic doctors, but also of the Hebrew ones, most of all of Rabbi Solomon, who among the Hebrew doctors has spoken most reasonably. I shall also sometimes, though very rarely, insert certain very absurd sayings of the Hebrews, not for holding them, or following them; but so that through these it may appear how great a blindness has befallen Israel, according to the saying of Paul the apostle in the eleventh chapter of Romans: for which reason also one must not cleave to the sayings of the Hebrews, except so far as they accord with reason and with the truth of the letter. Again, the prologues being omitted, I shall begin from the beginning of Genesis: both because I do not believe the remainder of my life to suffice for the exposition of the whole of Holy Scripture, and therefore I have not wished to linger in expounding the sayings of blessed Jerome, or of any other doctor whatever; and because the said prologues do little for the understanding of the books that follow, as it seems to me. I have, however, expounded the prologues of certain books on which I wrote before [0031A] I began from the book of Genesis. Lastly, because I am not so skilled in the Hebrew or Latin tongue that I cannot fall short in many things, I therefore protest that I intend to say nothing assertively, or determinatively, except as regards those things that have been manifestly determined by Holy Scripture or by the authority of the Church: but let all the rest be taken as said scholastically and by way of exercise. Wherefore I submit all things said and to be said to the correction of holy mother Church, and of any wise man, earnestly asking for a pious reader and a charitable corrector. Nevertheless, before I descend to the exposition of the letter, I set down beforehand seven rules for expounding Holy Scripture, which Isidore touches on in the first book *On the Highest Good*, chapter twenty. And these rules are called by some keys, because through them the understanding of Scripture is opened in many things.

The first is about the Lord Jesus Christ and his [0031B] mystical body, which is the Church: because on account of the connexion of the head to the body, Holy Scripture, under one context, as it were under the same person, sometimes speaks of both; and passes from the one to the other; for example, Isa. LXI: « He hath clothed me with the garments of salvation, and with the robe of justice he hath compassed me, as a bridegroom adorned with a crown, and as a bride adorned with her jewels. » For what is said here, « as a bridegroom, » etc., is understood of Christ, and what is added, « as a bride, » etc., is understood of the Church herself. Likewise Cant. 1: « Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth, for thy breasts are better than wine. » For when it is said, « let him kiss me, » etc., it is the word of the bride desiring to enjoy the bridegroom, and what is added, « for thy breasts are better, » etc., is the word of the bridegroom commending the bride. Hence, in things so connected, for the reason stated, the prudent reader ought to attend to what befits the head and what the body.

[0031C]The second rule is about the Lord's true body and his feigned body. For the Church, which is the mystical body of the Lord, as was said before, is like a net not yet drawn to the shore: and therefore it has the bad mixed with the good until the judgment, in which these will be separated from those; and therefore in Holy Scripture sometimes the bad are commended together with the good with whom they are mixed: as in Osee eleven: « Israel was a child, and I loved him. » And conversely sometimes the good are reproached together with the bad, as in Isaiah I: « The ox knoweth his owner, and the ass his master's crib: but Israel hath not known me: and my people hath not understood. » Sometimes also in the same text it is expressed what belongs to the good, and what to the bad, as is said in Canticles I: « I am black, but beautiful, daughter [var: Vulg. *filiae*, "daughters"] of Jerusalem, as the tents of Cedar, as the curtains of Solomon. » These are the words of the bride, who by reason of the bad [0031D] contained in the Church says: « I am black; » but by reason of the good adds: « but beautiful. » But what is added (as it were with an example) as the tents of Cedar, » is referred to the bad: for Cedar was the son of Ismael, as is had in Gen. XXV, from whom the Saracens descended. But what is added, « as the curtains of Solomon, » is referred to us. For by Solomon here is understood God himself, according to the catholic and Hebrew expositors; and therefore those are called his curtains with which the tabernacle was covered, in which the good worshipped God.

The third rule is about the spirit and the letter, according as the historical and the mystical sense are taken under the same letter: because the truth of the history is to be held, and yet it is to be referred to a spiritual understanding; in this way this rule is commonly expounded. It can also be expounded otherwise, so that it is referred to the literal sense only, as the others are too. Concerning which it must be considered that the same letter sometimes has a twofold literal sense; for example, in I Paral. XVII the Lord says of Solomon: « I will be to him a father, and he shall be [0032A] to me a son. » And it is understood of Solomon according to the letter, inasmuch as he was a Son of God by adoption in his youth: on account of which Nathan the prophet called him *Beloved of the Lord,* as is had in II Kings XII The aforesaid authority, « I will be to him a father, » etc., is also brought forward by the Apostle in Hebrews one, as said of Christ according to the letter: which is plain from this, that the Apostle brings it forward to prove that Christ was greater than the angels; but such a proof cannot be made through the mystical sense, as Augustine says against Vincent the Donatist, as was alleged above. For the aforesaid authority was fulfilled according to the letter in Solomon, but less perfectly, because he was a son of God by grace only, but in Christ more perfectly, who is the Son of God by nature. Now although both expositions are literal simply, the second nevertheless, which is about Christ, is spiritual and mystical [0032B] in a certain respect, inasmuch as Solomon was a figure of Christ.

The fourth rule is about species and genus, or about part and whole, when it passes from the one to the other, and conversely, as in Isa. XIII. First it speaks against Babylon specially, when it is said: « The burden of Babylon, » etc., and it passes to understanding the word of the whole world generally, by what is added: « From the height of heaven the Lord, and the instruments of his wrath, to destroy the whole land. » Afterwards it returns to speaking against Babylon specially, when it is said: « Behold, I will stir up the Medes against you [var: Vulg. *eos*, "against them"], who shall not seek silver, » etc., because Darius the Mede with Cyrus his nephew took Babylonia, and slew Balthasar the king of Babylon, as is had in the fifth chapter of Daniel.
