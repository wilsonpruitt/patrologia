## *On the first prologue of the Postill, which begins:* All these things are the book of life. *Likewise on the second prologue of the same Postill, which begins:* I saw in the right hand of him that sat upon the throne, etc. (cont.)

Concerning the second, namely whether every passage or text of sacred Scripture is expounded in the fourfold sense touched on above, it must be said that it is not; for Augustine says, II on Gen., In some things the literal sense alone is to be sought. Which is manifestly plain: for there are many things in the canonical books, of the Old Testament as well as of the New, which have nothing but the literal sense only. Which is plain in many things, and especially in the two precepts of charity, on which the whole law hangs, and the prophets, as is had in Matth. XXII. For although [0040C] in these precepts, namely: « Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with thy whole heart, and thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself, » many things are contained that are to be expounded, or are expoundable, pertaining as well to the object of charity as to its interior or exterior act, as also to its order, and other things of this kind: all those things nevertheless pertain to the literal sense only. For in this place the things signified by this word *God,* or by this word *neighbour,* and by this word *thou shalt love,* do not again signify other things, from which things so signified a spiritual sense might further be taken: hence the whole of what is handed down in the exposition of these precepts pertains only to their literal sense. And so commonly of the other moral precepts of the Old Testament, and of many other passages, which I omit for the sake of brevity. In the Gospels also many things are handed down to which one need not assign a mystical sense, but only a literal one: as in those things which are handed down as precepts, [0040D] as when it is said in John XIV: « You believe in God, believe also in me, » and the like. Likewise in those things which are given as counsels, as when it is said in Matthew XIX: « If thou wilt be perfect, go and sell all that thou hast and give to the poor. » In which, and the like, the spiritual sense is not to be sought, which would be taken from the things signified by the words: but only the literal sense, which is taken from the signification of the words, as is manifest. Likewise when in Luke VIII Christ interprets his parable, saying: « The seed is the word of God, » etc., it is manifest that those interpreting words need not have other spiritual senses, since the interpretation itself is expository of the preceding parable. Likewise the Epistles of Paul, which ought to be reckoned one of the chief parts of the New Testament, have throughout almost only the literal sense. For there he speaks as the universal doctor of the Church, expounding and harmonizing both Testaments. For although [0041A] in those Epistles deep and difficult things pertaining to faith, and the declaration of both Testaments, are handed down, they are nevertheless not handed down allegorically or anagogically, but only literally; for he himself, speaking historically or literally, shows of certain Scriptures how they are to be understood in the spiritual sense: as when in Galatians IV he says of the two sons of Abraham, « which things are said by an allegory. » Therefore the words of the Apostle are not to be allegorized, or moralized, and the like, but rather to be unfolded: so that from them true literal senses may be drawn out, from which, by proceeding discursively, many, great, and deep things are inferred by argument: as in the other human sciences many things are inferred discursively from certain and true principles, as in 1 p. q. 1, art. 7; all which things indeed pertain to the literal sense, from which they are rightly deduced.

And so it is plain that not every passage of sacred Scripture is to be expounded in the fourfold sense: but, as St. Thomas notably teaches in the 7th Quodlibet, q. 24, sacred Scripture is sometimes to be expounded in those four [0041B] senses, sometimes in three, sometimes in two, sometimes in one only. Hence those things which according to the literal sense regard the deeds of the Old Testament can be expounded in four senses; likewise those things which are said to the letter of Christ the head can be expounded literally of the deeds of Christ, allegorically by referring them to his mystical body, and morally by referring the same to our acts, which ought to be ruled according to him, and anagogically inasmuch as in Christ himself the way of glory has been shown to us. But those things which according to the literal sense are said of the Church can be expounded morally and anagogically. And those things which according to the letter are said morally are not customarily expounded except anagogically. But those things which according to the literal sense pertain to the state of glory are customarily expounded in no other sense, because that state is not a figure of other things, but is figured by all other things. All which things indeed, and others pertaining to this, are declared more fully in the said question.

[0041C]Concerning the third, namely: of every passage or text of sacred Scripture, is there only one literal sense? and it seems that there is not. For according to Augustine in the book *On the Usefulness of Believing*, three things pertain to the literal sense: of which the first is when the letter is simply expounded; and this he calls the historical sense. The second is when the cause of what is contained in the letter is assigned or expounded; which sense he calls etymological. The third is when the truth of that letter is shown not to be repugnant to the truth of another Scripture; which he calls anagogical. For example, when one reads Gen. II: « The Lord God built the rib which he had taken from Adam into a woman, » etc. The historical sense of that saying is when what is contained in the letter is simply expounded. But the etymological sense is when the cause is given why the woman was formed from the side of the man, and not from another part of the body; likewise why the rib was taken away from him sleeping and not waking, as is treated in the second book, dist. [0041D] 18. But the anagogical sense is when it is shown that the truth of this saying is not repugnant to the truth of another Scripture, where it is said in Gen. I of the creation of man: « Male and female he created them, » etc. From which letter it appears that male and female of the human species were created together at the same time, and not successively, in such a way that the woman would have been taken out of his side after the creation of the male.

But to one who attends well, it is not had from this consideration of Augustine that three kinds of exposition are contained under the literal sense in such a way that each of them is properly the literal sense: but the first exposition is only the literal sense, while the second is a rendering of the cause of that literal sense, and the third is a resolving of its contrariety. Hence the second and third expositions, although they pertain to the literal sense, because through them it is further perfected or elucidated, are nevertheless not properly to be called literal senses. Of which three kinds of exposition [0042A] pertaining to the literal sense may be understood that saying of II Esdr. VIII, where it is said: « And they read in the book of the Law of God openly and distinctly, and they applied themselves to understanding [var: Vulg. *distincte, et aperte ad intelligendum*, "distinctly, and plainly to be understood", without *apposuerunt*]. » For in that it is said, « they read in the book openly, » can be understood the exposition itself of the letter, which is as it were the door of all the expositions that follow, without the opening of which no one can rightly enter into the other expositions. And when it is said, « and distinctly, » there is understood the sense named anagogical by Augustine, because through proper and true distinctions are resolved the contrarieties which sometimes appear in the sacred Scriptures. And in that which follows: « And they applied themselves to understanding, » can be understood the knowledge of the cause of the law, or of the history, or of some saying of sacred Scripture. For since to understand is to read within, he who through knowledge of the effect arrives at knowledge of the cause is properly said to understand; which sense indeed is called etymological by Augustine.

But it must be further observed that under each kind [0042B] of the aforesaid three expositions pertaining, as has been said, to the literal sense, there can be many species. For not only one cause, but several, can be assigned for one saying: likewise contrarieties can be resolved not only in one way, but in several, as is manifest.

Likewise, as to the simple exposition of Scripture, which arises from the primary signification of the word, it is manifest that several literal senses are handed down, not only by expositors or writers commonly, but also by holy doctors, as is plain in Gen. I, in which place, as the Master says in the second book, dist. 13, certain of the holy Fathers who searched out the words and secrets of God seem to have written as it were diverse things. And so it is plain that it is not the case that of every text of sacred Scripture there is only one literal sense.

But if it be said against this that, although diverse men attribute diverse senses to some sacred letter, it does not follow from this that there are several literal senses of such a [0042C] letter; but that only that one is literal which the author intends: to this it must be said that, according to St. Thomas in the First Part, q. I, because the author of sacred Scripture is God, who comprehends all things at once by his simple intellect, it is not unfitting, as Augustine says in III *Confessions*, if even according to the literal sense of sacred Scripture there are several senses: which is to be understood provided that such senses are conformable to the letter, and that they are not repugnant to the determination of the Church or to right reason, as has been said: likewise that they are compatible with one another. But when it happens that two literal senses impassible with one another are attributed to some sacred authority, it is necessary to say that only one of them is literal: as when, in Daniel X, concerning the prince of the kingdom of the Persians, it is handed down by Blessed Gregory in the *Moral* that he was a good angel who presided over the kingdom of the Persians, while others say the opposite; and so it happens in many other cases.

Likewise it must be observed that the literal sense of sacred [0042D] Scripture is of such capacity or power that there are contained in it virtually all things pertaining to faith, or to good morals and virtues. Hence the Master, who compiled the sentences of the Fathers in a brief volume, into the exposition of which volume the later doctors or writers have brought in, in a manner, all theological matters, founded his whole work on the testimonies of this literal sense; for he says thus in his prologue: « We have put together the volume, God granting, from testimonies of truth founded for ever. » And it is certain that the testimonies of truth founded for ever are the testimonies of sacred Scripture taken according to the literal sense, from which sense alone a testimony, or argument, can be received, according to Augustine where cited above.
