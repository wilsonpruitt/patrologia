## *On the first prologue of the Postill, which begins:* All these things are the book of life. *Likewise on the second prologue of the same Postill, which begins:* I saw in the right hand of him that sat upon the throne, etc. (cont.)

But concerning this it must be considered that not all things which are had from sacred Scripture according to the literal sense are immediate, in such a way that they are understood from the primary signification of the letters: but sometimes there is required the addition of some truth, from which truth, joined to the literal [0043A] sense, another truth may be deduced. As when Christ, in Luke XX, from the testimony of the letter which says: « The God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, the God of Jacob, » concludes against the Sadducees, who denied that there is another life of men after the present one, arguing thus: « God is of the dead or of the living [var: Vulg. (Luke 20:38) *Deus autem non est mortuorum, sed vivorum*, "but he is not the God of the dead, but of the living"], » that is to say: since from the literal sense we have it of God, and that he is the God of Abraham, etc.: and reason dictates that God is said to be God in respect of something existing in the nature of things: therefore it follows that Abraham and Isaac were in the nature of things, which the Sadducees denied. And it is certain that this conclusion is not deduced from the sense of that letter: « The God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, » etc., unless the other true proposition mentioned above be added; and so the point is plain. And it can also happen that such a conclusion is concluded from the sacred letter by one deduction, or by several successive ones: just as it happens in the sciences, or the human liberal arts, that some conclusions are deduced at once from first principles naturally known, but others through many middle terms ordered one to another, as is manifest. [0043B] The true literal sense, therefore, which arises from the primary signification of the letter is to be called the first literal sense; while the others that follow are secondary senses, whose certainty depends on the certainty of the first sense, which is more certain than the secondary senses; just as first principles naturally known are more certain than the others deduced from them. Which consideration is indeed to be attended to in many passages of sacred Scripture.

Concerning the fourth, namely: when several literal senses are handed down of one authority of sacred Scripture, which of them is to be preferred to the others? it must be said that in this it does not seem that a single general rule can be given; but there are certain things to be considered concerning it, presupposing those things which are said above, in the first article, about the literal sense. The first is that that sense seems preferable whose opinion rests more upon reason. Hence in the Decrees, dist. 20, c. *Decretales:* « For the more each man rests upon reason, the greater the authority his words seem to have. » Likewise [0043C] that sense seems to be preferable to the others which accords more with the letter. Hence, according to Augustine in III *On Christian Doctrine*, doubtful things are to be determined according to the opinion which is taken from the plainer places of the Scriptures. And it is certain that the plainer places are those which accord more plainly with the letter. Likewise, other things being equal, the literal sense which is handed down by the holy doctors is to be preferred to the other senses handed down by other expositors; whence in the Decrees, c. *Allegato,* in the expositions of the sacred letters the holy doctors are placed before the supreme pontiffs, and *a fortiori* before other writers. Likewise, where two senses of the letter are had, neither of which is repugnant to the authority of the Church, nor to right reason, yet one is handed down by catholics and the other by unbelievers, then the sense of the catholics is to be preferred: both because it is rather to be presumed that those who are in the bosom of the Church, to which Christ promised the Spirit of truth, have the true understanding of sacred Scripture, [0043D] than unbelievers, who commonly oppose the truth of the faith; and so that occasion may not be given to unbelievers for believing this, that they themselves expound sacred Scripture better than catholics. Hence Pope Leo, in a certain epistle to the bishop of Alexandria, says that the enemies of the cross of Christ lie in wait for our deeds and words, therefore let us give them no occasion, even a slight one. By which reasoning it seems that, when some letter of sacred Scripture is expounded in the same way by catholics and by unbelievers, then the authority of such an exposition is to be attributed to the catholics, and not to the unbelievers, for the reason aforesaid; especially if such an exposition issued first from catholics: the contrary of which is found in some manner in the *Postill*, as will be plain below, in Genesis I, where it is asked why on the second day it is not said: « And God saw that it was good, » as on the other days. Yet because, where a plurality of literal senses occurs, it rarely happens that one of the aforesaid senses excels the others in all [0044A] respects, but rather one of those senses excels in one way, and another in another; therefore in such matters that saying of Rom. XIV seems to have place: « Let every man abound in his own sense, » saving always the reverence and authority of the saints.

Concerning the fifth, namely: can an effective argument be taken from any literal sense whatever of sacred Scripture? it must be said that, although an effective argument can be taken from the literal sense of sacred Scripture alone, as is plain through Augustine where cited above: yet an effective argument is not taken from any literal sense whatever of sacred Scripture. For since there are many texts in sacred Scripture which are expounded diversely even by the holy doctors, as above in the third article, it follows that from none of such senses can an effective argument be taken; because so one would be arguing as it were from a disjunctive to one of its parts. But well, if senses of this kind, though diverse, agree in something, an effective argument can be taken from there, at least as to that in which they agree; as in Dan. IX, in the computation of the seventy [0044B] weeks; where, although the expositors, Hebrews as well as Latins, differ among themselves, not only as to the term from which the weeks take their beginning, but also in the progress of the computation: yet because according to all of them their final term has long since passed into the past, therefore from that authority an effective argument is taken for concluding that Christ has already come, as will be plain in that place.

From which it follows that, since the literal sense of sacred Scripture in certain places is not the same with all, it can therefore happen that from some literal sense a valid argument could be taken against some, for instance against those holding the same literal sense, from which sense nevertheless a valid argument cannot be taken against others, namely those holding another exposition or literal sense: which diversity indeed happens rather often between us and the unbelievers. For there are some texts of sacred Scripture whose sense, which is literal and true among catholics, [0044C] is not sufficient for an effective argument to be taken from it against unbelievers or heretics, but it will not be taken away by this that such a sense of the catholics is true and is to be reckoned literal among catholics. For example, when the Psalmist says: « By the word of the Lord the heavens were established [n: (Psal. XXXII)], » etc., the true and literal sense among catholics is that by the word of the Lord, that is, by the Son of the Father, the heavens were established: which exposition is indeed among catholics as it were necessary, and consonant also with what is said in John I: « And the Word was with God, and all things by him, » that is, by the Word, « were made. » But from this sense no effective argument is taken against unbelievers who do not receive the Gospel: for they do not grant that there is any real distinction in divine things, such that there is some divine person proceeding eternally from another by way of a mental word. Hence, when it is said: « By the word of the Lord the heavens were established, » they say that here the Psalmist speaks in human fashion: just as when some things are done at the command [0044D] of some lord, it is said that they were done by his word. Yet from this authority a valid argument is taken among the faithful concerning the distinction of persons in divine things, as in the first book of the *Sentences*, dist. 2. From which it follows that a catholic expounding that authority of the Psalmist ought to expound it in a catholic manner, not caring whether an effective argument could be taken from it against unbelievers who deny the Trinity, or not: because it is not necessary that an argument be taken from every literal sense among all, as has already been said. Which question, nevertheless, the postillator seems to attend to in this place, and in not a few others, as will be plain in their places: for he seems to think that there is no literal sense except that from which a valid argument is taken even against adversaries, which nevertheless is not so to be understood, as has been said.
