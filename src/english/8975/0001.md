## To the Reader (cont.)

[0016B]But neither do we assume so much to ourselves as to think that we have committed no error at all in revising this work: since we too fell into difficulties equally troublesome; since one of our colleagues was kept from the work for almost a whole year by a long and very grave illness, and two others, taken up by the authority of superiors to the governance of souls and drawn away from the shades of the Academy to the heat of pastoral care, themselves also caused no small delay to the edition, and made the expectation of our bringing forth this offspring (as the Parisians said of their edition) a three-year one. Nevertheless we dare to say that the reader will not find errata of great moment, nor even frequent ones of a lighter mark: of which we observed there was an almost infinite number in other editions.

[0016C]But know thou, most courteous reader, that nothing at all has been changed by us in the old gloss, not the order, not the very words, except when we found them to be false or corrupt, from manuscript and ancient copies, and from the very originals of the saints; as neither did we change anything in Lyra's literal and moral *Postill*; hence some rather trifling etymologies, and certain grammatical slips (because they seemed to have been so written by the author himself, for postillators are not rarely wont to play the fool in these derivations) we left altogether untouched. But those things which were added to the old Gloss by the Parisians are marked with an asterisk or single little star; those which we added have a double asterisk shining before them, and by those marks they are distinguished from the authorities [0016D] of the old Gloss; and the same judgment holds of certain annotations which we have here and there inserted in Lyra's *Postill*, namely with little stars prefixed, so that they may thus be recognized to be not Lyra's words, but ours.

In the *Replies* of Mathias Tornigus, however, we assumed a little more authority to ourselves; namely, not rarely we struck out certain abuses which he heaped up against the venerable Burgensis too intemperately and too often. For that unseemly petulance, unworthy of a learned man, did not befit the edition of so necessary a work, and one which was to be in the hands of all, even of the enemies of the Church. For that author so defends his Lyra as to show openly that he does not [0017A] go down into the contest with Burgensis for the sake of finding the truth, but only lest Lyra should seem justly reproved; and although Lyra is most justly taxed by Burgensis in many things, and Lyra himself assumes to himself by the right of a doctor the licence of attacking others: Tornigus nevertheless takes it ill that Lyra is censured in those places where he deservedly comes to be censured, and there he pulls a contentious rope; and whereas it matters little to the Postillator's glory whether he is censured rightly or otherwise in indifferent matters which pertain neither to faith nor to morals; there Tornigus loads the venerable bishop, a holy man and one who deserved excellently of the Church of Spain, with abuses, and most frivolously [0017B] casts upon a faithful confessor the reproach of Judaism (as if salvation had not proceeded from the Jews, and as if his Lyra had not also once been a Jew). Those abuses, therefore, we deleted almost all of them, because they occurred too frequently: but beyond this, nothing; nor did we wish to change anything even in him.

And this, indeed, concerning our design, and concerning what we have accomplished in this edition (God leading). But perhaps it will not be unwelcome if we weave in some things about the very authors of the Glosses and Postills, collected chiefly from Sixtus of Siena and other learned men.

Therefore the principal author of the ordinary Gloss (namely that which is set around the sacred text) is celebrated by all as WALAFRID STRABUS, a Benedictine monk of the monastery of Fulda in Germany (another than Strabo, the English monk, brother of the Venerable Bede), who acquired that surname of Strabus from a defect of the eyes, a hearer, and scribe and amanuensis, of Rabanus Maurus, Benedictine abbot of the same monastery, [0017C] and afterwards archbishop of Mainz: a man abundantly polished in secular literature, most skilled in divine Scripture, and delighted by varied and assiduous reading of the ancient authors, a poet, too, not unsuccessful for that age. His, then, is this collection of expositions on Scripture, which posterity valued so highly that they called it as it were the ordinary gloss, or the very tongue of Scripture, by which namely we might understand Scripture speaking to us, since, reading the sacred words without it, we should think we were hearing a tongue which we had not known. The Master of the Sentences calls this Gloss κατ' ἕξοχὴν *the authority*, Book IV, d. 4; for, adducing the Epistle to the Hebrews, chapter VI, he speaks thus: « Hence the authority says that the Apostle spoke in the plural, the foundation of baptisms, because there is [0017D] baptism in water, in blood, in penance, » etc.; all of which thou wilt find in the ordinary gloss on that chapter. Other writers, however, seem to have enriched it afterwards, as Trithemius judges in his Catalogue, and as appears from the name of Remigius often set beside it, who is later in age than Strabus himself; unless perhaps we say that the name of Remigius was noted in place of Augustine, from whom Remigius, after his custom, took most things. For neither in the manuscript copies of the Gloss are the names of the authors noted everywhere, but this was the work of the diligence of later men. Now Strabus flourished under Louis Augustus, in the year of the Lord 840.

But the author of the interlinear Gloss is Anselm of Laon, also a Benedictine monk, surnamed [0018A] the Scholastic, a Gaul by nation, learned by continual study in the divine Scriptures and the volumes of the sacred doctors, and instructed not moderately in all disciplines, sharp in intellect, concise in eloquence, and a lover of a certain Laconic brevity. Whence also by a new and unaccustomed manner of explanation he expounded the books of both Testaments by certain very brief summaries of annotations taken from the Fathers: which, written in the tiniest characters, he fitted, with an industry no less curious than useful, between the very lines of the Scriptures inscribed in larger letters: and he called that labour the interlinear gloss, to distinguish it from the gloss of Strabus, which is written in the margins of the sacred text. He was renowned under the emperor Henry IV, in the year of the Lord 1110. He once had a [0018B] contest, when already an old man, with Peter Abelard, a monk of the same institute, who then, swelling with the pride of philosophy, spoke not soberly enough about matters of faith: though afterwards, corrected, and living holily in the monastery of Cluny under Peter the Venerable, he came to rest in a praiseworthy end. Each true gloss, as well the ordinary as the interlinear, was always of the greatest authority among all theologians, as can be manifest to anyone from Alexander of Hales, St Thomas, and the other principal masters of the school who everywhere make use of their authority.

Concerning Nicholas of Lyra Sixtus of Siena has this: « Nicholas of Lyra, English by nation or, as some write, from Lyra, a town of Brabant, a Jew by race, and imbued with Hebrew letters from earliest boyhood, but after [0018C] his conversion devoted to the Franciscan religion, a professor of scholastic theology, and a not ignoble expositor of its Sentences; endowed also with so true, pure and genuine an understanding of the divine Scriptures that in expounding them he had no equal among the most learned theologians of his time. He published on each of the volumes of both the Old and the New Testament eighty-five books of scholastic *Postill*, not only useful to students of the holy Scriptures, and especially to beginners hastening on to greater things, but even very necessary. In the first fifty of these books he expounded the whole body of Holy Scripture without the allurement of oratory and the wrappings of words, in plain and open speech: with the utmost diligence in each opening up and elucidating [0018D] the sense of the letter, which is as much more excellent than the other senses of divine Scripture as it is more difficult; bringing to this not only the expositions of the ancient Fathers of the Church, but also the opinions of the Rabbis, whose authority is chief among the Jews. This work completed, taking up the labour again, in the thirty-five later books he illustrated both Instruments with a moral exposition. He flourished under Pope John XXII, and the emperor Louis the Bavarian. » Thus Sixtus. Lyra published besides a third work on the whole Scripture of the Old Testament, which work he entitled "Of Differences," in which he most diligently noted all the expressions in which the Vulgate interpreter seemed to differ from the Hebrew source; this is extant separately, printed, [0019A] though it is very rarely found. But as to Sixtus's saying that Nicholas is thought by some to be English, I do not know on what foundation they rely. Those who think him a Brabanter have in their favour his epitaph which stood in the town of Lyra between Antwerp and Mechelen, in which this distich is found among other things: To whom Brabant most kindly gave the surname of ancient Lyra,
For he was sprung from the city of Lyra.
And the epitaph is closed with this distich Hence the hierophant sits with God, shining with a diadem,
And a glory worthy of the Brabantine soil.
Likewise in the edition of the Gloss made at Basel in the year 1520 there is found at the end of the sixth part: *The holy Bible with the ordinary gloss,* etc.; and, *Here ends the Postill of Nicholas of Lyra the Brabanter.* Now Nicholas of Lyra died in the great [0019B] Parisian college of the Minors in the year 1349, on the 14th day of October, where also an epitaph is found, from which others deduce that this same Nicholas was a Norman, from the little town of Lyre in Normandy; and a probable conjecture will also favour this, that he often uses examples from the French tongue, but nowhere from the Brabantine, or English. Let the reader choose what he pleases.

There follows Paul Burgensis, a Spaniard by nation, surnamed de Sancta Maria, a Hebrew by race, once a most eminent Rabbi among his fellow Hebrews; and after his conversion to the Christian faith, bishop first of New Carthage and then of Burgos in Spain, arch-chancellor of Castile and León: for the sake of his son Alphonsus, dean of the church of Compostela (whom he had begotten as a layman of [0019C] a wife), he discussed very many places from the several books of Holy Scripture, in which he tried to correct and supplement the explanations of Lyra, and he entitled the work *Additions*. He flourished under the emperor Sigismund, in the year of the Lord 1430. He wrote besides a notable work against Jewish perfidy, which he called *The Scrutiny of the Scriptures*. He was a man of the greatest authority in Spain, given as tutor to the boy king for his faith and prudence, and of known piety. With special affection he followed the doctrine of St Thomas and the order of the Dominicans, for whom he also built and endowed a splendid monastery in his city. And that city and the region of Burgos he so strengthened in the faith of Christ the Lord by the force of the divine word and by his zeal, that none thereafter [0020A] of them or of their descendants is known to have fallen away from that same faith, as Navarrus testifies in his counsels. And therefore his family is still celebrated and of approved nobility; called de Sancta Maria, which alone in Spain holds citizenship, and is admitted to all the honours of the commonwealth, all other families of Hebrew blood being excluded.

Against this Paul Burgensis there writes in these commentaries Matthew Dornick, or Tornigus, a Saxon by nation, a Minorite, once minister of the province of Saxony, an excellent professor of scholastic theology, and having entered the fight in defence of his doctor Nicholas of Lyra, he strove to overturn the additions of Burgensis, publishing new disputations, which he set beneath the commentaries of Lyra immediately after the additions of Burgensis, and [0020B] entitled *Replies,* or the correctory of the Burgensian corruptory. In these, though he noted many things learnedly enough, he nevertheless could not abstain from abuses, as we said, with which he assailed Burgensis quite undeservedly. He flourished under the emperor Frederick III, in the year of the Lord 1440.

Thou must moreover be advised, kind reader, that we have left the sacred text itself entirely in that form in which we found it in the ancient editions of the gloss and of Lyra: because thus it seemed to agree better with the expositions of Lyra himself: who did not everywhere read in the same way as our Vulgate now emended by the pontiffs. But at the end of each tome we have added the Roman corrections collected by D. Francis Lucas Burgensis, and to them we have joined our own conjectures, by which we wished to indicate what the Roman Fathers could have followed [0020C] in those corrections, and why from the various readings they approved these rather than those.
