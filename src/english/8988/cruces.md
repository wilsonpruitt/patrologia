### The three `[var: …]` markers

**0633B · *accipiens etiam recordationem fidei*** — 2 Tim I, 5 reads *recordationem accipiens
**ejus** fidei*. Migne inverts the first two words and, more materially, prints ***etiam***
where the Vulgate has ***ejus***. The change is load-bearing for the gloss, which closes
*cum **etiam** in feminis fuerit firma* — the gloss has picked up the *etiam* the lemma
introduced, and lost the *ejus* that in the Vulgate points at Timothy's own faith. Marker:
`[var: Vulg. *recordationem accipiens ejus fidei*, "receiving the recollection of that faith"]`.

**0633D · *Formam habens*** — 2 Tim I, 13 reads ***Formam habe***, an imperative addressed to
Timothy. Migne prints the participle at **both** occurrences (0633D and the VERS. 13 lemma at
0634B) and his gloss builds on the participle: *similiter ergo et tu formam habens, etc.* So
this is his text throughout, not a slip he ignores. Rendered "having a form" both times.
Marker fired **once, at the first occurrence**, per Pattern 14's "at the point of
divergence"; a second copy on the same words two paragraphs later would be noise.

**0637B · *Increpa in omnibus*** — 2 Tim IV, 2 reads *increpa in **omni patientia**, et
doctrina*. **This is the work's clearest 7a″ case and it runs the mechanism backwards:** the
gloss's whole sentence is *Timotheo acriorem suadet **patientiam***, "to Timothy he urges a
sharper *patience*" — the exposition rests on a word the lemma above no longer prints. A
translator conforming the lemma to the familiar English ("rebuke in all patience") would have
made the page read perfectly and destroyed the evidence. Rendered "Rebuke in all things."
with `[var: Vulg. *increpa in omni patientia*, "rebuke in all patience"; the gloss turns on
*patientia*]`.

## §2 · THE DIVERGENCES **DECLINED**, each with its reason

Pattern 14 fires on divergence "in a way that changes the sense." These do not, and marking
them would bury the three that do.

1. **0633C *Eum timete qui potest corpus et animam perdere in gehennam*** (Matth. X, 28,
   *potius timete eum qui potest et animam et corpus perdere*). Two changes: the opening is
   recast for the *Unde:* frame, and the doublet **body/soul is reversed**. Neither changes
   what the verse asserts, and the gloss turns on *who* is to be feared, not on the order of
   the pair (*Spiritus … dat timorem non mundi, sed Dei*). ⚠ Recorded rather than waved
   through, because 7914 @1079B is a case where a reversed doublet mattered; there the gloss
   was *about* the second member (*cinericeum colorem*). Here nothing hangs on it.
2. **0634C *Quia oportet,* etc.** — *quia* is not in II, 6. It is the citation-frame
   conjunction at the head of a fragment lifted out of its sentence, exactly the class Titus
   declined for a dropped *autem* / *-que* (8997 §1).
3. **0634C *Primum percipere.* / *De fructibus.*** — every word agrees; Migne simply glosses
   the two halves **in the reverse of the printed order** (II, 6 runs *primum de fructibus
   percipere*). That is his lemmatization, not a textual variant. Reproduced in his order,
   never reordered.
4. **0635B / 0635C, the *resurrectio* lemma split.** The VERS. 18 lemma prints *Dicentes
   resurrectionem **jam factam*** (Vulgate: *esse jam factam*, so *esse* is dropped); the
   re-quotation five lines later prints *Dicentes resurrectionem,* … ***esse factam*** — which
   **restores *esse* and drops *jam***. Neither omission changes the assertion (the elliptical
   accusative-and-infinitive is ordinary, and *jam* survives in the first form). ⚑ **The
   inconsistency between the two forms is the interesting datum**, and it is exactly the
   phenomenon the brief warns not to harmonize: the English renders the lemma "is already
   past" and the re-quotation "hath been made," because that is what each prints.
5. **0636A *Nequando det illis Deus poenitentiam*** — II, 25 has *Deus det illis*. Word order
   only, and **Migne is self-consistent**: he re-quotes it the same way at 0636B. English
   renders either identically.
6. **0637A *praepara animam tuam ad tentationes*** (Eccli. II, 1: *ad tentationem*). Singular
   to plural. Declined because Migne's own gloss two sentences earlier already reads *qui non
   praeparant ad **tentationes*** — the plural is his throughout, and the verse enjoins the
   same thing in either number.
7. **0638A *ut fidelis essem*** (I Cor. VII, 25: *ut sim fidelis*). This is not a quotation
   presented as one: it sits inside Augustine's own antithesis *non quia fidelis eram, sed ut
   fidelis essem*, where the imperfect subjunctive is required by the sequence of *eram*.
   Recast, not divergent.
8. **0638B *Multa mihi mala ostendit*** (IV, 14: *multa mala mihi ostendit*). *mihi* moved one
   word. Nothing turns on it; the gloss is about Alexander, not about the phrase.
9. **Non-lemma scripture, checked and left alone** (not italic, therefore outside Pattern 14
   by the runbook's own boundary, but collated anyway): 0634C *fodere non possim, mendicare
   confundar* against Luc. XVI, 3 *fodere non valeo, mendicare erubesco* — a paraphrase put in
   Timothy's mouth, not a citation; 0636C *charitas de corde puro, conscientia bona, et fide
   non ficta* against I Tim. I, 5, which has *et* before *conscientia*; 0635C–D *conformes
   imaginis Filii sui … praedestinavit, vocavit, justificavit, glorificavit* against Rom. VIII,
   29–30, which has *conformes fieri imaginis*. All three are the gloss quoting loosely in its
   own prose. **No `[var:]` on any of them, deliberately** — the marker is for lemmata and for
   scripture quoted **as** scripture.

## §3 · THE UNEXPRESSED SUBJECT — EVERY SUPPLIED PRONOUN, NAMED

The brief's dominant defect class, and this work is dense with it: Paul, Timothy, Christ, the
false teachers and the faithful hearer all move through one paragraph. Every "he / they / it"
the English supplies is listed with what fixed it.

**0633A, the ARGUMENTUM · *scribit … scribens ei*** — "**He** writes to Timothy." **Paul**.
Fixed by the closing participle *scribens ei ab urbe Roma*, which only the letter's author can
govern, and by the form (the standard Glossa prologue to a Pauline epistle).

**0633A · *gratias agit de bono quod habet*** — ⚠ **two different subjects in one clause.**
*agit* = **Paul**; *habet* = **Timothy**. Fixed by the VERS. 3 gloss below, which repeats it
with the possessor spelled out: *Agit gratias **de bono ejus***.

**0633A · *ubi suum videndi eum desiderium ostendit*** — "**his own** desire of seeing
**him**." ⚑ **The reflexive/non-reflexive pair decides it and nothing else does:** *suum* is
reflexive, so the desire is the **subject's** — Paul's; *eum* is non-reflexive, so the one seen
is **not** the subject — Timothy. (2 Tim I, 4 *desiderans te videre*.) This is the exact class
that produced last batch's Philemon failure, and here the morphology is unambiguous.

**0633B · *Agit gratias de bono ejus*** — ⭐ **REPAIRED after the blind read.** It first shipped
as "for **his** good," which names the class in this file and then loses it on the page: English
"his" defaults to the subject of "he gives thanks," i.e. Paul. ***ejus*, not *suo*, is Migne's
printed instruction that the possessor is NOT the subject** — he spent a word saying so. Now
reads **"for his [Timothy's] good."** Aggravating the original: the preceding sentence at 0633A
(*gratias agit de bono quod habet*) also carries an ambiguous "he," so nothing anywhere cued the
reader. ⚑ Same *ejus*/*sua* class that bit Philemon last batch.

**0633B · *Quod ergo praesens habuit, absens teneat … quae te commoneat*** — *habuit* and
*teneat* = **Timothy** (the sentence's subject is Timothy, named in the previous clause,
*Flevit enim Timotheus*). ⚠ Migne then shifts to the **second person** mid-sentence (*te
commoneat*) and the English shifts with him; see §5.

**0633B · *cum etiam in feminis fuerit firma*** — "since **it** was firm even in women." The
subject is ***fides***, from the lemma *recordationem fidei* immediately before; the women are
Lois and Eunice (2 Tim I, 5), whom the gloss does not name.

**0633B · *Commendata fide illius, ad eam … exhortatur*** — *illius* = **Timothy's** faith
(non-reflexive again); *exhortatur* = **Paul**; *eam* = *fidem*.

**0633C · *Hujus timoris non accepimus spiritum*** — first person plural, expressed. No supply.

**0633D–0634A · *Ostendit Deus … ostendit, inquam, per praedicationem Evangelii*** — subject
**expressed** (*Deus*). Named here because the neighbouring sentences are all Paul, and a
reader skimming would carry Paul into it.

**0634A · *persuadere quippe vult*** — **Paul**, from the question immediately above (*cum
seipsum … gentium magistrum vocet*), whose subject is Paul by *seipsum*.

**0634A–B · *Spe et magnificentia Salvatoris securus est … quod commendat illi … cum coeperit
judicare … pronuntiet*** — ⚠ **the densest site in the work; four verbs, two subjects, none
expressed.** *securus est* and both instances of *commendat* = **Paul**; *coeperit judicare*,
*puniens* and *pronuntiet* = **Christ**, i.e. the *Salvator* named at the head of the sentence
and the *illi* that Paul commends his salvation to. *istum* ("this man") = **Paul**, the one
who suffers *hic pro illo*. The chain is fixed by the lemma's own verse (I, 12, *potens est
depositum meum servare in illum diem*): the depositor is Paul, the keeper is Christ, the day is
the judgment.

**0634B · *Hic ostendit quo de se praedicta spectent*** — *ostendit* = **Paul**, *de se* =
Paul. Then *Ita laboro … et tu* is first and second person, expressed.

**0634B · *Simulate hi cum Apostolo fuerunt … recesserunt ab eo*** — *hi* = **Phigellus and
Hermogenes**, expressed in the lemma; *eo* = the Apostle, expressed in the sentence.

**0634B–C · *quod ne iste faceret … prohibet*** — *iste* = **Timothy** (and *infirmus
laborare non poterat* is the standing Glossa reference to I Tim V, 23, Timothy's weak stomach);
*prohibet* = **Paul**. ⚠ Read the other way round the sentence says the Apostle was too weak to
work, which is both false to the passage and incoherent with *prohibet*.

**0634C · *jubet eum sumere qui primus est … Primo enim debet ille suam necessitatem implere***
— *jubet* = **the Apostle**, expressed; *eum … qui primus est* and *ille* = **the labourer in
the word**, the man of the simile's *agricola vel vinitor*, i.e. Timothy and his like. The next
sentence's *ex his qui accepit* settles it: the one who takes first is the one who received.

**0634D · *Haec autem non propter Timotheum memorat*** — *memorat* = **Paul**; *praevidit* =
Paul. Timothy is expressed and is the person the recollection is **not** for.

**0634D · *Hic contumeliam notat quae ei ingerebatur*** — ⚠ *notat* = **Paul** and *ei* =
**Paul as well**, though *ei* is non-reflexive. The lemma decides it: *quasi male operans* is
II, 9's description of Paul in chains, and the gloss that follows says *sim alligatus corpore*
in the first person. Recorded because it is the one place in this work where the
reflexive/non-reflexive test would give the wrong answer if applied mechanically.

**0635A · *Justificati estis ut hic consequamini*** — ⚠ **plural**, and it is the only plural
address in the work. Rendered **"you"** per Pattern 17, against the singular **"thou"** used
for Timothy everywhere else. The referent is the *electi* of II, 10, the lemma's own verse, not
Timothy.

**0635A · *Est iste, quod consequentur salutem*** — "**they** shall obtain": the same
*electi*. *sumus mortui* and *sustinentes* are first person / participial, expressed.

**0635B · *ut intus in animo perdat, et foris victor accedat*** — "**he** loses … **he** comes
off victor." The subject is the **contender**, generalized out of *contentio* in the same
sentence and confirmed by the next sentence's *Nemo enim patitur se vinci*. Not *contentio*
itself (which is feminine and would give "she"), and not the *fratres* (plural).

**0635B · *Horum prodidit nomina, ut ab his spiritualiter caveat*** — ⭐ **REPAIRED after the
blind read.** *prodidit* and *designat* = **Paul**; *caveat* = **Timothy**, the person addressed
by *Haec commone* and by the whole chapter. This file named the right subject and the English
still shipped a bare "that **he** might beware," which attaches to the nearest antecedent, the

## §4 · THE CRUCES PROPER

### ⭐ 0637A · *quam Deus ipse saeculo nos promisit* — `[cj:]` FIRED

Migne prints, of the teachers who promise worldly happiness:

> *sed promittunt felicitatem hujus saeculi, quam Deus ipse saeculo **nos** promisit.*

Rendered as printed: "but promise the felicity of this world, which God himself promised us to
the world." **That is the opposite of what the paragraph argues**, and the next two sentences
say so on the same plate: *Ille praedicit labores usque in finem venturos saeculo: tu prospera*
("He foretells labours coming upon the world unto the end: thou, prosperous things"), and *ut
cum crediderit, **non speret prospera hujus saeculi***.

**The conjecture: *nos* is the plate's *non*.** One letter, a real word in a real slot, and the
restored sentence is precisely the argument: *quam Deus ipse saeculo **non** promisit*, "which
God himself did not promise to the world." This is the founding shape of Pattern 18 (11081's
*munde* for *mundo*): nothing is defective type, so `[sic:]` cannot reach it; Pattern 7 renders
it and the English then asserts what the author is refuting.

Marker: `[cj: *nos*; read *non*, "which God himself did not promise to the world"]`, additive —
Migne's word keeps its English and the conjecture stands beside it. **Not swallowed into a
marker, not silently repaired.** §0 records that the foot of leaf n322 carries no note, so
Migne had not answered this himself.

**A second conjecture considered and rejected:** that *nos* is sound and a separate *non* has
dropped out before *promisit*. It yields the same sense but glosses no printed word, so
Pattern 18's safety check (the marker must open with a word verbatim in the Latin twin) could
not be satisfied, and it is the less economical account.

### ⭐ 0634A · *non confundor, id est deficio* — DECIDED: RENDERED AS PRINTED, AND THE ASYMMETRY IS MIGNE'S

**Closed by the blind polarity read of 2026-08-18** (§9). This entry originally stood as an
open flag and the reader resolved it in favour of the rendering; the hedging is withdrawn.

Migne prints:

> *Ideo* non confundor, *id est deficio, vel non frustra patior…*

The English reads "Therefore *I am not confounded,* that is, **I fail**, or I do not suffer in
vain." **The asymmetry between the two appositives is real, and it is in the Latin, not in our
English.** They are not parallel glosses:

- ***id est deficio*** glosses the **bare verb** *confundor* — a one-word lexical equation, the
  commonest move in the Glossa — and therefore carries no negation of its own.
- ***vel non frustra patior*** glosses **the whole negated phrase** *non confundor*, which is why
  it has to print its own *non*.

Once the two are seen to be doing different jobs, nothing is missing from the plate. **Rendering
it "that is, [I do not] fail" would have been the silent repair** — it would have supplied a
negation Migne does not print (7a) and flattened a distinction his sentence actually makes. The
English preserves the asymmetry exactly, and the lemma's own *non* stands three words away where
the reader meets it.

No `[cj:]`, no `[sic:]`, and no residual doubt: *deficio* is the right word in the right slot
under the reading the sentence itself supplies.

### 0635D · *sed nutiles et sine disciplina* — `[sic: *nutiles*]`, WITH THE FORCE KEPT OUTSIDE

*nutiles* is not a Latin word: it is *inutiles* with its initial *in-* lost. Carried
untranslated in italics per Patterns 10 and 12.

⚑ **7a⁗ applies at full strength, because the quarantined run contains the negation.** The
whole point of the sentence is that *not all* questions are to be shunned, only the **un**helpful
ones — so the English supplies the sense in plain words beside the marker: "but the
**unprofitable** [sic: *nutiles*] ones and those without discipline." Wrapping *nutiles* and
letting the English go without a negative would have made the gloss forbid **profitable**
questions, which is the mechanism 7a⁗ was written to stop.

### 0636A · *aurursus dormire* — `[sic: *aurursus*]`, A FUSED WORD-DIVISION DEFECT

> *Multi enim homines cum a somno excitantur, litigare volunt, **aurursus** dormire, cum
> litigare prohibentur.*

*aurursus* is not a word. It is a Pattern 10 **fusion**, and the most economical account is
***aut rursus***, "or again" (*au* + *rursus*); ***ac rursus*** is the alternative and yields
the same connective force. Carried whole, in italics, inside the marker.

⚑ **Again 7a⁗-b: the fused run is exactly the size of a lexeme, and the lexeme is a
conjunction.** Quarantining it silently would leave "wish to wrangle, to sleep, when they are
forbidden to wrangle" — a list where the Latin has an alternative. The English carries the
force outside the marker: "wish to wrangle, [sic: *aurursus*] **or again** to sleep." The
conjecture is here; the connective is on the page.

### ⭐⭐ 0638C · *sed pro his qui abruperant amorem, sed timore succubuerunt* — DECIDED: THE TEXT CONSTRUES, AND THE CONJECTURE IS POSITIVELY WRONG

**Closed by the blind polarity read of 2026-08-18** (§9). This entry originally proposed that a
*non* had dropped before *abruperant*. **That conjecture is wrong, and recording why is more use
to the next reader than an open flag.**

Migne prints:

> *Non orat pro Alexandro, qui invidentia fraternitatem oppugnando peccaverat, **sed pro his qui
> abruperant amorem, sed timore succubuerunt**; orat ut eis ignoscatur.*

Rendered as printed: "but for those who had broken off love, but yielded through fear."

**The decisive evidence is two lines below, in Migne's own text:**

> *Multum enim interest inter eos qui hoc modo et eos qui illo modo peccant.*

"There is a great difference between those who sin in **this** manner and those who sin in
**that**." ***Both groups sin.*** Had the second group not broken off love at all, they would not
be sinners, the comparison would have nothing to compare, and the closing sentence of the
paragraph would be empty.

**So the second *sed* is not adversative against *abruperant*; it is adversative against
*invidentia* in the clause before.** The contrast the paragraph is drawing is over the *cause* of
the sin, not its existence: Alexander sinned **by envy**, assailing the brotherhood; these sinned
too, but **through fear**. That is exactly why Paul prays for the second group and not the first,
and why *ut eis ignoscatur* — pardon, which presupposes a fault — is the thing asked for.

**Nothing is missing from the plate, both *sed*s are carried, and the English as it stands is
sound.** The earlier reasoning — that `[cj:]` had no printed word to open on — reached the right
result for a weaker reason; the real ground is that there is no defect to conjecture about.

⚠ **Recorded as a rejected conjecture, deliberately.** A later reader meeting the doubled *sed*
will have the same instinct, and this entry exists so that he does not spend the session on it.

### 0638A · *reddentibus bona pro malis* — TWO READINGS, ONE TAKEN

> *Apostolum enim invenimus sine ullis bonis meritis, imo cum multis vitiis Dei gratiam
> consecutum, **reddentibus bona pro malis**.*

An ablative absolute whose participle is **active** and whose subject is not expressed.
(a) The agent is **God**, the sentence's own theme (Paul had vices; God gave grace: good for
evil) — so the clause is *reddente Deo* in sense, with the plural either loose or attracted to
*vitiis*. (b) *reddentibus* is a dative/ablative of persons, "to those rendering good for
evil," which leaves it hanging on nothing.

**(a) taken**, on the argument's own logic and on what the next two sentences say (*qui post
mala merita consecutus est gratiam*). The English leaves the agent unexpressed as the Latin
does: "good things being rendered for evil." No marker: nothing is defective and no word is
mistranslated, only a number is loose, which Pattern 9 exempts.

### 0637C · *retorqueatur os turturis ad ascellas* — RENDERED LITERALLY

Lev. I, 15 and V, 8 have the priest twist back the head (*caput*) of the turtle-dove *ad
collum* / *ad pennulas*. Migne prints ***os*** (mouth) and ***ascellas***. Both are rendered as
printed: "that the mouth of the turtle-dove be twisted back to its wings."

- ***ascellas*** takes **no marker**: it is the well-attested medieval form of *axillas*
  (whence Italian *ascella*), and Pattern 9 rules attested medieval spellings correct Latin for
  their period. "Wings" rather than "armpits" because the subject is a bird.
- ***os*** for *caput* is a real word in its slot and the sentence construes; it is a divergence
  from Levitical wording, not from a quoted lemma, so Pattern 14 does not reach it either.
  ⚑ **And there may be a reason it is *os*:** the same sentence opposes *opere* to ***ore***
  ("what thou preachest with the mouth") and closes on *in extremis **oris*** (the hem of the
  tunic, *ora*). The gloss is playing on the word, and emending it would flatten the play.

### 0637C · *libare / delibare / delibatio* — THE ETYMOLOGY KEPT IN LATIN

The gloss **is** the etymology (*Libare dicimus, degustare, fundere et immolare. Inde delibare
… passionem enim suam delibationem appellat*), so the three root-words stay Latin, in italics,
under the register rule for technical terms with no clean English equivalent — the shipped
precedent is 9741's *precaria* / *emphyteusis*, which are likewise italicized in the English
where the Latin twin has no italics. The lemma itself is Englished as required: *Delibor.* → "I
am poured out as a libation," which keeps the root visible to a reader who does not read Latin.

Same treatment, same reason, at **0638B**: *Pallium hic penulam dicit* → "He here calls the
*pallium* a *penula*." The gloss's whole content is the identification of two garment-words;
rendering both "cloak" would make it say nothing. The lemma is Englished (*Penulam.* → "The
cloak.").

### 0635A · *Contentio minus stabilis saepe generat scrupulum* — RENDERED AS PRINTED

"Contention, being less stable, often begets scruple." *minus stabilis* is odd (one expects a
noun for the mind, or *mentis*), but it is real Latin in a real slot and yields sense.
No conjecture is economical enough to offer; recorded for a reader with the plate.

### 0638B · *aedes Dianae apud Athenas* — MIGNE'S OWN ERROR OF FACT, REPRODUCED

The shrine-makers of Acts XIX are at **Ephesus**, not Athens, and their leader is Demetrius,
not Alexander. The gloss has conflated two chapters of Acts with I Tim I, 20. **Rendered
exactly as printed** ("who used to make shrines of Diana at Athens"). This is a mistake in
Migne's *content*, not in his type, and Pattern 7 covers it precisely: our business is to
reproduce what he printed, not to correct what the Glossa believed.

### ⭐ 0634C–D (VERS. 6) · *ex his qui accepit* — `[cj: *qui*; read *quae*]`, AFTER A SILENT EMENDATION WAS CAUGHT

> *Primo enim debet ille suam necessitatem implere ex his **qui** accepit, et post, aliis de his
> quae supersunt ministrare.*

**This entry replaces an earlier one that got the class wrong**, and the correction is instructive
enough to keep in view. The first reading treated *qui* as a Pattern 9 gender mismatch — *qui* for
*quae*, neuter plural agreeing with *his* — and concluded that English relatives carry no gender,
so there was nothing to mark. **That was a silent emendation dressed as an exemption.**

The printed *qui* **construes without changing a letter**: it is a nominative attracted to *ille*,
giving "**he who has received** ought first to fulfil his own need out of these things." Both
readings work; the English took the one that requires reading *qui* as *quae*, and took it with no
marker at all — two paragraphs from a correctly flagged `[sic: *nutiles*]` and one from a
correctly flagged `[cj: *nos*; read *non*]`. ⛔ **Consistent apparatus elsewhere in the chunk is
not evidence the apparatus was used here** — 7a″'s standing warning, and this is a live instance
of it.

The English sense is kept (the *quae* reading is the likelier one, since *de his quae supersunt*
in the very next clause shows Migne using the neuter plural relative for exactly this referent),
and the conjecture is now declared additively:
`[cj: *qui*; read *quae*, "those things which"; the printed *qui* construes instead as "he who has
received"]`.

### Three repairs made, and the one mechanism behind two of them

⭐ **REPAIR 1 · 0635B** — "that **he** might beware" → "that **[Timothy]** might beware." (§3)
⭐ **REPAIR 2 · 0633B** — "for **his** good" → "for **his [Timothy's]** good." (§3)
⭐ **REPAIR 3 · 0634C–D** — a silent *qui* → *quae* emendation now declared as `[cj:]`. (§4)
⭐ **REPAIR 4 · 0633C, 0638A** — two supplied "and"s removed. (§4)

**The mechanism, and it is worth stating once because it is the whole of the class:**

> **English cannot leave a possessor or a subject unmarked, so it supplies the nearest one — and
> Migne's *ejus* is the printed instruction that the nearest one is wrong.**

That is why repairs 1 and 2 are the same defect wearing two grammatical costumes. In both, this
cruces file had **already named the correct referent in §3** and the English had shipped a bare
pronoun anyway. ⛔ **Naming a subject in the apparatus is not the same as marking it in the text**
— the reader meets the sentence, not the crux. Where the Latin's morphology makes the choice
(*ejus* not *sua*; a purpose clause whose point is the addressee), the English has to spend a word
on it, and a bracketed name is the cheapest word available.
