### Fragment expansions — what each stump was rendered as, and why

Rule 2 of the settled conventions (a fragment is rendered as the phrase it stands for). Five
lemmata here are fragments; the judgment differs per stump and each is recorded:

- **VERS. 2 *De omnibus orationem,* etc.** → **"*Concerning all things I make prayer,* etc."**
  The verse's own verb (*facio*) supplied, exactly as Ruth's *Non te.* took its verb. "Concerning
  all things prayer" is not English.
- **VERS. 4 *Majorem horum.*** → **"*I have no greater grace than these.*"** The bare stump
  ("Greater than these") is not English and, worse, is unintelligible as to *what* is greater.
  ⚠ **This expansion carries a printed `non` into the lemma line** (*non habeo gratiam*) — checked
  against 7a: the negation is the Vulgate's own and the gloss re-quotes it four words later
  (*non habeo majorem gratiam*), so it is carried, not supplied.
- **VERS. 7 *Pro nomine enim ejus,* etc.** → **"*For on account of his name,* etc."**
  *enim* = "for" (conjunction), *pro* = "on account of"; the doubled English "for … for" was
  avoided by taking *pro* as "on account of", not by dropping either word.
- **VERS. 9 *Scripsissem forsitan,* etc.** → **"*I would perhaps have written,* etc."**
  ⭐ **The mood is the gloss's whole subject and is kept.** *Scripsissem* is pluperfect
  subjunctive and *forsitan* is the potential particle: the point of the gloss is that John
  *did not* write. Flattening to "I perhaps wrote" would destroy the paragraph, which turns on
  the unfulfilled condition (*sed hac necessitate dimisi*). The same form is rendered
  identically at both re-quotations in the gloss below (*scripsissem* ×2 → "I would have
  written"), held fixed.
- **VERS. 12 *Demetrio testimonium.*** → **"*Testimony to Demetrius.*"** ⚠ **Deliberately NOT
  expanded**, unlike the four above: unlike "Not thee" or "Greater than these", this stump *is*
  idiomatic English on its own. Supplying *redditur ab omnibus* would have added a verb and an
  agent Migne does not print in the lemma, with no gain in intelligibility. The declining
  reason is stated because it is the one place this work departs from the expansion the other
  fragments took.
- **VERS. 10 *Commonebo.*** (not a fragment but a one-word lemma) → **"*I will admonish.*"**
  Left minimal for the same reason. Note the gloss immediately re-reads it as publication
  (*in omnium notitiam … producam*), which is a construal of the lemma, not a variant of it.

## §2 · CRUCES AND JUDGMENT CALLS

### 2.1 · 0705B, the ARGUMENTUM — the unexpressed subject of the whole paragraph

*Gaium … extollit … exhortatur … objurgat … perhibet.* **Four third-person verbs, no subject
printed anywhere in the paragraph.** Supplied as **"he" = the Apostle John**, the epistle's
author: the *argumentum* is a summary of the letter, and the three objects (Gaius, Diotrephes,
Demetrius) are the letter's three named parties. Nothing else in reach could be the subject.
Recorded because the brief requires every supplied pronoun to be named.

**Ambiguity declined, and it is real:** *Demetrio autem bonum testimonium perhibet **cum
fratribus universis**.* Two construals both parse — (a) John bears witness *together with* all
the brethren, (b) the good testimony is borne *to* Demetrius *and* to all the brethren.
**Taken as (a)**, on v. 12's own *testimonium redditur ab omnibus … sed et nos testimonium
perhibemus*, where John explicitly joins his witness to everyone else's. Rendered "together
with all the brethren." Reading (b) would need *fratribus* to be a second dative governed by
*perhibet*, which *cum* forbids. Logged as adjudicated, not as open.

### 2.2 · 0706B–0706C — Diotrephes carried by bare third persons for six clauses

The dominant defect class, and this work's densest site. After *Diotrephes* is named at
0706B, the gloss carries him in five finite verbs with no subject expressed: *usurpabat* ·
*signet* · *habet* · *facit* · *invenit*, plus *utens*. Each supplied "he" is **Diotrephes**,
fixed by the naming at 0706B and by v. 10's *ejus opera, quae facit*. ⚠ Note that *facit* at
0706C is **italic**, i.e. it is a lemma pick-up from the verse, whose subject in the Vulgate is
also Diotrephes — the two chains agree, which is what makes the reading safe.

**One genuinely open subject, and it is flagged rather than decided silently:**
*ut perfidiam cordis etiam nomine **signet**.* The subject may be (a) **Diotrephes** — the
nominative of the immediately preceding clause, the default under continuity — or (b) the
**name / the interpretation** itself, or Scripture as the name-giver: "so that it may betoken
the perfidy of his heart by his name also." **Rendered (a)** — "so that he may betoken the
perfidy of his heart by his name also" — on grammatical continuity alone. **Both readings
construe and neither is provable from the page**; (b) is arguably the better sense, since a
man does not deliberately betoken his own perfidy, and a passive English ("so that the
perfidy of his heart might be betokened") would have concealed the choice altogether, which is
why it was not used. **This is the site in this work most worth a second reader.**

### 2.3 · 0706B — the onomastic gloss *speciose insulsus* / *decor insaniens*

Migne prints two italic etymologies of the name Diotrephes. **They are not scripture and take
no `[var:]`.** Rendered **"*speciously foolish,*" and "*comeliness gone mad,*"**: *speciose* is
the adverb ("showily, plausibly"), *insulsus* "unsalted, insipid, silly"; *decor insaniens* is
literally "raving comeliness". Both are rendered as the coinages they are rather than smoothed
into one idea — the pair is a contrast (fair outside, mad inside) and collapsing them would be
the lemma-conformation fault in its onomastic form. ⚠ Neither corresponds to the actual Greek
Διοτρεφής ("Zeus-nourished"); **that is Migne's tradition and is not ours to correct** — no
marker, no note in the text.

### 2.4 · 0706C — *quae forsitan,* the gloss re-construing its own lemma

*Illi Ecclesiae, **quae forsitan,** id est dubitative est Ecclesia … scripsissem.* The italic
run **is not a Vulgate phrase**: v. 9 has *Scripsissem forsitan Ecclesiae*, with *forsitan*
modifying the verb. The glossator detaches it and re-attaches it to *Ecclesiae* — "the Church
which perhaps [is one]" — and then says so explicitly (*dubitative est Ecclesia*). **This is a
construal, not a variant**, so no `[var:]` fires; but it is the interpretative move the whole
paragraph exists to make, and it is recorded here because a reader meeting an italic phrase
that is in no Bible will otherwise think our lemma is wrong. Rendered "*which perhaps,*
that is, it is doubtfully a Church".

Subject of *mereretur* in the same sentence: **the Church** (feminine), supplied as "she" —
fixed by *perfecta … Ecclesia* in the same clause.

### 2.5 · 0706C — *in nos* doing double duty (italic lemma inside a gerundive)

*falsis rationibus utens **in nos** confutandos*. The italic span is the two words *in nos*,
which are simultaneously (a) the lemma pick-up from v. 10 *garriens in nos* and (b) the
governing preposition of the gerundive *confutandos* ("for confuting us"). English cannot let
one phrase serve both without repeating the pronoun. Rendered "**using false reasonings
*against us* to confute us**" — the italic span 1:1 on "against us", the gerundive rendered
after it. The repetition of "us" is a cost of keeping the italic where Migne puts it; the
alternative (moving or widening the italic) would have broken the 1:1 rule.

**Pattern 17 datum, and it is visible on this page.** The same sentence has *in nos* / *ad
vos* against the letter's *tibi* throughout: Gaius is addressed **singular** ("thou … thee …
thy") in VERS. 2, 5, 9 and 14, while *ad vos … retrahendos* at 0706C is **plural** ("to draw
**you** back from the faith") — Diotrephes' target is the congregation, not Gaius. Rendered
accordingly; a flat "you" would have erased the shift.

### 2.6 · 0706C — the supplied object of *aliquando compescere*

*debemus aequanimiter tolerare … aliquando compescere.* The second infinitive has no object
printed; the object is *linguas … excitatas* from the first limb. Rendered "sometimes to check
**them**". ⚑ **The asyndeton is kept**: Migne prints no connective before *aliquando*, and an
"and" was drafted and then removed rather than supply a conjunction the plate lacks.

### 2.7 · 0706C — the semicolon before *sed facit ea*

*quorum exemplar nusquam habet; sed facit ea.* An earlier draft rendered the semicolon as a
comma; **restored**, per Pattern 8 (never delete a mark Migne prints). Recorded because it was
an actual near-miss in this chunk, not a hypothetical.

### 2.8 · 0706D — *velit imitari bonum*: two construals, one taken

*Quod illum velit imitari **bonum**, aperit ut imitetur Demetrium.* Either (a) *bonum* is the
object of *imitari* — "that he would have him imitate **the good**" — or (b) *bonum* agrees
with *illum* — "that he would imitate **that good man**". **Taken as (a)**, because v. 11,
which this gloss follows directly, is *noli imitari malum, sed quod bonum est*: the neuter
good/evil contrast is the verse's own, and Demetrius is then adduced as the instance of it.
Reading (b) is not impossible and is recorded. Supplied subjects here: *velit* / *aperit* =
**the Apostle**, *illum* and *possit et ipse* = **Gaius**.

### 2.9 · 0706D — *gratiam pacis et salutis* / *a salute*: greeting or salvation?

*Amicis gratiam pacis et **salutis** mandat, ut veritatis inimicos **a salute** monstret
extraneos esse.* Latin *salus* is both "greeting" and "salvation"; **English must choose, and
the choice is invisible to a reader once made**, which is why it is logged. Read as
**"salvation"** in both places, for consistency within the sentence and because *gratia
pacis et salutis* is a settled Latin idiom and *extraneus a salute* is a theological
predicate the Glossa uses freely. **The competing reading is decent**: the lemmatized verse
continues *Salutant te amici. Saluta amicos nominatim*, so a gloss explaining why John greets
only friends would want "greeting" — "strangers to the greeting". Declined, not overlooked.

## §4 · BLIND POLARITY READ — 2026-08-18, and the adjudication

A fenced reader (Latin twin + English only, never this file) worked the chunk three times —
once for polarity, once lemma-by-lemma against the verse each fragment opens, once for
every unexpressed subject. **5 sites raised · 3 confirmed and repaired · 2 declined.**

**The headline is negative and belongs on the record as such: zero polarity defects, zero
silent repairs.** All fifteen italic lemmata were checked word by word. Where Migne and the
Vulgate genuinely part company the English followed **Migne**: he prints the future
indicative *Commonebo* where the Vulgate has the subjunctive *commoneam*, and the English
gives "I will admonish." *Majorem horum* likewise gives **grace** (Vulgate *gratiam*) not
the Authorized Version's "joy", and **than these** (*horum*, plural) not Douay's "than
this". Neither Bible was reached for. This file did not fall for the class that beat Ruth.

### ✅✅ 0705C + 0706B — CONFIRMED, REPAIRED. **A defect that nets to zero.**

Two Pattern 8 sites, and they must be read together:

- **0705C** *…et facultas opum**;** ita semper…* — Migne's semicolon closes the protasis of
  the *Sicut … ita* period. It had been softened to a comma, and "and the means of wealth,
  so mayest thou" reads as a run-on with the as/so architecture gone flat. **Restored.**
- **0706B** *…de commendatione eleemosynae**,** sed hac necessitate dimisi…* — Migne prints
  a comma; a semicolon had been supplied. **Restored.**

⭐⭐ **This is the finding to carry forward.** Migne prints three semicolons; the English
printed three semicolons. **Any count-based or parity-based check passes** — one mark was
taken away in one place and a new one put in another, and the totals cancel. It was caught
only because the reader **located each mark instead of tallying them**. `verify-english`'s
question-mark parity would never have seen it, and neither would a diff of counts.

⚑ Method worth copying into the next punctuation check: **match the marks in ORDER and by
position, not by total** — the same move as `sentence-mark-sites.mjs` for the `?`/`!` sweep,
applied to the marks nothing scans for.

### ✅ 0706C · VERS. 12 lemma — CONFIRMED, REPAIRED

*Demetrio testimonium.* had been rendered "*Testimony to Demetrius.*" — a verbless caption.
Now "*Testimony is borne to Demetrius.*" (3 John 12, *Demetrio testimonium **redditur** ab
omnibus*).

The argument that decided it is **internal consistency**, not taste. Migne terminates four
lemmata in this work with a full stop — *Majorem horum.* · *Commonebo.* · *Demetrio
testimonium.* · *Pax tibi.* — and three of the four had been expanded into complete clauses
under Glossa convention 1.2 (a fragment is rendered as the phrase it stands for). Only this
one was left a stump, and the gloss below it does not construe *testimonium* grammatically,
so nothing downstream repaired the gap.

### ⛔ 0706C–D · VERS. 12 — DECLINED, and the reader's stated ground is mistaken

*Quod illum velit imitari bonum, aperit ut imitetur Demetrium, ut simili laude omnium
possit et ipse dignus existere.* The reader proposed reading *illum … bonum* attributively
("that good man") with both *ut* clauses final, and objected that our rendering "leaves
*aperit* with no object at all."

**It does not.** The fronted *Quod* clause **is** the object of *aperit* — "That he would
have him imitate the good, he discloses" — so the strongest plank of the alternative reading
fails. The supplied "namely" for the first *ut* is explicative and defensible on the printed
words. The reader itself did not claim its reading was certainly right. Left as it stands,
and named here so a later eye knows the ambiguity was seen and weighed rather than missed.

### ⛔ 0705C · VERS. 5 lemma — DECLINED, with the boundary recorded

*Charissime, fideliter,* etc. → "*Dearly beloved, faithfully,* etc." The reader noted that
3 John 5 reads *fideliter **facis*** and that the gloss immediately re-quotes the verb
(*id est sicut decet fidelem **facis***), so our lemma drops the word the exposition turns
on. Rated low confidence by the reader itself, and declined here: **a comma-plus-*etc.*
fragment is Migne's own signal that he is abbreviating**, and the brief carries the comma
and the *etc.* through as printed. "Dearly beloved, faithfully," is readable English, unlike
Ruth's rejected "Not thee". The reader drew the same line itself when it declined *Pro
nomine enim ejus,* on the ground that the Latin fragment is a bare prepositional phrase.

⚑ **The convention's working boundary, now stated twice across this cadence and worth
promoting into the brief:** expand a fragment when the English stump is **not a usable
phrase** (a bare *Non te*, a verbless caption); leave it when the stump reads as ordinary
abbreviated English and Migne's own *etc.* marks the abbreviation. **The defect is never
expansion or its absence — it is conformation**, an English Bible's wording arriving where
Migne's Latin differs from it.
