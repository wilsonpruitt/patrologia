## §2 · THE CRUCES

### 2.1 · 0641D · *in agnitione quae est* — the relative changes antecedent. `[var:]` TAKEN.

Migne's gloss re-quotes Philem. 6 as ***evidens fiat in agnitione quae est. In Christo
Jesu.*** The Vulgate reads *in agnitione omnis operis boni, **quod** est in vobis in Christo
Iesu* — a **neuter** relative referring back to *operis boni*, "of every good work which is
in you." Migne's *quae* is feminine and can only attach to *agnitione*, and the phrase
*omnis operis boni* and *in vobis* are both gone. The gloss then builds on exactly that:
*In Christo Jesu. Id est, cum cognitione boni aeterni* — it takes the *in Christo Jesu* as
qualifying the **acknowledgment**, not the good work. Rendered as printed; `[var:]` beside
it with the received reading. **No speculation offered as to the cause** (compression by
the glossator, a copy-text, or the compositor — that is a research question).

⚠ Related but NOT marked: two lines earlier the gloss paraphrases *omne bonum esse **in te**
per Christum* where the Vulgate has *in vobis* (plural). That sits in the gloss's own prose,
not in an italic lemma, so it is the glossator addressing Philemon, not a divergent citation.
Logged, not marked.

### 2.2 · ⭐ *VISCERA* — ONE rendering, "bowels", held rigid across both lemmata

This is the word the work turns on. It carries **two** `VERS.` lemmata (7 *Viscera
sanctorum*, 12 *Ut mea viscera*) plus a third occurrence at v. 20 (*Refice viscera*), and
Paul's whole argument is that Onesimus goes back as Paul's own *viscera*. The two members
**meet** here, so the rendering is fixed and rigid: **bowels**, everywhere.

**Why "bowels" and not "heart(s)" or "inmost self":** Migne's own first gloss on VERS. 7 is
***Id est filii sanctorum charissimi*** — *viscera* read as **offspring**. The second gloss
in the same sentence reads it as the seat of **affection** (*sanctorum affectus quievit in
te*). VERS. 12's gloss reads it as Paul's **self** (*suum animum in illo significet recipi*).
A single English word has to be able to mean all three, and "bowels" is the only one in the
register that does — it carries the affective sense and the archaic sense of *offspring*
("the bowels of thy body"), which is precisely what the *filii* gloss requires. "Hearts of
the saints" cannot mean children and would make Migne's first gloss unintelligible;
"inmost parts" cannot either. The devotional/anatomical split the brief warned against is
therefore not merely inconsistent, it destroys the link the gloss is built on.
**Ruling: *viscera* = bowels, all three sites, no exceptions.**

### 2.3 · ⭐ ONESIMUS'S NAME — Migne does NOT draw the pun out; the *utilis* chain is its only trace

The epistle puns on Onesimus = "useful" (*qui tibi aliquando **inutilis** fuit, nunc autem
et mihi et tibi **utilis***). **Migne's gloss never prints *inutilis*, and never mentions
the name's meaning or the Greek.** The lemma at VERS. 11 stops at *aliquando*, before the
negative, so the lemma itself carries no polarity at all. What survives is a four-link
*utilis* chain, and it is held rigid:

- VERS. 11 *Nunc tibi **utilis** erit* → "Now he will be **useful** to thee"
- VERS. 11 *Si enim Paulo futurus est **utilis*** → "if he is going to be **useful** to Paul"
- VERS. 12 *videns in illo **utilitatis** spem* → "seeing in him the hope of **usefulness**"
- VERS. 16 *ut, oblitteratis peccatis, **utilis** reverteretur* → "that he might return **useful**"

"Profitable/unprofitable" (Douay, AV) was **declined**: it is the familiar English Bible
wording, which §7a″ forbids importing, and it breaks the tie to *utilitatis*. Nothing was
added to make the pun visible that Migne does not print.

### 2.4 · ⚠ 0642B, 0642C · *Dominus* = the MASTER, not the Lord — Migne capitalizes both

`ut eo ipso exstinguat iracundiam **Domini**` and `quanto amplius **Domino**` are printed
with a capital D. Both are rendered **"the master" / "his master"**, not "the Lord":

1. The argument is Chrysostom's on Philem. 11 — the fault is confessed so as to defuse the
   *owner's* anger; the person who might be angry with Onesimus is Philemon.
2. The same word is printed **lowercase** twice in the VERS. 16 gloss for the same referent
   (*offenso proprio **domino***, *domino suo aequalis*), where "master" is not in doubt.
3. *quanto amplius Domino* closes a comparison whose other term is *Paulo* — a man.

Capitalization is a 19th-century typographic habit and carries no lexical force in Latin;
English must choose a word, and *dominus* in this context means master. This is a rendering
of the printed word, not an emendation of it. ⚠ **Distinguished from *in Domino* at VERS. 16
and v. 20, which IS the Lord** (it is the scripture lemma, opposed to *in carne*). Fixed
vocabulary for the work: ***dominus* = master · *magister* = teacher · *servus* = slave ·
*custodia* = custody · *carcer* = prison · *vinctus* = prisoner · *vincula* = bonds**.

### 2.5 · 0642B · *Senex.* is verse 9 printed under VERS. 10 — NOT reordered

*Senex* is Philem. **9** (*ut Paulus senex*), glossed inside the VERS. 10 paragraph. Migne's
ordering; reproduced as printed, per the brief's rule on out-of-sequence verse numbers. The
same thing happens on a larger scale at VERS. 18, whose paragraph run glosses vv. 20 and 21
(§2.7). Nothing renumbered, nothing moved.

### 2.6 · 0642C · *Fratrem et in carne,* — a conflated re-quotation. `[var:]` DECLINED.

Migne: *humiliat eum dicens: **Fratrem et in carne,** quia ex uno Adam omnes, et **in
Domino** per fidem.* The Vulgate of v. 16 has *carissimum fratrem, maxime mihi: quanto autem
magis tibi **et in carne, et in Domino**?* — so every word of Migne's italics is in the
verse, but not in that order and not as one phrase: the glossator has stitched *fratrem* to
*et in carne … et in Domino*.

**Declined**, with the reason, because a blind reader will look here. Pattern 14 fires where
the divergence **changes the sense**, and it does not: the Vulgate already makes Onesimus a
brother in the flesh and in the Lord, and the gloss's stitched phrase asserts nothing more.
Marking every compressed re-quotation in a lemma-and-gloss commentary would be spam on the
page. Recorded here instead.

### 2.7 · 0642D · *Ita frater ego te fruar in Domino.* — the Vulgate's FULL STOP is gone

The Vulgate punctuates v. 20 *Ita frater. Ego te fruar in Domino* — two sentences. Migne
prints it as a single unstopped run. Words identical; punctuation only. Under Pattern 8 the
plate's marks ride as printed, so the English is one clause: "*So brother may I enjoy thee
in the Lord.*" **No comma was supplied around the vocative** — that would be the same
smoothing in miniature, and the divergence is exactly what this line records. No `[var:]`:
the sense is unaffected and the pattern is for wording, not for pointing.

### 2.8 · ⚠⚠ 0642D · *ut NON amplius faciat quam postulatur ab eo* — the negative reverses the argument. `[cj:]` TAKEN, negative CARRIED.

Migne: *Provocat eum sic blandiendo, **ut non amplius faciat, quam postulatur ab eo.** Solet
enim fieri, ut qui de se videt bene sentiri, meliorem se praebeat.*

Rendered literally, with the *non*: "so that he may **not** do more than is asked of him."
**The negative contradicts the paragraph it stands in, and the verse it glosses:**

- its own next sentence gives the mechanism — a man well thought of *meliorem se praebeat*,
  **shows himself better**, i.e. does more;
- the lemma is Philem. 21, whose Vulgate continues *sciens quoniam et **super id, quod dico,
  facies*** — "knowing that thou wilt do **beyond** what I say";
- *provocat … blandiendo* (coaxing him forward) is not a device for capping performance.

Negation fidelity is absolute, so the *non* stays and no word was translated as its opposite.
But *non* is a real word, so `[sic:]` cannot reach it and Pattern 7's literal English then
asserts what the glossator did not — which is exactly Pattern 18's gap. Marker placed:
`[cj: *non amplius*; read *amplius*, "that he may do more than is asked"]`. The printed run
*non amplius* is verbatim in the Latin twin (the verifier's substring test passes), so the
conjecture is offered against words Migne actually prints. **The English is unchanged; the
gloss is additive.** ⚠ The alternative reading — that flattery is meant to keep Philemon
from exceeding the request — was considered and rejected: it leaves *meliorem se praebeat*
and Philem. 21 both stranded.

### 2.9 · 0641B–0641C · anacolutha rendered as printed, not repaired

Three sentences do not complete their construction. All are rendered as closely as the
printed words allow and none is smoothed:

- **VERS. 5**: *Quia enim in Christum credebat … et ita … serviebat.* A *quia*-clause with
  **no apodosis** — the gloss never says what the *because* explains. English keeps the
  bare "For because…" opening.
- **0641C, VERS. 6**: *ut fides tua communicans fidei nostrae* — **no verb**; the parallel
  clause beside it has *evidens sit*. An English copula was supplied ("that thy faith **be**
  communicating"), which is elision of a Latin *sit*, not an emendation; recorded so the
  choice is visible.
- **0641D, VERS. 6**: *ita evidens sit, hoc merito cognoscatur* — the connective *ut* before
  the second subjunctive is unexpressed; rendered as the two jussives Migne prints, in
  asyndeton, with his comma.

### 2.10 · 0641D · *hoc merito* — two constructions, both construe. NOT adjudicated.

*ita evidens sit, hoc merito cognoscatur omne bonum esse in te per Christum.* Either
(a) *hoc merito* = ablative, "by this desert/merit", or (b) *hoc* = "hereby" with adverbial
*merito* = "deservedly". Rendered (b) — "let all good **hereby** be **deservedly** known to
be in thee" — because (a) would introduce a *meritum* the gloss nowhere else discusses,
while (b) needs nothing outside the sentence. Both readings are recorded rather than one
chosen silently; no marker, since nothing here is defective.

### 2.11 · *communicatio* — fixed English across the four occurrences

*communicatio* / *communicans* / *communicas* all sit in one sentence and are one argument,
so: **communication · communicating with · thou communicatest to**. "Fellowship" and
"partaking" were declined because they break the tie to the verb, and "communication" is
what the lemma has to be for the twice-quoted *communicatio fidei tuae* to read the same
both times.

⚠ Two different Latin words land on one English word and it is harmless: *refrigeratus est*
(VERS. 7) → "has been refreshed" and *Refice* (v. 20 lemma) → "Refresh". They are 60 lines
apart, no argument links them, and the Vulgate's own *requieverunt* / *refice* pairing is
what the glossator is following. Recorded so a later sweep does not read it as a collapse.

## §9 · BLIND POLARITY READ — 2026-08-18, and the adjudication

A fenced reader (Latin twin + English only, never this file). **5 sites raised · 3 confirmed
and repaired · 2 recorded without repair.** **Zero polarity defects**: all eight negations
Migne prints are carried (*Non dicit · non imperat · non autem retributionem · non de
humili · non ait · ne ille non crederet · non solum … sed · ut non amplius faciat*), and
none is invented. **Zero silent repairs** — Douay's *utilis* → "profitable" was resisted at

Douay's "refreshed **by** thee".

### ✅ 0642C · VERS. 12 — CONFIRMED, REPAIRED. Reference mis-bound by English word order.

**Printed:** *Onesimum profugum [0642C] recurrentem ad divinum auxilium **cum esset in
custodia** Apostolus baptizavit…*

**Was:** "The Apostle baptized Onesimus, a fugitive [0642C] running back to the divine help,
**when he was in custody**, seeing in him…"
**Now:** "The Apostle, **while he was in custody**, baptized Onesimus, a fugitive [0642C]
running back to the divine help, seeing in him…"

*esset* prints no subject, and the work has already named who is in custody **twice** — the
ARGUMENTUM (*scribens ei ab urbe Roma **de carcere***) and VERS. 1 (*Eo ergo tempore **in
custodia erat.** Ideo ait se vinctum*). It is **Paul**. Our word order stranded the *cum*
clause after "a fugitive running back to the divine help", so the nearest available
antecedent became **Onesimus** — putting on a public page the claim that the runaway slave
was in prison, which Migne does not print and which makes nonsense of *profugum
recurrentem*: a man in custody is not a fugitive running for refuge. **No word was wrong,
only the binding** — invisible to every automated check.

### ✅ 0641D · VERS. 6 — CONFIRMED, REPAIRED. ***ejus*, not *sua*, and the morphology decides it.**

**Printed:** *Hoc enim orat **Apostolus,** ut operatio **ejus** cum cognitione fiat
bonitatis, id est, ut tali mente **operetur,** ut in futurum inde **exspectet** praemium
Jesum Christum…*

**Was:** "…that **his** working may be done…" **Now:** "…that **that man's** working may be
done…"

*ejus* is the genitive of *is* — by definition **not** the subject of *orat*. The working is
Philemon's, and our own English had identified it correctly one clause earlier (*operatio
fidei qua **servis** Christo et sanctis* → "the working of faith by which **thou** servest
Christ and the saints"). But "the Apostle" then stood immediately before "his working", so
every following pronoun read as Paul: the page said Paul prays that **Paul's own** working
be done with knowledge and that **Paul** look for Christ as his reward. That is a different
sentence from the one printed, and it detached the gloss from the lemma it expounds.

"That man's" is the literal value of *ejus* and matches this work's own register — VERS. 11
already renders *ille* as "that man" two paragraphs later. Migne's own shift from second
person (*tua*, *servis*) to third (*ejus*, *operetur*, *exspectet*) is his, and is preserved.

### ✅ 0642B · VERS. 11 — CONFIRMED, REPAIRED, and the mark chosen deliberately

Migne prints *…exstinguat iracundiam Domini**,** non ait: Nunc tibi utilis erit…* — one
sentence. Our English had **supplied a terminal stop** and made it two. Now a **semicolon**.

⚑ **Why a semicolon and not Migne's comma, when the 3 John read (8989 §4) restored a comma
in the same cadence — the two are not inconsistent.** Pattern 8b's test is whether English
*refuses* the mark. At 8989's 0705C English does not refuse Migne's semicolon, so it was
restored exactly. Here English does refuse a bare comma between two finite main clauses
(*exstinguat … non ait*), so the minimal faithful accommodation is the mark that keeps his
sentence **one sentence**. The full stop was the over-correction; recasting his finite
*non ait … sed introduxit* into participles to save the comma would have been a larger
liberty than the punctuation it rescued.

### ⬜ 0641C · VERS. 7 — *viscera*: the rigidity is right, and there is one site where NO English word works

The fixed rendering **"bowels" holds at all three sites** — *Viscera sanctorum* (0641C),
*Ut mea viscera* (0642C), *Refice viscera* (0642D) — with no drift, and the reader verified
it independently. But at the one place where **Migne glosses the word himself** it fails:
*Viscera sanctorum. Id est **filii sanctorum charissimi***. *Viscera* in Latin covers
**offspring** (*viscera mea* = "my own child"), which is why the gloss can say "that is, the
most beloved sons of the saints" — and "bowels … that is, the most beloved sons" is an
inference no English reader can follow.

**Nothing was changed, and that is the finding.** "Hearts" fails *filii* just as badly and
fails *Refice viscera*; "children" fails the other two sites and would be a silent repair of
the boldest kind. **No English word carries both senses**, so no rendering can be made to
work at every site. "Bowels" is the literal choice, not a Douay reach (Douay merely agrees),
and the polysemy stays visible in the facing Latin column.

⚑ **The general lesson, against §2's own ruling:** a fixed-vocabulary pair earns its
rigidity where the two members meet — but rigidity cannot manufacture a sense the English
word does not have. Where the author glosses his own word, the gloss is the test of the
rendering, and a rendering that survives the test everywhere except at the author's own
definition is the best available, not a correct one.
