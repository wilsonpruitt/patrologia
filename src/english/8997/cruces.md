### The two `[var: …]` markers

**0637D · *Secundum fidem electi.*** — the Vulgate reads *secundum fidem electorum Dei*.
Migne's *electi* is a real Latin form and the gloss immediately construes it as the
nominative plural — *quam tenent vel qua salvantur **electi Dei*** — so the divergence is
not a slip the gloss ignores; the gloss reads the lemma as printed. English *the elect* is
number-neutral and carries either. Marker: `[var: Vulg. *electorum Dei*, "of the elect of
God"]`.

**0640D · *gratia Dei et Salvatoris,* etc.** — the Vulgate has *gratia Dei Salvatoris
nostri* (Tit. II, 11), one person, no *et*. Migne's *et* would ordinarily split it into
two, and the gloss then binds it back into one — *id est Deus et Salvator noster, scilicet
Dei Filius*. The added conjunction is therefore load-bearing for what the gloss does with
it, which is the Pattern 14 test. Marker: `[var: Vulg. *gratia Dei Salvatoris nostri*,
without *et*]`.

### The five divergences DECLINED, with reasons

Pattern 14 fires on divergence "in a way that changes the sense". These five do not, and
marking them would be noise on a page that carries two real ones:

1. **0639A *Unius uxoris virum*** vs Vulg. *unius uxoris vir* — accusative for nominative;
   the form is that of I Tim. III, 2, from which the phrase is habitually quoted. **English
   has no exponent for the case** (Pattern 9's own argument): "the husband of one wife"
   either way. Recorded, not marked.
2. **0639B *esse sine crimine*** vs Vulg. *sine crimine esse* — word order inside a
   fragment the gloss has already recast as its own syntax (*necesse est … eum esse*).
3. **0640B *Non vino multo*** vs Vulg. *non multo vino servientes* — adjective/noun order.
   Sense identical; the gloss turns on the wine, not the order.
4. **0641A *Eramus enim et nos aliquando*** vs Vulg. *Eramus enim aliquando et nos* —
   *et nos* fronted. "For we also were once" renders either.
5. **0638D *Haec est vita aeterna*** (Joh. XVII, 3, *Haec est autem…*) and **0639D *Vidit
   Deus…*** (Gen. I, 31, *Viditque Deus…*) — a dropped *autem* and a dropped enclitic
   *-que* in two proof-texts quoted inside the gloss. Both are connectives at the head of a
   quotation lifted out of its sentence; neither changes what the verse asserts.

## §2 · *PRESBYTERI* AND *EPISCOPUS* — THE GLOSS TREATS THEM AS **ONE OFFICE**, AND SAYS SO

This was the standing instruction not to resolve. The gloss resolves it itself, in as many
words, at **0639C**:

> *Hic aperte ostendit presbyterorum nomine episcopos supra fuisse designatos.*

Rendered "Here he plainly shows that under the name of presbyters bishops were designated
above." The chain is unbroken across three verses and one column: VERS. 5 glosses
*Presbyteros* on why the plural is used (*ut … pastoralis curae onus facilius sustineatur,
per multos divisum*); VERS. 7's *Oportet enim* — whose Vulgate continuation is
*episcopum* — glosses it as *irreprehensibilem episcopum*; and then the sentence above
states the identification outright. **The gloss treats them as one office.**

**What the English does about it: nothing, deliberately.** *Presbyteros* → **presbyters**,
*episcopum* → **bishop**, *presbyterii* (Argumentum) → **presbytery**, each rendered as it
stands and kept lexically distinct throughout, so that the identification the gloss makes
is visible as *the gloss's own act* and not as our vocabulary. "Priest" was considered and
rejected on the brief's own ground: it imports the judgement into the word. A reader who
wants the identification finds it at 0639C, in Migne's sentence, where it belongs.

⚑ Note also that the **Argumentum** already frames the epistle as being *de constitutione
presbyterii* — the presbytery, not the episcopate — which is why VERS. 7's identification
is the interesting sentence in the chapter rather than a throwaway.

## §3 · THE UNEXPRESSED SUBJECTS — EVERY SUPPLIED PRONOUN, NAMED

The brief's dominant defect class. Every "he/she/it/they" the English supplies is listed
here with what fixed it. The household code (ch. II) is where the subject shifts fastest.

**The Argumentum · *commonefacit et instruit*** — "**He** admonishes Titus." Paul. Fixed by
the closing participle *scribens ei a Nicopoli*, which only the letter's author can govern,
and by the form itself (this is the standard prologue to a Pauline epistle).

**0637D · *Quidam nobilitate*** — "**Certain men** by nobility": *quidam* is expressed, and
the exempla that follow (Moses, David, Mary) fix it as persons, not the abstract *conditio*.

**0639A · *Primo dicit quid agere debeat et quales ordinare presbyteros*** — **two different
subjects in one clause.** *dicit* = Paul (he is the one saying); *agere debeat* and
*ordinare* = **Titus**, from Tit. I, 5 *reliqui te Cretae … ut constituas presbyteros*.
Likewise *Dum haec recolit … monet exsequi*: Paul recalls, Paul admonishes, **Titus**
carries out, and *sicut ei disposuerat* = as Paul had appointed for Titus (*sicut et ego
disposui tibi*).

**0639B · *quotidie fieri non quiescunt … quae … remittantur*** — "**they** do not cease to
be committed daily." The subject is **sins**, neuter plural, and it is not printed: it is
lifted out of *sine peccato* two clauses earlier, and confirmed by the neuter plural
relative *quae … remittantur*. Nothing else in the sentence is plural.

**0639B · *nec frontem habet ad alios talium cohabitator*** — the subject IS printed
(*cohabitator*); the English keeps it as a full noun phrase ("one who dwells with such
persons") rather than a pronoun, because a bare "he" here would attach to the *filii* of
the lemma instead of to the father who would be ordained.

**0639D · *Hoc ipsi negant*** — "**This they deny.**" *ipsi* is expressed but empty: its
referent is the Judaizers of Tit. I, 10–14 (*maxime qui de circumcisione sunt*, *mandatis
hominum*), which VERS. 14 has just glossed one paragraph above. **Not** the *mundis* of the
lemma.

**0639D · *intelligi voluit*** — "**he** willed this to be understood." **Moses**, from
*quaedam Moyses prohibuit* in the immediately preceding clause; the whole sentence is about
what Moses' prohibitions were for. Not God, though *quas Deus creavit* stands inside the
same sentence — and that is exactly the trap: God is the nearer noun and the wrong subject.

**0640B · *Sunt enim plurima quae … vix fieri patiantur*** — "which at an age of this kind
**they** scarcely suffer to be done." ⚠ **Two readings construe** and the choice is worth a
second eye. (a) *senes* (the aged men of the lemma) are the subject of the deponent
*patiantur*: "there are very many things which men of that age scarcely allow to be done."
(b) *plurima* is the subject: "very many things which scarcely allow themselves to be
done." **(a) was taken**, because *patior* deponent is active in meaning and wants an
animate sufferer, and because the whole `[n: (CHRYS.)]` note is an inventory of what old
age *does* — but (b) cannot be excluded from the printed words alone.

**0640B · *earum incessus, motus, vultus, sermo*** — "**their** gait": the **aged women**
(*anus*), from Tit. II, 3 *anus similiter in habitu sancto*, which is the lemma's own
verse. Feminine plural genitive fixes it; the *senes* of VERS. 2 are masculine.

**0640B · *Solent enim esse quaedam*** — "certain **women**": *quaedam* feminine plural,
still the *anus*, continuing from *Non vino multo* (Tit. II, 3).

**0640B · *Quos … diligunt, ament***, VERS. 4 — "whom **they** love … let **them** love":
the **young women** (*adolescentulae*), from Tit. II, 4 *ut prudentiam doceant
adolescentulas, ut viros suos ament*. The subject changes from the previous paragraph's
*anus* at the verse boundary and nothing in the sentence marks it: *viros suos* does.

**0640C · *Hortare senes, et anus, et adolescentulas*** — the three groups are expressed;
no supply needed. Same at 0640C VERS. 10 (*ad senes, et anus, et servos*), where the
addition of *servos* is what makes the paragraph a summary of the whole household code.

**0640C · *ut ita ornent … doctrinam Salvatoris*** — "**that they may adorn**": the subject
is *omnes praedictos* in the same sentence, i.e. all three groups. Expressed.

**0640C · *quantum in ipso est occidit eos*** — "**he** kills **them**": the man who lives
ill (*Omnis … qui male vivit*, expressed) kills **those over whom he is set** (*eorum
quibus praepositus est*, expressed). Both printed; no supply.

**0640D · *veniet praemia daturus*** — "**Who** will come": Christ, from *magni Dei et
Salvatoris* in the lemma; the relative *Qui* is printed and its antecedent is the lemma
above, per the brief's own rule.

**0641A · *Per quam dona nobis spiritualia abunde tribuit*** — "**he** bestowed": **God our
Saviour**, from Tit. III, 4 *benignitas et humanitas apparuit **Salvatoris nostri Dei***,
of which the lemma *Benignitas* is the opening word. The relative *quam* is *benignitas*
itself.

**0642A · *mandat Tito, ut priusquam veniat Nicopolim, praemittat eos*** — **three
subjects, two of them unexpressed.** *mandat* = **Paul** (the sentence's *per epistolas
Pauli* names him, and only he charges Titus); *veniat* and *praemittat* = **Titus**, named
as the addressee of *mandat Tito* and fixed by Tit. III, 12, where it is Titus who is to
hurry to Paul at Nicopolis. **The trap here is *veniat***: read of Paul it would say Paul
comes to Nicopolis, which the Vulgate does have him doing (*ibi enim statui hiemare*) — but
then *praemittat* would have no subject at all, and the point of the sentence is that Zenas
and Apollos are to be sped on their way **before Titus leaves**.

**0642A · *quia hujus professionis erat in Synagoga*** — "**he** was of this profession":
**Zenas**, the lemma's own subject (*Zenam legisperitum*), not Apollo, who enters the
sentence only as the term of comparison and gets his own lemma next.

## §4 · THE FIXED WORD-PAIRS THE BRIEF NAMED

- ***munda* / *immunda*** (0639D) — **clean / unclean**, rigid across all four occurrences,
  including *ab immundis munda discrevit* → "separated clean things from unclean", where
  the two members meet. ⚑ **The brief also named *pollutis* / *incredulis* for this verse:
  neither word occurs.** Migne's gloss on Tit. I, 15 stops at *Omnia munda mundis* and never
  quotes the verse's second half (*coinquinatis autem et infidelibus*). Recorded as a
  checked absence, not an omission.
- ***crimen* / *peccatum*** (0639A–B) — **crime / sin**, and this pair carries the whole
  paragraph: *Si dixisset, sine peccato, nullus … posset ordinari* is unintelligible unless
  the two words stay apart in English. Kept apart at all seven occurrences, including the
  return of *esse sine crimine* at 0639C.
- ***sobrii*** (0640A) — **sober**. The brief anticipated *sobrii / sobrias / sobrie* across
  ch. II; **only *sobrii* is printed** (Tit. II, 5's *sobrias* and II, 12's *sobrie* are in
  verses this gloss does not touch). One word, one rendering, nothing to link.
- ***sanam doctrinam* / *doctrinam Salvatoris*** (0640A, 0640C) — one *doctrina*, one
  English word: **sound doctrine** / **the doctrine of the Saviour**, and *doctrine* again
  at both non-lemma occurrences. Not varied.
- ***benignitas* / *humanitas*** (0641A) — ***humanitas* is not printed.** Migne's lemma
  stops at *Benignitas,* etc. Rendered **Benignity** rather than "kindness", deliberately,
  so that the English word for *humanitas* stays unspent should the pair ever be quoted
  whole elsewhere in the Glossa block.
- ***sectator* / *sectantes*** (0640D) — **a pursuer of good works** / **pursuing a pure
  life**, kept on one root, because the gloss's argument is that the second is what the
  first means.
- ***contradicunt* / *contradicentes* / *contradicere*** (0639C) — all **contradict**;
  *arguere* → **reprove**, *redarguere* → **confute**, kept distinct so that the lemma's own
  verb and the gloss's intensified one do not collapse into one English word.
- ***facta* / *verba* / *mores*** (0640A) — **deeds / words / manners**, rigid through
  VERS. 16, where the antithesis is repeated five times.

## §5 · THE CRUCES PROPER

### 0640A · *fides tantum meorum proprie fides est daemoniorum* — RENDERED LITERALLY, NO MARKER

Migne prints, closing the chapter:

> *Nam si confitemini verbis et non factis, fides tantum meorum proprie fides est
> daemoniorum.*

*meorum* ("of my own", gen. pl. of *meus*) has no antecedent anywhere in the work and no
grammatical work to do beside *fides tantum*. Rendered as printed: "the faith only of my own
is properly the faith of devils."

**Two conjectures, both plausible, neither adopted.** (a) ***verborum***: "the faith of
words only is properly the faith of devils" — this is what the sentence's own antithesis
(*verbis … non factis*) demands, and it is the reading the Augustinian source behind the
paragraph supports. (b) ***talium***: "the faith of such men", picking up *Tales sunt omnes
mali catholici* three sentences earlier.

**`[cj: …]` declined, and the ground is Pattern 18a.** The bar is that the printed word make
the sentence *assert something the author is arguing against*. It does not: it makes the
clause fail to construe, which 18a explicitly leaves declined ("mood, voice, number and
person anomalies that merely fail to construe stay declined"). *meorum* is neither a
negation nor a correlative, so the 2026-08-15 extension does not reach it either. And with
two live conjectures competing, a `[cj:]` would have to choose one and print it on the
reading page as ours. It stays here.

⚑ **Note what is NOT wrong with the sentence:** every negation is carried (*non factis*),
and the surrounding argument survives intact, because the corruption is in the subject of
the last clause rather than in its force. This is the shape 7a⁗ warns about in reverse —
nothing is quarantined, so nothing is swallowed.

### 0637D · *Conditione non tamen peccati misera servitute. sed Dei.* — MIGNE'S FULL STOP KEPT

The plate sets a full stop between *servitute* and *sed*, mid-construction. Pattern 8:
never delete a mark he prints. The English keeps the stop and the lower-case *but*: "By
condition, yet not by the wretched servitude of sin. but *of God.*" The same shape recurs
at **0638D** (*Haec res. hic fructus apostolici officii.*) and is kept the same way: "This
is the matter. this the fruit of the apostolic office."

Capitalization was the live question, since English capitalizes after a stop. It is left
lower-case because capitalizing would be *our* repair of *his* punctuation, and the whole
point of carrying the stop is that a reader sees the plate's own sentence division.

### 0639C · *Quis enim audet apertissime loquendo contradicere veritati.* — A QUESTION MIGNE PUNCTUATES AS A STATEMENT

*Quis … audet* is interrogative in form and the sentence is plainly rhetorical, but the
plate prints a full stop. **Pattern 8: never supply a `?` he does not print.** Rendered "For
who dares, by speaking most openly, to contradict the truth." with the stop. This is the
work's only would-be question, and it is why the English carries **0 question marks against
the Latin's 0** — the parity is real, not an accident of a dropped clause.

### 0637D · *et ideo per ea magis laborandum* — *ea* NEUTER, NO CLEAN ANTECEDENT

Rendered "and therefore one must labour the more through these things." *ea* is neuter
plural and the only candidates in the sentence are feminine (*fidem*, *quam*, *qua*) or
masculine (*electi*). Conjecturally *eam* (= *fidem*), "one must labour the more for it".
Rendered as printed; no marker — the sentence asserts nothing false, it merely leaves the
referent open, and English "these things" is as open as *ea*.

### 0642A · *praemittat eos ita solite* — *solite* FOR VULGATE *sollicite*?

Tit. III, 13 reads *Zenam legisperitum et Apollo **sollicite** praemitte*, and the gloss is
paraphrasing that very command. Migne prints *solite*, "in the accustomed manner" — a real
adverb, which occupies its slot and yields sense. Rendered as printed ("he send them on
ahead in the accustomed manner"). No `[cj:]`: under 18a the printed word must make the
sentence say something the author is arguing against, and "in the accustomed manner" is
merely a weaker claim than "carefully", not a contrary one. Recorded because a later reader
with the plate in hand may want to check the type.

### 0641A–B · *Haereticus est qui … qui vitandi sunt* — SINGULAR TO PLURAL, KEPT

The gloss defines the heretic in the singular (*Haereticus est qui … impugnat … astruit …
confirmet*) and then closes on a plural relative (*qui vitandi sunt … correcti exercitatiores
essent*). English has a full exponent for this shift, so it is rendered visibly: "A heretic is
he who … ; who are to be avoided: because, being the more frequently corrected, they would be
the more practised for evil." No marker. Pattern 9 normalizes only where English *cannot* show
the defect; here it can, so showing it is the honest treatment (Pattern 12's test (2), run in
the other direction).

### 0641B → 0642A · THE COLUMN JUMP — CHECKED, NOT REPORTED

The anchors run … 0641A, 0641B, **0642A**, 0642B, skipping 0641C–D. Per the runbook's known
false positive 1, band arithmetic manufactures phantom gaps and only text broken mid-word or
mid-construction across an anchor is a signal. The break here falls cleanly between
*qui vitandi sunt:* and *quia frequentius correcti* — a colon boundary, both halves complete.
**Checked and deliberately not reported**; no `[ed:]`, no patch, no plate call.

## §6 · SUPPLIED WORDS IN FRAGMENTARY LEMMATA (Ruth convention 3)

Three lemmata are fragments that are not English on their own. Each is rendered as the phrase
it stands for, per the pilot's settled convention, and each supply is named here because the
convention licenses supply and therefore has to be auditable:

1. **0640B *Ut viros suos.*** → "*That they may love their own husbands.*" Supplies *ament*
   from Tit. II, 4. The gloss's very next words (*ament etiam pudica dilectione*) confirm the
   verb the fragment opens.
2. **0640B *Ut non blasphemetur.*** → "*That the word of God be not blasphemed.*" Supplies
   *verbum Dei* from Tit. II, 5. ⚠ Where Migne repeats the phrase in his own running prose at
   the end of the same paragraph (*ut non blasphemetur, etc.* — **not** italic, so not a
   lemma), the English supplies nothing and reads "that it be not blasphemed, etc." The
   convention covers lemmata; it does not license supply in the gloss's own sentences.
3. **0640C *Ut doctrinam Salvatoris.*** → "*That they may adorn the doctrine of the Saviour.*"
   Supplies *ornent* from Tit. II, 10 — which Migne then picks up as a lemma of its own two
   sentences later (*Ornent.* → "*Let them adorn.*"). The small overlap is his, not ours.

A fourth was considered and **declined**: **0640A *Factis autem.*** → "*But by deeds.*" The
Vulgate continues *negant*, but "But by deeds" is already English and already the phrase the
gloss expounds; supplying "they deny" would add a verb the gloss itself supplies four words
later (*Si negatio … fit*).

## §7 · READ CLEAN

Named so a blind reader can tell checked ground from unexamined ground:

### ✅ 0642A · VERS. 13 — CONFIRMED, REPAIRED. **The dropped correlative was concealing a broken plate.**

**Printed:** *…praemittat eos **ita** solite, **ut** nihil in viatico illis desit.*
**Was:** "…he send them on ahead **in the accustomed manner, so that** nothing be lacking…"
**Now:** "…he send them on ahead **so customarily that** nothing be lacking…"

*ita* had gone unrendered, and its bare "so that" had nothing to answer to. ⭐⭐ **This is the
finding worth carrying forward, because the dropped particle was doing damage beyond
itself.** Rendered with the correlative intact, the sentence visibly **does not construe** —
and that is the point: *solite* is almost certainly the plate's corruption of ***sollicite***,
which is the Vulgate's own word at the very verse being glossed (Titus 3:13, *Zenam legis
peritum et Apollo **sollicite** praemitte*) and which makes *ita … ut* work perfectly
("send them on so carefully that nothing be lacking"). Our English rendered the corrupt word
faithfully, per Pattern 7 — and then quietly dropped the particle that would have exposed
the break, so the page read as a bland, complete sentence.

⚑ **A defect can hide a second defect by making the page read smoothly.** Fidelity to the
words is not fidelity if the connective that reveals the trouble is left out. No marker
proposed: Pattern 18's census is closed (18a), and this is recorded as a reading problem.

### ✅ 0640B · VERS. 2 — CONFIRMED, REPAIRED. An invented subject with no antecedent.

**Printed:** *Sunt enim plurima quae in hujusmodi aetate vix fieri patiantur.*
**Was:** "…which at an age of this kind **they** scarcely suffer to be done."
**Now:** "…which at an age of this kind **scarcely suffer themselves to be done**."

Both readings construe and the choice is not free of doubt — §2 had taken *senes* as the
subject on the ground that deponent *patior* wants an animate sufferer. But **there is no
antecedent for "they" in reach**: the subject of the two preceding sentences is *senectus*,
singular, and the only plurals nearby are the vices themselves; a reader must jump back past
a singular subject to *Senes* in the lemma three sentences up. Taking *quae* as the subject
of the deponent in a relative clause of characteristic **supplies nothing**, and it is what
the whole Chrysostom excerpt is about — what old age can no longer manage.

⚑ **The rule applied: where two readings construe, prefer the one that supplies no unprinted
word.** The near-unparseable English was itself the tell.

### ✅ 0639B · VERS. 6 — CONFIRMED, REPAIRED. An idiom rendered word by word.

*…nec **frontem habet ad** alios talium cohabitator.* "A face for others" → "**the face to
confront others**." *frontem habere ad* is the idiom of effrontery — the standing to face
someone down. Pattern 12's boundary governs: **normalize the language, mark the page.** The
harder half of the sentence was already right, the nominative *cohabitator* correctly
identified.

### ⛔ 0640A — *fides tantum meorum*: DECLINED as an edit, and the reader agrees it is Migne's

*Nam si confitemini verbis et non factis, fides tantum **meorum** proprie fides est
daemoniorum.* The reader independently reached §2.1's conclusion — the argument has run its
whole length on *verbis / factis*, and the clinching sentence wants ***verborum***: "a faith
of **words** only is properly the faith of devils." It also confirmed that this is **the
plate's loss, not ours**: *meorum* is a real form, so `[sic:]` has nothing to wrap, and 18a
forecloses the other marker. Rendered as it stands. The English is unreadable at the
argument's climax and that is Migne's page, faithfully shown.

### ⛔ 0639B — negation scope, DECLINED: the English mirrors the Latin's own looseness

*Id est, **nec** saltem de luxuria accusatos, **vel** sibi **non** subditos.* The reader
warned that "not so much as accused of wantonness, or not subject to him" lets a reader take
the second member as a property of faithful children. **But the Latin is loose in exactly the
same way**: *nec* negates *accusatos*, while the second member carries its own *non*, so a
reader of Migne must distribute the sense precisely as a reader of our English must. Tidying
it would be repairing his syntax, not translating it. Reader's own confidence: medium, with
the admission that the distribution is available. Named here rather than smoothed.

### ⛔ 0640C — the bare "it" in the re-quoted *ut non blasphemetur*: DECLINED, and it is an artifact of the convention

The lemma *Ut non blasphemetur.* is expanded to "That the word of God be not blasphemed"
(convention 1.2, a fragment rendered as the phrase it stands for); the identical Latin
re-quoted four lines down is rendered bare, "that **it** be not blasphemed." The
inconsistency is real but it is **produced by the convention working correctly** — the lemma
is expanded because it is a lemma, and the re-quotation is not. Carrying "the word of God"
into the second would supply words Migne does not print there.

⚑ Worth knowing for the remaining Glossa books: **fragment expansion in a lemma will
routinely look inconsistent with the same words re-quoted bare in the gloss.** That is by
design, not drift.
