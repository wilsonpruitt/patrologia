## §1 · WHAT THIS WORK IS — complete, not a stub

Migne prints **the preface only** for Baruch, and he says why himself, in the work's one
note: *Antiqua exemplaria Glossae ordinariae textum tantummodo Baruch exhibent, nulla
comitante expositione* — the old copies of the *Glossa ordinaria* give the text of Baruch
alone, with no exposition accompanying it. **There is no missing material.** The work is
complete as Migne printed it: a preface, and an editorial note stating that the gloss
tradition carries nothing further on this book. Anyone reading this page should take the
brevity as the *Glossa*'s own testimony about Baruch, not as an abridgment of ours.

The parallel case in the corpus is **8948 (Esther) @0748C**, where the same editorial voice
records that the ancient copies carry no exposition from that point to the end of the book
and that chapters 11–16 are therefore passed over. Same class of note, same handling
(below). It is a hint, not an authority — but it is the same editor writing about the same
manuscript tradition, and the two agree.

## §3 · THE `[nt:]` DECISION (Pattern 15) — and why it is not close

The single `[n:]` in the Latin twin reads, with Migne's own internal spacing:

    [n: ( Antiqua exemplaria Glossae ordinariae textum tantummodo Baruch exhibent, nulla comitante expositione. )]

**Converted to `[nt:]` and translated, at the same marker index, in the English chunk only.**
The Latin twin is untouched and keeps `[n:]`.

Pattern 15 decides by **content, not length**: `[n:]` is reserved for *citations* — locators
into a source corpus. This note contains no locator of any kind. No book, no chapter, no
work title, no *Vide*, no *tom.*, no *cf.* It is a finite verb making an **assertion about
the manuscript tradition** — *exemplaria … exhibent*, with an ablative absolute stating what
does not accompany the text. That is a scholion, and left in Latin it would leave an English
reader a Latin sentence in the middle of the page carrying the one fact that explains why
the page is short.

**The rival reading, and why it lost.** The note could be read as a *see-under* — an
editorial cross-reference of the kind 8948 keeps as `[n:]` (*Vide inter opera B. Hieronymi,
tom. VIII.*). It lost on two counts. First, that note names a place a reader can go
(*inter opera … tom. VIII*); this one names none. Second, this note's content is precisely
the proposition the reader needs in order to understand the work, and Pattern 15 exists for
exactly that case. **Judged by content, the call is unambiguous.**

⚑ **Migne's internal spacing inside the parentheses is reproduced character for character** —
the space after `(` and before `)` — following 8948's precedent. The `[nt:]` renders as
`.notecite.prose`, and per Pattern 15 the indexer keeps prose notes out of `fontes[]`: this
note is not a source and must not be indexed as one.

## §4 · THE SCOPE CALL — *in Hebraeo canone non habetur, sed tantum in Vulgata editione*

**This is the one sentence in the work with real doubt in it, and it is brief-test 2a's
named population: a negative near a `sed … tantum` pivot.**

Printed: *Liber iste … in Hebraeo canone **non** habetur, **sed tantum** in Vulgata
editione, similiter et epistola Jeremiae.*

- **The reading taken:** the `non` governs the **locus** — the prepositional phrase *in
  Hebraeo canone* — not the verb *habetur*. Migne is not denying that the book is had; he is
  saying **where** it is had and where it is not. The proof is structural, not stylistic:
  the second limb *sed tantum in Vulgata editione* has **no verb of its own** and must
  borrow *habetur* by ellipsis. A verb that the affirming limb borrows cannot be the thing
  the denying limb negates. The `sed … tantum` correlative pivots on the two ablative
  phrases, and the negative sits on the first of the pair.
- **The rival, and why it lost:** reading `non habetur` as a flat denial of existence
  ("is not had at all, except in the Vulgate"). It construes, and in isolation it reads
  perfectly — which is what makes it dangerous. It loses because it makes *tantum* modify a
  bare exception rather than the phrase it stands in, and because it would make the second
  limb assert existence rather than restrict location, which the shared verb forbids.
- **How the English holds the scope:** "is not contained in the Hebrew canon, but only in
  the Vulgate edition." The English negative stands before the verb, as the Latin's does,
  and the **"but only"** correlative fixes it on the phrase — the same construction the
  brief cites as handled correctly at **8960 @0740B** (*non affliguntur ex desperatione,
  sed…*), where a `non` standing before the verb is shown by the `sed` limb to govern the
  phrase. The verb ellipsis in the second limb is carried into English as ellipsis, so
  nothing is supplied.
- ⚠ **What was NOT done:** the sentence was not recast into "is found only in the Vulgate"
  or any other positive paraphrase. That would read better and would delete a printed
  negative (Pattern 7a).

## §5 · THE UNEXPRESSED SUBJECT — *hic scripta sunt*

*Propter notitiam autem legentium **hic scripta sunt**.* The subject is unexpressed and the
participle is **neuter plural** against a singular *liber iste*. **Named:** the subject is
the two books just listed — *liber iste* (masc.) and *epistola Jeremiae* (fem.) — for which
Latin takes the neuter plural as standard. This is what tells us the sentence is about both
books, not about Baruch alone.

Rendered "**they** have been written here." English "they" supplies no gender and no number
beyond what the Latin prints; naming the books explicitly ("these two books") was rejected
as supplying text Migne does not print. The following *indicant* is plural and agrees, which
confirms the antecedent.

- **`praenotatur`** — rendered "is headed with the name of Baruch." Not a coinage: the

  "Entitled" (8930/0002's rendering, with *sub … nomine*) is equally attested; "headed"

## Blind read (2026-08-23) — 1 site, ours, repaired

A fenced reader (no access to this file, no grep of `src/english/`) read the chunk against the
Latin. **One site, and it is ours.**

**col. 0064, in the `[nt:]` note.** Migne prints *Antiqua exemplaria Glossae ordinariae **textum
tantummodo Baruch** exhibent, nulla comitante expositione.* The English read "exhibit the text of
Baruch **only**, with no accompanying exposition" and now reads "exhibit **only** the text of
Baruch, with no accompanying exposition."

*tantummodo* sits second in its noun phrase and restricts *textum … Baruch* as a whole. The
contrast set is fixed independently by the ablative absolute that follows — *nulla comitante
expositione* names the thing excluded, and it is **the exposition**. English post-nominal "only"
attaches by default to the phrase on its left, so "the text of Baruch only" asserts *Baruch and no
other book* — a claim about which books the old copies contain, not about whether they carry a
gloss. A reader can retro-fit the right scope from the following clause, but the English's default
host was not the Latin's. Nothing was added and nothing dropped; every count passed.

⚑ Corpus control, class named: `textum tantummodo Baruch` matches the ordinary shape throughout
`src/latin/` — *tertiam tantummodo partem*, *tres tantummodo cubitos*, *simplicem tantummodo
historiam*, *sensum tantummodo moralem* — particle in second position, restricting the whole
phrase. The `sed tantum` in the body is offered only as corroboration, and it is the same class
(a restrictive particle inside a phrase); the finding rests on the ablative absolute.

⛔⛔ **The lesson, and it is the recorded one arriving again: this work's cruces contains a careful
adjudication of the scope of `non habetur … sed tantum` in the body — correct, argued from the
correlative, with the smoother positive paraphrase explicitly rejected for deleting a printed
negative — and the SAME CLASS in the note went straight past, in the very note the translator was
converting to `[nt:]`.** Using the apparatus somewhere is not evidence it was used where it was
needed. Ruth's four silent repairs were made by a translator marking `[sic:]` correctly two columns
away; here the distance was two lines.
