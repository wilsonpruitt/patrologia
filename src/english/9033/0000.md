## PREFACE. TO THE EMPEROR LOTHAIR.

[0551B]To the most glorious and most excellent emperor LOTHAIR, ever Augustus: ANGELOMUS, the least of monks, entreating by entreating, entreats to obtain everlasting glory.

Lately, when I was keeping watch in your sacred palace — under the pretext, I say, of the handing down of the liberal arts and of the unfolding, namely, of Holy Scripture — your sagacious prudence deigned, as it is not unmindful, to summon me to your attentive presence, and to hold converse with me. But after the spiritual conversations of the heavenly life, and the supports of prayers, it pleased you by commanding — nay rather, by threatening [ *minitando* ] — to command that, by the office of the pen, according to the sense of the ancient fathers and the frugality of my slender wit, I should set in order a little work upon the Song of Songs of Solomon, where the conjunction of Christ and the Church, [0551C] and the mysteries of the allegories, are proved to be contained beyond the rest of the τοῖς βιβλοῖς of the Old Testament; and that, so to speak, I should not refuse to expound it, and should not omit to display it to the presence of your eminence — whereby your diligence might have it, often frequented after the ordering of the commonwealth and brought back to your ears by reading, stretching itself out in contemplation away from the tumult of the affairs of the empire, and above all from the loss of your most holy consort, who by God's granting, as we believe, has been enrolled into the fellowship of the holy women and inscribed on the roll of blessedness — by reading contemplative things for the sake of consolation, and like the solitary turtledove, whose nature it is, as the natural philosophers report, that if once she has by mischance lost her mate, she does not seek another, but keeping chastity remains in a manner a widow, groaning alone and doing penance: of which we read in [0551D] the same book: « The voice, he says, of the turtledove is heard in our land [n: (Cant. II)], » that is, of Christ saying: « Do penance, for the kingdom of heaven will draw near [n: (Matth. III)]. » [var: Vulg. *appropinquavit*, "hath drawn near"] And fair are thy cheeks as the turtledove's, the little channels of the cheeks being flooded with a shower of tears, so that you might have been able to pour out in secret to the Lord prayers for your own excesses and for the absolution of the most devoted empress. Which having heard, dreading the weight of so great a matter, astonished — nay, stupefied — I began to wonder why it had pleased your clemency to enjoin so great a work, to be dreaded even by the most erudite men, upon me, unlearned, and one who has not attained all the disciplines of the liberal arts, and feeble in the oracles [0552B] and blessings of Holy Scripture, when you had in your monarchy many doctors who could have handled it honorably and catholicly. But considering the censure of your rule, and silently turning over that apostolic word by which it is said [n: (I Petr. II)]: « Be subject to every human creature for the Lord's sake, whether to the king as excelling, or to leaders as sent by him » [var: Vulg. *propter Deum*, "for God's sake"] , but rather that word of the Lord's, where he promises: « Amen, he says, I say to you, whatsoever you ask praying, believe that you shall receive, and it shall be done for you [n: (Marc. II)]. » [var: Vulg. Marc. XI, 24 *Propterea dico vobis, omnia quaecumque orantes petitis … et evenient vobis*] Perceiving also, by the inward weighing of my mind, that what was commanded by so great a majesty would not be without the divine assent, with rash mouth I presumed to gainsay by no apologetic and no winding of excuse, but rather, as far as [0552C] I could, relying on divine supports, I attempted, though fearful, to comply with the imperial commands. But not counting it worth a straw that, without the assurances and likewise the orders of my magnificent lord and abbot, namely Drogo your uncle, lest I should seem to exceed the discipline of the Rule, I should have had to set my pen to the writing — not only because he was the son of the emperor Charlemagne, and brother of your most holy and most devoted father Louis Caesar, but because he was a chief bishop and my distinguished abbot. And when by suggestion I had brought it in to his sacred ears, and had set forth the order of the matter, which having been drunk in by his ears, he not only refused, but — as he is most gentle, and persuadable in affability, most excellent in kindness, and most fervent toward every good — [0552D] after the manner of his modesty he not only commanded frankly by free authority, but with soothing tranquillity and devout address, that I should not refuse to undertake, as best I might, the [sic: *angustale*] work and spiritual ornament. Hearing which, so now at last emboldened by his exhortations, driven on by your nurturing utterances and holy commands, and above all relying on divine helps and stirred up by the prayers of the holy doctors: whatever, O most learned prince, I shall be able to find scattered among the holy doctors in our keeping — especially in the sayings of the most blessed Pope Gregory, of the ornament of Roman eloquence, since I find it compacted throughout by none alone [0553A] — I shall attempt to take in hand out of the tradition of my master, by the conjecture of my own wit, with the pen running, in a manner of speech rather clipped and compendious, though unpolished; and I shall endeavor to finish it in the form of a little book, so that your highness may have as it were an *enchiridion*, that is, a little book excerpted and in a manner handy, which, God favoring, it may please you to read often at fitting hours after the labors of empire, after the cares of the Church, ruminating upon divine things unto consolation. And because he cannot run through the most broad fields, namely of Holy Scripture, let it at least not irk him, at rest, to search out our *stromata* briefly. But it must indeed be known that, as is the custom of certain writers, we have not assigned all the names of the several doctors man by man on the little page in the margin, marked with letters; but rather, out of their sayings, we have judged it right to fit together some things of the [0553B] expositors from among many kinds — by briefly cutting short, that is, the manifold matters, the superfluities being removed — and, following their sense, to join together some things from the more prolix by excerpting, and, by interpolating some things of our own, to enlarge the longer ones. Thus assuredly, thus, with the various allusions of the dramas running through the short trains, which by knitting together in turn we have ordained to fill out the little book of sentences, not greatly fearing those who twist the nose as though they were wise, nor those who are readier to reprehend than to imitate, and who cannot fulfil what they contrive to reprehend by sneering. For we know, we know, that in the concord of the organ the pipes of bronze are some longer, some shorter; but when one blast has been administered by the bellows, and a trained hand commands, [0553C] and the fingers handle one — though the pipes are diverse — they sweetly render to astonished ears a harmony as a *salpinx*, the voices of trumpets and a concord, breathing forth melody in sweet song. So indeed, since some sentences are longer, the superfluities being cut away, and others shorter, the necessary things being added, we shall have dared — the Holy Spirit breathing through one body, the hand commanding and the fingers touching the pen — with the sentences glued together in turn, to fill out the dictations of the *enchiridion*, with the help of him who in this book says prophetically concerning the mingled gifts of the Church: Cypress with spikenard, spikenard with saffron, sweet cane and cinnamon with all the trees of Lebanon, myrrh and aloes with all the chief ointments. [var: Vulg. Cant. IV, 14 *nardus et crocus*, "spikenard and saffron"] But in what manner these virtues of the Church, alternating, are fitted together among themselves in mingled fashion, we shall say afterward in its own [0553D] place. Whence now at last I suppliantly entreat the imperial ornament, and equally those to whom it may perchance fall to read it, that what I have striven to do — after the manner of physicians and perfumers, who, that they may be able to compound various ointments and antidotes in due measure, take pains to weigh them evenly in the scales of the balance, so that the one may not overbalance the other, nor may they leave behind the less useful of the others, but may compound their own with an even scale — namely, to weigh sentences in the equal scale of the mind, and in a manner to compound honeys and antidotes for souls: that he neither shudder at it nor let it grow squalid, but read it through, surveying it with diligent assay, and gratefully search out the medicines of the soul. But before the prolegomena come to their end, it must be noted that in this book one ought to seek nothing carnal and nothing historical, [0554A] but ought not to doubt that the mysteries of the allegories are contained in it. For in this book kisses are named, breasts are named, cheeks are named, thighs are named. In which words the sacred description is not to be derided, but the greater mercy of God is to be considered, because while he names the members of the body, he thus calls us to his own love. Whence it is to be considered how wonderfully and how mercifully he works with us, who, that he might kindle our heart to the incitement of sacred love, will descend even to the words of base love, as Pope Gregory expounds. But whence he humbles himself in speaking, thence he exalts us to understanding, because from the discourses of this love we learn with what power we are to be fervent in the love of the Godhead. But this must be diligently observed by us, lest, when we have heard the words of outward [0554B] love, we remain at the perceiving of outward things. For this Scripture is in you just as a picture is in colors and in things. And he is exceedingly foolish who so clings to the colors of the picture that he is ignorant of the things. For if we embrace the words that are spoken outwardly and are ignorant of the senses, then, as though ignorant of the things that are depicted, we hold the colors alone. For we know that after the human race was driven out from the joys of paradise, and coming into this pilgrimage of the present life, it has a heart blind to spiritual understanding. To which blind heart, if it were said with the Lord's voice, "Follow God" or "Love God," it would not, through the torpor of insensibility, take in cold what it heard. Therefore by certain riddles the divine speech speaks to the torpid and cold soul, and out of things that it knows [0554C] it secretly insinuates a love that it does not know; and for the soul set far beneath, away from God, as the aforesaid treatise-writer says, he has made as it were a kind of engine, that by it she may be lifted up to God. For, riddles being interposed, while she recognizes in the words something that is her own, in the sense of the words she understands what is not her own, and through earthly words eternal things are hoped for. Therefore, O most unconquered Caesar, we pray that you seek nothing in this volume after the historical manner, but that you search out the flowers of the allegories together with the moral understanding, which in certain places we have determined to append for your consolation, lest, pressed down by the allusions of the allegories alone, your highness should violently grow weary in reading contemplative things. Let him also hear what is said through the same Solomon: « The glory of God is to conceal a word, and the glory of kings to search out [0554D] a speech [n: (Prov. V)]. » For to a mind seeking God, God appears the more gloriously the more subtly he is searched out that he may appear. We ought therefore to seek what God conceals in mysteries. And so there follows: "and the glory of kings to search out a speech." For they who not only reign over lands, but have known how to rule and to search out their own bodies, or the motions of the flesh, are kings. To whom it is also said through the Psalmist: « And now, O kings, understand; be instructed, you that judge the earth [n: (Psal. II)], » and the rest. The glory of kings, therefore, is to search out a speech, because it is the praise of those who live well to scrutinize the secrets of the commandments of God. Search therefore the Scriptures, most glorious emperor, so that, by penetrating and understanding the divine mysteries, your wisdom and diligence may be able to search out and to rule the desires and [0555A] motions of the flesh, and to teach the rest who are subject to your rule, and most honorably to govern their acts by prudence and by counsel. These things therefore having been tasted beforehand — nay rather, urgently demanded — [0556A] we shall at last endeavor to bend the pen to those things which you have commanded. We pray that your glory, and the ornament of the Franks, may ever fare well in Christ, prevailing with prosperous successes.
