## CHAPTER THE FIRST. (cont.)

« Fair are thy cheeks as the turtledove's. » After the holy kisses received, after all the gladness numbered out above, after the battle of the aforesaid sons of the mother, after the knowledge of herself, after the care of what sort she is and by whom she was created, after her power compared to the Lord's cavalry, the voice of Christ speaks to the Church. And now we are taught that the beginnings of her beauty are known, before the other members, from her cheeks. And these, for a great thing, are compared to the turtledove, that by its riddles he might show that she is to be drawn far away from the tumultuous disturbance of this world. This beauty, then, of the cheeks, which are adorned with the blush of chastity [0578A] and the whiteness of modesty, and are in the face of the body: it is no wonder if the persons of those who adorn the face of the Church are compared to them. Whence in the cheeks of the Church are the ancient Fathers, who stand first in that very face of honor and are red with the comeliness of martyrdom. But the Church is compared to the turtledove on account of the variety of diverse goods, or the chastity of virtues. Which bird alone before other birds, withdrawn from the throng of men, loves to inhabit secret places, and there sets its nest and brings up its young, where its enemy either never or scarcely has access. So assuredly the holy Fathers, so the holy and faithful soul, which is called his love by the Word of God, by which soul indeed — by fleeing and by rejecting the fellowships of the wicked, whatever is seen to be precious in the present life — in that [0578B] manner she is known to make a heavenly offspring, and she sends to heaven the offspring begotten by the word of her doctrine and nourished by the example of her life. For the turtledove, as we tasted beforehand in the preface, is related by those who have described the natures of birds to be of such chastity that it knows no mate beyond one. Of which, if one shall by chance have failed, never now is another joined to the other, but in all its life it seeks by longing its own lost mate. To which, through the comeliness of chastity and of modesty, the Church is fittingly compared in the beauty of her cheeks. For he says: « Fair are thy cheeks as the turtledove's. » As if it were said by Christ: With so great a variety of virtues have I adorned thee, that thou shouldst corrupt by no seduction of depraved teachers the chastity of the faith promised to me. These things indeed have been said according to the order of history; but [0578C] according to the opinion of some, a loftier sense is said to lie hid in this comparison. For to certain men it seems that the cheeks of the Church's beauty are compared to that turtledove of which it is said in the Psalm: « For the sparrow hath found herself a house, and the turtledove a nest for herself where she may lay up her young [n: (Psal. LXXXIII)]. » Where no wise man will be ignorant that the figure of the Holy Spirit is named in the sparrow, and that of the unspotted flesh assumed in the turtledove. Then, namely, is the Church praised as fair in her cheeks, which lie open to all, by the good and by the evil, by both, when through the greatest goods of chastity she shall have drawn ever so little into the likeness of the man assumed by Christ. Then is the whole body of the Church discerned to be most fair, when the modest look of the cheeks is admired by the impious for the glory of chastity. For then [0578D] is chastity proved of great power by its own enemies, when those who assail it will not be able to conquer it. For as the members which are in the head, if they have been fair, excuse the foulness of the other members which are covered with garments, so also those which through priestly honor are known to be members of the head of the Church adorn by their own chastity the foulness of the people's negligence. And on the contrary there is nothing so deformed, so abject in the body of the Church, save when in the aforesaid members a most base life or reputation is discerned. And because holy Church in her prelates and teachers imitates by chastity and by variety of virtues the image of the turtledove, that is, of Christ, she is necessarily praised by him, saying: « How fair are thy cheeks as [0579A] the turtledove's. » It follows: « Thy neck is as jewels. We will make thee golden *murenulae*, inlaid with silver. » In the neck there seem to be praised those who by the word of edification instruct the rest unto salvation, who transmit the spiritual food of the word, by interpreting it in whatever way, to the members of the body of Christ, whom belief alone supports; who through golden doctrine and through the jewel-like example of life are displayed upon the neck of the Church. For by the jewel the adornment of gems is discerned. But that their appearance may become fairer, they have need of the material of gold, by which, being joined together, they may be held in their own place. This too, the very natural talent of our nature, which is hewn out from a stony heart into the likeness of a gem, has need of the most shining help of the Holy Spirit, through whom the words of the divine law of the New and Old Testament, [0579B] which declared or showed that the Saviour of the world had come — when, chained together as by one little chain of truth, the word is made, passed through the jewel-like minds of souls — adorns the neck of the Church through the concord of faith, through the sense of unanimity. And because the holy teachers, who are designated by the neck, adorned with virtues, minister the word of preaching to others, and what they teach in word they carry out in works. Which works are expressed by the jewels; and because they preach the Scripture of both Testaments, which is designated by the golden *murenulae* — namely, because it shines within with the gold of spiritual senses, and glitters without with the silver of heavenly eloquence — rightly is the Church praised in them when it is said: « Thy neck is as jewels. *Murenulae* » or little chains « of gold we will make thee, inlaid [0579C] with silver, » because the holy teachers cannot have the gifts aforesaid unless they receive them from him, in whom are all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge hidden. It follows:

« While the king was at his repose, my spikenard gave forth its odor. » Spikenard is an aromatic shrub, with a heavy, as they say, and thick root, but short and black and brittle, though it is rich, smelling in its place like cypress, of a rough taste, with a small and dense leaf, whose tips spread themselves out into ears; and therefore the perfumers celebrate the spikes and the leaves of spikenard for a twofold endowment. And this is what Mark says: « Of ointment of precious spikenard [n: (Marc. XIV)]. » Namely, because that ointment which Mary brought to the Lord was compounded not only from the root of the spikenard, but also — [0579D] that it might be the more precious — by the addition of its spikes and leaves the grace of its odor and of its power had been heaped up. The naturalists also relate of spikenard that it is the chief ointment, whence deservedly the anointing of the head and feet of the Lord was offered. There are indeed many kinds of it, but all are herbs, except the Indian, which is more precious. But mystically this devotion of Mary ministering to the Lord designates the faith and the piety of holy Church, which speaks here in the song of love, saying: « When the king was at his repose, my spikenard gave forth its odor. » Which words she assuredly both fulfilled once according to the letter by the hands of Mary, and does not cease daily to fulfill spiritually in all her members. Which, spread through the whole [0580A] world, glory and say: But thanks be to God, who always maketh us to triumph in every place, « for we are the good odor of Christ unto God. » When she confesses with worthy reverence, praises and preaches the might of his divine power, which is his together with the Father, she assuredly pours precious ointment upon his head. But when she receives with equally worthy reverence the mysteries of the humanity assumed, she assuredly poured upon the feet of the Lord the ointment of pistic spikenard, that is, faithful and true: because that nature of his by which he deigned to touch the earth, that is, to dwell among men, she commends with pious preaching, and venerates with devoted services. Whence the repose is understood as the burial of Christ, although he had then reclined at table in the house of Simon the leper. But the spikenard is the women themselves who [0580B] came at first dawn to the tomb with spices, to anoint Christ and to spread abroad to believers the report of his resurrection. Whence it was said to them: « Go, tell my brethren that he goeth before you into Galilee; there you shall see him [n: (Matth. XXVIII)]. » [var: Vulg. Matth. XXVIII, 10 *ite nuntiate fratribus meis ut eant in Galilaeam, ibi me videbunt*, "go, tell my brethren that they go into Galilee, there they shall see me"; the clause *praecedit vos in Galilaeam, ibi eum videbitis* stands at Matth. XXVIII, 7] Or the repose of Christ is the beatitude of the heavenly secret. For she says: « When the king was at his repose, my spikenard gave forth its odor: » because, namely, while Christ the king rests in the beatitude of the heavenly secret, the spikenard gives forth its odor, that is, the virtues of the saints in the Church minister to us the grace of great sweetness. Or also the repose of the king is understood as the holy Church of the elect. In her Christ found rest for himself, as he himself speaks through the Prophet: « This is my rest for ever and [0580C] ever [n: (Psal. CXXXI)]. » The spikenard is said of the Church on account of the odor of the virtues. Whence the Apostle says: « We are the good odor of Christ unto God in every place [n: (II Cor. II)]. » Which Church, when the devil has seen her enriched, adorned with innumerable jewels of graces, and, the blandishments of all the sweet things of the present life being despised, hastening to that one sweetness of eternal rest, he sends in innumerable inflictions of bitterness, both by himself and by his satellites, at every moment. But because God the Redeemer is always in the midst of her, she is not moved from the footstep of truth, nor is she disturbed on account of the assault of the impious rushing upon her, because Christ is both all things and in all. For if bitter things are brought upon her for the love of Christ, they grow sweet with the dew of future hope and of everlasting [0580D] gladness. Therefore it follows:

« A bundle of myrrh is my beloved to me; he shall abide between my breasts. » Holy Church, or the faithful soul, perseveres in the praise of her Creator, whom she ponders as having suffered in the bitterness of his passion. Whence, always having in the memory of recollection how near he is to the breasts of the bosom, and how he suffered for her unto the glory of her redemption, she says: « A bundle of myrrh is my beloved to me; he shall abide between my breasts. » As if she had said: The death of my beloved, which he underwent for my salvation, shall always abide in my memory, for imitation and for the glory of praise.
