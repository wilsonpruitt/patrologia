## CHAPTER III.

« In my little bed by night I sought him whom my soul loveth. I sought him, and found him not. » « I came, as is added in certain codices, and called [0602B] him, and he answered me not. » These things therefore, although they were foretold and seem to have been fulfilled in the Lord's burial — while the women keep vigil at the Lord's sepulchre, when the apostles mourn unbearably — yet the things which are subjoined look further to higher matters; as if the Church, or each holy soul, were to say more openly: Long since with much zeal I sought him, but because I was still subject to the enticements of my flesh, and blinded by the darkness of a deep ignorance, I found not the light of truth, that is, the Lord. In another way the Church or the holy soul seeks her beloved in her little bed, when, undisturbed by every commotion of the world, in the quiet of her heart she thinks upon her Lord. Whom, seeking, she therefore finds not at all, in order that, kindled with love of him, seeking more ardently. By night [0602C] we seek, because even if the mind already keeps watch in him, yet the eye is still dim. « I called him, and he answered me not. » Whoever believes Christ to be man only, will not find him. [d: And whoever does not believe deity in Christ, will not find him.] And whoever does not believe deity in Christ, calls indeed upon the Lord, the Son of God, in the nights of his perverse senses, but he does not answer him, as he did not answer king Saul, from whom God had withdrawn. Without cause therefore does he spend labour in seeking, who has not confessed in both natures — equal to men without sin, and coeternal with the Father — God who redeemed, and the man through whom God redeemed those who were lost; of whom the Psalmist says: « A brother hath not redeemed, a man shall redeem [n: (Psal. XLVIII)]. »

[0602D] « I will rise, and will go about the city: through the streets and the broad ways I will seek him whom my soul loveth. I sought him, and I found him not. » I purposed in my mind to rise from the little bed of carnal pleasures, and to go about lands and seas, and to approach the mysteries of the philosophers, but not even so did I find him. It can also be understood otherwise. The city in this place is understood as the holy Church of the elect, or Holy Scripture. But the streets and the broad ways he calls the books of the law and of the [sic: *prophatarum*], in which the Church or the elect soul, while she ardently seeks her beloved, that is, Christ, finds him — whom she did not discover in the books of the philosophers.

« The watchmen who keep the city found me. Have you seen him whom my soul loveth? » [n: (Psal. CXXVI.)] The watchmen who keep the city, that is, the Church, [0603A] are the apostles or the other teachers, who found the anxious heathen world by the searching out of truth. Of whom it is said: « Unless the Lord keep the city, they watch in vain that keep it [n: (Psal. CXXVI)]. » [var: Vulg. Ps. 126:1 is singular — "in vain hath he watched that keepeth it" — and reads "in vain" with a different word]

« When I had a little passed by, I found him whom my soul loveth. » That is, when I gave myself over to their teaching, at once I found the light of truth which I sought. Otherwise: as we seek, the watchmen who keep the city find us, because the holy Fathers, who keep the state of the Church, meet our good endeavours, that they may teach us either by their word or by their writing. And when we have a little passed them by, we find him whom we love, because our Redeemer, even if in humility he was a man among men, yet in divinity he was above men. [0603B] For unless a man has passed by all the saints with the steps of faith and with a pure mind, and has believed Christ more excellent than all the saints, he will by no means be able to find him. « I held his hand, nor will I let him go, till I bring him into my father's house, and into the chamber of her that bore me. » [var: Vulg. Song 3:4 reads "I held him" and "into the house of my mother"] I hold him with firm faith, until at the end of the world, by the office of preaching, I bring him into the house and into the chamber of the Synagogue, which bore me in the Lord, « and there shall be one fold and one shepherd [n: (Joan. X)]. » There follows:

« I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem, by the roes and the harts of the fields, that you stir not up, nor awake my beloved, till she please. » Wherever therefore in this song like words of believers at divers times are found, the persons of divers [0603C] peoples are brought in. For here the person of that people seems to be introduced which after Christ's ascension, wearied with longing, sought him whom her soul loved — the Church, namely, from the Gentiles, which, being a little fortified with the peace of tranquillity, desired to rest in the sleep of security; and therefore the bridegroom repeats this verse, lest you should think he had less solicitude for the Church gathered from the Gentiles than for the Jews, but that of the two there might be one bride, and she most beloved — which little verse we have already expounded above. The daughters of Jerusalem are the souls of the elect, which behold the vision, that is, the peace of that heavenly Jerusalem. The roes or the harts are understood as the Fathers of the New and of the Old Testament, whose pastures are in the height of contemplation.

[0603D] « Who is she that goeth up by the desert? » This, I think, is the nation going up by the desert, which Paul the teacher of the Gentiles, gathered from Jerusalem as far as Illyricum, and anointed with the ointments of doctrine, and sprinkled with the spices of the mysteries of the sacraments, leads all the way to the bed of the peaceable king, of which we read in what follows. Whence the Synagogue wonders how the people of the Gentiles, cleansed by no mystery of circumcision, instructed by no admonition of the prophets, had suddenly gone up from the lowest pleasures, through the desert of heathenism and of idolatry, to the high summits of the virtues and to the embraces of the bridegroom. Truly it was a desert place where the name of Christ had not been named. « As a little rod of smoke, of the spices [0604A] of myrrh and frankincense, and of all the powders of the perfumer. » That is, kindled with the fire of love, or straining toward heavenly things with every effort of the virtues. By myrrh, the mortification of carnal pleasures; by frankincense, the equality of prayers; and in the divers powders of the perfumer, the odour of all the virtues. But morally, when the holy Church of the elect raises herself from this world in holy prayers with burning love, she goes up through the desert which she has deserted. Smoke is born of incense. And by the Psalmist it is said: « Let my prayer be directed as incense in thy sight, O Lord [n: (Ps. CXL)]. » Smoke is wont to shake out tears. And so the smoke from the spices is the compunction of prayer conceived out of the virtues of love. Which prayer, however, is called a little rod of smoke, because while it asks for heavenly things alone, it goes forth so straight [0604B] that it is not in the least bent back to the seeking of earthly and temporal things. And it is to be noted that it is called not a rod but a little rod, because sometimes, in the ardour of compunction, the force of love is of such subtlety that not even the mind itself, which enlightened has deserved to have it, [can] comprehend it. Well is this said, "of myrrh and frankincense." For frankincense by the law of the Lord is burnt in sacrifice. But by myrrh dead bodies are embalmed, lest they be corrupted by worms. They therefore offer the sacrifice of myrrh and frankincense who both afflict the flesh, lest the vices of corruption have dominion over them, and burn the fragrant victim of their love in the sight of the Lord, and show forth themselves to God in holy virtues. Whence there also is subjoined: « And of all the powders of the perfumer. » The powder of the perfumer [0604C] is the virtue of good working. And it is to be noted that the virtues of those who work well are called not perfumes but powders. For when we do any good things, we offer perfumes. But when we go back over those very good things which we do, and take heed by the judgment of review lest there be anything sinister in them, we make, as it were, powder out of perfumes, so that we may burn our prayer to the Lord more subtly by discretion and by love.

« Behold threescore surround the bed of Solomon, of the most valiant of Israel. » [var: Vulg. Song 3:7 reads "threescore valiant ones surround it"; Migne's lemma omits the word "valiant," which the gloss below expounds] Solomon, who is interpreted *the peaceable,* signifies the Lord Jesus Christ. The bed of Solomon is the quiet mind of the saints, in whom he himself rests as in a bed. The threescore valiant ones are the holy preachers, and most valiant warriors, who with a clean heart are worthy to see God. [0604D] For the number six is perfect, on account of the six days in which God made the world and the six ages of the world. But the number ten pertains to the decalogue of the law. Which, when it has been multiplied by six, they make up sixty. Six times ten are sixty.

« All holding swords, and most expert in war: every man's sword upon his thigh, because of fears in the night. » For they hold the sword of the spirit, which is the word of God, who fulfil in works what they teach with the mouth. But the sword is a watchful guard, pressing down the concupiscences of the heart, lest the uncleanness of life stain the word of preaching. But the fears of the night are the ambushes of the devil and hidden temptations. For neither is it to be believed of Solomon, a king of such [0605A] greatness, who so overflowed with immense riches that the weight of his gold could not be reckoned, and silver in his days had no price, that he made himself a litter of the woods of Libanus, as there follows:

« King Solomon made him a litter of the woods of Libanus. » The litter of Solomon is the holy Church, which lifts believers to the banquets of eternal blessedness, which is built of those strong in soul as of woods that cannot rot.
