## Appendix to the Translation of the Body of St. Hunegund



## 1.

[0071C]To describe the miracles of the saints, and to set down their notable deeds in writing for the knowledge of those who come after, ought above all to be judged by everyone as no idle labor for the faithful, but honorable and at the same time necessary. For when the dirges of fables are thrust by recitation upon the ears of fools, ears already inclined toward evil, the enticed spirit grows numb, the mind grows dull, and, having abandoned its own light, it flows downward, coveting the ruinous allurements of corruption and forsaking the path of uprightness; and so it comes about that the heart of the senseless, walking down the slopes at the follies it has heard, weaves for itself the chain by which it may be dragged and at last plunged into hell. But when the merits of the saints are published and brought forth into the hearing of the people, and their powers are proclaimed for the sake of profit, the faithful mind, rising from the sleep of death to life, stretches out its arms from the darkness of unbelief toward the pursuits of holiness; and rising up to prosperous things, it burns for brave ones, and takes precaution against the precipices of wickedness by seizing the road of pious intention. And having in this way been loosed from the knots of perdition, it hastens with free foot to the joys of the heavenly life, so far that [0071D] it may be able to see the God of gods in Zion, that is, in the watchtower of peaceful vision. Wherefore, brethren, let it not weary the charity of your love to hear a certain miracle which our Lord Jesus Christ willed to display, by his own condescension, in the moving of the body of his most holy virgin Hunegund. And concerning this moving — since the occasion of it has offered itself to us from close at hand — let it not be tedious to linger over it a little while [0072C] out of reverence, so that how that moving was carried out, or brought to completion, may reach the knowledge of the faithful by letters, however unskilled. For the affectionate intention of pious devotion has its own reward.

## 2.

In the year, then, of the Lord's incarnation one thousand and fifty-one, when that best of princes Henry, the father of King Philip, was governing the monarchy of the kingdom of the Franks, and when the abbot Macarius likewise was administering the care of the monastery of Homblières, it seemed good to the brethren who serve the Lord as soldiers under the rule of blessed Benedict that the body of their lady Hunegund ought — if the means should be at hand — to be moved; so that of her whose soul they did not doubt was honored among the choirs of virgins in the heavenly bridal chamber, they might not neglect to lay away her glorious body on earth in an honorable tomb. For at that time the sacred pearl was wrapped only in a little wooden casket, which offered to those looking on from outside a cheap dwelling, but which within kept a treasure altogether most dear. And that little structure [0072D] had been so fenced about and closed up with iron plates — the most devoted bones clinging within it — that it scarcely lay open to any openings, save by a very few contrivances of the mechanical art. Over the same there stood as well another somewhat larger shrine, built with no less skill, likewise fortified with iron bindings and very carefully secured with a great many nails, walled about on both sides with shrewd care [0073A] and industry, lest, that is, it should lie easily open to captious or fraudulent little wits. For he by no means desires to be plundered who thus lays up his treasure under hidden and most solid coverings. That the body of the virgin Hunegund, beloved of God, had been laid away in such a manner, we have received from those who saw it and reported it to us — from the time, that is, of the finding and the translation of the same virgin, which was celebrated in the year of the Lord's incarnation nine hundred and forty-six, in the reign of Louis, when Bertha, a woman consecrated to God, was at that time ruling the abbey of Homblières. The description of that finding, and of the translation as well, together with the notable miracles divinely made manifest there, the lord abbot BERNER of pious memory took pains to compose by lamplight, in clear style and lucid speech, for those willing to learn of them — he who, by God's disposing, first succeeded to this little place, the nuns who were there having been driven out on account of the obscene allurements of carnal pleasure. But let us for the present leave these things aside and attend, as far as we are able, to the little work we have begun. [0073B]

## 3.

When therefore the monks, as we have said before, were discussing back and forth together with their spiritual father whether they could in any way bring to effect what they indeed longed for with the highest desire, and yet did not presume to begin; then — the fortune of the place smiling upon them rather quietly, and God's providence foreseeing all things — it happened that a certain man from the town of the glorious martyr St. Quentin, who was leading his life by trade and merchandise, Baldwin by name, came to our monastery for the sake of devotion and the profit of his soul, that under the claim of mutual love the brethren might deign to grant him their fellowship. The brethren, together with the Father, embracing that petition with a willing mind on account of that precept of the Lord in which it is said: *Give, and it shall be given unto you; and, with the same measure that ye have meted, it shall be measured to you again [n: (Matth. VII, 2)]*; and: *All that cometh to me I will not cast out [n: (Joan. VI, 37)]*, gratefully bestowed on him the fellowship and the benefits of the place, as is the custom of monks. For that man, layman though he was, was [0073C] intent upon religion, giving his effort to works of mercy, and kept the devotion of faith by the purpose of his heart, as the outcome of the matter afterward made plain. For having taken the habit in that same place, he afterward proved himself a devoted monk. Drawn on at length by this familiarity, the man made it his care to frequent the cloisters of the monastery, having become, as it were, another Adam made out of the brethren. Whence it came about that, coming often, he rather frequently noticed the brethren whispering about the business we have mentioned. But since they were afraid to begin — as has been said, lest, according to the Gospel, men should mock them, saying that these began to build and were not able to finish — the aforesaid man, stirred (as human judgment holds) by divine prompting, since he too desired to honor the limbs of the holy virgin, called together the assembly of the elders, pouring out the first words of this address: "Since, venerable Fathers," he said, "I gather that certain of you exchange conversations one with another, and wish to place the body of the most holy virgin Hunegund, our lady, in a more splendid coffer if the means should be at hand — my heart [0073D] is made glad, and I promise my help so far as I can: only do not delay to begin the little work you have desired. But if you do not at all dare to undertake such a piece of workmanship, I myself take the whole business upon myself, and, relying on the help of almighty God, I will carry it through to the end, provided only that the people's offering be granted me." When the monks had heard this, being gladdened beyond measure, they rendered thanks to the Creator of all things and blessed the man in his promise, venerating the prerogative of the Godhead at so great, so sudden, so unhoped-for a joy. What need of words? Having undertaken the business, that man had a coffer put together in embossed work, in length the measure of two half-cubits, in unbroken height a cubit at either end, half a cubit at the sides on both sides, the rest of the structure sloping most becomingly [0074A] upward, wrought in the fashion of panels, elaborated with chased and sculptured workmanship. On the surface of this little work are seen the images of the holy apostles and prophets set forth, with most beautiful and polished panels of gleaming gold and shining silver, the figures arranged in order three by three on either side. Moreover on one of the ends the image of St. Hunegund and of St. Eligius, bishop of Noyon, the same virgin's patron at baptism, is seen raised more worthily; while on the other the majesty of Christ our Lord is seen set forth with sufficient elegance — whose kingdom and rule abides without end forever. What more? At last, the work having been hastened and at the same time, as has been said, splendidly completed, the lord abbot Macarius, who at that season, as has been mentioned beforehand, was administering the affairs of the place of Homblières, takes care to invite the lord bishop of Noyon, and summons the neighboring abbots — namely the lord Gerard of *Insula*, the lord Rainer, vicar of St. Praejectus, [0074B] and the lord Boniface Waleran of Mont-Saint-Quentin, and besides these a great many other governors and knights of the territory of Vermandois. He also gives notice to monks and clerics to be present at this act, lest, that is, so dear a pledge should seem to be handled or moved in secret, as though it were something stolen or unknown, without the knowledge and testimony of honorable and religious persons. When therefore the day of the appointed term had come, on which these things were to be done, rumor flying abroad — than which there is no swifter messenger — there is an assembly of elders, a gathering of noble men; a crowd of country folk and of women with their little ones flows together rejoicing. And not without reason: for they reckoned that, by the reverence and due honor to be paid to so great a mother, and at the same time by her intercession, they would be taken under her patronage, and that, asking her suffrages for their misdeeds, they would obtain pardon. Meanwhile the approach to the glorious treasury is unbarred; the iron bindings, knit together with shrewd care, are lifted away, mallets being applied, along with all the rest; the lids are wrenched off; the cradle of the precious body is laid open.
