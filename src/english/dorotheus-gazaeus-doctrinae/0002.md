[1617] OF OUR FATHER AMONG THE SAINTS, ABBA DOROTHEUS: INSTRUCTION I

On renunciation.

1. In the beginning, when God made man, [b: B] he set him in paradise (as the divine Holy Scripture says), [n: Gen. ii, 15.] having adorned him with every virtue, and gave him the commandment not to eat of the tree that was in the midst of paradise. And he was in the delight of paradise, in prayer, in contemplation, in the midst of glory and honour, with his senses sound, and being in the state that is according to nature, just as he had been created. For God made man according to the image of God, that is, immortal, master of himself, adorned with every virtue. But when he transgressed the commandment and ate of the tree of which God had commanded him not to eat, then he was cast out of paradise, and fell out of what is according to nature, and was in what is contrary to [1620] [b: A] nature, that is, in sin, in love of glory, in love of the pleasures of this life, and in the other passions, being lorded over by them; for he had made himself their slave through the transgression. Then, little by little, wickedness increased and death reigned. Nowhere was there reverence for God, but everywhere ignorance of God. Only a few, as I said, the fathers, moved by the natural law, knew God: such were Abraham and the other patriarchs, and Noah, and Jacob; and, to put it simply, a few; those who knew God were very rare indeed. For then the enemy spread out all his wickedness, since sin reigned; then began idolatry, polytheism, sorcery, murders, and the rest of the devil's wickedness. Then the good God, having pity on his creature, gave the written law through Moses, in which he forbade some things and prescribed others, saying, as it were, "Do this; do not do that." He gave a commandment, and at once he says, "The Lord thy God is one Lord," [n: Deut. vi, 4.] so as for the time being to draw their mind away from polytheism. And, "Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy soul and with all thy mind." [n: ibid. 5.] Everywhere thou shalt proclaim that God is one and there is no other. For in saying, "Thou shalt love the Lord thy God," he showed that God is one, and that there is one Lord. And again, in the Ten Commandments: "Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve, and to him shalt thou cleave, and by his name shalt thou swear." [n: ibid. 13.] Then he adds, "Thou shalt have no other gods, nor the likeness of anything that is in heaven above or that is on the earth beneath." For they worshipped all created things.

2. So the good God gave the law as a help, for turning back, for the correction of wickedness; and yet it was not corrected. He sent prophets, and they too could do nothing. For wickedness prevailed, as Isaiah says: "Neither wound, nor bruise, nor festering sore: it is not possible to apply a plaster, nor oil, nor bandages"; [n: Isa. i, 6.] as if to say, the wickedness is not partial, nor in one place, but in the whole body; it surrounds the whole soul, it holds all its powers in its grip; "it is not possible to apply a plaster," and so on: that is, everything has been enslaved to sin, everything is held fast by it. And Jeremiah too says, "We healed Babylon, and she was not healed"; that is: we made thy name manifest, we proclaimed thy commandments, the benefits, the promises, we foretold the risings of enemies, and yet she was not healed; that is, she did not repent, she did not fear, she did not turn back from her wickedness. As he says elsewhere too, "They did not receive correction," [n: Jerem. ii, 30.] that is, admonition, teaching. And in the psalm he says, "Their soul abhorred all manner of food, and they drew near unto the gates of death." [n: Psal. cvi, 18.]

3. [1621] [b: A] Then at last the good and man-loving God sends his only-begotten Son. For it belonged to God alone to heal and to overcome such a disease; and the prophets were not ignorant of this. Hence David too said plainly, "Thou that sittest upon the Cherubim, show thyself; stir up thy might and come to save us." [n: Psal. lxxix, 2, 3.] And, "Lord, bow the heavens and come down," [n: Psal. cxliii, 5.] and all such sayings. And the other prophets too, each of them, certainly cried out many things in various ways, some begging that he would come down, others fully assured that he was certainly coming down. So our Lord came, having become man for our sake, so that, as holy Gregory says, he might heal like by like: soul by soul, flesh by flesh. For he becomes man in every respect apart from sin. He took our very substance, [b: B] the first-fruits of our lump, and becomes a new Adam, according to the image of him who formed him. For he renews what is according to nature, and makes the senses sound again, as they were from the beginning. Having become man, he renewed the man who had fallen; he set free the one who had been enslaved to sin, who was held by it by force. For man was led by the enemy by force and tyranny, and almost even those who did not want to sin were sinning by force; as the Apostle says, speaking in our person, "For the good that I would, I do not; but the evil that I would not, that I do." [n: Rom. vii, 19.]

4. So God, having become man for our sake, freed man from the tyranny of the enemy. For he cast down all his power, crushed his very strength, and delivered us from being under his hand, from being enslaved to him, unless indeed we ourselves willingly choose to sin. For he gave us authority, as he said, to tread upon serpents and scorpions and upon all the power of the enemy, [n: Luc. x, 19.] having cleansed us from every sin through holy baptism. For holy baptism forgives every sin and wipes it out. But again the good God, knowing our weakness and foreseeing that even after holy baptism we would sin again (as it is written, "For the heart of man is diligently set upon evil things from his youth" [n: Gen. viii, 21.]), gave us, according to his goodness, holy commandments that cleanse us, [b: D] so that, if we will, we may be cleansed again through keeping the commandments, not only from our sins but also from the passions themselves. For the passions are one thing, and the sins another. The passions are anger, vainglory, love of pleasure, hatred, evil desire, and the like; while the sins are the very acting-out of the passions, when someone actually does them, when he carries out through the body those deeds which the passions suggest to him. Of course, it is possible for someone to have the passions and yet not act on them.

5. [1624] [b: A] So, as I said, he gave us commandments that cleanse us even from our passions themselves, from the very evil dispositions of our inner man. For he conveys to him the discernment of good and evil, he wakes him up, he shows him the causes from which he comes to sin; and he says, "The law said, Thou shalt not commit adultery; but I say, Do not even desire. The law said, Thou shalt not kill; but I say, Do not even be angry." [n: ibid. 22.] For if thou desirest, even if thou dost not commit adultery today, yet the desire will not stop troubling thee within until it throws thee into the act as well. If thou art angry and provokest thyself against thy brother, then at some point thou fallest into speaking ill of him, and then into plotting against him; and so, going on little by little, thou comest at last even to murder. Again the law says, "An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth," and so on; but he urges us not only to receive with long-suffering the blow of one who strikes us, but also to turn the other cheek to him with humility. For then the aim of the law was to teach us not to do what we do not wish to suffer; so it held us back from doing evil through fear of suffering it. Now what is sought, as I said, is to cast out the hatred itself, the love of pleasure itself, the love of glory itself, and the other passions.

6. The aim of our Master Christ now, to put it simply, is to teach us whence we came into all these sins, whence we fell into all these evil days. First, then, he freed us, as I have already said, through holy baptism, giving us the forgiveness of sins; and he gave us authority to do good if we will, and no longer to be dragged, as one might say, by force into evil. For the one who is enslaved to sins is weighed down and dragged by them, as it says, "Every one is bound fast with the cords of his own sins." [n: Prov. v, 22.] Then he teaches us through the holy commandments how to be cleansed even from the passions themselves, so that we may not through them fall again into the same sins. Then he shows us the cause from which one comes to contempt and disobedience even of the commandments of God themselves; and so he furnishes us with the cure for this too, that we may be able to obey and be saved. What then is this cure, and what is the cause of the contempt? Hear what our Lord himself says: "Learn of me, for I am meek and lowly in heart, and ye shall find rest unto your souls." [n: Matth. xi, 29.] See, here in brief, in a single word, he taught us the root and cause of all evils, and its cure, the root of all good things. He showed that self-exaltation cast us down, and that it is impossible to find mercy otherwise than through its opposite, which is humility. For self-exaltation begets contempt and ruinous disobedience, just as humility begets [1625] obedience and the salvation of souls. I mean the humility that is real: not the lowliness that is in word only, or in outward bearing, but a disposition made truly lowly in the heart itself.
