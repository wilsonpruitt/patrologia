⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1+2+3⟧ 7. ⟦s1+2+3⟧ So whoever wishes to find true lowliness and rest for his soul, let him learn humility, and let him see that in it is all joy and all glory and all rest, just as in pride is everything opposite. ⟦s1+2+3⟧ For whence did we come into all these afflictions? ⟦s1+2+3⟧ Is it not through our pride? ⟦s1+2+3⟧ Is it not through our folly? ⟦s4⟧ Is it not through our indulging our own evil choice? ⟦s4⟧ Is it not through letting the bitterness of our will prevail? ⟦s5⟧ But whence else? ⟦s6⟧ Was not man created in all delight, in all joy, in all rest, in all glory? ⟦s7+8+9⟧ Was he not in paradise? ⟦s7+8+9⟧ He was commanded, "Do not do [b: B] this"; and he did it. ⟦s7+8+9⟧ Dost thou see pride? ⟦s7+8+9⟧ Dost thou see the stiff neck? ⟦s7+8+9⟧ Dost thou see insubordination? ⟦s10⟧ So God, seeing that shamelessness, says, "This one is a fool; this one does not know how to be glad. ⟦s11⟧ Unless he has some bad days, he is off, he is utterly lost. ⟦s12⟧ For if he does not learn what affliction is, he does not learn what rest is." ⟦s13⟧ Then he gave him what he deserved, and cast him out of paradise. ⟦s14+15⟧ So he was handed over to his own self-love and his own wills, that he might break his bones, that he might learn not to follow himself but the commandment of God, that the very misery of disobedience might teach him the rest of obedience; as it says in the prophet, "Thy apostasy shall chasten thee." [n: Jerem. ii, 19.] Yet the goodness of God, as I have often said, did not overlook his own creature, but again he exhorts, again he calls: "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest"; [n: Matth. xi, 28.] as if to say: See, you have laboured; see, you have suffered misery; see, you have tasted the evil of your insubordination. ⟦s14+15⟧ Come, then, turn back; come, know your helplessness, that you may come to your rest and your glory. ⟦s16⟧ Come, live through humility, you who were put to death through high-mindedness. "Learn of me, for I am meek and lowly in heart, and ye shall find rest unto your souls." [n: ibid. 29.]

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ 8. ⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ Alas, my brethren, what pride does! ⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ Alas, what humility can do! ⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ What need was there for all these roundabout ways? ⟦s5⟧ For if from the beginning he had humbled himself, and obeyed God, and kept the commandment, he would not have fallen. ⟦s6⟧ Again, after he had disgraced himself, God gave him an opening to repent and find mercy, and his neck stayed high. ⟦s7⟧ For he came saying to him, "Adam, where art thou?" [n: Gen. iii, 9.] in place of, "From what glory to what shame hast thou come?" ⟦s8⟧ And then he asks him, "Why didst thou sin? ⟦s8⟧ Why didst thou [1628] transgress?", prompting him particularly to say, "Forgive." ⟦s9⟧ Nowhere humility. ⟦s10⟧ And where is the "Forgive"? ⟦s11⟧ Nowhere repentance, but the opposite. ⟦s12⟧ He contradicts and answers back: "The woman whom thou gavest me." [n: ibid. 13.] He does not say, "My wife made a fool of me," but, "The woman whom thou gavest me," as one might say, "The calamity thou broughtest down upon my head." ⟦s13+14+15+16⟧ For so it is, brethren: when a man does not hold to blaming himself, he does not shrink from accusing even God himself. ⟦s13+14+15+16⟧ Then he comes to her and says to her, "Why didst thou too not keep the commandment?", as though saying particularly, "At least say 'Forgive,' that thy soul may be humbled and thou mayest find mercy." ⟦s13+14+15+16⟧ And again, nowhere the "Forgive." ⟦s13+14+15+16⟧ For she too answers, saying, "The serpent deceived me"; as if to say, "If he sinned, what is that to me?" ⟦s13+14+15+16⟧ What are you doing, wretches? ⟦s13+14+15+16⟧ Make one prostration, acknowledge your fault, have pity on your nakedness! ⟦s13+14+15+16⟧ And not one of them thought fit to blame himself; not one was found with even a little humility.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 9. ⟦s2⟧ And so, see now, simply, where our state has come; see into what kinds and how many evils it has brought us: justifying ourselves, holding to our own will, following ourselves. ⟦s3⟧ These are the children of pride, God's enemy; just as the children of humility are blaming oneself, not trusting one's own understanding, hating one's own will. ⟦s4⟧ For by these one is counted worthy to recover oneself and to return to what is according to nature, through the cleansing of the holy commandments of Christ. ⟦s5+6+7⟧ For without humility one cannot submit to the commandments, nor come to any good at all, as abba Mark said: "Without contrition of heart it is impossible to be rid of wickedness and to acquire virtue." ⟦s5+6+7⟧ So [?] through contrition of heart a man accepts the commandments, is rid of wickedness, acquires the virtues, and so returns to his own rest. ⟦s5+6+7⟧ All the saints knew this, and hastened by every lowly way of life to unite themselves to God. ⟦s5+6+7⟧ For there were some lovers of God who after holy baptism not only cut off the acting-out of the passions, but wished to conquer the passions themselves and to become free of passion: such were holy Antony, and Pachomius, and the other God-bearing Fathers.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 10. ⟦s2⟧ Since, then, they had the aim of cleansing themselves, as the Apostle says, "from all filthiness of the flesh and spirit," they knew that it is through keeping the commandments, as we have already said, that the soul is cleansed, and, so to speak, the mind is cleansed too, and recovers its sight, and comes to what is according to nature ("for the commandment of the Lord is far-shining, enlightening the eyes" [n: Psal. xviii, 9.]). ⟦s2⟧ They perceived that, being in the world, they could not easily achieve virtue, and they devised for themselves a strange life, a strange way of living, I mean the solitary [1629] [b: A] life; and they began to flee the world and to dwell in the deserts, in fasts and sleeping on the ground and vigils and the rest of hardship, and in renunciation of all homeland and kin, money and possessions; and, simply, they crucified themselves to the world. ⟦s3⟧ And not only did they keep the commandments; they also offered gifts to God. ⟦s4⟧ And I will tell you how. ⟦s4⟧ The commandments were given to all Christians, and every Christian is bound to keep them: they are, as one might say, the dues owed to the emperor. ⟦s5⟧ Who says, "I am not paying the emperor his dues," and escapes punishment? ⟦s6⟧ But there are great and illustrious men in the world who not only pay their dues to the emperor but also offer him gifts; and these are counted worthy of great honour, of great rewards and dignities. ⟦s6⟧ So too the Fathers not only kept the commandments but also offered gifts to God. ⟦s7⟧ And the gifts are virginity and poverty. ⟦s7⟧ These are not commandments; they are gifts. ⟦s8+9+10⟧ For nowhere is it written, "Do not take a wife, do not beget children." ⟦s8+9+10⟧ Nor again did Christ give a commandment, saying, "Sell what thou hast." ⟦s8+9+10⟧ Indeed, when the lawyer came to him saying, "Good Master, what shall I do to inherit eternal life?", he answered, "Thou knowest the commandments: Thou shalt not kill, thou shalt not commit adultery, thou shalt not steal, thou shalt not bear false witness against thy neighbour," [n: Matth. xix, 16-21.] and so on. ⟦s8+9+10⟧ And when the man said, "All these have I kept from my youth," he adds, "If thou wilt be perfect, sell what thou hast and give to the poor," and so on. ⟦s11⟧ See, he did not say "Sell what thou hast" as one commanding, but as one counselling. ⟦s12⟧ For to say, "If thou wilt," is not the word of one commanding, but of one counselling.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 11. ⟦s2+3⟧ As we said, then, the Fathers offered to God, besides the other virtues, the gifts of virginity and poverty; and, as we said before, they crucified the world to themselves, and then strove that they might also crucify themselves to the world, as the Apostle says, "The world is crucified unto me, and I unto the world." [n: Galat. vi, 14.] What then is the difference? ⟦s4⟧ The world is crucified to a man when the man renounces the world and becomes a monk, and leaves parents, money, possessions, business, buying and selling: then the world is crucified to him, for he has left it. ⟦s4⟧ And this is what the Apostle says: "The world is crucified unto me." ⟦s5⟧ Then he adds, [b: D] "and I unto the world." ⟦s6⟧ How then is a man crucified to the world? ⟦s7⟧ When, after being rid of outward things, he struggles against the pleasures themselves, against the very desires for things, and against his own wills, and puts his passions to death; then he too is crucified to the world, and is counted worthy to say with the Apostle, "The [1632] world is crucified unto me, and I unto the world." ⟦s8⟧ Our Fathers, as we said, having crucified the world to themselves, strove through struggles and crucified themselves to the world as well. ⟦s9+10+11+12⟧ We, for our part, think we have crucified the world to ourselves, because we left it and came to the monastery; but we will not crucify ourselves to the world. ⟦s9+10+11+12⟧ For we still have its pleasures, we still have its attachments; we are attached to its glory, we are attached to food, to clothes. ⟦s9+10+11+12⟧ If there is a good tool, we are attached to it, and we let that little tool do in us [?], as abba Zosimas said. ⟦s9+10+11+12⟧ We think we have gone out of the world and left its things, and we come to the monastery, and through paltry things we satisfy our attachment [b: B] to it. ⟦s9+10+11+12⟧ And this befalls us from great folly: that having left great and precious things, we satisfy our passions over the merest trifles. ⟦s9+10+11+12⟧ For each of us left what he had: the one who had great things left the great things, and the one who had whatever it might be, he too left what he had, each according to his means; and we come to the monastery and, as I said, through paltry and worthless things we satisfy our attachment. ⟦s9+10+11+12⟧ We ought not to do so. ⟦s9+10+11+12⟧ But as we renounced the world and its things, so we ought also to renounce the very attachment to material things, and to know what this renunciation is, and why we came to the monastery, and what the habit is that we receive, and to fit ourselves to it, and to struggle after the manner of our fathers.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 12. ⟦s2⟧ The habit we wear is a colobion, which has no sleeves, a leather belt, an analavos, and a cowl. ⟦s3⟧ These are symbols, and we ought to learn what the symbols of our habit signify to us. ⟦s4⟧ Why do we wear a colobion that has no sleeves? ⟦s4⟧ All the others have sleeves; why do we not? ⟦s5+6+7+8+9⟧ The sleeves are symbols of the hands, and the hands are taken to mean the practical life. ⟦s5+6+7+8+9⟧ So when a thought comes to do something through the hands of the old man, say, to steal or to strike, or simply to commit any sin whatever through the hands, we ought to attend to our habit and learn that we have no sleeves; that is, we have no hands to do anything of the old man. ⟦s5+6+7+8+9⟧ And our colobion also has a purple mark of some kind. ⟦s5+6+7+8+9⟧ What does the purple mark mean? ⟦s5+6+7+8+9⟧ Every man who serves as a soldier of the emperor has purple on his cloak. ⟦s5+6+7+8+9⟧ For since the emperor wears purple, all who serve him as soldiers put purple on their cloaks, that is, the imperial garment, so as to show by this that they are the emperor's and serve under him. ⟦s5+6+7+8+9⟧ So we too take the purple mark on our colobion, showing that we have enlisted under Christ and are bound to endure all his sufferings, all that he endured for us. ⟦s10+11⟧ For when our Master suffered, he wore the [1633] [b: A] purple: first, as King, for he is the King of those who reign and Lord of those who rule; [n: Apoc. xix, 16.] and then, as one mocked by those impious men. ⟦s10+11⟧ So we too, having the purple mark, profess, as I said, to bear all his sufferings. ⟦s12⟧ And as the soldier does not leave his service and go off to become a farmer or a merchant, since he then falls from his rank, as the Apostle says, "No man that warreth entangleth himself with the affairs of this life, that he may please him who enlisted him"; [n: II Tim. ii, 4.] so we too ought to contend and to be free of care for all the things of this world, and to give ourselves to God alone, as it says, "that there may be a virgin waiting well, and undistracted." [n: I. Cor. vii, 34.]
