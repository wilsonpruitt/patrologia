[1640] Instruction II. *On humility.*

1. One of the elders said: Before everything we need humility, being ready at every word we hear to say, "Forgive." For through humility all the works of the enemy and adversary are destroyed. Let us inquire what the force of the [1641] [b: A] elder's saying is. Why does he say, Before everything we need humility, and not rather, Before everything we need self-control? For the Apostle says: Everyone who strives is self-controlled in all things. Or why does he not say, Before everything we need the fear of God? For Scripture says: The beginning of wisdom is the fear of the Lord; [n: Psal. cx, 9.] and again: By the fear of the Lord everyone turns away from evil. [n: Prov. xvi, 6.] Why does he not say, Before everything we need almsgiving, or faith? For it says: By alms and faiths sins are purged away; and the Apostle says: Without faith it is impossible to please God. [n: Hebr. xi, 6.] If then it is impossible to please God without faith, and by alms and faiths sins are purged away, and by the fear of the Lord everyone turns away from evil, and the beginning of wisdom [b: B] is the fear of the Lord, and he who strives is self-controlled in all things, how then does he say, Before everything we need humility, and leave aside all these things that are so necessary? The elder wants to show us that neither the fear of God itself, nor almsgiving, nor faith, nor self-control, nor any other one of the virtues can be accomplished without humility. That is why he says, Before everything we need humility, being ready at every word we hear to say, "Forgive"; for through humility all the works of the enemy and adversary are destroyed.

2. See, brethren, how great is the power of humility. See what force there is [b: C] in saying, "Forgive." And why is the devil called not only enemy but also adversary? He is called enemy because he is a hater of men, a hater of good, a plotter; and he is called adversary because he sets himself to hinder [sic: *ἐμποδίσας*] every good deed. Does someone want to pray? He stands against him, hindering him through evil imaginings, through captivity of mind, through acedia. Does someone want to give alms? He hinders him through love of money, through sloth. Does someone want to keep vigil? He hinders him through sloth, through slackness; and so he sets himself against us in every good thing we undertake. That is why he is called not only enemy but also adversary. So through humility all the works of the enemy and adversary are destroyed.

For humility is truly great, and [b: D] each of the saints travelled by way of humility itself, and through toil cut the journey short; as it says: Look upon my lowliness and my toil, and forgive all my sins; [n: Psal. xxiv, 18.] and, I was brought low, and the Lord saved me. [n: Psal. cxiv, 6.] Humility alone can indeed bring us in, as the elder abba John used to say, but more slowly. So let us too humble ourselves a little, and we are saved; even if we cannot toil, being weak, let us be eager to humble ourselves. And I trust in the mercy of God that, if this very little is done with lowliness, we too shall find ourselves in the place of the saints, there with those who toiled much and served God. Yes, we are weak and cannot [1644] [b: A] toil; can we not humble ourselves?

Blessed, brethren, is he who has humility. Great is humility. That saint marked out well the man who has true humility; that is why he said: Humility is not angry, and angers no one. And the thing seems strange; for humility is opposed to vainglory alone, and guards a man, as it seems, from that. Yet a man grows angry over money and over food; how then does he say that humility is not angry and angers no one? Humility is great, as we said, and is able to draw the grace of God into the soul. And then that grace of God, when it comes, shelters the soul from these two other heavy passions; for what is heavier than to be angry [b: B] and to provoke one's neighbour to anger, as someone said? Anger is utterly foreign to monks; truly, if such a man is not quickly sheltered by humility, little by little he comes into the state of a demon, troubling and troubled.

3. That is why he says that humility is not angry, and angers no one. But why do I say that it shelters from the two passions? It shelters the soul from every passion; from every temptation humility shelters the soul. When saint Antony saw all the snares of the devil spread out, and groaning asked God who could pass through them, what did God answer him? "Humility passes through them." And what other marvel did he add? "And they do not even touch it." Dost thou see [b: C] the power, sir? Dost thou see the grace of the virtue? Truly, nothing is stronger than humility. If anything grievous befalls the humble man, at once he turns upon himself, at once he condemns himself as deserving it; he will not bear to blame anyone, he will not bear to lay the blame on others; and so he passes through untroubled, unafflicted, in all rest. That is why he is neither angry nor angers anyone. That is why the saint said well, Before all things we need humility.

4. There are two humilities, as there are also two prides. The first pride is when someone despises his brother, when he belittles him as being nothing, [b: D] and himself as above him. Such a man, if he does not quickly come to his senses and take pains, little by little comes into the second pride as well, so as to be proud even against God; and he ascribes his good deeds to himself and not to God. Truly, my brethren, I once saw someone come into this pitiable state. At first, if one of the brethren said anything to him, he despised every one of them, and said, "Who is that? There is no one but Zosimas and those with him." Then he began to belittle these too, and to say, "There is no one but Macarius"; and after a little he began to say, "Who [1645] [b: A] is Macarius? No one but Basil and Gregory." Then after a little he began to belittle these too, saying, "Who is Basil, and who is Gregory? No one but Paul and Peter." I say to him, "Truly, brother, thou art going to despise these too." [sic: *ἔχει*] Believe me, after a short time he began to say, "Who is Peter, and who is Paul? No one but the Holy Trinity." So in the end he grew proud against God himself, and so he lost his mind. That is why we ought to struggle, my brethren, against the first pride, so that we do not little by little fall into complete pride as well.

5. There is also a worldly pride, and a monastic. Worldly pride is when someone is proud against his brother as being richer, or [b: B] handsomer than he, or as dressing better than he, or as nobler than he. So when we see ourselves vainglorious over these things, or that our monastery is bigger or better off, or that we have many brethren, we ought to know that we are still in worldly pride. Sometimes too a man is vainglorious over natural gifts. I mean something like this: a man is vainglorious that he has a fine voice and chants well, or that he is capable and serves neatly. These are more respectable than the first; yet they too belong to worldly pride. Monastic pride is when a man is vainglorious as keeping vigil, as fasting, as devout, as a man of good conduct, [sic: *πολιτείας*] as zealous. And sometimes one humbles himself for the sake of glory. These [b: C] belong to monastic pride. It is possible for us not to be proud at all; but if we are, let it at least be in monastic things and not in worldly ones. See, we have said what the first pride is and what the second; likewise we have said [sic: *εἴπωμεν*] what the worldly is and what the monastic pride is. Let us learn then what the two humilities are as well.

6. The first humility is to hold one's brother more understanding than oneself, and above oneself in everything; and simply, as that saint said, to put oneself beneath all. The second humility is to ascribe one's good deeds to God. This is the perfect humility, the humility of the saints. It comes about naturally [sic: *φυσικῶν*] in the soul from the working of the commandments. For as with trees: when they bear much fruit, the fruit itself bends the branches down and draws them down, while the branch [b: D] that bears no fruit rises upward and grows straight; and there are some trees that, as long as their branch goes upward, bear no fruit; but if someone takes a stone and hangs it on the branch and draws it down, then it makes fruit: so it is also with the soul. When it is humbled, then it bears fruit, and the more fruit it makes, the more it is humbled. For the nearer the saints draw to God, the more they see themselves sinners. I remember that we were once speaking about humility, and one of the notables of Gaza, hearing us say this, that the nearer a man draws to God, the more he sees himself [1648] [b: A] a sinner, was astonished, and said, "How can this be?" He did not understand, and wanted to learn the meaning. I say to him, "Sir, first man of the city, tell me, what dost thou hold thyself to be in thy city?" He says, "I hold myself great, and first in the city." I say to him, "And if thou goest to Caesarea, what dost thou hold thyself there?" He says, "I hold myself lower than the great men there." I say to him, "And if thou goest to Antioch, what dost thou hold thyself?" He says to me, "I hold myself as one of the common folk." I say to him, "And if thou goest to Constantinople, near the emperor, what dost thou hold thyself there?" He says to me, "I hold myself a pauper." Then I say to him, "See, so are the saints: the nearer they draw to God, the more they see themselves sinners. For Abraham, [b: B] when he saw the Lord, called himself earth and ashes; and Isaiah said, O wretched and unclean am I. [n: Isa. vi, 5.] Likewise Daniel too: when he was in the pit with the lions, and Habakkuk came with the bread, saying to him, Take the dinner which God has sent thee, he answered, saying, Has God then remembered me? [n: Dan. xiv, 36.] What lowliness his heart had! He was in the pit with the lions, and they did not harm him, and not once only but a second time; and after all this he marvelled, saying, Has God then remembered me?"
