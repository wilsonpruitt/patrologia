[1653] [b: D] 2. So let us be eager, brethren, to keep our conscience while we are in this world, not letting it convict us in any matter, not trampling on it in anything at all, even if it be the least thing. For know that from these small things, cheap by nature, we come to despising the great. For when someone begins to say, "What does it matter if I speak this word? What does it matter if I eat this little thing? What does it matter if I look into this matter?", from this "What is this?" and "What is that?" one contracts an evil and bitter canker, and begins to despise the great and weightier things too, and

[1656] [b: A] to trample on his own conscience; and so then, advancing by degrees, he is in danger of falling into complete insensibility. Therefore see, brethren, that we do not neglect small things; see that we do not despise them as of no account. They are not small: it is a canker, it is a bad habit. Let us be watchful, let us take care of the light things while they are light, so that they do not become heavy. Both doing right and sinning begin from small things and lead to great ones, either good or evil. Therefore the Lord charges us to keep our conscience, as though bearing solemn witness to each one in particular and saying to him, "See what thou art doing, wretch; be watchful: Be well disposed to thine adversary while thou art in the way with him." And he adds the fear and the danger of the matter, saying, "Lest at any time he deliver thee to the [b: B] judge, and the judge to the officers, and they cast thee into prison." And what then? "Amen I say to thee, thou shalt not come out thence until thou hast paid the last farthing." For conscience convicts us, as I said, as to the good and the evil, and shows us what to do and what not to do. And it accuses us again in the age to come as well; therefore he says, "Lest at any time he deliver thee to the judge," and the rest.

3. Keeping conscience has many distinctions. For one must keep conscience toward God, toward the neighbour, and toward things. Toward God: that one not despise his commandments, but that even in what no man sees him do, and in what no one requires of him, he keep conscience to God in secret. I mean, for example: he neglected prayer; an impassioned thought rose into his heart, and he was not watchful and did not hold himself tight, but [b: C] assented. He saw [sic: *Οἶδεν*] his neighbour saying or doing something, and, as may happen, suspected and condemned him. In short, all that happens in secret, which no one knows but God and our conscience, we need to keep watch over. And this is conscience toward God. Conscience toward the neighbour is that one do nothing whatever in which he sees that it afflicts or wounds the neighbour, whether by deed, or by word, or by gesture, or by look. For there is a gesture too, as I always say, that wounds the neighbour; there is a look that can wound; and in short, whatever a man knows he does on purpose to give his neighbour a thought, his conscience itself is defiled, knowing that he contrives it in order to harm or afflict; and this is keeping conscience toward the [b: D] neighbour. Keeping it toward things is that one not misuse a thing; that he not let a thing be spoiled or thrown away; but if he sees [sic: *ἤδη*] something lying thrown down, he should not overlook it, even if it is cheap, but pick it up and put it in its proper place. That he not misuse his own clothes. For it happens, suppose, that one could wear his garment another week or two, and he goes and straightaway washes it before its time and beats it, and instead of its serving him another five months or more, he wears it out with washing and makes it useless; and this is against conscience. Likewise with bedding: often one could meet his need with one pillow, and he looks for

[1657] [b: A] a big mattress; at times he has a hair one, and wants to change it and get another, new or handsome, out of vanity or out of acedia. One could be content with one patchwork blanket, and he looks for a rug, and perhaps even quarrels if he does not get it. And if he begins also to look at his brother and say, "Why does this one have that, and I do not?", such a one is not making progress. Again, he spreads his garment or his covering in the sun, and neglects to take it in, and lets it spoil; and this too is against conscience. Likewise with food: one could meet his need with a little vegetable or pulse, or a few olives, and he will not bear to do so, but looks for other food, sweeter or more costly; all these are against conscience. The Fathers [b: B] say that a monk ought never to let his conscience weigh him down in any matter whatever. So we need, brethren, to be watchful always and to guard ourselves from all these things, that we may not fall into the danger. For the Lord himself forewarned us, as we said above. God grant us to hear and keep these things, that the words of our Fathers may not become a judgment to us.

Instruction IV. *On the fear of God.*

1. Saint John says in the Catholic Epistles, "Perfect love [b: C] casts out fear." What then does the saint wish to signify to us by this? What love does he speak of to us, and what fear? For the Prophet says in the psalm, "Fear the Lord, all ye his saints"; and we find countless other such things in the holy Scriptures. If then the saints too, loving the Lord as they do, fear him, how does he say, "Love casts out fear"? The saint wishes to show us that there are two fears, one introductory and one perfect; and that the one belongs to beginners, to the devout, as one might say, and the other to the saints who have been perfected, who have reached the measure of love. I mean, for example: someone does the will of God through fear of punishments; this one, as we said, is still a beginner; he does not yet do [b: D] the good for its own sake, but for fear of the strokes. Another does the will of God loving God himself, loving him in particular so as to be well-pleasing to God. This one knows what the good itself is; this one has come to know what it is to be with God. See, this is the one who has the true love, which the saint calls perfect; and this love brings him to the perfect fear. For such a one fears and keeps the will of God no longer because of the strokes, no longer so as not to be punished, but, as we said, because he has tasted the very sweetness of being with God; he fears lest he fall from it, he fears lest he be deprived of it. So this

[1660] [b: A] perfect fear, which comes from this love, casts out the introductory fear. And that is why he says that perfect love casts out fear. But it is impossible to come to the perfect fear except through the introductory.

2. For there are three dispositions, as Saint Gregory says, through which we can please God. Either we please him fearing punishment, and we are in the state of the slave; or, pursuing the gains of the wage, we fulfil what is commanded for the sake of our own benefit, and in this we are like hirelings; or we do it for the good itself, and we are in the state of the son. For the son, when he comes to understanding, does his father's will, not fearing to be beaten, nor in order to get a [b: B] wage from him, but loving him, keeping toward him in particular the very love and honour due a father; and he is persuaded that all that his father has is his. Such a one is counted worthy to hear, "Thou art no longer a slave but a son, and an heir of God through Christ." Such a one no longer fears God, as we said, plainly with that introductory fear, but loves him, as Saint Antony says: "I no longer fear God." And when the Lord says to Abraham, after he had offered his son, "Now I know that thou fearest God," he signifies the perfect fear which comes from love. For how could he say, "Now I know"? Abraham had shown mercy, he had done so much, he had obeyed [b: C] God and left all his own, and sojourned in a strange land and among an idolatrous nation, where there was not even a trace of godliness; and on top of these he bore that terrible trial of the sacrifice of his son; and after these things he said to him, "Now I know that thou fearest God." Plainly he meant the perfect fear, that of the saints. For they no longer do the will of God for fear of punishment or to receive a wage, but because they love, as we have often said, fearing to do anything against the will of the beloved. And that is why he says, "Love casts out fear." For they no longer act from fear, but fear because they love.

3. This is the perfect fear; but it is not possible to come to the perfect, as we said before, unless one [b: D] first has the introductory fear. For it says, "The beginning of wisdom is the fear of the Lord"; and again it says, "Beginning and end is the fear of God." By beginning he means the introductory fear, after which comes the perfect, that of the saints. So the introductory fear belongs to our state;

[1661] [b: A] it guards the soul from all evil, as tinning guards a vessel. For it says, "By the fear of the Lord everyone turns aside from evil." If then one turns aside from evil through fear of punishment, like the slave who fears his master, he comes by degrees to doing the good as well, and little by little he begins to hope for some reward for working the good, like the hireling. When then he persists in fleeing evil through fear, as we said, like a slave, and again in doing the good through hope, like the hireling, abiding [sic: *χερονίζων*] with God in the good, and joined to God in proportion, he tastes then and comes to some perception of the truly good itself, and no longer wants to be parted from it. For who can any longer part him, [b: B] as the Apostle said, from the love of Christ? Then he reaches the measure of the son, and loves the good for its own sake, and fears because he loves. And this is the great and perfect fear. Therefore the Prophet too, teaching us the difference, said of this fear: "Come, children, hear me; I will teach you the fear of the Lord." Set your mind on each phrase of the Prophet, how each word of his has force. First he says, "Come to me," calling us to virtue. He adds also "children": the saints call children those who are transformed through their word from evil to virtue, as the Apostle says, "My little children, of whom I travail again until Christ be formed in you." Then, after [b: C] calling us and urging us to such a transformation, he says, "I will teach you the fear of the Lord." See the boldness of the saint. We, when we want to say something good, always say, "Do you want us to talk a little and exercise ourselves about the fear of God, or about some other virtue?" But not so the saint; with boldness he said, "Come, children, hear me; I will teach you the fear of the Lord. What man is there that desires life, that loves to see good days?" Then, as though someone answered, "I do; teach me how to live and to see good days," he teaches him and says, "Stop thy tongue from evil, and thy lips from speaking guile." See, he first cuts off the working of evil through the fear of God. "Stop thy [b: D] tongue from evil" is not to wound the neighbour's conscience by anything, not to speak ill of him, not to provoke him; and "Thy lips from speaking guile" is not to deceive the neighbour. Then he goes on, "Turn aside from evil." He first named certain particular sins, speaking ill and deceit, and so he went on to speak comprehensively of all evil: "Turn aside from evil," that is, flee simply every evil, turn aside from everything that brings sin. Nor again did he say only this

[1664] [b: A] and fall silent, but added, "And do good." For sometimes one does no evil, yet does no good either; again sometimes one does no wrong, yet shows no mercy; sometimes one does not hate, yet does not love. Well then did the Prophet say, "Turn aside from evil, and do good." See, he shows us that sequence of the three states which we spoke of before: how through the fear of God he has led us by the hand to turning aside from evil, and so urges us on to set foot on the good as well. For if one is counted worthy to be idle from evil and to flee from it, then naturally he works the good, guided by the saints. Having said this, he very finely and fittingly adds, "Seek peace and pursue it." And he did not say "seek" only, but pursue it at a run, that thou mayest overtake it.
