[1664] [b: B] 4. Set your mind well on this saying, and see the precision of the saint. When one is counted worthy to turn aside from evil, and is eager then, with God, to work the good as well, at once the wars of the enemy come upon him. Then he struggles, labours, is crushed, not only fearing to turn back again to evil, as we said of the slave, but also hoping, as we said before, for the wage of the good, like a hireling. So while he is warred upon, and wars and boxes with the enemy, he does the good, but with much affliction, with much crushing. But when help comes to him from God, and he begins then to be in a certain habit regarding the good, then he sees rest, then he comes within reach of peace, then he perceives [b: C] what the affliction of war is, and what the joy and gladness of peace. Then he seeks it; then he is eager and runs in pursuit, that he may overtake it, that he may possess it perfectly, that he may make it dwell in himself. And what then is more blessed than the soul counted worthy of this measure? Such a one, as we have often said, is in the measure of the son. For truly "Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God." Who makes that soul do the good any longer for anything else but the enjoyment of the good itself? Who knows that joy, but the one who has experience of it? Then such a one comes to know the perfect fear as well, as we have often said. See, we have heard what the perfect fear [b: D] of the saints is, and what the introductory fear is, the fear of our state, and from what one flees and where one comes through the fear of God. Now we want to learn also how the fear of God comes; we want to say what the things are that separate us from the fear of God.

5. The Fathers said that a man acquires the fear of God from keeping the remembrance of death and the remembrance of punishments, and from examining himself each evening how he passed the day, and again each morning how he passed the night, and from not being bold, and from cleaving to a man who fears God. For it is said that a brother asked one of the elders, "What shall I do, Father, to fear God?" And the elder says to him, "Go, cleave to a man who fears God, and from his fearing God he will teach thee too to fear God." And we drive the fear

[1665] [b: A] of God away from us by doing the opposite of these: by having no remembrance of death or of punishments, by not attending to ourselves, by not examining how we have passed our time, but living indifferently and keeping company with the indifferent; by being bold. This is worse than all; this is utter destruction. For what drives the fear of God from the soul so much as boldness? So when abba Agathon was asked about boldness, he said that it is like a great burning wind: when it comes, everyone flees before its face, and it ruins the fruit of the trees. Seest thou, sir, the power of the passion? Seest thou its onrush? [sic: *ὀργήν*] And being asked again, "Yes, is boldness so grievous?", he says, "There is no passion more grievous than boldness; for it is the mother [b: B] of all the passions," since it drives the fear of God from the soul. For if "by the fear of the Lord everyone turns aside from evil," surely where there is no fear of God, there is every passion. God deliver our souls from the deadly passion of boldness.

6. Boldness takes many forms. One is bold by word, by touch, and by look. From boldness one comes to idle talk, to talking of worldly things, to making jokes and stirring up unseemly laughter. It is boldness too to touch someone without need; to stretch out a hand at someone who is laughing, to push someone, or [b: C] snatch something from him, to stare at someone shamelessly. All these come from there being no fear of God in the soul; and from these one comes little by little to complete contempt. That is why, when God was giving the commandments of the law, he said, "Make the sons of Israel reverent." For without reverence one does not honour even God himself, nor attend even once to any commandment whatever. That is why nothing is more terrible than boldness; that is why it is the mother of all the passions: since it casts out reverence, since it drives away the fear of God, since it begets contempt. From our having boldness with one another and being shameless toward one another, you also speak evil to one another and wound one another. And if one of you sees something unprofitable, he goes off and babbles about it, [b: D] and puts it into the heart of another brother of his; and not only is he himself harmed, but he harms his brother as well, putting evil poison into his heart. And often the brother had his mind on prayer or on some other good thing, and that one comes and gives him something to chatter about, and not only hinders his profit but brings temptation upon him. And nothing is heavier than this, nor more ruinous, than for someone to harm not only himself but his neighbour too.

7. That is why it is good that we have reverence, brethren; that we fear harming ourselves and one another, that we honour one another, and that we are eager

[1668] [b: A] not to toss our heads, nor ⟨to stare⟩ into one another's faces. For this too, as one of the elders said, is a form of boldness. And if it happens that someone sees his brother sinning, let him neither despise him, keeping silent and letting him perish, nor again revile him, nor speak ill of him, but with compassion and the fear of God let him tell someone who is able to correct him; or let him speak to him himself with love and lowliness, saying, "Forgive. My brother, as a heedless man I see that perhaps we are not doing this thing well." And if he does not listen, tell another in whom thou seest he has confidence, or tell his superior or the abba, according to the weight of the fault, and be free of care. But, as we said, let him speak with the aim that his brother be corrected, [b: B] and not by way of chatter, not speaking ill of him, not setting him at nought, not wanting, as it says, to make an example of him, not condemning him, not putting on the appearance of correction while inwardly holding any of the things I have named. For truly, even to his own abba, if one speaks, and does not speak for the correction of the neighbour, or because of harm to himself, it is sin; for it is speaking ill. But let him feel out his own heart, whether he has some impassioned motion, and then let him not speak. But if he examines himself with precision and sees that he wants to speak out of compassion and for profit, yet some impassioned thought troubles him within, let him report to the abba with lowliness, telling both his own part and his neighbour's, and saying, "My conscience bears me witness that I want to speak for correction, but I perceive that there is some [b: C] thought mixed in within. Whether because I once had something against the brother, I do not know; whether it is a false accusation that wants to hinder me from speaking and from correction taking place, I do not know." And then the abba tells him whether he ought to speak or not. But sometimes one speaks neither for his brother's profit nor because of harm to himself, nor out of some rancour, but just recounting it, as in idle talk. And what need is there of this chatter? Often too the brother learns that he spoke about him, and is thrown into turmoil, and affliction comes of it, and another addition of harm comes too. For when one speaks for profit alone, as we said, God does not allow turmoil to arise; he does not let affliction or [b: D] harm follow.

8. Be eager, as we said, to guard your tongue as well, that no one speak evil to his neighbour, nor wound anyone, either by word or deed or gesture or in any way whatever; and do not be touchy, so that when one of you hears a word from his brother, he is stung at once, or even answers him evilly, or stays aggrieved against him. These are not the ways of those who want to be saved; these are not the ways of those who struggle. Acquire the fear of God, and see that you meet one another with reverence, each bowing his head before his brother, as we said, each humbling himself before God and before his brother, and cutting off his will for him. Truly it is a good thing if one does something, and

[1669] [b: A] gives way to his brother and prefers him; the one who gave way profits more than the other. I do not know that I ever did a good thing. But if I have been sheltered at all, I know that I was sheltered by this: that I never ranked myself above my brother, but always put my brother before me.

9. Once, when I was still in abba Seridos's monastery, the attendant of the elder abba John, the companion of abba Barsanuphius, fell sick, and the abba charged me with attending the elder. And I used to kiss the door of his cell from outside, as one venerates the precious cross; how much more serving him! For who would not have wished to be counted worthy of such a saint? He had a wonderful [b: B] word besides, and each day, always after I had finished my service to him, I made him a prostration to receive his leave and withdraw, and he would always say something to me. For the elder had four sayings, and, as I said, each evening as I was about to withdraw, he would always say one of the four to me; and he spoke thus: "Says he, once": for it was the elder's habit to say this before every saying. "Says he, once, brother: God preserve love. The Fathers said, Keeping the neighbour's conscience begets humility." Again, the next evening he said to me: "Says he, once, brother: God preserve love. The Fathers said, I never set my will before my brother's." Another time again he said: "Says he, once, brother: God preserve [b: C] love. The Fathers said, Flee human things and thou art saved." Again he said: "Says he, once, brother: God preserve love. The Fathers said, Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ." Always the elder had one charge to give me from these four, as I withdrew each evening, like someone handing another provisions for the road. And so I kept them as a safeguard for my whole life. And yet, though I had such confidence in the saint and was so disposed toward his service, I had only to perceive that one of the brethren was grieved, wanting to serve him himself, and I went to the abba and entreated him, saying, "It is more fitting for this brother, [b: D] if thou thinkest well, sir." And neither the abba nor the elder himself allowed me. Yet for my part I did all in my power to prefer the brother. And in the nine years I spent there, I do not know that I spoke a rotten word to anyone; and yet I had a service, lest anyone say I had none.
