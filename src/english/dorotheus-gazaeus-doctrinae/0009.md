[1669] 10. And believe me, I know that a brother once walked behind me from the infirmary to the church insulting me, and I went on before him without uttering a single word to him; and moreover, when the abba learned of it, I do not know who told him, and wanted to rebuke him, I stayed holding his feet, saying, "No, for the Lord's sake: it is I who

[1672] [b: A] erred; the brother has done nothing." And another again, whether from temptation or from simplicity, God knows from what, spent a year making his water by night at my head, so that my very bedding was soaked. Likewise some others of the brethren came daily and shook out their mats in front of my cell, and I saw such a multitude of bugs coming into my cell that I could not keep up with killing them; for they were countless because of the heat. Then when I went off to sleep, all of them gathered on me; and sleep did come, from my great weariness, but when I woke from sleep, I found my whole body eaten. And I never said to any of them, "Do not do so," or, "Why doest thou [b: B] so?" Nor do I know myself, as I said, ever to have spoken a word that wounded or grieved anyone. Learn you too to bear one another's burdens; learn to reverence one another. And if one of you hears a word from someone that displeases him, or suffers something against his will, let him not at once lose heart, not at once be stung, not be found, in a time of struggle and profit, with a heart slack, unpractised, without strength, unable to take any assault whatever: as with the melon, if a little splinter touches it, at once it makes a wound and it rots. But rather have a firm heart, have long-suffering, that your love for one another may conquer all that befalls. And if one has a service, or if one of you is [b: C] found to have any business whatever, whether with the gardener or with the cellarer or with the cook or simply with any one of those who serve with you, let both the one who comes on the business and the server who supplies it be eager before everything to keep his own state, and never let himself, whether through turmoil or antipathy or attachment or any will of his own, turn aside from the commandment of God; but whatever the matter turns out to be, small or great, let him ⟨not⟩ despise it and neglect it; for indifference is a bad thing. Nor again let him rank it above his own state, so as to harm his own soul, if so it falls out, for the sake of getting the matter done. For whatever matter you find yourselves in, even if it is very [b: D] pressing and important, I do not want you ever to do anything with contentiousness or with turmoil; but be fully assured that every work you do, whether great, as we said, or small, is one eighth of the thing sought; while keeping your own state, even if it happens that you fail in the service because of it, is three and a half eighths. See how great the difference is.

11. So when you do a thing, if you want to do it perfect and whole, be eager both to do the thing itself, which is as the eighth,

[1673] [b: A] and to keep your own state unharmed, which is the three and a half eighths. But if there follows the necessity of being swept along and going out of the commandment, and of being harmed or harming, so that the need of the service may be met, it is not good that one should lose the three and a half eighths to keep the one eighth. Since if you know that someone does this, such a one does not do his service ⟨with⟩ knowledge. For either from vainglory or from man-pleasing he persists in contending, punishing both himself and his neighbour, so as to hear afterwards that no one could beat him. Bravo! a great feat of valour! This is not victory, brethren; this is loss, this is ruin. See, I tell you: if I send one of you on any errand whatever, and he sees turmoil [b: B] rising up, or any other harm whatever, let him cut it off; and never harm yourselves or one another; but let the errand go and not be done, only do not trouble one another. Since, as I said, you lose the three and a half eighths; and this is plain folly. I say these things to you, not so that you at once lose heart and cut tasks off, or that you be indifferent and throw things away straightaway, [sic: *ἀφρῇ*] and trample your conscience, wanting to be free of care; nor again that you disobey, and each of you say, "I cannot do this; I am harmed; it does not suit me." For on that reckoning you will never do any service whatever, nor can you ever fulfil a commandment of God. But give all your [b: C] strength to doing each service of yours with love, submitting to one another with humility, honouring, entreating. Nothing is stronger than humility. But if one sees [sic: *ἤδη*] his neighbour afflicted at the moment, or himself, cut it off, give way to one another, do not persist until harm follows as well. For it is better, see, I have said it ten thousand times, that the need not be done as you wish, but as it meets the necessity, and not out of obstinacy or out of self-justification, however plausible it looks, ⟨than⟩ to trouble yourselves or afflict one another and lose the three and a half eighths. For great is the difference in the loss. And it often happens that one loses even the eighth itself and does nothing at all. For such are the ways of contentiousness. [b: D] At the very outset, all the works we do, we do in order to profit from them; [and what profit is there, if we do not humble ourselves to one another?] but on the contrary we trouble and afflict one another. And you know that someone says in the Gerontikon that from the neighbour come life and death. Meditate on these things always by yourselves, brethren; exercise yourselves in the words of the holy elders; be eager with love and the fear of God to seek your own profit and one another's. So you can profit from all that happens, and make progress by the help of God. May God himself, as the lover of mankind, grant us

[1676] [b: A] his fear. For it says, "Fear God and keep his commandments, for this is required of every man."

Instruction V. *That one ought not to follow one's own understanding.*

1. In the Proverbs it says, "Those who have no guidance fall like leaves; but salvation is in much counsel." See the force of the word, brethren. See what holy Scripture teaches us: it makes us safe, that we may not follow ourselves, that we may not hold ourselves wise, that we may not believe that we can govern ourselves. We need help; we need those who steer us, after God. Nothing is more wretched, nothing more easily taken, than those who have no one [b: B] to guide them on the way of God. For what does it say? "Those who have no guidance fall like leaves." The leaf at first is always green, flourishing, pleasant; then little by little it dries and falls; and then it is disregarded and trampled on. So too is the man who is not steered by anyone. At first he always has fervour for fasting, for vigil, for stillness, for obedience, for other good things; then after a little, that fervour being quenched, and he not having anyone to steer him, to feed and kindle his fervour, he dries up so, without perceiving it, and falls, and then becomes subject to the enemies, and they do with him whatever they will. But of those who [b: C] disclose their affairs and do everything with counsel, it says, "Salvation is in much counsel." It does not say "much counsel" meaning that one should take counsel with everyone; plainly, with the one in whom he ought to have confidence; and not to keep silent on some things and tell others, but to disclose everything and take counsel on everything. For to such a one, exactly, salvation is in much counsel. For if a man does not lay all his affairs before another, and especially if he is found to come from a bad habit or even a bad upbringing, the devil finds in him one will or one self-justification, and through it throws him down. For when the devil sees someone who does not want to sin, he is not so untalented at doing evil as to suggest to him so crudely [sic: *ἀφαιρῇ τις*] one of the manifest sins; he does not say to him, "Go, [b: D] fornicate," nor, "Go, steal." For he knows that we do not want these things, and he does not trouble to tell us what we do not want; but he finds us having, as I said, one will or one self-justification, and through that, with a show of reason too, he harms us. Whence again it says, "The wicked one does evil when he mingles self-justification." The wicked one is the devil; and he does evil [sic: *κακοποιῆσαι*] when he mingles self-justification, that is, with our self-justification. For then he is strongest, then he harms most, then he works most. For when we hold on to what is our own and follow our self-justifications, then, as though doing a good thing, we plot against ourselves, and do not even know how we are being destroyed. For how can

[1677] [b: A] we know the will of God, or seek it at all, trusting ourselves and holding on to our own will?

2. That is why abba Poemen said that the will is a bronze wall between man and God. See the force of the word. And again he added, saying, "It is a rock that strikes back": that is, opposing and striking against the will of God. "If then a man forsakes it, he too says, In my God I shall leap over a wall. My God, his way is blameless." Very wonderfully he said it. For then one sees the way of God to be without blemish, when he forsakes his own will; but when he indulges his own will, he does not see the way of God to be blameless. But whatever he hears about [b: B] safety, at once he finds fault with it, spits on it, turns away, strikes against it. For how can he bear with anyone, or be persuaded by any counsel whatever, who holds on to his own will? Then the elder speaks of self-justification too: "And if self-justification works together with the will, the man is not easily brought round." Bravo! what sequence the words of the Fathers have! It is death in particular for self-justification to be found together with the will: great danger, great fear. Then the wretch falls utterly. For who persuades such a one to believe that another man has seen what is profitable for him better than he? Then he gives himself over utterly to follow his own will and his own thought; and then, as the enemy wishes, he brings him down. That is why it says, "The wicked one does evil when he mingles self-justification." [b: C] "And he hates the sound [sic: *εἶχον*] of safety." It says so because the wicked one not only hates safety, but cannot even hear its voice; he hates the very sound of safety, that is, the very speaking of anything about safety. I mean, for example: before the one who asks about his profit has acted on anything, before the enemy knows whether he keeps what he hears or does not keep what he hears, he hates altogether the very asking of someone and the hearing of something profitable; the voice itself, the very sound of such words, he hates and turns away from. And shall I tell you why? He knows that his villainy is already being found out through the asking [sic: *διὰ αὐτοῦ*] and the exercising of a word about profit; and there is nothing he so hates and fears as being known, since he no longer finds a way to plot as he wants. For if the soul makes itself safe by [b: D] reporting everything and hearing from someone who knows, "Do this; do not do this; this is good; this is not good; this is self-justification; this is will"; and again it hears, "It is not the time for this thing"; and another time it hears, "Now is the time": the devil does not find on what pretext to harm the man, nor how to throw the soul down, since it is always, as I said, steered, and made safe on every side, and in him is fulfilled, "Salvation is [sic: *Ὑπάρχεται*] in much counsel." But the wicked one does not want this; he hates it. For he wants to do evil, and rejoices rather in those who have no guidance. Why? Because they fall like leaves.
