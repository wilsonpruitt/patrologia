[1693] 8. So we too, let us acquire love, let us acquire tender mercy toward our neighbour, so that we may be kept from the terrible evil of speaking ill, and from condemning or despising anyone. Let us help one another as our own [b: D] members. Who, having a wound on his hand or on his foot or on any one of his other members, sickens at himself, or cuts off his own member, even if it festers, and does not rather cleanse it, wash it, put plasters on it, seal it with the cross, apply holy water, pray, and ask the saints to pray for it, as abba Zosimas used to say? In a word, he does not give it up, does not turn away from his own member or from its stench, but does everything that it may be healed. So we too ought to suffer with one another, to give help, by ourselves, by others more able, and to devise and do everything to help both ourselves and others. For we are members of one another, as [1696] [b: A] the Apostle says: For if we are all one body, and each one members of one another, and if one member suffers, all the members suffer with it. What do the coenobia seem to us to be? Do they not seem to us to be one body and members of one another? Those who govern [sic: *διηκονιῶντες*] are the head; those who watch over and correct are the eyes; those who help by word are the mouth; the ears are those who obey; the hands are those who work; the feet are the agents and those who hold the services. Art thou the head? Govern. Art thou an eye? Watch, consider. Art thou a mouth? Speak, help. Art thou an ear? Obey. Art thou a hand? Work. Art thou a foot? Serve. Let each one serve the body according to his strength; and be eager always to help one another, whether by teaching and putting [b: B] the word of God into a brother's heart, or by comforting him in a time of affliction, or by giving him a hand in his work and helping. In a word, each according to his strength, as I said, be eager to be united with one another; for the more one is united to his neighbour, the more he is united to God.

9. And I tell you an example from the Fathers, so that you may understand the force of what is said. Suppose there is a circle on the ground, that is, a round line drawn with a compass from a centre. The very middle of the circle, as far as the centre, is what is properly called its centre. Set your mind on what is said. Think of this circle as the world, the very middle of the circle as God, and the straight lines from the circle [b: C] to the middle as the ways, that is, the ways of life, of men. So, in so far as the saints go inward, longing to draw near to God, in proportion to their going in they come near to God and to one another; and the more they draw near to God, the more they draw near to one another; and the more they draw near to one another, the more they draw near to God. Understand separation in the same way. For when they stand away from God and turn outward, it is plain that the more they go out and withdraw from God, the more they withdraw from one another; and the more they withdraw from one another, the more they withdraw from God. See, such is the nature of love. In so far as we are outside and do not love God, so far each of us stands apart from his neighbour; but if [b: D] we love God, the nearer we come to God through our love for him, the more we are united in love to our neighbour; and the more we are united to our neighbour, the more we are united to God. May God count us worthy to hear what is profitable to us, and to do it. For the more we take thought and are eager to put into practice what we hear, the more God always enlightens us and teaches us his will.

Instruction VII. *On accusing oneself.*

1. Let us seek, brethren, why it is that sometimes a man hears a grievous word and lets it pass untroubled, as if he had hardly heard it, and sometimes he hears it and is troubled at once. What is the cause of such a difference? And has the matter a single difference [1697] [b: A] of cause of this kind, or several? I see that it has many causes, but there is one that begets, so to say, all the many causes; and I tell you how. First, it happens that a man comes from prayer, or from a good sitting in his cell, and finds himself, so to say, in a good state, and he bears with his brother and lets it pass untroubled. Again, it happens that a man finds himself with an attachment to someone, and so bears without affliction what that person brings upon him. Again, there are times when a man despises the one who wants to harm him, as though making light of what comes from him, and not even heeding him as a man, nor taking any account of him over what he says. And I tell you something of this sort, so that you may marvel. There was a [b: B] brother in the coenobium before I left there, and I saw that he was never troubled or afflicted with anyone, though I saw many of the brethren insulting and vexing him in various ways; yet that young man bore what came from each of them as if no one at all were troubling him. So I always marvelled at the excess of his forbearance, and wanted to learn where he had acquired this virtue. And once I take him aside privately and make a prostration to him, begging him to tell me what thought he always has in his heart, when he is insulted or suffers any harshness from someone, that he shows such long-suffering. And he answers quite naturally, with disdain, and says to me: "But am I to [b: C] heed these good-for-nothings, or take what comes from them, as fine dogs take what comes from men?" Hearing this, I lowered my ears and said in myself, "This brother has found the way." And signing myself, I went away praying that God would shelter both me and him.

2. So it happens, as I said, that a man is also not troubled out of contempt; and this is manifest perdition. But for a man to be troubled with a brother who afflicts him comes either from his not being found in a good state at that hour, or from his having some dislike for him. There are many other causes of this too, which have been spoken of variously. But the cause of all trouble, if we [b: D] seek it with exactness, is not accusing ourselves. From this comes all this crushing of ours; from this we never find rest. And it is no wonder, for we hear from all the saints that there is no other way but this; and we see that no one ever travelled another way and found rest; and we expect to have rest, or to keep a straight road at all, never bearing to accuse ourselves! Truly: if a man practises ten thousand ways of life but does ⟨not⟩ keep this way, he will never cease afflicting and being afflicted, and losing all his labours. But what [1700] [b: A] joy, what rest has he not, wherever he goes, he who accuses himself, as abba Poemen also said? For if anything befalls him, whether loss, or dishonour, or any affliction whatever, he has already reckoned himself deserving of it, and is never troubled. Is there anything freer from care than this?

3. But someone says, "And if my brother afflicts me, and I search myself and find that I gave him no occasion whatever, how can I accuse myself?" Truly, if a man searches himself with the fear of God, he finds that he certainly gave cause, whether by deed, or by word, or by manner. And even if he sees himself, as he says, to have given no cause at all in any of these at present, very likely he afflicted him at another time, [b: B] either in this same matter or in another; very likely he afflicted another brother and ought to suffer for that, or often for some other sin too. So if, as I said, a man searches himself with the fear of God and probes his own conscience exactly, he finds himself certainly to blame. Again, there are times when a man sees himself as sitting in peace and quiet, and when a brother says a word to him that grieves him, he is troubled, and so thinks he is reasonably afflicted by it, saying against him, "If he had not come and spoken to me and troubled me, I would not have sinned." This too is mockery, and this too is false reasoning. For did the one who said the word to him put the passion into him? He showed him the one that was in him, [b: C] so that, if he will, he may repent of it. For this man is like a loaf of clean fine wheat, lovely on the outside, but inside something [?]; and when someone breaks it, then its filth appears. So too this man sat in peace, as he thought, but he had the passion within him and did not know it. His brother said one word to him, and it brought out the filth hidden within. So if he wants to obtain mercy, let him repent, and be cleansed, and make progress, and he sees that he ought rather to thank his brother, as having become for him the cause of so great a benefit. For temptations no longer weigh on him as before; but the more he makes progress, the lighter he finds them. For the more the soul makes progress, the stronger it becomes, and able to bear what [b: D] comes upon it. Just as, if there is a strong animal and someone loads it with a burden, it carries it with ease; and if it does happen to stumble, it gets up at once, and they hardly even notice that it stumbled; but if it is a poor wretched animal, anything at all weighs it down, and if it falls it needs much help to get it up: so it is with the soul too. The more it practises sin, the more wretched it is made by it. For if sin makes a man wretched and rots the one who has it, then whatever happens weighs on him; but if a man makes progress, the things that once weighed on him become lighter to him by degrees. So it benefits us greatly, and brings us into much rest and progress, to count ourselves, and no one else, in what befalls us [1701] [b: A] to blame, above all since nothing can happen to us without the providence of God.
