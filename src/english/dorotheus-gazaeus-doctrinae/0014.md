[1708] Instruction VIII. *On rancour.*

1. The Fathers said that to be angry is foreign to monks, and likewise [b: B] to grieve anyone. And again: if anyone has mastered anger, he has mastered the demons; but if anyone is overcome by this passion, he is altogether a stranger to the monastic life; and so on. What then ought we to say of ourselves, who do not stop even at anger and wrath, but sometimes drive ourselves on even to rancour? What else but mourn this pitiable and inhuman state of ours? So let us be watchful, brethren, and help ourselves with God, that we may be delivered from the bitterness of this ruinous passion. For sometimes a man makes a prostration to his brother when agitation, as may happen, or affliction has fallen between them, and even after the prostration he remains grieved and has thoughts against him. Such a man ought not to [b: C] disregard them, but cut them off quickly. For it is rancour, and there is need of much watchfulness, as I said, so that he does not linger in them and come into danger. For when he made the prostration, he made it as for the commandment's sake, and he cured his wrath for the present, but against rancour he did not yet struggle; [sic: *οὔτω*] and that is why he remained grieved against his brother. For rancour is one thing, wrath another, anger another, and agitation another.

2. And I tell you an illustration, so that you may understand. A man lighting a fire first has a little ember: that is the word of a brother who has hurt you; see, it is still only a little ember. For what is thy brother's word? If thou bear it, thou hast quenched the ember. [b: D] But if thou go on thinking, "And why did he say it to me? I too can say something to him. And if he had not wanted to hurt me, he would not have said it; and believe me, I too can hurt him," see, thou hast put on little sticks, or whatever it may be, like the man lighting the fire, and thou hast made smoke, which is agitation. And agitation is this very movement and clash of thoughts, which rouses and emboldens the heart. And insolence is the retaliating uprising against the one who grieved us, which also becomes audacity, as the blessed abba Mark said: "Malice dwelt on in the thoughts emboldens the heart; but destroyed by prayer and hope, it breaks it." For if thou hadst borne the small word of thy brother, thou couldst have quenched, as I said, even that little ember [1709] [b: A] before the agitation came. Yet even this, if thou wilt, thou canst easily quench while it is fresh, by silence, by prayer, by a single prostration from the heart. But if thou go on smoking, that is, emboldening and rousing thy heart by thinking, "Why did he say it to me? I can say something to him," then out of this very clash, so to speak, and winding together of the thoughts, as the heart is chafed and heated, there comes the kindling of anger. For anger is the boiling of the blood about the heart, as saint Basil says. See, anger has come: this is what they call quick temper. So if thou wilt, thou canst quench even this before it becomes wrath; but if thou go on troubling and being troubled, thou art like the man who puts wood on the hearth and makes the fire burn the more, and so [b: B] then there come live coals, which is wrath.

3. And this is what abba Zosimas said when he was asked what the saying means, "Where there is no anger, strife is quiet." For if at the beginning of the agitation, when it begins, as we said, to smoke and throw out some sparks, a man forestalls it and blames himself and makes a prostration before it is kindled and becomes anger, he is at peace. Again, after anger has come, if he does not grow quiet but goes on being troubled and emboldened, he is, as we said, like the man feeding wood to the fire, and it goes on burning until it makes great live coals. And just as live coals become charcoal and are put away, and last ever so many years without decaying, and even if someone pours water on them they do not rot, so wrath too, if it lingers, becomes [b: C] rancour; and then, unless a man sheds his blood, he is not rid of it. See, I have told you the difference; understand it. See, you have heard what the first agitation is, and what anger is, and what wrath is, and what rancour is. Do you see how from one word it came to so great an evil? For if from the beginning he had put the blame on himself and borne his brother's word, and had not wanted to avenge himself and say two words for one, or even five, and return evil for evil, he could have escaped all these evils. That is why I always tell you: while the passions are fresh, cut them out before they harden against you, or you will suffer for it. For it is one thing to pull up a small plant, and another to uproot a great tree. Nothing surprises [b: D] me more than that we do not even know what we sing. Every day we sing, and curse ourselves, and do not understand. Ought we not to know what we sing? For we are always saying, "If I have repaid those who repay me evil, may I then fall empty before my enemies." [sic: *ἀποπέσῃ μοι*] What is "may I fall"? As long as a man stands, he has strength to resist his enemy: he strikes, he is struck, he wins, he loses; for he is still standing. But if he once falls, how can he still wrestle with his enemy while he lies on the ground? And we pray against ourselves, [1712] [b: A] not only that we may fall before our enemies, but that we may fall empty as well.

4. Let us say what it is for a man to fall empty before his enemies. To fall is to have no more strength to rise, but to lie on the ground. And empty is ⟨to have⟩ no good at all, by which one might at least be able to rise. For the one who rises can take care of himself again, and at some time comes again to the fight. Then we say, "May the enemy then pursue my soul and overtake it": not only pursue but overtake, so that we are in his hands, so that we are beaten in everything, in every matter; and may he throw us down, if we repay those who repay us evil. And not only do we pray this, but also that he trample our life into the earth. What is our life? Our life is [b: B] the virtues; and we pray that our life be trampled into the earth, so that a man becomes wholly earthly, having all his mind down on the earth. "And may he make my glory dwell in the dust." And what is our glory, but the knowledge that comes into the soul through keeping the holy commandments? So this is what we say: that he may make our glory, as the Apostle says, in our shame, that he may lay it in the dust, and make our life and our glory earthly, so that we think nothing according to God, but all things bodily, all things fleshly, like those of whom God said, "My spirit shall not abide in these men, because they are flesh." See, singing all this, [b: C] we curse ourselves if we return evil for evil; and how much evil we return for evil, and it does not trouble us, but we are indifferent!

5. Now one may return evil for evil not only in deed but in word and in bearing. And a man may seem not to return evil in deed, yet is found, as I said, returning it in word or in bearing. For sometimes a man makes a gesture, or a movement, or a look, and troubles his brother. For one can wound one's brother even by a look or a movement; and this too is returning evil for evil. Another is careful to return evil for evil neither in deed nor in word nor in bearing or movement; but he has grief in his heart toward his brother, and [b: D] is upset against him. See how great a difference of state! Another has no grief at all toward his brother; but if he hears that someone has hurt him, or that anyone at all grumbled against him or reviled him, he always enjoys hearing it; and so he too is found returning evil for evil in his heart. Another neither holds malice nor rejoices to hear reviling against the one who hurt him, but even grieves if he is hurt; yet he is not glad that he should fare well either; but if he sees him honoured or at rest, [1713] [b: A] he is upset; and this too is a kind of rancour, though a lighter one. But a man should rather rejoice in his brother's rest, and do everything for his care, and devise each thing for his care and rest. Now we said at the beginning of the talk that there is someone who makes a prostration and still remains grieved against him; and we say that by making the prostration he cured his wrath, but against rancour he had not yet struggled. And there is another who, if it happens that someone hurts him, and they make prostrations and are reconciled to each other, is at peace with him and keeps no evil memory of him in his heart; but if it happens that after some days the other says something to him that hurts him, he begins to remember the first things too, and to be troubled not [b: B] only about the second but about the first as well. This man is like someone who has a wound and puts a plaster on it, and for the time, by the plaster, he has healed the wound and it has closed over; but the place is still weaker, and whenever someone throws a stone at him, that place more than the whole body is easily struck, and at once begins to bleed. So it happened to him too: he had a wound and put on a plaster, which is the prostration, and for the time he healed the wound, as the first man did, that is, the wrath; he began to take care of the rancour too, by being careful to keep no evil memory in his heart; for this is the closing over of the wound. But he has not yet wiped it out completely, [b: C] but still has a remnant of rancour, which is the scar, from which the whole wound [sic: *τραῦμα σῶμα*] is easily torn open again when it takes a small blow. So he ought to struggle to wipe out the scar completely as well, so that the hair grows over the place and no disfigurement is left, nor can it be seen at all that there was a wound in that place. How can he achieve this? By praying with his whole heart for the one who grieved him, and saying, "O God, help my brother, and me through his prayers." And so he is found praying for his brother, which is a token of compassion and love, and humbling himself by asking for help through his prayers. And where there is compassion and love and lowliness, what power has anger, or any other [b: D] passion? As abba Zosimas said, "If the devil sets in motion all the engines of his malice, with all his demons, all his schemes are idle and are crushed by the lowliness of Christ's commandment." And another elder says, "He who prays for his enemies will be free of rancour." Work, and understand well what you hear. For truly, unless you work at these things, ⟨you cannot⟩ take them in by word. What man who wants to learn a craft takes it in by word alone? Surely he first keeps at it, making and spoiling, making and destroying, and so, little by little, toiling and enduring, he learns the craft, God seeing his toil and his purpose and working with him. But we want to take in the craft of crafts by word, [1716] [b: A] not setting our hand to the work? And how can that be? So let us attend to ourselves, brethren, and work with zeal while we still have time. May God grant us to remember and keep what we hear, so that it may not be to our judgment on the day of judgment.
