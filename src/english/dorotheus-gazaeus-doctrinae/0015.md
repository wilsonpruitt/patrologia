[1716] Instruction IX. *On lying.*

1. I want to remind you a little, brethren, about lying. For I see that you are not very careful to guard your tongue, and from this we are easily dragged into many things. See, my brethren, that in every matter, as I always tell you, there is habit, both toward the good and toward the bad. So there is need [b: B] of much watchfulness, so that we are not robbed by lying. For no one who lies is united with God; lying is foreign to God. For it is written that lying is of the evil one; and again, that he is a liar, and the father of it. See, he calls the devil the father of lying; but God is the truth. For he himself says, "I am the way, and the truth, and the life." See from what we separate ourselves, and to whom [sic: *τίνι τρόπῳ*] we cleave through lying: plainly, to the evil one. So if we truly want to be saved, we ought with all zeal to love the truth and to guard ourselves from every lie, so that it does not separate us from the truth and from the life. Now there are three kinds of lying: there is the one who lies in his mind; and there is the one who lies in word; and there is [b: C] also the one who lies with his very life. The one who lies in his mind is the one who entertains suspicions. If he sees someone talking with his brother, he suspects, and says, "They are talking about me." And if they break off the conversation, again he suspects that they broke it off because of him. If someone says a word, he suspects that he said it to hurt him; and in short, in every matter he speaks so of his neighbour, saying, "He did this because of me, he said this because of me; he did this for that reason." This is the one who lies in his mind. For he says nothing true, but everything is suspicion. From this, then, come meddling, backbiting, eavesdropping, quarrelling, judging. And sometimes he suspects a thing, and from the event it turns out true; and so, he says, [b: D] wanting to correct himself, he meddles, reasoning, "If someone speaks against me, I see what fault of mine he is accusing me of, and I correct myself." Well, first, this beginning is from the evil one; for he began [sic: *ἤρξατε*] through lying: not knowing, he suspected what he did not know. How then can a bad tree bear good fruit? But if he really wants to correct himself, when a brother says to him, "Do not do this," or "Why didst thou do this?" let him not be troubled, but make a prostration and thank him; then he will be corrected. And if God sees that such is his purpose, he will never let him go astray, but will surely send the one who is to correct him. But to say, "So as to be corrected, I trust my suspicions," [1717] [b: A] and then to eavesdrop and meddle, is the devil's own justification, when he wants to lay snares.

2. Once when I was in the coenobium, I was tempted to grasp a man's state from his movements. And such a thing befell me: once, as I was standing, a woman passed by carrying a jar of water, and I do not know how I was carried away, and I looked at her eyes; and at once my thought suggests to me that she is a harlot. When the thought had suggested this, I was much distressed, and I make it known to the elder, abba John, thus: "Master, if I see someone's movement without wanting to, and my thought [b: B] suggests to me his state, what ought I to do?" And the elder makes answer to me thus: "What then? Is it not sometimes the case that a man has a natural defect and by struggle sets it right? One cannot learn a man's state [sic: *κατάττασίν*] from this. So never believe thy suspicions; for a crooked rule makes even straight things crooked. Suspicions are false, and they do harm." From then on, if my thought told me of the sun that it is the sun, or of darkness that it is darkness, I did not believe it. Nothing is worse than suspicions; they are so harmful that, when they linger in us, they begin to persuade us that we actually see things that neither exist nor are happening. And I tell you a wonderful thing about this, which I witnessed when I was still in the coenobium. We had a brother [b: C] there much troubled by this passion, and he so believed his own suspicions that he was fully convinced, of each suspicion of his, that it was certainly as his thought drew it for him and could not be otherwise. So, as the evil advanced with time, the demons persuaded him so far into delusion that once he went into the garden spying. For he was keeping watch and eavesdropping; and he thought he saw one of the brethren stealing figs and eating them. It was a Friday, and not yet even the second hour. When then it had persuaded him that he had seen the thing himself, in truth, he hides himself, he says, and goes out in silence, and watches again at the hour of the [b: D] synaxis, to see what the brother would do at communion, the one who had, so he thought, stolen the figs and eaten them. And when he saw him washing his hands to go in and communicate, he runs and says to the abba, "So-and-so, the brother: see, he is going in to share in holy communion with the brethren; but give orders that it not be given to him. For I know he was stealing figs from the garden this morning and eating them." Meanwhile that brother goes in with much compunction to the holy offering; for he was one of the devout. When the abba saw him, he calls him before he comes near the priest who was giving communion, and takes him aside, and says to him, "Tell me, brother, what is it thou hast done today?" He was taken aback, and says to him, "Where, master?" The [1720] [b: A] abba says to him, "When thou wentest into the garden this morning, what wast thou doing there?" Again the brother says, as one astonished, "Master, I have not even seen the garden today, nor was I here in the coenobium this morning; see, I have only just come in from the road. For straight after the vigil was dismissed, the steward sent me on such-and-such an errand." Now the errand he spoke of was many miles away, and the brother had arrived just at the hour of the synaxis. The abba sends for the steward and says to him, "Where didst thou send this brother?" The steward answers just as the brother had said, "I sent him to such-and-such a village"; and he makes a prostration, saying, "Forgive, sir: thou wast resting after the vigil, and that is why I did not bring him to receive [b: B] leave from thee." When the abba was assured, he dismissed them to go and communicate. And he calls the brother who had the suspicions, and rebukes him, and excludes him from holy communion; and not only this, but he called all the monks after the synaxis and told them with tears what had happened, and exposed the brother before them all, aiming at three things by this: that the devil be put to shame and the sower of suspicions be made an example; that the brother's sin be forgiven through that disgrace and he receive help from God for the future; and to make the brethren more careful never to allow ourselves to trust our own suspicions. And having admonished us and the brother much about this, he said that nothing is more harmful than suspicions, and he showed it by the example that had happened.

3. And the Fathers said other such things in various ways, to make us safe against the harm of suspicions. So let us strive with all our strength never to believe our own suspicions. Nothing so draws [b: C] a man away from God, away from heeding his own sins, and makes him always meddle in what does not concern him; and from this comes nothing good, and from this come countless afflictions, and from this a man never has leisure to acquire the fear of God. And if evil suspicions are sown in us through our malice, let us at once turn them into good thoughts, and they will not harm us. For suspicions are evil, and never let the soul be at peace. See, this is lying in the mind. And the one who lies in word is he who, suppose, is too lazy to rise for the vigil, and does not say, "Forgive me, for I was lazy about rising," but says, "I had a fever, I was dizzy, I could not get up, [b: D] I had no strength," and says ten false words so as not to make one prostration and be humbled. And if someone blames him in some matter, he keeps changing his words and dressing them up so as not to bear the blame. Likewise, if it happens that he has words with his brother, he does not stop justifying himself and saying, "But thou saidst, but thou didst, but I did not say it, but he said it, but this, but that," just so as not to be humbled. Again, if he desires something, he cannot bear to say, "I desire this," but keeps twisting his words, saying, "I suffer from this, and I need that," or "I was told to do this," and tells ever so many lies until he [1721] [b: A] satisfies his desire. For every sin comes about either through love of pleasure, or through love of money, or love of glory. Likewise lying too comes about through these three: a man lies either so as not to be blamed and humbled, or to carry out a will, or to gain something; and he does not stop twisting this way, twisting that way, contriving whatever he can say until he accomplishes his aim. Such a man is never believed: even if he says a true word, no one can believe him; even what is true in him is found doubtful. But sometimes a necessity arises in a matter, and unless one conceals a little, [sic: *πικρὸν*] the matter comes to greater trouble and affliction. When then such a circumstance arises, and a man sees himself forced on this account to alter a word, so that there may not be, as I said, [b: B] greater trouble and affliction, or danger: as abba Alonius said to abba Agatho, "Look, two men committed a murder in thy presence, and one of them fled into thy cell; and look, the magistrate seeks him and asks thee, saying, 'Was a murder done in thy presence?' Unless thou use discretion, thou deliverest the man to death." So when some great necessity arises, one should not even so be unconcerned, but repent and weep before God, and hold such a moment as a time of temptation; and not this often, but once in a long while. For just as with theriac and a purgative, taken often they harm, but if one takes [sic: *λάθῃ*] them once in a while out of great need they help him, so one ought to treat this matter: that even if out of necessity he wants to alter [b: C] a word, not often, but perhaps once in a long while, if he sees, as I said, great necessity; and even that once in a while, showing to God with fear and trembling both his purpose and the necessity; and he is sheltered; for otherwise he is harmed even by this.
