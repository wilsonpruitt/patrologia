⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [b: D] 4. ⟦s2⟧ But let us probe what is said, in order. ⟦s3⟧ He calls confusion Babylon; for so it is interpreted, from Babel, which is Sychem. ⟦s3⟧ He calls enmity the daughter of Babylon. ⟦s4⟧ For first the soul is confused, and so it brings about the sin. ⟦s4⟧ And he calls it wretched since (as I have told you at other times too) evil is without being and without subsistence: it comes to consist out of what is not through our negligence, and again through our right doing it is destroyed and passes into what is not. ⟦s5⟧ So the saint says, as if to it: Blessed is he who shall repay thee the repayment which thou didst repay us. ⟦s6⟧ Let us learn what we gave, and what we received in return, and what we want to repay. ⟦s7⟧ We gave our will, and received [1740] [b: A] sin in return. ⟦s8⟧ So the word calls blessed those who repay it this; and to repay it is to do it no more. ⟦s9⟧ Then he adds: Blessed is he who shall seize and dash thy little ones against the rock. ⟦s10⟧ As if to say: Blessed is he who does not give the things born of thee, that is, the evil thoughts, room even at the outset for the evil to grow in him and be acted out; but quickly, while they are still infants, before they are fed and grow strong against him, seizes them and dashes them against the rock, which is Christ, and destroys them by fleeing to Christ.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [b: B] 5. ⟦s2⟧ See how both the elders and holy Scripture all agree and call blessed those who strive to cut off the passions while they are young, before experiencing their pain and bitterness. ⟦s3⟧ Let us be eager then, brethren, that we may obtain mercy. ⟦s3⟧ Let us toil a little and we shall find much rest. ⟦s4+5+6+7+8+9+10⟧ The Fathers said how one ought to cleanse oneself in detail: every evening one should examine oneself, how one passed the day, and again in the morning, how one passed the night, and repent to God for whatever one has sinned in, as is likely. ⟦s4+5+6+7+8+9+10⟧ But truly we, since we sin much, need, because of our forgetfulness, to search ourselves every six hours as well: how we have passed the time and in what we have sinned. ⟦s4+5+6+7+8+9+10⟧ And each of us should say to himself: "Did I perhaps speak and wound my brother? [b: C] Did I perhaps see him doing things, and condemn him, or despise him, or speak ill of him? ⟦s4+5+6+7+8+9+10⟧ Did I ask the cellarer for something, and he did not give it me, and I murmured against him? ⟦s4+5+6+7+8+9+10⟧ Was the food not well cooked, [sic: *μὴ, τὸ ἔψη τὸ καλῶς*] and I said a word and shamed the cook and afflicted him? ⟦s4+5+6+7+8+9+10⟧ Or, being fussy, did I murmur to myself?" ⟦s4+5+6+7+8+9+10⟧ For if someone murmurs to himself, it is a sin. ⟦s4+5+6+7+8+9+10⟧ Again he says, "Did the canonarch say a word to me, or another of the brethren, and I did not bear it, but opposed him?" ⟦s4+5+6+7+8+9+10⟧ So every day we ought to probe ourselves, how we passed the day. ⟦s4+5+6+7+8+9+10⟧ Likewise one ought to search oneself how one passed the night as well: whether one rose with eagerness for the vigil, or whether one made light of the one who woke him, or murmured [b: D] against him. ⟦s4+5+6+7+8+9+10⟧ For one ought to know that the one who wakes him for the vigil does him great good and becomes the procurer of great goods for him, waking him to pray to God for his sins and to be enlightened. ⟦s4+5+6+7+8+9+10⟧ How ought one not to thank such a one? ⟦s4+5+6+7+8+9+10⟧ Truly one ought to hold that it is almost through him that one's salvation comes.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1+s2+s3+s4⟧ [1741] [b: A] 6. And I tell you a wonderful thing about this, which I heard about a great clear-sighted elder: standing in the church, as the brethren were beginning to chant, he saw someone in shining raiment come out of the sanctuary, holding as it were a little bowl containing holy water, and a spatula; [sic: *μύλην*] and he dipped that spatula into the bowl and went round all the brethren, sealing each of them. And of the places of those who were not present, some he sealed and others he passed by. Again, when they were about to be dismissed, he saw him [sic: *ἔβλεπον*] coming out of the sanctuary and doing the same thing likewise. So one day the elder held him, and throws himself at his feet, begging to learn what he was doing and who he was. And that one in shining raiment says to him, [b: B] "I am an angel of God, and I have been charged at the synaxis to give this seal to those who are found in the church at the beginning of the psalmody, and to those who stay until the dismissal, because of their eagerness and zeal and their good purpose." The elder says to him, "And how is it that thou sealest the places of some who are not present?" The holy angel answers him, saying, "Those of the brethren who are zealous and good in purpose, but are absent through some unavoidable sickness with the blessing of the Fathers, or again are occupied by some commandment under obedience, and for that reason are not present, these receive their seal even when absent, since in disposition they are with [b: C] those who chant. Only to those who can be present and through negligence are not present have I been charged not to give the seal, since they have made themselves unworthy." See, look what a gift he procures for his brother who rouses him to the rule of the vigil. Be eager then, brethren, never to be deprived of the seal of the holy angel. And if it happens that someone is distracted, and another reminds him, he ought not to be indignant, but attending to the good, he should thank the one who reminds him, whoever he may be.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 7. ⟦s2⟧ When I was in the coenobium, the abba made me guestmaster with the consent of the elders. ⟦s3⟧ I was also just up from a great sickness. ⟦s4⟧ So guests would come, and I would spend the evening with them; and then camel-drivers, [b: D] and I would see to their needs. ⟦s4⟧ And often, after I had gone off to sleep, another need would come up again and wake me. ⟦s5⟧ Meanwhile the hour of the vigil would arrive, and as soon as I snatched a little sleep, see, the canonarch would wake me. ⟦s5⟧ So I found myself, whether from the toil or from the sickness (for I was still being stolen upon by little fevers), undone, [1744] [b: A] like a man not in possession of himself. ⟦s6+7+8+9+10⟧ So I answered him, borne down by sleep: "Very good, sir. ⟦s6+7+8+9+10⟧ May God remember thy charity [sic: *μνησθείη ἡ ἀγάπη ὁ Θεὸς*] and give thee the reward. ⟦s6+7+8+9+10⟧ Thou hast bidden: I am coming, sir." ⟦s6+7+8+9+10⟧ Then, when he went off, I would fall asleep again; and I was very much afflicted that I was slow to get up for church. ⟦s6+7+8+9+10⟧ And since I could see no way to keep up with him, I asked two of the brethren, one to wake me, and the other not to let me doze at the vigil. ⟦s6+7+8+9+10⟧ And believe me, brethren, I revered them as though my salvation were through them, and I almost venerated them. ⟦s6+7+8+9+10⟧ So you too ought to be so disposed toward those who rouse you to the rule of the church and to every good thing. ⟦s6+7+8+9+10⟧ So, as we were saying, one ought to search [b: B] how he passed the day and the night: whether he stood at the psalmody with watchfulness; and whether at prayer he was taken captive by passionate thoughts; or whether he listened attentively to the divine readings; or whether he left the psalmody and went out of the church in distraction. ⟦s6+7+8+9+10⟧ If someone searches himself in this way every day, and is eager to repent for what he has sinned and to correct himself, he begins to lessen his evil; and if he used to do nine, he does eight; and so, progressing little by little with God, he does not let the passions grow firm against him. ⟦s11+12⟧ For it is a great danger for someone to fall into the habit of a passion; since, as we said, not even if such a man wishes is he able alone to overcome the passion, unless he also has help from [b: C] some saints.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1+s2+s3+s4+s5+s6+s7+s8+s9+s10+s11+s12+s13+s14+s15+s16+s17+s18+s19+s20+s21+s22⟧ 8. Do you want me to tell you about someone who had a passion in habit? Hear a thing worthy of much weeping. When I was in the coenobium, I do not know how, the brethren simply took it, half in jest, to lay their thoughts before me. The abba too said so, and with the consent of the elders entrusted me with the care of this part. So one day one of the brethren comes and says to me, "Forgive me, and pray for me, for I steal and eat." I say to him, "Why? Art thou hungry?" And he says to me, "Yes: I am not satisfied by the brethren's table, and I cannot ask." I say to him, [b: D] "Why dost thou not go and lay it before the abba?" He says, "I am ashamed." I say to him, "And dost thou want me to go?" And he says to me, "As thou biddest, sir." So I went off and told the abba, and he says to me, "Of thy charity, take care of him, as thou knowest how." Then I take him, and say to the cellarer in his presence, "Of thy charity, at whatever hour this brother comes to thee, give him whatever he wants, and refuse him nothing." Hearing this, the cellarer says to me, "Thou hast bidden; so I do." That brother does so for a few days; and then he comes saying to me, "Forgive me, sir, for I have begun to steal again." I say to him, "Why? Does the cellarer not give thee whatever thou wantest?" "Yes. Forgive me: he gives me whatever I want. [1745] [b: A] But I am ashamed before him." I say to him, "Thou art not ashamed before me, art thou?" He says to me, "No." I say to him, "Then if thou wantest anything, come and take it from me, and do not steal." For at that time I had the service of the infirmary. So he would come to me and take as much as he wanted. Then, after some days, he began to steal again, and comes afflicted and says to me, "See, I am stealing again." I say to him, "Why, my brother? Do I not give thee whatever thou wantest?" He says, "Yes." I say to him, "Then why dost thou steal?" He says to me, "Forgive me, I do not know why; I just steal, like that." Then I say to him, "Tell me truly, what dost thou do with the things thou stealest?" He says to me, "I give them to the donkey." And it was found that that brother was stealing morsels of bread, dates, figs, onions [b: B], simply whatever he found, and hiding them, some under his bedding and some elsewhere. And at last, not finding what to do with them, when he saw them spoiling, he would go and throw them away, or put them before the beasts.

⟦s1+s2+s3+s4+s5+s6+s7+s8+s9+s10+s11+s12+s13+s14+s15+s16+s17+s18+s19+s20+s21+s22⟧ 9. See, do you see what it is to have a passion in habit? Do you see what wretchedness, what misery? He knew that it was bad, he knew that he was doing badly; he was afflicted, he wept; and yet the poor wretch was dragged along by the bad custom he had made for himself through his earlier negligence. And well did abba Nisthero say: if someone is dragged down by a passion, he has become the slave of the passion. May [b: C] the good God deliver us from evil habit, lest he say to us too: What profit is there in my blood, when I go down to corruption? [n: Psal. xxix, 10.] I have told you in various ways also how someone falls into a habit. For he who is angry once is not yet called irascible; nor is he who fornicates once called a fornicator; nor is he who shows mercy once called merciful. But both virtue and vice, from being practised continually, produce a certain habit in the soul; and then that very habit either punishes it or gives it rest. And how virtue gives the soul rest, and how vice punishes it, we have said in various ways: that virtue is natural and is in us. For the seeds of virtue cannot be blotted out. So I said that the more we do good things, the more we come to be in the habit of virtue; that is, we take up again our own habit, we return to our own [b: D] health, as from ophthalmia to our proper light, or from any other sickness whatever to our own and natural health. But with vice it is not so: rather, through the practice of evil we take on a foreign habit, contrary to nature. It is as if we come into the habit of some pestilential sickness, so that we can no longer be healed without many tears, which are able to move the compassions of Christ upon us. Just as we find in bodily things too. [1748] [b: A] There are some foods that produce, say, a melancholic humour. For example, cabbage is melancholic, and lentils, and some other such things. So it is not from eating cabbage or lentils or one of such things once or twice [sic: *οἱ*] that a melancholic humour arises against a man; but if he does it often, then, the humour increasing, it stirs up fevers, and they burn the one who has them; and it brings a man into countless other troubles as well. So too with the soul: if someone keeps on sinning, an evil habit arises in the soul, and it is this that punishes it.
