⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 5. ⟦s2⟧ So it is with the soul as well. ⟦s3⟧ First one must cut away every old attachment and the bad customs. ⟦s4⟧ And the holy Basil says: "This is no small struggle, for us to get the better of custom. ⟦s5⟧ For a habit confirmed over a long time takes on, for the most part, the strength of nature." ⟦s5⟧ So one must struggle, [sic: *Λέγει*] as I said, against the bad customs and against the passions, and not only ⟨against these, but also⟩ against their causes, which are roots. ⟦s6⟧ And if the roots are not thrown out, the thorns are bound to grow again. ⟦s7⟧ For there are some passions that have no strength if one cuts off their causes: for instance, envy in itself is nothing, but it has certain causes, and love of glory is one of them. ⟦s8⟧ For a man who wants to be glorified envies the one who is [b: C] glorified, or preferred before him. ⟦s9⟧ Likewise anger too arises from other causes, and above all from love of pleasure. ⟦s10⟧ Evagrius too mentions this, speaking of a certain saint who used to say: For this reason I forgo pleasures, [sic: *ἐπέρω*] to cut away the occasions of wrath. ⟦s11⟧ And all the Fathers say that every passion is born from these three: love of glory, love of money, and love of pleasure, as I have told you in various ways. ⟦s12⟧ So one must cut off not only the passions but their causes too, and so tend one's character well, through repentance and weeping, and then begin to sow the good seed, which is good works. ⟦s13⟧ For just as we said of the field, that if one does not put in good seed, the [b: D] weeds come up and find the earth good and soft from the tending, and root more deeply in it, so it is with a man too: if, after tending his character and repenting of his former deeds, he neglects to do good works and acquire the virtues, it happens to him as the Gospel says: When the unclean spirit goes out of a man, it passes through waterless places seeking rest, and not finding it, then it says: I will return to [1757] [b: A] my house from which I came out. ⟦s14⟧ And when it comes it finds it unoccupied, empty, that is, of all virtue, and swept and adorned. ⟦s15+16⟧ Then it goes and takes with it seven other spirits more wicked than itself, and they go in and dwell there, and the last state of that man becomes worse than the first. [n: Luc. xi, 24-27.] For it is impossible for the soul to stay in the same state; it is always moving on, either for the better or for the worse. ⟦s17⟧ That is why each one who wants to be saved needs not only not to do evil, but also to do good, as it says in the psalm: Turn from evil and do good. ⟦s18+19⟧ It did not say "Turn from evil" only, but also "Do good." ⟦s20⟧ For example: if someone was used to doing wrong, he must not only stop doing wrong but also act justly. ⟦s21⟧ If [b: B] he was profligate, he must not only stop being profligate but also be self-controlled. ⟦s22⟧ If he was irascible, he must not only not be angry but also acquire meekness. ⟦s23+24⟧ If someone was insolent, he must not only stop being insolent but also humble himself. ⟦s25⟧ And this is turning from evil and doing good. ⟦s26⟧ For each passion has its contrary virtue: pride has humility, love of money has almsgiving, profligacy self-control, faintheartedness has patience, anger meekness, hatred love. ⟦s27⟧ In short, every passion, as I said, has its contrary virtue.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 6. ⟦s2⟧ I have often told you these things. ⟦s3⟧ So just as we drove out the virtues and brought in their passions, so we must toil not only to drive out the [b: C] passions but also to bring in the virtues and set them in their own place. ⟦s4⟧ For we have the virtues by nature, given us by God. ⟦s5+6⟧ For as soon as God made man, he sowed the virtues in him, as he says: Let us make man after our image and after our likeness. [n: Gen. i, 26.] "After our image" he said, because God made the soul incorruptible and free to choose; "after our likeness" he says with respect to virtue. ⟦s7+8⟧ For he says: Be merciful, as your heavenly Father is merciful. ⟦s9+10⟧ Be holy, for I am holy. [n: ibid. Levit. xi, 44; I Petr. i, 16.] And again the Apostle says: Be kind to one another. [n: Ephes. iv, 32.] And the psalm says: The Lord is good to those who wait for him; [n: Thren. iii, 25.] and all such sayings. ⟦s11⟧ This is what "after our likeness" means. ⟦s12⟧ So God gave us the virtues by nature; but the passions we do not have by nature; [b: D] for they have no substance or subsistence at all, but just as darkness has no subsistence of its own being, but is a condition of the air, as the great Basil says, that comes about through the privation of light, so it is with the passions too. ⟦s13+14+15+16⟧ The soul, turning aside from the virtues through love of pleasure, built up the passions and made them firm against itself. ⟦s13+14+15+16⟧ So we need, as I said, just as with the field, after doing all the tending, to sow the good seed at once, so that it may bear good fruit in return. ⟦s13+14+15+16⟧ Again, the one who sows his field must, as soon as he puts the seed in, hide it and bury it in the earth; otherwise the birds come and snatch it away, and [1760] [b: A] it is lost. ⟦s13+14+15+16⟧ And after he has hidden it, he waits for the mercy of God, until he sends the rain and the seed grows. ⟦s13+14+15+16⟧ For if the farmer toils endlessly, cleaning and tending, and then sowing, unless God rains on his seed, all his toil comes to nothing. ⟦s13+14+15+16⟧ So we too must do: even if we do some good, we must cover it with humility, and cast our weakness upon God, begging him to look favourably on our toil, otherwise it comes to nothing. ⟦s17⟧ Again, there are times when, even after it has rained and the seed has sprouted, the rain does not come down again in its season, and the shoot withers and is lost. ⟦s18⟧ For the seed needs rain, and the shoot likewise [b: B] as it grows, until it is made firm; and not even so can one be free from care. ⟦s19⟧ For sometimes, even after it has grown and put out the ear, locusts come, or hail, or something of that kind, and destroy the fruit. ⟦s20⟧ So it is with the soul too. ⟦s21⟧ When a man has toiled and cleansed it from all the passions we spoke of, and been diligent in all the virtues, he must always look to the mercy of God and to his shelter, so that they may not forsake it and it be lost. ⟦s22⟧ For just as we said of the seed too, that even after it has sprouted and grown and borne fruit, unless the rain comes down again as it grows, it withers and is lost, so it is with man. ⟦s23⟧ If after he has done so much God lifts his shelter from him a little and forsakes [b: C] him, he is lost. ⟦s24⟧ And forsaking comes upon a man when he does something against his own state. ⟦s25⟧ For example: if someone is reverent and comes to indifference, or lowly and comes to insolence. ⟦s26⟧ And God does not forsake the indifferent man so much when he is indifferent, nor the insolent man when he is insolent, as he forsakes the reverent man if he becomes indifferent, and the lowly man if he becomes insolent. ⟦s27⟧ This is what it is to sin against one's own state; from this comes forsaking.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 7. ⟦s2+3⟧ That is why the holy Basil judges the sin of the reverent man one way and that of the indifferent man another. [sic: *διαφόρου*] And when a man guards himself from this too, he must take care that if he does some little thing, he does not do it [b: D] with vainglory, or to please men, or according to some human thought, and so lose that very little he was doing, as we said of the locust and the hail and the like. ⟦s4⟧ Again, on the land, even when the fruit suffers no harm but is kept safe until harvest, not even so can the farmer be free from care; for there are times when, even after a man has harvested the field and done all his toil, a wicked man comes with hatred and sets fire to that crop, and destroys the whole crop and that man's toil. ⟦s5⟧ Until he sees that he has cleaned it well and put it in the barn, he cannot be free from care. ⟦s5⟧ Likewise a man too, when he has been able to escape all these things we spoke of, must not even so be free from care. ⟦s6⟧ For it happens [1761] [b: A] that after all this the devil finds a way to lead him astray, either through self-justifications, or through pride, or by casting in thoughts of unbelief or of evil heresy; and not only does he destroy all his toils, but he also separates him from God. ⟦s7⟧ And whatever he could not do to him through the deed, he does to him through a single thought. ⟦s8⟧ For there are times when even a thought can separate a man from God, if he accepts it and consents to it. ⟦s9⟧ So he who wills ⟨to be saved⟩ in truth ought not to be free from care until his last breath. ⟦s10⟧ There is need, then, of toil and much care, and of praying to God continually, that he himself may shelter and save us by his goodness, to the glory of his holy name.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Instruction XIII. [b: B] *On bearing temptations without disturbance and with thanksgiving.* ⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 1. ⟦s2⟧ Abba Poemen said well that the mark of the monk shows in temptations. ⟦s3⟧ For the monk who comes in truth to serve the Lord ought, according to Wisdom, [n: Eccli ii. 1.] to prepare his soul for temptations, so that he may never be taken aback or troubled by what befalls him, believing that nothing happens without the providence of God. ⟦s4⟧ And where there is God's providence, what happens is wholly good and for the profit of the soul. ⟦s4⟧ For all that God does with us he does for our good, loving us and sparing us. ⟦s5⟧ And we ought, as the Apostle said, in everything to give thanks to his goodness, [n: Ephes. v. 20; I Thess. v, 18.] and never [b: C] lose heart or be small of soul at what befalls us, but receive without disturbance what comes upon us, with humility and with hope in God, [sic: *μετὰ Θεὸν*] persuaded, as I said, that all that God does with us he does in goodness, and does loving us, and does well. ⟦s6⟧ And they cannot turn out well otherwise, except as God has thus shown mercy. ⟦s7⟧ If a man has a friend and is fully assured that he loves him, whatever he suffers from him, even if it is grievous, he holds that he did it in love, and never believes of his friend that he means to harm him. ⟦s7⟧ How much more ought we to hold of God, who formed us, and brought us from not being into being, and for our sake became man, and died for us, that he does all things [b: D] with us in goodness and does them loving us?
