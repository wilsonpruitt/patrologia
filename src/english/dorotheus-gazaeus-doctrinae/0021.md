[1761] 2. And in the case of a friend one may reason that he does it loving me and sparing me; but he surely does not also have the understanding to manage my affairs, and so, as is likely, he harms me even without wishing to. But with God we cannot say this. For he is the fountain of wisdom, and knows all that is profitable to us, and manages our affairs toward it, down to the most trivial. Again, one can say of a friend that he loves, and spares, and has understanding to manage my [1764] [b: A] affairs, but he does not also have the power to help me in what he thinks would profit me. Nor can we say this of God. For all things are possible to him, and nothing is impossible before him. So then we know of God that he loves and spares his own creature, and that he is the fountain of wisdom and knows how to manage our affairs, and that nothing is impossible to him, but all things serve his will; and we ought to know that all he does he does for our good, and to receive it with thanksgiving, as we said before, as from a benefactor and good Master, even if the things are grievous. For all things come about by just judgment, and God, being so merciful, does not overlook even the slightest [b: B] affliction of ours. [sic: *ψυχοῦσαν*] But often someone wavers within himself, saying, "And what if a man sins, from affliction, in the things brought upon him? How can he hold that they are for his good?"

3. We do not sin in the things brought upon us except because we are lovers of ease, and unwilling to bear a little affliction or to suffer anything unlooked-for; since God does not allow anything beyond our strength to be brought upon us, as the Apostle said: God is faithful, who will not let us be tempted beyond what we are able. [n: I Cor. x, 13.] But it is we who have no patience, who will not toil even a little, nor bear to receive anything with lowliness; that is why we are crushed, and the more we strive to escape [b: C] temptations, the more we weigh ourselves down, and grow faint, and cannot even get free. There are some who swim in the sea out of need, and if they know the art of swimming, when the wave comes against them, ⟨they stoop⟩ under it until it passes, [sic: *ὑπὸ*] and then they go on swimming unharmed. But if they choose to oppose the wave, it pushes them out and hurls them a long way. Again, as they begin to swim, another wave comes over them; if again they oppose it, again it pushes them out, and they only wear themselves out, achieving nothing. But if, as I said, they stoop to the wave and humble themselves under it, it passes, harming them in nothing, and they go on swimming as long as they like, and doing their work. So too with temptations: [b: D] if a man bears the temptation with patience and lowliness, it passes harmlessly; but if he keeps on being afflicted, troubled, blaming everyone, he punishes himself, making the temptation heavier against himself, so that instead of profiting he is harmed. For temptations greatly profit the one who bears them without disturbance.

4. And if a passion troubles us, we ought not to be disturbed at that either. For a man to be disturbed when troubled by a passion comes of ignorance [1765] [b: A] and pride, and of not knowing his own state, and of fleeing toil, as the Fathers said. That is why we do not advance: because we do not know our own measures, nor have patience in the work we begin, but want to acquire virtue without toil. [sic: *ἀρχὴν*] For why is the passionate man taken aback when troubled by a passion? Why is he disturbed when he has acted it out? Thou hast it, and art thou disturbed? Thou hast its pledges, and dost thou say, "Why does it trouble me?" Rather endure, struggle, call upon God. For it is impossible for one who has fallen into the working of the passions not to have the affliction of them. Their vessels, as abba Sisoes said, are within thee; give them their pledge, and they go away. By the vessels he meant [b: B] the causes. So as long as we have loved these and acted them out, it is not possible for us not to be taken captive by the passionate thoughts, which force us even against our will to act out the passions, since we betrayed ourselves willingly into their hands. This is what the prophet says of Ephraim, [sic: *τὴν τοῦ Ἐφραῒμ*] who overpowered his adversary, that is, his own conscience, and trampled down judgment, because he sought Egypt, and was taken by force among the Assyrians. [n: Osee v, 13.] Egypt, say the Fathers, is the will of the flesh that inclines us toward bodily rest and teaches the mind to be more self-indulgent; and the Assyrians are the passionate thoughts that trouble and confuse the mind and fill it with unclean idols, and carry it off by force, even unwilling, into sin in act.

5. [b: C] So if a man gives himself up willingly to the self-indulgence of the body, he is compelled, even unwilling, to be carried off by force to the Assyrians and to serve Nebuchadnezzar. The prophet, knowing this, was in anguish, and said to them: Do not go down into Egypt. What are you doing, wretches? Humble yourselves a little; bend your shoulder and serve the king of Babylon, and dwell in the land of your fathers. Again he rouses them, saying: Do not be afraid before his face, for God is with us to deliver us from his hands. Then he foretells also the affliction coming upon them if they disobey God. For if, he says, you go into Egypt, you will become a desolation, and subject, and a curse, and a reproach. And they in their turn say [b: D] to him: We will not stay in this land, for we will go into Egypt, and we shall see no war, and shall not hear the sound of the trumpet, and shall not hunger for bread. So they went down and served Pharaoh willingly. Then they were carried off by force to the Assyrians, and served them even unwilling.

[1768] [b: A] 6. Set your minds on what is said. Before a man acts out the passion, even if the thoughts march against him, he is still in his own city, and he has God helping him too. So if he humbles himself before God and bears the yoke of affliction and temptation with thanksgiving, and struggles a little, the help of God delivers him. But if he flees the toil and goes down into the self-indulgence of the body, then he is carried off by force and necessity into the land of the Assyrians, and serves them even unwilling. Then, at last, the prophet says to them: Pray for the life of Nebuchadnezzar, for his life is your salvation. [n: Baruch. i, 11, 12.] Nebuchadnezzar is this: for a man not to grow faint at the affliction of the temptation that befalls him, [b: B] nor to kick against it, but to bear it with lowliness, as one who owes the suffering, and to hold that he is not worthy even to be rid of the burden, but rather that the temptation should last and be made firm against him, whether he knows the cause in himself or, for the present, does not, believing that with God nothing is without judgment or unjust. As that brother said, grieving and weeping [sic: *κλέων*] when God took away the temptation: "Lord, am I not worthy to be afflicted a little?" Again, it is written that the disciple of a great elder was warred upon by fornication. And the elder, seeing him toiling, says to him, "Wilt thou have me beseech God, and he will lighten the war from thee?" And he said, "Though I toil, abba, yet I see fruit for myself from the labour. Rather beseech God [b: C] for this, that he give me patience."

7. See: [sic: *Εἶδον*] these are truly the ones who want to be saved; this is bearing the yoke with humility and praying for the life of Nebuchadnezzar. That is why the prophet says: For in his life is your salvation. For the brother to say, "I see fruit for myself from the toil," is like saying, "In his life is my salvation." As the elder too shows him: "Today I know that thou art making progress, and thou surpassest me." For when a man struggles against sin in act, and begins to fight, and against the passionate thoughts in the mind he humbles himself, is crushed, struggles, then through the affliction of the struggles he is cleansed little by little and returns to what is according [b: D] to nature. So, as we said, it is from ignorance and pride that a man is disturbed when troubled by a passion; rather he ought with humility to know his own measures, and to endure, praying, until God works his mercy with him. For if a man is not tempted and does not see the affliction of the passions, he never even struggles to be cleansed. And the psalm says about this: [1769] [b: A] When the sinners sprang up like grass, and all who work peered out, that they may be destroyed for ever and ever. The sinners springing up like grass are the passionate thoughts. For grass is weak and has no strength. So when the passionate thoughts spring up in the soul, then all who work iniquity peer out, that is, show themselves, and these are the passions, that they may be destroyed for ever and ever. For when the passions show themselves to the one who struggles, then they are destroyed by him. [sic: *τοῖς λεγομένοις*]

8. Mark the order of the word. First the passionate thoughts spring up, then the passions peer out, and so they are destroyed. [b: B] All this belongs to those who struggle. But we, doing sin in act and always championing the passions, do not know when the passionate thoughts spring up, nor when the passions peer out, so as to struggle against them; but we are still down in Egypt, at Pharaoh's pitiable brickmaking. And who will grant us even to come to a sense of our bitter slavery, so that we may be humbled and make haste to find mercy? When the sons of Israel were in Egypt serving Pharaoh, they did the brickwork; and those who work at bricks are always bent down, looking at the ground. [sic: *κεκαυμένοι*] So too the soul, if it is mastered by the devil and does sin in act, he tramples down its [b: C] mind, and makes it think nothing spiritual, but always both think and do earthly things. Then they built him, from the bricks they had made, three strong cities: Pithom and Raamses and On, that is, Heliopolis. And these are love of pleasure, love of glory, and love of money, of which all sin is made up. And when God sent Moses to bring them out of Egypt and out of slavery to Pharaoh, he made their works heavier, and said to them: Idlers you are, you are idle; that is why you say, Let us go and serve the Lord our God. [n: Exod. v, 17.] Likewise the devil, when God has already nodded to have mercy on a soul and lighten it of the passions through his Word, or through one of his servants, then he too [b: D] weighs the passions more heavily upon it, and fights it more fiercely.

9. But the Fathers, knowing this, strengthen a man through their teaching and do not let him be afraid. One says: "Hast thou fallen? Get up. And if thou hast fallen again, get up. And again, and so on." Another again says: "The strength of those who want to acquire the virtues is this: that if they fall, they do not lose heart, but take care again." And so each of them, simply, in his own way: this one, thus, gives a hand to those who struggle and are afflicted by the enemy. For they too received it so from the divine Scripture, which says: [1772] [b: A] Shall he who falls not rise? Or he who turns away not turn back? [n: Jerem. viii, 4.] Turn to me, children, and I will heal your bruises, says the Lord; [n: Jerem. iii, 22.] and all such sayings. And when the hand of God weighed heavy on Pharaoh and on his servants, and he was willing to send away the sons of Israel, he says to Moses: Go, serve the Lord your God; only leave your sheep and your oxen behind; [n: Exod. x, 24.] and these signify the thoughts in the mind, which Pharaoh wanted to master, hoping through them to draw the sons of Israel back to himself. And Moses says to him there: No; but thou too shalt give us sacrifices and whole burnt offerings, which we shall offer to the Lord our God, and our cattle shall go with us, and we shall not leave behind [b: B] even a hoof. [n: ibid. 25, 26.] And when Moses brought the sons of Israel out of the land of Egypt, he took them across the Red Sea; and God, wishing to bring them to the seventy palm trunks and the twelve springs of water, first ⟨led⟩ them to Marah, and the people were afflicted, finding nothing to drink [sic: *ποιεῖν*] because the water was bitter; and through Marah he brought them to the place of the seventy palms and the twelve springs of water. So too the soul, when it ceases from sin in act and crosses the sea of the mind, must first toil, struggling and being much afflicted, and so through afflictions enter into the holy rest. For through many afflictions we must enter into the kingdom of heaven. [n: Act. xiv, 21.] For afflictions move [b: C] the mercy of God toward the soul, as the winds move the rain. And just as the rain, coming down for long, rots the shoot if it is still tender, and its fruit is lost, while the winds little by little dry it out and make it firm, so it is with the soul too. Slackness [sic: *ἄνευσις*] and freedom from care and rest make it soft and dissolve it; but temptations brace it and unite it to God, as the prophet says: Lord, in affliction we remembered thee. [n: Isa. xxvi, 16.] So, as we said, we must not be disturbed nor fall into acedia in temptations, but endure and give thanks in afflictions, and beseech God continually in lowliness to work mercy with our weakness and shelter us [b: D] from every temptation, to his glory. Amen.
