⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Instruction XV. ⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *On the holy fasts.*

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 1. ⟦s2⟧ In the law God commanded the sons of Israel to tithe every year all that they acquired; and doing so they were blessed in all their works. ⟦s3⟧ Knowing this, the holy apostles took counsel, for the help and benefit of our souls, to hand this on to us in a greater and loftier way: to tithe the very days of our life and, as it were, to dedicate them to God, so that in this way we too may be blessed in our works, and may each year make atonement for the sins of the whole year. ⟦s4⟧ And having reckoned it up, they hallowed for us, out of the [b: C] three hundred and sixty-five days of the year, these seven weeks of the fasts; for so they set apart seven weeks. ⟦s5⟧ But the Fathers in time saw fit to add to them one week more, partly to train beforehand and, as it were, smooth the way for those who are about to enter on the toil of the fasts, and partly also honouring the fasts with the number of the holy Forty Days which our Lord fasted. ⟦s6⟧ For eight [sic: *ἑπτὰ*] weeks, taking away the Saturdays and the Sundays, make forty days, the fast of holy Saturday being honoured on its own, because it is the most sacred and the only fast among all the Saturdays of the year. ⟦s7⟧ But the seven weeks, [b: D] without the Saturdays and the Sundays, make thirty-five days; then, adding the Great Saturday and half of the bright and light-bringing night, they are thirty-six and a half days; which is the tenth of the three hundred and sixty-five days of the year, with great exactness. ⟦s8⟧ For of three hundred the tenth is thirty, and of sixty the tenth is six, and of five, the half. ⟦s9⟧ See: thirty-six and a half days, as we said. ⟦s9⟧ This is the tithe, as one [1789] [b: A] might say, of the whole year, which the holy apostles hallowed [sic: *ὑγείασαν*] for us for repentance, being a cleansing of the sins, as I said, of the whole year. ⟦s10+11⟧ So whoever keeps himself well and as he ought in these holy days is blessed, brethren. ⟦s10+11⟧ For even if it happened that he, being a man, sinned, whether from weakness or from negligence: see, God has given these holy days, so that if a man is zealous with watchfulness and humility to take care of himself and to repent in them, he is cleansed of the sins of the whole year; and then his soul has rest from the burden, and so he comes purely to the holy day of the Resurrection, and partakes without condemnation of the holy mysteries, made a new man through the repentance of these holy fasts; and he abides in spiritual joy and gladness, keeping feast with God the whole of holy Pentecost. ⟦s12⟧ For Pentecost is the resurrection of the soul, as it says; and this is also the symbol of it, that we do not bend the knee in the holy church the whole of Pentecost.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ 2. [b: B] So each one who wants to be cleansed from his sins of the whole year through these days must first guard himself from indifference about foods. ⟦s3⟧ For indifference about foods, as the Fathers say, begets every evil in a man. ⟦s4⟧ Then he must likewise take care not to break the fast without great necessity, not to go after pleasant foods, not to weigh himself down with a surfeit of foods or of drinks. ⟦s5⟧ For there are two kinds of gluttony. ⟦s6⟧ Sometimes a man is warred on in the matter of sweetness, and does not always want to eat much, but wants what he eats to be pleasant. ⟦s7⟧ And it happens that such a man eats a food that pleases him, and is so overcome by his pleasure in it that he keeps that food in his mouth, chewing it a good [b: C] while, and has not the heart to swallow it because of the pleasure. ⟦s8⟧ This is called *laimargia*, throat-madness. ⟦s9⟧ Another is warred on in the matter of surfeit, and does not want good foods, nor does he care about sweetness; but whether they are good or bad, he wants only to eat, whatever they may be; he cares about nothing but filling his belly. ⟦s10⟧ This is called *gastrimargia*, belly-madness. ⟦s11⟧ And I will tell you the reason for these names as well. ⟦s11⟧ Among the outsiders, *margainein* is said for being mad, and the madman is called *margos*. ⟦s12⟧ So when that disease and madness comes upon a man for filling the belly, it is called *gastrimargia*, from *margainein*, that is, to be mad in the belly. ⟦s13⟧ When the pleasure is about the throat [b: D] alone, it is called *laimargia*, from being mad in the throat. ⟦s14⟧ These things, then, he who wants to be cleansed from his sins must guard against and flee with all watchfulness. ⟦s15+16+17+18⟧ For they are not according to the body's need but according to passion; and if he puts up with them, they become sin for him. ⟦s15+16+17+18⟧ It is as with living lawfully in marriage and committing fornication: the act is the same, but it is the aim that makes the difference in the [1792] [b: A] matter. ⟦s15+16+17+18⟧ For the one comes together for the begetting of children, the other to satisfy his own love of pleasure. ⟦s15+16+17+18⟧ So too one can find with eating: eating out of need and eating for pleasure are one and the same act, but it is the aim that makes the sin. ⟦s15+16+17+18⟧ Eating out of need is when a man fixes for himself how much to take in the day, and watches whether what he fixed weighs on him, and he needs to take off a little from it, and he takes it off; or whether it did not weigh on him, but his body was not sustained by it, and he needs to add a little more, and he adds a little; and so he tests his need well, and then keeps to what he has fixed, and not for pleasure but with the aim of keeping up the strength of his body. ⟦s15+16+17+18⟧ And what he does take, he should take with prayer, and condemn himself [b: B] in his thought as unworthy of any comfort whatever; and not look to it if some, for some need, as is likely, or necessity, are given care, so that he too does not seek rest, or think that resting is a light thing for the soul.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 3. ⟦s2⟧ Once when I was in the coenobium, I went to visit one of the elders (for there were many great elders there), and I find the brother who served him eating with him, and I say to him privately: "Dost thou know, brother, that these elders whom thou seest eating and having a little care are like men who got a purse, and kept working and putting into that purse until they filled it; and after [b: C] sealing it, they worked again and gathered for themselves another thousand coins or so, so that in time of need they might have something to draw on and keep what was in the purse safe. ⟦s3⟧ So these too kept working and treasuring up for themselves, and after sealing [sic: *σφαγίσαι*] their treasures, they worked a little more, and they have it for the time of sickness or of old age, so as to draw on it, while they keep the rest treasured up. ⟦s4⟧ But we, who have not even got the purse, what do we spend from?" ⟦s5⟧ That is why, as I said, even if we take something for need, we ought to condemn ourselves and reckon ourselves unworthy of every kind of care, and [b: D] even of the monastic life itself, and not take it with an easy mind. ⟦s6⟧ And so it does not become condemnation for us. ⟦s7⟧ And so much for self-control of the belly.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 4. ⟦s2⟧ But we need not only to keep our diet, but also to abstain from every other sin, so that as we fast with the belly, so we may fast with the tongue too, abstaining from speaking ill, from lying, from idle talk, from reviling, from wrath, in a word from every sin done through the tongue; likewise to fast with the eyes: not to look at vain things, not to be bold with the eyes, not to stare at anyone shamelessly. ⟦s3⟧ Likewise to hold back the hands and the feet from every evil [1793] [b: A] deed. ⟦s3⟧ And so fasting, as saint Basil says, an acceptable fast, abstaining from every evil worked through all our senses, let us come to the holy day of the Resurrection, as we have already said, new and pure and worthy of partaking of the holy mysteries; having first gone out to meet our Lord, and received him with palms and olive branches, sitting on a colt and entering the holy city. ⟦s4⟧ What does his sitting on a colt mean? ⟦s5⟧ He sat on a colt so that, as the Word of God, [sic: *λόγον*] he might turn back the soul that had become irrational, says the prophet, and been made like the senseless beasts, and subject it to his own divinity. ⟦s6⟧ And what is meeting him with palms and olive branches? ⟦s7⟧ When a man goes out to war [b: B] against the adversary and comes back victorious, each of his own people meets him with palms, as a victor. ⟦s8⟧ For the palm is the symbol of victory. ⟦s9⟧ Again, when a man is wronged by someone and wants to go to one who is able to give him justice, he carries olive branches, crying out and asking to be shown mercy and to get help; for olives are the symbol of mercy. ⟦s10⟧ That is why we too meet our Master Christ with palms, as a victor, for he conquered the enemy for us; and with olive branches, asking mercy from him, so that we too may conquer through him, asking, and may be found carrying his prizes of victory, not only for the victory which he won for us, but also for that which we too [b: C] won through him, by the prayers of all the saints. ⟦s11⟧ Amen.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Instruction XVI. ⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *To some cell-dwellers who asked him about visiting.*

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 1. ⟦s2⟧ The Fathers say that sitting in the cell is half, and visiting elders is half. ⟦s3⟧ The saying means that both in the cell and outside the cell watchfulness is needed alike, and a man's knowing why he ought to keep stillness by himself, and why he ought to visit Fathers and brethren. ⟦s4+5⟧ For if a man is watchful over this aim, [b: D] he is zealous to do as the Fathers said. ⟦s4+5⟧ When he sits in the cell, he prays, meditates, does a little handiwork, takes care of his thoughts as far as he can; and when again he goes visiting at any time, he considers and looks at his state, whether he is helped by meeting the brethren or not, and whether he can go back to his cell unharmed; [sic: *ἀνακάμψει*] and if he sees himself harmed in anything, he learns [sic: *μανθάνειν*] his own weakness; he learns that he has as yet acquired nothing from stillness, and goes back humbled to his own cell, weeping, repenting, entreating God for his weakness; and so then he sits attending to himself. ⟦s6+7⟧ Then again he comes among men, and sees whether he has remained overcome [1796] [b: A] in the same things as before, or in others; and so again he goes back to his cell, doing the same things likewise, weeping, repenting, beseeching God about his state. ⟦s8⟧ For the cell lifts up, and men test. ⟦s9⟧ So the Fathers said well that sitting in the cell is half, and visiting elders is half.
