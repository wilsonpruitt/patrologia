[1804] 3. And being in subjection, never trust thy heart; for it is blinded by its old attachments. And do not be guided in anything by thine own judgment, and do not settle anything with thyself without asking and counsel; and do not reckon or think thine own views more reasonable and more just than those of him who directs thee, nor become an examiner of his works, and, led astray, often put him to the test. For this is a deceit of the evil one, to hinder subjection in all things with faith, and the safe salvation that goes with it; and so thou art subject with rest, and walkest the road of the Fathers without danger and without straying. Force thyself in everything, and cut off thy will; and by the grace of Christ, having come through custom into the habit of cutting it off, thou dost it from then on without forcing and without affliction, so that [b: B] it falls out that thine is always done as thou wilt; rather, thou willest as it is done, and art at peace with all. These things, however, hold in matters where there is no transgression of a commandment of God or of the Fathers. Strive to find in everything how to accuse thyself, and hold fast, with knowledge, to counting thyself for nothing. And believe that our affairs, even down to the most trifling, are under the providence of God, and thou wilt bear without agitation whatever comes upon thee. Believe that dishonours and insults are healing medicines for the pride of thy soul, and pray for those who revile thee as for true physicians; being persuaded that he who hates dishonour hates lowliness, and he who flees those who provoke him flees meekness. Do not wish to know thy neighbour's wickedness, and do not admit suspicions against him; [b: C] and if they are sown through our wickedness, strive to change them into thinking well. And in everything give thanks, and acquire [sic: *κατὰ*] goodness and holy love. And before all things let us all keep our conscience in everything: in things towards God and towards our neighbour, and in material things. And before saying or doing anything, let us examine whether it is according to the will of God; and so, having prayed, let us speak or act, and cast our weakness down before God; and his goodness will go with us in all things.

[b: D] Instruction XVIII. *On the one who has the service of the cellar.*

Dost thou wish not to fall into anger and rancour? Have no attachment at all to material things, and lay claim to no vessel of any kind; nor again despise it as the least of things; but if anyone wants something from thee, give it; and if through disorderliness or contempt it is [1805] [b: A] broken, or lost, do not be anxious. Thou oughtest to do this not as one who despises the vessels of the monastery (for thou art bound to take care of them with all thy strength and all diligence), but as one who wishes to keep himself free of agitation and turmoil, always showing God what is in thy power. And thou wilt be able to achieve this if thou dost not treat [sic: *οἰκῇς*] the things as thine own, but as dedicated to God, and thyself as entrusted only with the care of them. For the one prepares thee not to be attached, as I said, and the other not to despise. But if thou hast not this aim, be assured, thou wilt never cease being agitated and agitating others.

Question.

[b: B] Since my thought rejoices in these words and wants to be so, why am I not found ready at the hour of the deed?

Answer.

Because thou dost not meditate on them always. If thou wilt have them at the right time, meditate on them always, and be in them; and I trust in God that thou wilt make progress. Join prayer with meditation. Tend the sick: first, so that thou mayest acquire compassion from it, as I have often said; and then, so that when thou too fallest sick at some time, God may raise up one to tend thee. For "with what measure," it says, "ye mete, it shall be measured to you again." If thou art eager to do a thing with conscience, as far as thy strength goes, thou [b: C] oughtest to know and assure thyself that thou dost not yet know the true way; and thou oughtest to receive it without agitation, without affliction and with joy, when thou hearest that thou hast slipped in the thing thou thoughtest to do with conscience. For by the judgment of thy companions either what is lacking is surely set right, or what is done well is made safer. Strive to make progress, so that if affliction befalls thee, either in body or in spirit, thou mayest be able to bear it without being afflicted, without heaviness, and with patience. If thou hearest that thou didst a thing which thou didst not do, do not be troubled at all, nor burn; but at once make a prostration to him who told thee, saying to him with lowliness, "Forgive me, and pray for me," and keep silence after that, as the Fathers said. But if thou art asked by [b: D] him whether the thing is true or not, then make a prostration with lowliness, [sic: *τοπεινώσεως*] telling with truth how the thing was. And after telling it, make a prostration again with lowliness, saying, "Forgive me, and pray for me." [sic: *μένοι*]

Question.

What shall I do, since I do not have the same state when I meet the brethren?

Answer.

The same state when meeting the [1808] [b: A] brethren thou canst not yet have; but meanwhile strive not to be scandalized in anything, nor to condemn anyone, nor to speak ill of anyone, nor to pay attention to a word or deed or movement of the brethren that does not profit thee; but rather strive in everything to build thyself up, and do not wish to show off in word or deed and be vainglorious. Acquire freedom in thy diet and in thy speech, down to the slightest thing. Know that if anyone is warred on, or afflicted by some passionate thought, and acts it out, [sic: *ἐξαγορεύῃ*] he strengthens it against himself, giving it, as it were, power to war on him and afflict him the more. But if again he struggles, and opposes his thought, and does the things contrary to it, as we have often said, the passion grows weak and becomes powerless [b: B] to war on him and afflict him; and so, struggling little by little and helped by God, he overcomes even the passion itself. God will shelter us through the prayers of all the saints. Amen.

[b: C] Instruction XIX. *Various sayings of the same, in brief.*

Abba Dorotheus used to say that it is impossible for one who has his own understanding, or his own thought, to submit and to follow his neighbour's good.

He said again that, being subject to passion, we ought not to trust our own heart at all; for a crooked rule makes even straight things crooked.

He said again that it is no great thing not to judge, or even to have compassion on one who is in affliction and who submits to thee; but the great thing is not to judge, or trample back on, the one who contradicts thee through his own passion, nor to join in agreement with one who has judged him; and to rejoice with one who is preferred [b: D] before thee.

He said again that he who does not despise all material things, and glory, and bodily rest, and moreover his self-justifications too, cannot cut off his [1809] [b: A] wills, nor is he freed from wrath and grief, nor does he give his neighbour rest.

Again he said: Do not demand love from thy neighbour. For he who demands it is agitated if it does not come back to him; but rather do thou show love to thy neighbour, and give him rest, [sic: *ἀναπαύει*] and so thou bringest thy neighbour too to love.

Again he said that if anyone does a thing according to God, temptation is sure to come to him; for every good thing is either preceded or followed by temptation; nor is what is done according to God made firm unless it is tested by temptation.

Again he said: Nothing makes for union so much as rejoicing in the same things and thinking the same things.

Again he said: Not to despise one's neighbour's kindness [b: B] belongs to humility; for one must receive it with thanksgiving, even if it is small and the least.

And again he said: I, if a matter befalls me, would gladly have it done by my neighbour's counsel, and fare badly by his counsel if so it turns out, rather than be guided by my own counsel and fare well.

Again he said: It is good in every matter to practise for ourselves a little short of our need. For it does not profit anyone to take his rest in everything.

Again he said: In every matter that has befallen me I have never wanted to get round it for myself by human prudence; but I always do the little that is in my power, in whatever it may be, and leave the whole to God.

Again he said: He who has no will of his own always [b: C] does his own; for he has nothing of his own besides. Whatever happens, it gives him rest, and he is found always doing his own. For he does not want things to happen as he wills, but wills them as they happen.

Again he said: One ought not, at the very time when the brother sins, to correct him then and there, [sic: *κατ᾿ αὐτοῦ*] nor yet at another time for the sake of one's own vindication.

He said again that love according to God is stronger even than natural love.

He said again: No evil, not even in jest. It happens that at first a man does evil with laughter, but afterwards, even against his will, he becomes fixed in it.

[b: D] He said again that one must not want to be freed from a passion as wanting to escape its affliction, but as hating it thoroughly, as it says: "I hated them with a perfect hatred; they became enemies to me."

He said again that it is impossible for anyone to be angry against his neighbour unless first his heart is lifted up against him, and he despises him, and holds himself superior to him.

He said again: It is a sign that a man acts out a passion willingly when, being reproved or corrected about it, he is agitated. But to bear without agitation the [1812] [b: A] reproof about it, that is, correction, is a sign that he acted it out being overcome, or in ignorance.

Instruction XX. *A letter of the saint to a brother who asked him about insensibility of soul and about the cooling of love.*

1. For insensibility of soul, brother, continual reading of the divine Scriptures helps, with the compunctive sayings of the God-bearing Fathers; and the remembrance of the fearful judgments of God, and of the ⟨going forth⟩ of the soul from the body, and of the fearful powers that are to meet it, with whom it wrought wickedness in this brief and pitiable life. And further, of being about to stand before the dread and [b: B] impartial judgment seat of Christ, and to be required to give account not only for deeds but also for words and thoughts, before God and all his angels and, in a word, the whole creation. Remember continually also that sentence which the fearful and just Judge will speak to those on the left: "Depart from me, ye cursed, into the everlasting fire, prepared for the devil and his angels." It is good also to remember the great afflictions of men; for hardly so is the hard and insensible soul softened, and comes to a sense of its own evil state. And thy weakness in love for the brethren comes to thee from thy accepting thoughts of suspicion [sic: *ὑποψυχίας*] and trusting thine own heart, and [b: C] from wanting to suffer nothing of thine own purpose. Dost thou wish, then, to be helped by God? [sic: *βοηθούμενος*] First of all, do not trust thine own suspicions at all, and strive with all thy strength to humble thyself to the brethren, cutting off thine own will for them. If one of them insults thee or afflicts thee in some other way, pray for him, as the Fathers said, as for one who does thee great benefits, as for the physician of thy love of pleasure. From this thine anger is lessened, since according to the holy Fathers love is a bridle of anger. And before all, beseech God to give thee watchfulness and understanding to know what is his good and acceptable and perfect will, and further [b: D] strength to be made perfect for every good work.
