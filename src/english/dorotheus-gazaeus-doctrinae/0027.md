⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Instruction XXI.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Question 1.

⟦s2⟧ How shall I be able to cut out boldness, and master my tongue?

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1813] [b: A] Answer.

⟦s2⟧ By mourning.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ Question 2.

⟦s1+2⟧ And how can I keep mourning while going in and out among men and doing services? ⟦s1+2⟧ And is there mourning without tears?

⟦s1+2⟧ Answer.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ Mourning is not through tears, but tears are through mourning. ⟦s3⟧ He who is among men, if he cuts off his own will, and does not pay attention to men's faults, acquires mourning. ⟦s4⟧ For from this his thoughts are gathered together, [sic: *συνάγοντες*] [b: B] and being gathered they beget godly grief in the heart, and the grief, tears.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Question 3.

⟦s2⟧ What does bearing dishonour contribute to stillness?

⟦s2⟧ Answer.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ A man's debtor, if he does not pay the debt, is a debtor wherever he goes, and has no freedom to sit anywhere at rest. ⟦s3⟧ But if first he is wearied by men's reproaches, he is ashamed, and then pays the debt. ⟦s4⟧ And when he is freed, then with confidence and much boldness he can [b: C] go about in public, and sit wherever he wants. ⟦s5⟧ So too a man, if he does what is in his power to bear insults, dishonours, losses, for the sins he has committed, learns lowliness and toil, and through these his sins are forgiven, as it is written: "See my lowliness and my toil," and the rest. [n: Psal. xxiv, 18.] And consider how many insults and reproaches our Master Christ endured before the cross, [sic: *πρὸς τοῦ σταυροῦ*] and after these he came to the cross. ⟦s6+7⟧ So no one can come into perfect, fruitful stillness and into the holy repose of perfection unless first he suffers with Christ and bears all his sufferings, remembering the Apostle who says, "If we suffer with him, we are also glorified with [b: D] him." [n: Rom. viii, 17.] So do not be led astray; for there is no other way of salvation but this. ⟦s8⟧ May the Lord go with thee according to his wills, so that he may well lay the foundation of thy building upon the rock, which is Christ. [n: I Cor. x, 4.]

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ Question 4.

⟦s1+2⟧ Which way saves more, the one through toils or the one through lowliness? ⟦s1+2⟧ Tell me, master; and about forgetfulness, and close ties with certain people, and the guarding of the eyes.

⟦s1+2⟧ Answer.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ True toil is not apart from lowliness. [1816] [b: A] For toil by itself is in vain, and is counted for nothing. ⟦s3⟧ For it says, "See my lowliness and my toil, and forgive all my sins." ⟦s4⟧ So he who has this arrives quickly; and he who has lowliness with contempt, [sic: *ταπείνωσίν μου*] he too has the like. ⟦s5⟧ Contempt holds the place of toil. ⟦s5⟧ But he who has lowliness by itself enters indeed, but more slowly. ⟦s6⟧ And if anyone wants to hold fast true lowliness, let him not count himself for anything in any matter at all; and this is true lowliness. ⟦s7+8+9+10⟧ He who receives the fire which the Lord came to cast upon the earth [n: Luc. xii, 49.] does not receive forgetfulness and captivity, since he always has the sense of the fire. ⟦s7+8+9+10⟧ Take then an example. ⟦s7+8+9+10⟧ If a man is found at the point of death, and fire comes near him, at once he feels it from the [b: B] pain; and into whatever matter a man is taken captive, let a coal of fire fall on him. [?] the fire does not stay in the captivity, brother, but is quenched; for then it is no longer even fire. ⟦s7+8+9+10⟧ If thou wouldst be freed from captivity too, thou canst not otherwise, unless thou acquire for thyself this fatherly fire; for by its heat those things vanish. ⟦s7+8+9+10⟧ And one acquires [sic: *κτᾶσαι*] this fire by longing according to God. ⟦s11⟧ Not to have much friendship with someone of thine own age is good; for such custom does not let mourning come. ⟦s12⟧ Nor have friendship with anyone else who takes mourning away from thee; for it does not profit thee, but rather even harms. ⟦s13⟧ No one can acquire any good thing except in much toil. ⟦s14⟧ So train [b: C] thine eyes not to look at anyone, and they do not fill thy heart with dreadful boldness, which brings the monk's toils utterly to ruin. ⟦s15⟧ All these things, brother, unless the heart does them, seeking the Lord night and day, a man cannot make progress. ⟦s16+17⟧ If thou art at leisure for these things, thou comest to them; for it says, "Be at leisure," and the rest. [n: Psal. XLV, 11.] The Lord grant thee to understand these things.

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Question.

⟦s2+3+4⟧ What is contempt compared with lowliness? ⟦s2+3+4⟧ And what again is contrition of heart? ⟦s2+3+4⟧ And if one acquires [sic: *κτᾶσαί*] contempt, humbling himself in his heart, is there need also of the reproaches and insults from men outside, and of meaner [b: D] deeds? ⟦s2+3+4⟧ Further, ought the humble-minded to practise lowly things, or also to use lowly speech?

⟦s2+3+4⟧ Answer.

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ Contempt has two modes: one from the heart, and the other brought upon one by men. ⟦s3⟧ The contempt from men is greater than that from the heart. ⟦s4⟧ For that from the heart has not so much toil as that from men. ⟦s5⟧ For the heart labours greatly at the contempt brought [1817] [b: A] upon it from outside. ⟦s5⟧ But to run of oneself to mean deeds does not bring one to progress, but perhaps rather to vainglory; whereas to be commanded and not to contradict, but to do it with obedience, this makes for progress, and for acquiring contempt. ⟦s6⟧ Contrition of heart is not letting it wander with alien thoughts. ⟦s7⟧ Lowliness is not counting oneself for anything in any matter at all, and cutting off one's own will in everything; so that to be subject to all, and to bear without agitation what comes from others, this is true lowliness, in which vainglory finds no place.

⟦s8⟧ But using lowly speech brings vainglory, and is rather harmful than profitable; it is enough to say, "Forgive, [b: B] and pray for me."

⟦s8⟧ Question 6.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ Should one cut off the will in good things, or in indifferent things, or even in those where transgression of a commandment comes in? ⟦s3⟧ And if a command laid on me turns out to be beyond my state, ought I to decline it, so as not to bring on grief and agitation later? ⟦s4⟧ And further, if someone turns out to have need of the abba, to ask him something, and tells me to help him in the matter, should I consent, or not, [sic: *εἰ μὴ φέρει*] since it brings me honour, in that I count myself something at all? ⟦s5⟧ And further, if someone asks me to make a prayer, shall I be willing to set my hand to it and help someone? ⟦s6⟧ How far must one decline, or contend, where there is transgression of a [b: C] commandment?

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ Answer.

⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ Brother, he who wishes to be [?] ought not to have a will at all in anything; and teaching this the Lord said, "I came into the world not to do my own will," and the rest. [n: Joan. vi, 38.] For he who wants to do this and that, the things for which he begs off, either shows himself more discerning than the one who commands him, or is mocked by demons; both of which are evil and devilish. ⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ So thou oughtest to obey in everything. ⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ For thy abba who commands thee bears thy judgment, as one who will be required to give account for thee. ⟦s5⟧ If what is commanded seems heavy to thee, ask him, and leave the matter to his discernment. ⟦s6⟧ But if it is brethren [b: D] who have given the command, and thou seest or thinkest that the matter has harm in it, or is beyond thy strength, again ask the abba, and what he tells thee, do; for if thou wilt judge not only the matters but also the men, thou wilt draw affliction on thyself. ⟦s7⟧ But where the matter seems to be good, there show obedience to the brethren; and where the thought hesitates, or it is beyond thy strength, or has harm, lay it before thy abba, and what he discerns, do. ⟦s8⟧ For he knows what he is doing, and how he cares for thy [1820] [b: A] soul; and be at rest. ⟦s9⟧ Believe then that whatever he says to thee is according to God; and everything according to God is profitable, and brings no grief or agitation. ⟦s10+11+12+13⟧ For from good no evil comes forth: "For every good tree brings forth good fruits." [n: Matth. vii. 17.] And as for someone's asking thy help with the abba, do it as fulfilling a commandment which thou hast heard from the abba to do. ⟦s10+11+12+13⟧ For if he sets thee as door-keeper, saying, "Tell me of every man who comes," dost thou count thyself something of thyself, or fulfil an order? ⟦s10+11+12+13⟧ If the abba says to tell him, or not to tell, thou hast nothing to do with it. ⟦s14⟧ And as for when thou goest into a brother's cell, or he himself tells thee to pray: up to the third time say, "Forgive," and if after the third he persists, then do it with lowliness; and do thou too say it to someone three times, and if he does not want [b: B] to, stop in peace, and do not afflict him. ⟦s15⟧ And this is the true way of God, in which take heed to thyself to work without hypocrisy, in a pure heart, God giving thee his hand and helping thee by his grace.

⟦s15⟧ Question 7.

⟦p14⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ Ought one to condescend to the body in the matter of diet when one has not yet entirely broken down? ⟦s3⟧ And if someone is in poor habit with regard to the usual food; and if someone, suffering nothing, declines this food as producing this passion, does he do well?

⟦p15⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Answer.

⟦s2⟧ If a man sees himself not troubled by [b: C] passion, nor warred on, he ought to condescend a little to the body; but if he is warred on, no. ⟦s3⟧ For it is plain that this apparent weakness too is from the demons. ⟦s4⟧ For in true weakness, the kind that is from God, the warfare too slackens. ⟦s5⟧ So, where there is warfare, it is profitable to wear the body down, even if it falls into sickness; for it brings the soul out. ⟦s6⟧ But if the cause of the irregularity is plain, and if perhaps from a journey, or from severe wounds, the body is sick, one ought to condescend, but with gravity and not beyond what is needed; since the demons too are busy at their own. ⟦s7+8⟧ As for poor habit, there are times when one can compel the thought a little; but if one cannot, one condescends a little. ⟦s9⟧ For this too is a weakness. ⟦s10⟧ But if anyone [b: D] learns that this food produces passion, he ought not to eat of it; for it is found to be a defeat for him, and he ought to be on his guard. ⟦s11⟧ Strive not to cast this into forgetfulness, and believe that with those who are helped and saved by God thou art being saved.

⟦s11⟧ Question 8.

⟦p16⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ If, eating with the brethren, I have taken what I need sooner, is it good that I rise, or should I stay until all rise? ⟦s3⟧ And if I measure out the portion of my bread over all the relishes, do I do better?

⟦p17⟧ ⟦s1+s2⟧ Answer.

⟦s1+s2⟧ Sitting at table, if thou seest that thy need [1821] [b: A] is met, and thou canst not sit without eating, rise. But to sit and not eat is greater than that. And to make thy morsel stretch [sic: *ἀπεκτεῖναι*] over all the cooked dishes is better than both.

⟦s1+s2⟧ Question 9.

⟦p18⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ What shall I do, since I fear the shame of dishonour; and if I fall into conversation with certain people, I am drawn into it greatly and taken captive, so as almost to forget myself; and always too I am, as it were, ashamed to leave them?

⟦p19⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Answer.

⟦s2⟧ If it tends to edification, and there is no cutting off of a more necessary matter, stay while it is being [b: B] discussed; but if it is unprofitable, say, "Forgive; I am still weak." ⟦s3⟧ And as for shame, consider the shame before all the people which will be in the Lord's presence for sinners, and count the temporary one as nothing.
