⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 6. ⟦s2⟧ Only let us take care, brethren, and guard [1829] [b: A] ourselves from sin in act. ⟦s3+4⟧ For I have told you often before that every sin in act puts us under his hand again, since of our own will we cast ourselves down and enslave ourselves. [sic: *καταδηλοῦμεν*] For is it not a shame and great wretchedness, after he has redeemed us from Hades by his own blood, and after we hear these things, if we take ourselves off into Hades again? ⟦s5⟧ Are we not worthy of a still worse and more pitiable punishment? ⟦s6⟧ May God, who loves mankind, have mercy on us, and give us watchfulness to understand and to help ourselves, so that we may find a little mercy.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Instruction XXIII. ⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *An interpretation of the sayings of St Gregory [b: B] that are sung for the holy martyrs.*

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 1. ⟦s2⟧ It is good to sing from the words of the holy God-bearing fathers for this reason: that everywhere they are always eager to teach us all that tends to the enlightening of our souls; and in them it is set before us always to learn, from the very words fitted to the day, the power of the commemoration being kept, whether it is a feast of the Lord, or of holy martyrs, or simply of fathers, whatever holy and notable day it may be. ⟦s3⟧ So we too ought to sing with watchfulness, and to set our mind on the power of the words of the saints; so that not only the mouth, as it says in the Gerontikon, but our heart too may sing with the mouth. ⟦s4⟧ Now we learned from the former psalm a few things, according to our power, about the [b: C] holy Pascha; let us see again what St Gregory wants to teach us about the holy martyrs too. ⟦s5⟧ For the psalm about them which we said just now, from his words, says: *Living victims, rational whole burnt offerings*, and so on. ⟦s6⟧ What is *living victims*? ⟦s7⟧ A victim is anything dedicated for sacrifice to God: suppose a sheep, or an ox, or anything of the kind. ⟦s8⟧ Why then does he say of the holy martyrs, *living victims*? ⟦s9⟧ Because the sheep that is brought for sacrifice is first slaughtered and dies; and then it is divided and cut in pieces for God. ⟦s10⟧ But the holy martyrs were cut in their flesh while living, being scraped, tortured, dismembered. ⟦s11⟧ For sometimes the executioners cut off their hands too, and feet and tongues, and [b: D] gouged out their eyes, and besides this [sic: *ἐπὶ τὸ τούτων*] scraped their sides, so that the very structure and arrangement of their inward parts could be seen. ⟦s12⟧ All this, as I said, the saints endured while living, still having their souls; and that is why they are called *living victims*. ⟦s13+14⟧ And *rational whole burnt offerings*: why? ⟦s15⟧ Because a sacrifice is one thing ⟨and a whole burnt offering another⟩. ⟦s15⟧ Sometimes they do not offer the whole sheep, but only its first-fruits, as it says in the law: *The right arm, and the lobe of the liver, and the two kidneys*, [n: Levit. iii, 3, 4.] and things like these; those who offered these were bringing sacrifices; they were offering, as it were, the first-fruits; and this is [1832] [b: A] called a sacrifice. ⟦s16⟧ But it is a whole burnt offering when they burn the whole sheep or the ox, or whatever is offered, all of it, as it says there: *The head with the feet and the entrails*; [n: Levit. iv, 11.] and sometimes the hide too and the dung, and, in a word, they burn up the whole of it entirely. ⟦s17⟧ This is called a whole burning. ⟦s18⟧ In this way the sons of Israel performed both the sacrifices and the whole burnings according to the law.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 2. ⟦s2⟧ Now those sacrifices and whole burnings were symbols of the souls that wish to be saved and to offer themselves to God. ⟦s3⟧ And I tell you a few things about these too, from what the Fathers said, so that when you read them you may lift up your understanding a little, and your soul [sic: *ἡμῶν*] may be fattened. ⟦s4⟧ The arm, they say, is the practical life; and the hands [b: B] are taken to mean this, as we have also said on various occasions. ⟦s5+6⟧ The arm is the strength of the hand. ⟦s5+6⟧ So they offered the strength of the right hand, that is, of good works; for they take the right to mean the good; and all the other things that we said, such as the lobe of the liver, and the two kidneys, and the fat from them, and the loins, and the fat on the thighs, and the heart, and the breast, and all such things. ⟦s7+8+9+10⟧ These too are likewise symbols. ⟦s7+8+9+10⟧ For all these things, as the Apostle says, happened to them as types; and they were written for our admonition. [n: I Cor. x, 11.] And I tell you how the soul, as St Gregory says, is threefold. ⟦s7+8+9+10⟧ For it has the desiring part, and the irascible part, and the rational part. ⟦s11⟧ So they offered the lobe [b: C] of the liver. ⟦s12⟧ The Fathers take the liver to mean desire; and the lobe of the liver is its tip. ⟦s13⟧ So they offered, in symbol, the tip of the desiring part, that is, its first-fruits, its finest and more precious part; which signifies loving nothing before God, preferring nothing out of all things desired to the desire for God. ⟦s14⟧ For we said that they offered him the more precious part; and the kidneys too, and the fat from them, and the loins, and the fat on the thighs, signify the same by a certain analogy; for there, they say, desire is. ⟦s15⟧ See, these are the symbols of the desiring part. ⟦s16⟧ And the symbol of the irascible part is the heart; for there, they say, anger is; and St [b: D] Basil signifies this too, saying: "Anger is a boiling of the blood around the heart." ⟦s16⟧ And the breast is the symbol of the rational part; for they take the chest to mean this. ⟦s17⟧ That is why they say that Moses, when clothing Aaron in the high-priestly vestment, [sic: *ἄνθυμα*] put the oracle on his breast, according to God's command. ⟦s18⟧ So all these things, as we said, are symbols of the soul which through the practical life, with God, cleanses itself and returns [sic: *ἐπανεχομένης*] to what is according to nature. ⟦s19⟧ For Evagrius says that a rational soul acts according to nature when its desiring part longs for virtue, its irascible part fights for it, and its rational part applies itself to the contemplation of the things that have come to be.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ 3. [1833] [b: A] So when they offered a sheep, or an ox, or any other such thing for sacrifices, the sons of Israel took these parts from what was offered and laid them on the altar before the Lord, and it is called a sacrifice. ⟦s3+4⟧ But it is a whole burnt offering when they offered the victim whole and entire, and burned it up just as it was, sound, complete, perfect; as we said above too, this is the symbol of the perfect; this belongs to those who say: *See, we have left everything and followed thee.* [n: Matth. xix, 27.] To this measure the Lord urged that man to come who said to him, *All these I have kept from my youth.* ⟦s5⟧ For he answered him, saying: "*One thing thou lackest.*" ⟦s6⟧ What? ⟦s7+8⟧ This: *Take up thy cross, and come, follow me.* [n: ibid. 20, 21.] So the holy martyrs in this way [b: B] offered themselves whole to God, and not only themselves, but also what was theirs and what was about them. ⟦s9⟧ For we are one thing, as St Basil says, and what is ours is another, and what is about us another; I have told you this on other occasions too. ⟦s10⟧ We are the mind and the soul; ours is the body; and what is about us is possessions and the rest of material things. ⟦s11+12⟧ So the saints offered themselves to God with all their heart, all their soul, all their strength, as it is written: *Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind.* [n: Matth. xxii, 37; Marc. xii, 30; Luc. x, 27.] For they despised not only children and wives and glory and money and all the rest of their property, but even their own bodies; and that is why they are called whole burnt offerings. ⟦s13⟧ And rational, since man is a rational [b: C] living being, and they are perfect victims to God. ⟦s14+15⟧ Then what follows: *Sheep that know God and are known by God.* ⟦s16⟧ How *knowing God*? ⟦s17⟧ As the Lord himself showed and taught, saying: *My sheep hear my voice, and I know mine, and am known by mine.* ⟦s18⟧ Why did he say, *My sheep hear my voice*? ⟦s19⟧ Instead of saying: They obey my word and keep my commandments, and so they know me. ⟦s19⟧ For through keeping the commandments the saints draw near to God, and the more they draw near to him, the more they know him and are known by him. ⟦s20⟧ Since God knows all things, the hidden things too, and the deep things, and the things that are not, why then does he say of the saints, *known by God*? ⟦s21⟧ Because, as I said, [b: D] drawing near to him through the commandments, they know him and are known by him. ⟦s21⟧ For when someone turns away and puts himself far from another, to that extent he is said not to know ⟨him and not to be known by him; and⟩ drawing near, he is likewise said to know and to be known. ⟦s22+23+24⟧ In this sense, then, God too is said not to know sinners, in so far as they put themselves far from him. ⟦s22+23+24⟧ That is why the Lord himself says to such: *Amen I say to you, I do not know you.* [n: Matth. xxv, 12.] So the saints, as I have often said, the more they acquire the virtues through the commandments, the more they are made God's own; and the more they are made his own, the more they know him and are known by him. ⟦s22+23+24⟧ *Whose [1836] [b: A] fold is not to be trodden by wolves.* ⟦s25⟧ A fold is the name for a fenced place, where the shepherd gathers the sheep and guards them, so that they may not be torn to pieces by wolves or plundered by robbers. ⟦s26⟧ But if the fold is somewhat rotten in any part, it is found easy to get into, and is readily plotted against both by the wolves and by the robbers. ⟦s27⟧ The fold of the saints, then, is made safe and guarded on every side, as the Lord said, where thieves do not break through, [n: Matth. vi, 20.] nor can any other harmful thing plot against it. ⟦s28+29⟧ So let us pray, brethren, that we too may be counted worthy to be shepherded together with them, and to be found also in the place of that blessed delight and of their rest. ⟦s28+29⟧ For even if we have not reached the state of the saints, even if we are not worthy to be in [b: B] their glory, still we can avoid falling from paradise, if we are watchful and do ourselves a little violence; as St Clement too says: even if one is not crowned, let him yet strive to be found not far from those who are crowned. ⟦s30+31⟧ For just as in the palace there are great and splendid ranks (suppose the senate, the patricians, the generals, the prefects, the silentiaries: for these are ranks of high honour), and there are also some others serving in the same palace for a few gold pieces; yet they too are said to serve the emperor, and they are inside the palace, even if they do not have the glory of the great; still, for the present they are there. ⟦s32⟧ And it happens that they too, advancing little by little, attain great ranks and splendid dignities. ⟦s33⟧ So let us too strive to escape sin in act, so that for the present we may get clear of Hades; and so, through God's love for mankind, we can attain even the very entrance into paradise, by the prayers of all the saints. ⟦s34⟧ Amen.
