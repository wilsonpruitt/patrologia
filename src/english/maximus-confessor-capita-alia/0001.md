⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1401] *Of our holy father Maximus.*

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ OTHER CHAPTERS

⟦s1⟧ VERSES

⟦s2+3⟧ A spring flowing with the dew of ethical words
⟦s2+3⟧ Here thou shalt find, if thou go after it genuinely:
⟦s2+3⟧ The labours of Maximus the Confessor, friend.
⟦s2+3⟧ Embrace them, and thou shalt learn the gain.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 1. ⟦s2+3⟧ It is incumbent on every Christian who believes rightly in God not to be without care, but always to look for and await temptation, so that when it comes he may not be taken aback nor troubled, but to endure with thanksgiving the toil of the affliction, and to consider what he says, singing psalms with the Prophet: *Prove me, O Lord, and try me.* [n: Psal. xxv, 2.] And he did not say, «Thy chastening has overthrown me», but, *has set me upright to the end.* [n: Psal. xvii, 36.]

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 2. ⟦s2⟧ The beginning of the good is the fear of God; and its end, the longing for him. ⟦s3⟧ The beginning of every good thing is a word put into practice, and a practice informed by the word. ⟦s4⟧ Therefore neither is practice good without the word, nor the word that goes forth without practice.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 3. ⟦s2⟧ Practice is: of the body, fasting and vigil; of the mouth, psalmody; and better than psalmody, prayer; and silence, more precious than speech. ⟦s3+4⟧ And the practice of the hands is what is done by them without murmuring; and of the feet, what is accomplished through them at the first bidding. ⟦s5⟧ And the practice of the soul is self-control with simplicity, and simplicity made active by self-control. ⟦s6+7⟧ And of the mind, prayer in contemplation, and in prayer the contemplation of God.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 4. ⟦s2⟧ Before all the virtues go mercy and truth, whose offspring is humility, and the discernment which, according to the Fathers, accrues from it. ⟦s3⟧ Without which neither of them will be able to see its own end.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 5. ⟦s2⟧ Practice, when it dishonours the yoke of the word, is found wandering hither and thither among unprofitable things, like some heifer; [1404] and the word, when it refuses the precious tunics of practice, is not seemly, even if it puts on the figure of being somehow such in seeming.

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 6. ⟦s2⟧ The courageous soul, like a woman, keeping throughout her whole life two lamps burning, the practical and the contemplative, does what is due; but the contrary, again, she who is slackened by pleasures.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 7. ⟦s2⟧ Voluntary hardship does not suffice the soul for perfect deliverance from vice, unless, fired through the involuntary as well, she is not dissolved.

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 8. ⟦s2⟧ The soul, like a sword, for perfect deliverance from vice, unless it passes through fire and water, that is, voluntary and involuntary labours, is not kept unbroken against the shocks of what befalls.

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 9. ⟦s2⟧ Just as of voluntary temptations there are three more general causes, health, wealth and good repute, so those of the involuntary are also three: losses, sicknesses and revilings. ⟦s3⟧ And these become for some unto upbuilding, and for others unto pulling down.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 10. ⟦s2⟧ There subsist alongside, in the soul, desire and grief; in the body, pleasure and pain. ⟦s3⟧ And the cause of pain is pleasure; for, wishing to escape the painful sense that goes with pain, we flee for refuge to pleasure; and of grief, desire or infirmity.

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 11. ⟦s2⟧ The virtuous man possesses the good inward; the vainglorious, contrived. ⟦s3⟧ And evil the earnest man has on the surface; but the lover of pleasure, in depth.

⟦p14⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 12. ⟦s2⟧ Seldom and not with care is he who hates evil found turned toward it; but more often and more attentively he who still clings to its causes.

⟦p15⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 13. ⟦s2⟧ Those for whom repenting is not by intent, for them offending too is continual; but for those for whom sinning is against intent, repentance is fully assured, and its cause is not frequent.

⟦p16⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 14. ⟦s2⟧ Let sense and conscience go together with the uttered word, so that the divine Word, who said that he is found in the midst of these, [n: Matth. xviii, 20.] may not be put to shame through [1405] the rashness or immoderation of what is said or done.

⟦p17⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 15. ⟦s2+3⟧ It is not he who does not wrong his soul by deeds who has thereby also kept it undefiled by words; nor he who guards these who has thereby also not stained his thoughts. ⟦s4⟧ For he who sins does this in three ways.

⟦p18⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 16. ⟦s2⟧ Thou wilt not be able to behold the face of virtue while still gazing with pleasure on that of vice. ⟦s3⟧ And the second will appear hateful then, when thou desirest the taste of the first, and estrangest thy sight from so great a form.

⟦p19⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 17. ⟦s2⟧ It is through thoughts, not through things, that the demons principally make war on the soul. ⟦s3⟧ For things in themselves are necessary. ⟦s4⟧ And of things, hearing and sight are the cause; of thoughts, custom and the demons.

⟦p20⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 18. ⟦s2+3⟧ Threefold is the sinning part of the soul, being found in deeds and words and thoughts; and sixfold, the good of sinlessness. ⟦s4⟧ For one must keep the five senses and the uttered word without stumbling. ⟦s5⟧ In which he who does not stumble, it says, *is a perfect man, able to bridle* the members of the body too. [n: Jac. iii, 2.]

⟦p21⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 19. ⟦s2⟧ The irrational part of the soul is divided sixfold: into the five senses, and into the uttered word, which is divided without division together with what suffers, being passionless; but takes the impress of its vice when it is found passionate.

⟦p22⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 20. ⟦s2⟧ Neither can the body be cleansed without fasting and vigil, nor the soul without mercy and truth, nor yet the mind without contemplation and converse with God. ⟦s3⟧ For these pairings are most notable in these.

⟦p23⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 21. ⟦s2⟧ The soul, encircled by the virtues spoken of, renders its fortress unbattered by the din of temptations: which is patience. ⟦s2⟧ *For in your patience possess your souls*, [n: Luc. xxi, 19.] the Word says. ⟦s3⟧ But if things should hold otherwise, like some unwalled city it is wont to be shaken, even by noises far off, by the levers of cowardice.

⟦p24⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 22. ⟦s2⟧ Not as many as are prudent in the things of reason are prudent also in the things of notion; but as many as are so in the things of notion will be found so also in the things of sense outwardly. ⟦s3⟧ For even if sense has all men as tributaries, yet they do not render her like tributes. ⟦s4⟧ For out of artlessness most do not know how, in the things that belong to her, to honour her as she herself demands.

⟦p25⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 23. ⟦s2⟧ Prudence, though uncut by nature, is cut into different cuts. ⟦s3⟧ For to one a greater part of it is given, to another a lesser; until practical virtue, having grown, and having the generic virtues running together with it, [1408] fills up the good that is possible to each. ⟦s4⟧ For most, according to the lack of a life of practice, have also had prudence allotted them.

⟦p26⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 24. ⟦s2⟧ In the things according to nature few will be found prudent; but in the things contrary to nature, many. ⟦s3⟧ For through fear concerning them, having emptied out their whole natural prudence, they have little prudence there, and the most in superfluous things, and those not praiseworthy by nature.

⟦p27⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 25. ⟦s2⟧ Of reasonable silence the right time and the measure are the fellow-banqueters; and its feast is truth; by which the father of lies, coming upon a soul gone abroad, finds nothing of what he seeks.

⟦p28⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 26. ⟦s2⟧ The truly merciful man is not he who voluntarily provides superfluities, but he who yields necessities to those who snatch them away.

⟦p29⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 27. ⟦s2⟧ Some by material wealth acquire the immaterial, by the ordinances of almsgiving; others by the immaterial divest themselves of the material, having come into the sense of the unfailing.

⟦p30⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 28. ⟦s2⟧ Dear to everyone is to be rich in good things; but grievous it is for one who has been divinely enriched not to be granted to rejoice in it for very long.

⟦p31⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 29. ⟦s2⟧ Outwardly the health of the soul is in view; but inwardly, at the bottom of sense, its infirmity is wont to hide. ⟦s3⟧ And if it must by all means be that the one comes about outwardly, by the reaping of reproofs, and the other inwardly, by the renewal of the mind, foolish is he who shakes off the reproofs and is not ashamed to lie forever in the hospital of insensibility.

⟦p32⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 30. ⟦s2⟧ Be not exasperated against him who has involuntarily operated on thee; but looking to the loathsomeness that has been emptied out, call thyself wretched, and call blessed the God who became for thee the cause of this economy.

⟦p33⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 31. ⟦s2⟧ Do not lose heart at the severity of thy infirmity; but through the more efficacious remedies of love of labour get far from it, thou who carest for the health of the soul.

⟦p34⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 32. ⟦s2⟧ Do not shrink from him who strikes thee timely; but thou shalt go to him, and he will show thee how great is the evil chafing thy sense from beneath; and thou shalt eat the sweet relish that comes of health, after what is to be cast out from the bitterness has been consumed.

⟦p35⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 33. ⟦s2⟧ As much as thou feelest the labours, so much welcome him who shows them to thee through reproofs. ⟦s3⟧ For he becomes for thee the cause of perfect purification, [1409] without which the mind cannot come to be in the pure place of prayer.

⟦p36⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 34. ⟦s2+3⟧ When being reproved, one must either keep silent or answer gently to the accuser: not so that the one reproved may establish his own case, but so as to raise up him who reproves in ignorance, who has perhaps stumbled.

⟦p37⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 35. ⟦s2⟧ He who is well disposed to one who has grieved him unjustly, before being summoned by him, has lost nothing of what belongs to him. ⟦s3⟧ But he who is so after the summons has lost half the yield. ⟦s4⟧ And he gains the whole of what was sown beforehand who is never found put out of the assembly through grief; and he adds a wage to himself too who in everything puts the fault upon himself.

⟦p38⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 36. ⟦s2⟧ Neither does the high-minded man recognize what his own shortcomings are, nor the humble-minded man his own good things. ⟦s3⟧ For base ignorance covers the one; and the other, an ignorance pleasing to God.

⟦p39⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 37. ⟦s2⟧ The proud man does not wish to be measured against his equals in good things; but in the contrary things he reckons his own shortcoming bearable, compared with those who surpass him.

⟦p40⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 38. ⟦s2⟧ Blame renders the soul firm; but praise, slack and more sluggish toward good things.

⟦p41⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 39. ⟦s2⟧ The substance of wealth is gold; and of virtue, humility. ⟦s3⟧ As then he who lacks gold is poor, even if he does not appear so to those outside, so without humility he who contends will not be virtuous.

⟦p42⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 40. ⟦s2⟧ Just as a merchant without gold is not by nature a merchant, even if he is very apt for trade, so neither without humble-mindedness will the ascetic be found in possession of the sweets of virtue, even if he trusts wholly in his own good sense.

⟦p43⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 41. ⟦s2⟧ He who ascends by humble-mindedness becomes lower than his own way of thinking; and higher, he who is without it, who does not voluntarily endure even to be measured against the least; and therefore at the first seats he displays what is grievous. [n: Luc. xiv, 7; xx, 46.]

⟦p44⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 42. ⟦s2⟧ It is good for the contestant to think less of himself than his working, and to do more than his cowardice. ⟦s3⟧ For so he would be found both venerable to men and before God *a workman not ashamed*.

⟦p45⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 43. ⟦s2⟧ He who fears to be seen a stranger to those reclining in the bridal chamber must either be set right in all the [1412] commandments of God, or lay hold of the one, humble-mindedness.

⟦p46⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 44. ⟦s2⟧ Mix self-control with simplicity, and yoke truth to humble-mindedness, and thou shalt be seen a fellow at the hearth of justice; at whose board, as at a table, every other virtue loves to gather.

⟦p47⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 45. ⟦s2⟧ Blind is truth without humble-mindedness. ⟦s3⟧ Therefore it uses contradiction as a tutor, toiling to prop itself on something, but finding nothing except the stronghold of rancour.

⟦p48⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 46. ⟦s2⟧ A kindly character bears witness to the beauty of virtue; and steadiness of the limbs, to a peaceful soul.

⟦p49⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 47. ⟦s2+3⟧ The first good is to stumble in no part; and the second, not to cover over one's fault out of shame, nor yet to be shameless in it, but rather to be humbled, and when accused to join in accusing oneself, and to accept the penance gladly. ⟦s4⟧ And if this is not done, all that is offered to God is without strength.

⟦p50⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 48. ⟦s2⟧ Besides voluntary hardship one must also accept the involuntary: that from slanders, I mean, and losses and sicknesses. ⟦s3⟧ For he who does not accept these, but chafes, is like one who wishes to eat his bread not with salt but with honey alone; who does not always have pleasure as his companion, but always has surfeit as his neighbour.

⟦p51⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 49. ⟦s2⟧ Being master, he appears clothed in the guise of a slave who washes his neighbour's torn garment with inspired words, or stitches it up with gifts. ⟦s3⟧ But let him who does this look to it, lest, because he performs this as a slave, he also lose, along with the wage, the dignity of authority that belongs to him, through vainglory.

⟦p52⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 50. ⟦s2⟧ Just as *faith is the substance of things hoped for*, [n: Hebr. xi, 1.] so prudence is that of the soul, and humility that of virtue. ⟦s3⟧ And it is a wonder how things complete in themselves have been rendered incomplete without their accidents.

⟦p53⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 51. ⟦s2⟧ *May the Lord keep thy going in*, it says, *and thy going out*: [n: Psal. cxx, 8.] that is, of foods and of words, by self-control. ⟦s3⟧ For he who holds himself with self-control over the going in and going out of foods and words flees the desire that comes from the eyes, and the anger that comes from senselessness, of which the contender must take care even before all things, one must strive in every way to calm. ⟦s4⟧ For in these the practical is strengthened, and the contemplative made firm.

⟦p54⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 52. ⟦s2⟧ But some take much care over the going in of foods, and are careless in disposition over the going out of words. ⟦s2⟧ Such men do not know how to put away anger from the heart, and desire [n: Eccle. xi, 10.]
