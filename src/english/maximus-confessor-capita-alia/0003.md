⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1425] 110. ⟦s1⟧ Through the concept the mind passes over to the intelligibles; through thought, reason passes to the rational things; and to practice, through imagination, sense.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 111. ⟦s2⟧ The mind, gathered into itself, contemplates nothing at all of the things that are by thought; but bare minds and divine radiances, gushing forth peace and grace.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 112. ⟦s2⟧ The mind of a thing is one thing, and its principle another; and other again is what is referred to sense. ⟦s2⟧ And the one is essence; the other, accident; the other, the difference of the subject.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 113. ⟦s2⟧ The mind, cutting open many roads, shows itself insatiable; but gathered to the one road of prayer, it appears straitened before its completion, begging the one who shares with it to be released toward the things from which it came forth.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 114. ⟦s2⟧ The mind, led from above, will not run back up there again unless it makes a perfect contempt of the things below, through its leisure for divine things.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 115. ⟦s2⟧ If thou canst not make thy soul be with its own thoughts alone, at least compel thy body to live alone, keeping in mind continually the wretchedness that attends it; for so in time, by the mercy of God, thou wilt be able to run back up to the first dignity of thy nobility.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 116. ⟦s2⟧ The practical man can easily subject the mind to prayer; the contemplative, prayer to the mind. ⟦s3⟧ The one by drawing in sense from the shapes that appear; the other by carrying the soul over to the principles hidden in the words. ⟦s4⟧ And the one persuades the mind to conceive principles of bodies; the other, to comprehend those of incorporeals. ⟦s5⟧ And incorporeal are the principles of bodies, both their properties and their essences.

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 117. ⟦s2⟧ When thou freest thy mind from voluptuousness in bodies and foods and money, then whatever thou doest will be reckoned a pure gift to God; and it will be given thee in return that [1428] the eyes of thy heart be opened, and that thou meditate clearly on the laws of God hidden in it, which will be reckoned above honey and the honeycomb [n: Psal. xviii, 11.] to thy intelligible throat, by the sweetness that issues from them.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 118. ⟦s2⟧ Thou wilt not be able to make the mind superior to the desire of bodies and money and of necessary foods, unless thou bring it into the pure country of the just; in which the memory both of death and of God, sprouting up again, will wipe out from the earthly heart every upgrowth of a wish.

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 119. ⟦s2⟧ Nothing is either more fearful than the notion of death, or more wonderful than the memory of God. ⟦s3⟧ For the one is productive of saving grief; the other, bestowing of gladness. ⟦s4+5⟧ For *I remembered God*, says the Prophet, *and was gladdened*. [n: Psal. lxxvi, 4.] And the Wise man: *Remember thy last things, and thou shalt not sin*. ⟦s6⟧ For it is impossible for anyone to come into possession of the second without having taken experience of the harshness of the first.

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 120. ⟦s2⟧ As long as the mind does not see the glory of God with unveiled face, the soul cannot say in its sense: *But I shall be delighted in the Lord; I shall exult in his salvation*. ⟦s3⟧ For the veil of self-love lies upon its heart, so that the foundations of the world are not uncovered to it, which are the principles of the things that have come to be; and this is not taken away from it without voluntary and involuntary labours.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 121. ⟦s2⟧ Not after the flight from Egypt, which is sin in activity; nor after the crossing of the sea, of the slavery, I mean, that is by relation; but after the sojourn in the desert, which lies between the effects and the movements of vice, can the leader of Israel's people spy out the land of promise, which is passionlessness, sending forth his power of sight and of oversight.

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1+s2+s3⟧ 122. Those who sit in the desert, that is, in inactivity of evils, have the good things of that blessed land by hearing alone … [?] but those who spied out what is in it have come, that is, into the soul's comprehension of the contemplation of the things seen. And those deemed worthy to enter into it were filled, with their whole sense, with the principles flowing from it as milk and honey, [n: Exod. iii, 8.] of the first and second contemplation, I mean.

⟦s1+s2+s3⟧ 123. He has not yet been crucified with Christ who still has natural motions of the flesh; nor buried with him, who drags along psychic musings: how then shall such a one rise together [1429] with him, to conduct his life in newness of life? [n: Rom. vi, 4.] ⟦p14⟧ ⟦s1+s2⟧

⟦p15⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 124. ⟦s2⟧ There being three more comprehensive virtues of the soul, fasting, prayer and silence, it is necessary that he who is about to be released from prayer should come to rest in contemplation; he who is released from silence, in moral conversation; he who fasts, in a second and granted food.

⟦p16⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 125. ⟦s2⟧ As long as the mind is among its own things, it keeps what is according to the likeness, being found good and compassionate. ⟦s3⟧ But having come to be among the things of sense, if it goes down lightly and [not] fittingly, giving and taking experience, being vigorous among them, and comes back again to itself, it will be found like some general heedless about the time and the need, cutting away the greater part of his force in the fighting. ⟦s4⟧ Yet not all will be found clear of that, as many as were not strong enough to come within the enclosure.

⟦p17⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 126. ⟦s2⟧ The sensible sun makes its own rays strangers to a shut house; but the intelligible would not send forth its own upon the soul that does not keep its senses shut from the things seen.

⟦p18⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 127. ⟦s2⟧ He is a man of knowledge who disposes his descents magnificently, but the ascents of the soul with lowly seemliness.

⟦p19⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 128. ⟦s2⟧ A bee roaming a meadow gathers from there the materials for honey; and a soul gazing round upon the ages puts from there a varied sweetness into the understanding.

⟦p20⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 129. ⟦s2⟧ The deer that has eaten serpents runs, to quench the venom, to springs of waters; [n: Psal. xli, 2.] and the soul wounded by divine arrows draws the unceasing yearning that wounded it.

⟦p21⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 130. ⟦s2⟧ Bare thoughts grow upon the solitary life; after-thoughts upon the life of two; but from the much-[1432]divided soul thoughts are driven away, and minds alone, bare of bodies, drawing near to it, disclose to it the principles concerning providence and judgement, uncovering them like some foundations of the earth.

⟦p22⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 131. ⟦s2⟧ The thoughts are neither of the irrational part of the soul, for there is no thought in irrational creatures; nor of the noetic, since there is none in angels either; but being offspring of the rational part itself, using imagination as a ladder, they go up from sense to the mind, reporting to it the things of sense; and they go down from it to sense, suggesting to it the things of the mind.

⟦p23⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 132. ⟦s2⟧ The wicked thoughts will be found like men brought up from the deep and trying to lay hold of something for help, when vice, like a merchant ship, is in danger from a flood of tears.

⟦p24⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 133. ⟦s2⟧ According to the underlying quality of the soul, the thoughts around it gather either like pirates to sink it, or like oarsmen to help it in its danger: the one sort dragging it out to a sea of notions of unseemly thoughts; the other coasting along the nearer shore and running the mind in to calm beaches.

⟦p25⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 134. ⟦s2⟧ The soul that yearns to cast off, as the last tunic, the thought of vainglory, which is the seventh, unless it strips off those before it, will not be able after them to put on the eighth. ⟦s3⟧ This the divine Apostle knows how to call a heavenly dwelling, and knows that those who for its sake go stripped of material things put it on with groanings. [n: II Cor. v, 2.]

⟦p26⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 135. ⟦s2⟧ To perfect prayer angel thoughts are wont to come near; to the middle, spiritual thoughts; to the introductory, the thoughts of natural philosophy.

⟦p27⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 136. ⟦s2⟧ As the nobility of the grain shows itself through the ear, so the purity of contemplation is wont to show itself through prayer; and the ear has, for the turning away of the seed-picking birds, the awns that surround it like spears; and the other, the thoughts that philosophize in temptations, for their destruction.

⟦p28⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 137. ⟦s2⟧ The visible things of the soul, through practice, are like the silvered wings of a dove; [1433] and the intelligible things, I mean the back, through contemplation are set as in the paleness of gold; [n: Psal. lxvii, 14.] for the soul not so made beautiful cannot fly and be at rest where the dwelling of all who rejoice is. [n: Psal. lxxxvi, 7.]

⟦p29⟧ ⟦s1⟧ A meadow here filled with fruits
⟦s1⟧ Of spiritual practice and contemplation.

⟦p30⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 138. ⟦s2⟧ Firstfruits of threshing floor and winepress the ancients were of old commanded to offer in the temple. ⟦s3⟧ But now we must offer to God, as firstfruits of practice, self-control and truth; and of contemplative virtue, love and prayer: through the former cutting back the impulses of irrational desire and of anger; through the latter, empty notions and the plots that come from them.

⟦p31⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 139. ⟦s2⟧ The beginning of practice is self-control and humility, truth, temperance and humble-mindedness; its end, peace of thoughts and sanctification of body.

⟦p32⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 140. ⟦s2⟧ Practice is not only being able to do good things well, but also doing them as one ought, the doer bringing along with him the time and the measure suited to the things to be done.

⟦p33⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 141. ⟦s2⟧ Contemplation is not only contemplating how bodies stand in nature, but also to what their principles look.

⟦p34⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 142. ⟦s2⟧ There is neither safe practice without contemplation, nor true contemplation without practice. ⟦s3⟧ For practice must be reasoned, and contemplation practised; so that by the one vice may be found powerless, and by the other virtue mighty in the good pleasures of kindness.

⟦p35⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 143. ⟦s2⟧ The limit of the practical is the mortification of the passions; the end of the soul of knowledge, the contemplation of the virtues.

⟦p36⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 144. ⟦s2⟧ As matter to form, so practice to contemplation; and as an eye to a face, so will contemplation be seen to practice.

⟦p37⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 145. ⟦s2⟧ In the stadium of practical virtue many run, but one receives the prize: [n: I Cor. ix, 24.] he who desires to come through contemplation to its limit.

⟦p38⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 146. ⟦s2⟧ The practical man drinks a drink of compunction [n: Psal. lix, 5; xxii, 5.] in prayer; but with a most excellent cup the [1436] contemplative is made drunk. ⟦s2⟧ The one philosophizing among the things according to nature; the other not knowing even himself in praying.

⟦p39⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 147. ⟦s2⟧ The practical man is not permitted to persevere long in spiritual contemplation. ⟦s3⟧ For he is found like one who has lodged as a guest with someone, and quickly slips out of his house.

⟦p40⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 148. ⟦s2⟧ The practical, in praying, enter into the gates of the commandments of God; and the contemplative, as into courts of virtues, with hymns: the one giving thanks that they have been loosed from their bonds; the other, that they have even taken captive those who war against them.

⟦p41⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 149. ⟦s2⟧ The strength of contemplation must be found to match the strength of practice, lest, like a ship that does not carry sails proportionate to it, one undergo either danger in violent winds through their excess, or loss of the winds through their smallness relative to the hull.

⟦p42⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 150. ⟦s2⟧ Take the pious thoughts as the oarsmen of the rational ship; and as the oars, the vital powers of the soul: the incensive power and desire, wish and choice. ⟦s3⟧ Of these the practical man has need always; but the contemplative not always. ⟦s4⟧ For at the time of prayer, bidding farewell to them all, he seats himself at the helm of discernment and keeps watch through the whole night of contemplation, offering praises to the One who holds the universe together. ⟦s5⟧ And at times, taking up also some song of yearning, he sings to his own soul, looking out at the heavings of the other sea and, as it were, its surging motions, struck with awe at the divine words and statutes.

⟦p43⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 151. ⟦s2⟧ He who is midway in practice and contemplation makes his voyage neither wholly with the oars, like sailors, nor altogether with the intelligible sails; but works the business of a fair voyage through both: bearing gladly both the labours of practice, because of the modest share of contemplation, and the principles of imperfect contemplation, because of being helped by practice.

⟦p44⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 152. ⟦s2⟧ The contemplative, having nature running together with his inclination, like a current, makes the voyage without labour; but the practical, finding his relation opposed to his choice, undergoes a great surge of thoughts, so as all but to come to discouragement through the weight.

⟦p45⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 153. ⟦s2⟧ Neither is land that has not been well worked wont to give back to the sower an abundant and clean crop, nor will he who pursues practice, unless he pursues it carefully and without ostentation, see the fruit of prayer abundant and pure.

⟦p46⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1437] 154. ⟦s2⟧ In enmattered things the mind has thought as its fellow-worker; but in immaterial things, unless it refuses it, it will have it as a thorn buffeting it. [n: II Cor. xii, 7.]

⟦p47⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 155. ⟦s2⟧ The practical man has a veil upon his heart in prayer, the knowledge of sensible things, which cannot be uncovered because of the relation; but the contemplative alone, because he is without relation, can see in part the glory of God with unveiled face. [n: II Cor. iii, 18.]

⟦p48⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 156. ⟦s2+3⟧ Prayer with spiritual contemplation is the land of promise, in which flows, as honey and milk, the knowledge of the principles of God concerning providence and judgement; prayer with some natural contemplation is Egypt, in which a memory of the grosser desires comes upon those who pray. [n: Exod. iii, 8.] But simple prayer is the manna in the desert, which, because of its uniformity, for those who cannot endure shuts off, through desire, the good things of the promise; but for those who persevere in such straitened food, procures the better taste, and one that abides.

⟦p49⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 157. ⟦s2⟧ Practice with contemplation will be reckoned as a body with a governing spirit; without contemplation, as flesh with an elective spirit.

⟦p50⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 158. ⟦s2⟧ Of the rational soul, sense is the court; the understanding, the temple; the mind, the high priest. ⟦s3⟧ In the court, then, stands the mind that is cut about by untimely thoughts; in the temple, the one cut about by timely ones; and in none of these, the one deemed worthy to enter the divine sanctuary.

⟦p51⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 159. ⟦s2⟧ *Lamentation and song and woe* [n: Ezech. ii, 9.] are found in the house of the practical soul, because of the toil; but *the voice of exultation and confession* [n: Psal. xli, 5.] is heard, because of knowledge, in that of the contemplative.

⟦p52⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 160. ⟦s2⟧ The practical man desires to depart, because of the labours, and to be with Christ; but the contemplative is better pleased to remain in the flesh, [n: Philipp. i, 23.] both because of the joy he receives from prayer and because of his neighbour's benefit in earthly things.

⟦p53⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 161. ⟦s2⟧ With the more learned, contemplation goes before practice; with the more rustic, practice before contemplation; but both end in one good end. ⟦s3⟧ And this will be seen sooner in those with whom contemplation goes before practice.
