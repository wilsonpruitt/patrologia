⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0288] THE DISPUTATION WITH PYRRHUS

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1+2+3⟧ *Record of the inquiry held concerning the questions that had been stirred about the ecclesiastical doctrines, in the presence of Gregory, the most pious patrician, and of the most holy bishops who were found with him, and of the other God-beloved and glorious men, conducted by Pyrrhus, the former patriarch of Constantinople, and Maximus, the most devout monk, in the month of July, in the third indiction: Pyrrhus championing the novelty brought in by himself and by his predecessor in Byzantium, that is, of one will; Maximus pleading for the patristic and apostolic teaching that came down to us from above. ⟦s1+2+3⟧ Through the said surpassing man, I mean Gregory the most honoured patrician, the said men, that is, Pyrrhus and Maximus, were brought face to face with one another; and Pyrrhus began the discussion with Maximus, speaking thus:*

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ What evil have we done thee, lord abba Maximus, I and my predecessor, that thou thus disparagest us everywhere, giving us among all a repute for heresy? ⟦s3⟧ And who ever so, though thou wast unknown to him by sight, face to face, honoured or revered thee as we did?

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Since God hears, no one so, to use thy own words, honoured or revered me as you did; but since you have now set aside the Christian doctrine, I judged it a fearful thing to set the favour shown to me before the truth.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ Holding what opinion, then, have we set aside the way of thinking of the Christians?

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Holding one will of the Godhead of Christ and of his manhood; and not only holding it, but also, to the ruin of the whole body of the holy Church, having set this forth through a new Ekthesis.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ What then? ⟦s2⟧ Does he who holds one will of Christ seem to thee to have been moved away from the Christian teaching?

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Entirely so. ⟦s3⟧ For what is more unholy than to say that the same one, by one and the same will, before the incarnation constituted all things from things that are not, and holds them together and provides for them and directs them savingly; but after the becoming-man sought food and drink, and changed place for place, and did all the other things that are beyond all slander and blame, through which he also showed the economy pure of all illusion?

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ Is Christ one, or not?

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Yes, plainly one.

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0289] PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ If then Christ is one, he certainly also willed as one; and if he willed as one, his will too is certainly one, and not two.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ To say something and not first to distinguish the significations of what is said is nothing other than to confound all things, and to be in haste to leave unclear the things about which the inquiry is; which is alien to a learned man. ⟦s3⟧ Tell me this, then: Christ, being one, is he God only, or also man only, or both together, God at once and man?

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ Plainly, God at once and man.

⟦p14⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Christ, then, being by nature God and man, did the same one will as God and man, or as Christ only? ⟦s3⟧ But if Christ willed principally as God and man, plainly he willed dually, and not singularly, being one and the same. ⟦s4⟧ For if Christ is nothing other than his natures, from which and in which he exists, plainly, being one and the same, he both willed and operated correspondingly to his own natures, that is, as each is of a nature to do; since neither of them is without will or without operation. ⟦s5⟧ And if Christ, correspondingly to his own natures, that is, as each is of a nature to do, both willed and operated, and his natures are two, then his natural wills too are certainly two, and the essential operations equal in number to them.

⟦s6⟧ For just as the number of the natures of the one and same Christ, piously understood and spoken, does not divide Christ, but presents the difference of the natures preserved even in the union; so also the number of the wills and operations that belong essentially to his natures (for according to both his natures, as has been said, the same one was volitive and operative of our salvation) does not introduce division, God forbid, but shows only their guarding and preservation even in the union.

⟦p15⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ It is impossible that with the wills those who will should not be introduced as well.

⟦p16⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ This absurdity you declared in your writings too, moved not by reason but by authority, having taken Heraclius as your ally in this, because you too had joined in his unlawful and illicit mingling and confirmed it with a blessing. ⟦s3⟧ For if it be granted that with the wills those who will are introduced as well, then with those who will the wills too will certainly be introduced as well, by the reasonable reciprocation; and according to you it will be found, of the superessential and supergood and blessed Godhead, that because of its one will its hypostasis too is one, according to Sabellius; and because of the three persons, the wills too are three, and for this reason three natures, according to Arius; since, according to the patristic definitions and canons, [0292] the difference of the wills introduces a difference of natures.

⟦p17⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ It is impossible that in one person two wills should coexist with each other without opposition.

⟦p18⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ If it is not possible for two wills to be in one and the same person without opposition, then with opposition, according to thee, it is possible. ⟦s3⟧ And if this, thou hast so far confessed that they are two; and thou dissentest not as to the number, but only as to the contrariness. ⟦s4⟧ It remains, then, to inquire into the cause productive of the conflict. ⟦s5⟧ What then dost thou say this is? ⟦s6⟧ Is it the volition according to nature, or sin? ⟦s7⟧ But if thou sayest the volition according to nature, of this we know no other cause than God; then, according to thee, God is the creator of the conflict. ⟦s8⟧ But if sin, yet God incarnate did no sin, nor did he have any opposition whatever in his wills according to nature. ⟦s9⟧ For when the cause is not, plainly neither will the effect be.

⟦p19⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ Is willing, then, of nature?

⟦p20⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Yes, simply willing is of nature.

⟦p21⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ If willing is of nature, and the more eminent of the Fathers spoke of one will of God and of the saints, then in this way there will be one nature too of the saints and of God.

⟦p22⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ It was said above too that one who makes an account concerning truth must distinguish the significations of what is said, because of the error that comes from homonymy. ⟦s3⟧ For I in turn will ask thee: did the saints who spoke of one will of God and of the saints say this looking to the essential and creative will of God, or to what is willed? ⟦s4⟧ For the will of the one willing and what is willed are not the same, just as neither are the power of sight of the one seeing and what is seen; for the one belongs to him essentially, the other lies outside. ⟦s5⟧ But if they spoke looking to the essential, they will be found not only introducing the saints as connatural with God and co-creators, but also contradicting themselves, having said that things of other essence cannot have a common will. ⟦s6⟧ But if to what is willed, then the Fathers called what was willed a will causally, or, as some like to say, by misuse; and nothing absurd will follow for those who define simply willing as being of nature.

⟦p23⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ If by our wills we differ both from ourselves and from one another, now willing this, now not willing the same, and this is of nature and of its characterizing principle, we shall be found not only differing from one another in nature, but also converting it countless times.

⟦p24⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Willing and the how of willing are not the same, [0293] just as neither are seeing and the how of seeing. ⟦s3⟧ For willing, as also seeing, is of nature, and belongs to all things connatural and of one genus; but the how of willing, as also the how of seeing, that is, willing to walk and not willing to walk, and seeing to the right or to the left, or up or down, or toward desire or toward the observation of the principles in beings, is a mode of the use of willing and seeing, belonging to the user alone and separating him from the others, according to what is commonly called difference. ⟦s4⟧ And if we have nature bearing witness to this, then willing to eat or not willing to eat, or willing to walk or not willing, is not a destruction of being of a nature to will, but of the how of willing; that is, it is the coming-to-be and passing-away of the things willed. ⟦s5⟧ For neither, if we suppose that the things which came to be from God, being things willed by him, pass away, will his essential and productive will, conceived before them, pass away together with them.

⟦p25⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ If thou callest the will natural, and the natural is certainly also compelled, how is it not necessary that those who call the wills in Christ natural destroy every voluntary movement in him?

⟦p26⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Not only does the divine and uncreated nature have nothing natural that is compelled, but neither does the intellectual and created. ⟦s3⟧ For what is by nature rational has as a natural power the rational appetite, which is also called the volition of the intellectual soul; according to which, willing, we reason; and reasoning, willing, we wish. ⟦s4⟧ And willing, we inquire, and consider, and deliberate, and judge, and are disposed, and choose, and have impulse, and use. ⟦s5⟧ And since it belongs to us according to nature, as has been said, to have rational appetite, that is, to will, and to reason, to deliberate and to inquire, and to consider, and to wish, and to judge, and to be disposed, and to prefer, and to have impulse, and to use, the natural things of intellectual beings are therefore not compelled.

⟦s6⟧ And how, once this is granted, will such a premise not be shown more absurd than every absurdity? ⟦s7⟧ For if according to it the natural is certainly also compelled, and God is God by nature, good by nature, creator by nature, then God will be God and good and creator by necessity; which even to conceive, let alone to say, is of the utmost blasphemy. ⟦s8⟧ For who is it that brings on the necessity? ⟦s9⟧ And consider, if it seems good, my friend, the blasphemy of such a premise in this way too. ⟦s10⟧ For if he who calls the wills in Christ natural destroys, according to thee, every voluntary movement in him, it is necessary that the things which will naturally have an involuntary movement, and the things which do not will naturally, a voluntary one. ⟦s11⟧ Not only God, then, who is beyond the things that are, but also all intellectual and rational beings, being volitive by nature, will have an involuntary movement; but inanimate things, not being volitive, will have a voluntary movement. ⟦s11⟧ Except that the blessed Cyril, in the third chapter against the censures of Theodoret, freed us from superfluous trouble, declaring expressly: «Nothing natural [0296] is involuntary in the intellectual nature.» ⟦s12⟧ And this is open to whoever wishes to learn, taking that chapter in hand.

⟦p27⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ Since one must accept with good grace the true things that come to light from the examination, and with great clarity the argument has shown that the wills in Christ are natural: yet it is possible, just as from the two natures we speak of one composite, so also from the two natural wills to speak of one composite; so that both those who say two wills, because of the natural difference of the natures in Christ, and those who say one, because of the utmost union, may no longer be at variance with one another for the sake of mere words; since «not in names», says Gregory, the great in theology, «is the truth for us, but in things».

⟦p28⟧ ⟦s1+2+3⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s1+2+3⟧ Dost thou see that you err from this, from being altogether ignorant that compositions belong to things that are in the hypostasis, and not to things contemplated in another and not by their own principle? ⟦s1+2+3⟧ And this is the common way of thinking of all, both of the philosophers outside and of the God-wise mystagogues of the Church. ⟦s4⟧ But if you speak of a composition of the wills, you will be forced to speak also of the composition of the other natural things, if indeed you wish to show the principle of your own doctrine coherent, that is, of the created and the uncreated, of the infinite and the bounded, of the undefined and the defined, of the mortal and the immortal, of the corruptible and the incorruptible; and you will be carried away into absurd suppositions. ⟦s5⟧ And how will what is from the wills be called a will? ⟦s6⟧ For it is not possible for the composite to be named by the designation of the things composed. ⟦s7⟧ Or in that way what is from the natures too will be called a nature, according to the heretics of old. ⟦s8⟧ And besides this you again separate him from the wills of the Father, having marked by a composite will a nature too that is composite and his alone.

⟦p29⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ Then did their natural things, just as the natures, have nothing in common?

⟦p30⟧ ⟦s1+2+3⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s1+2+3⟧ Nothing, except only the hypostasis of those same natures. ⟦s1+2+3⟧ For just as the same one was, without confusion, the hypostasis of those same [natures, so also he was, without division, the union of what belonged to them by nature].

⟦p31⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ What then? ⟦s2⟧ Did not the Fathers, whose words have been established as law and canon of the Church, call both the glory and the outrage common? ⟦s3⟧ For «one thing», he says, «is that from which the glory is common, and another that from which the outrage is».
