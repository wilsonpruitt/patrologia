⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0297] without the conversion and commixture of either part into the other according to the natural principle. ⟦s2⟧ If then thou callest the will common by the mode of the exchange, thou wilt call the wills not one but two; and thy cleverness has again turned round on thee into that very thing from which thou wast eager to flee.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ What then? ⟦s3⟧ Was not the flesh moved by the nod of the Word united to it?

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Thou dividest Christ in saying so. ⟦s3⟧ For by his nod Moses too was moved, and David, and all who have become receptive of the divine operation by the putting-off of the human and fleshly idioms. ⟦s4⟧ But we, following the holy Fathers in this as in all things, say that the very God of the universe, having become man without change, not only as God willed, the same one, correspondingly to his own Godhead, but also as man, the same one, correspondingly to his own manhood.

⟦s5⟧ For if the things that are, having come to be from things that are not, have also a power that clings to being, not to non-being; and proper by nature to this power is the impulse toward what constitutes them and the recoil from what destroys them; then the superessential Word too, having been essentiated humanly, had also the power that clings to the being of his manhood; whose impulse and recoil he showed, willing, through operation: the impulse, in using natural and blameless things to such a degree that he was not even thought to be God by the unbelievers; and the recoil, at the time of the passion, in voluntarily making the contraction toward death. ⟦s6⟧ What absurdity, then, has the Church of God committed in confessing in him, together with his human and created nature, without omission also the principles creatively implanted in it by him, without which it is impossible for the nature to be?

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ If fear belongs to us naturally, and this is among the blameworthy things, then according to you the blameworthy things, that is, sin, lie in us naturally.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Again from the homonymy thou misleadest thyself, not the truth. ⟦s3⟧ For there is fear both according to nature and contrary to nature. ⟦s3⟧ And fear according to nature is a power that clings to being by way of contraction; fear contrary to nature, an irrational contraction. ⟦s4⟧ The fear contrary to nature, then, the Lord did not admit at all, inasmuch as it comes from a betrayal of the reasonings; but the fear according to nature, as indicative of the power that lays claim to being, inherent in the nature, he received, willing, for our sake, as good. ⟦s5⟧ For in the Lord the natural things do not go before the volition, as in us; but just as he truly hungered and thirsted, yet hungered and thirsted not in the mode that is according to us, but in the mode above us, for it was voluntarily; so also, truly fearing, he feared not according to us, but above us. ⟦s6⟧ And, to speak universally, everything natural [0300] in Christ has joined to the principle according to it also the mode above nature, so that both the nature may be confirmed through the principle, and the economy through the mode.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ So then, to escape this subtle technical reasoning, hard for the many to grasp, let us confess the same one perfect God and perfect man, declining all the rest, since "perfect" displays along with itself the natural things.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ If to speak of the natures without the property belonging to each, or of Christ as perfect God and perfect man without the marks of the perfection, is, according to thee, of complete piety, let the synods be anathematized, and before them the Fathers, who legislated for us to confess not only the natures but also the property of each nature; and not only the same one perfect God and perfect man, but also the marks of the perfection, that is, saying that the same and one is visible and invisible, mortal and immortal, corruptible and incorruptible, tangible and intangible, created and uncreated. ⟦s2⟧ And according to this same pious concept they also piously laid down as doctrine two wills of the same and one, not only through the defined number of the two, but also through the pronoun "one and another", and through the analogy of saying "divine" and "human". ⟦s3⟧ For the nature of number is not circumscribed in one mode.

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ If it is not possible for these terms to be spoken because of the spite of the heretics, let us be content with only what was said synodically, and say neither one nor two wills.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ If one must confess only what was said synodically, then neither must one say "the one nature of God the Word incarnate", which comprehends the whole piety of the mystery, since it was not pronounced synodically. ⟦s3⟧ Besides, even so you will be forced to confess the wills too together with the natures and their idioms. ⟦s4⟧ For if you say that the things belonging naturally to the natures of Christ are their idioms, and willing is implanted naturally in each of his natures, then along with the other natural idioms you will be compelled to confess the wills too together with the natures.

⟦s5⟧ Moreover, if the synods anathematized both Apollinarius and Arius over the term "one will", each of them having misused it for the establishment of his own heresy, the one wishing to show by it the flesh consubstantial with the Word, the other striving to introduce the Son as of other essence relative to the Father, how is it possible for us to be pious if we do not confess the terms contrary to the heretics? ⟦s6⟧ And the fifth synod, to pass over all the rest, having decreed word for word that it receives «all the writings of the saints Athanasius and Basil and the Gregories and certain others, specifically defined as approved teachers»,[0301] in which the two wills too are set down, has also handed down two wills.

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ And does it not seem to thee that the expression "natural will" strikes the ears of the many?

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Setting aside the divine, how many species of life dost thou say there are in the things that are?

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ Say thou thyself.

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ There are three species of life.

⟦p14⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ Which are these?

⟦p15⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ The vegetative, the sensitive, the intellectual.

⟦p16⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ So it is.

⟦p17⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ And since each is distinguished from the others by some principle of its making, what is the principle that distinguishes and particularizes each species?

⟦p18⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ This too I ask thee to say.

⟦p19⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Proper to the vegetative is the nutritive and growth-giving and generative movement; to the sensitive, the movement by impulse; to the intellectual, the self-determining.

⟦p20⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ Very clearly and unconfusedly hast thou set forth the property of each species of life.

⟦p21⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ If the property of each [species] has been shown clear and unconfused, I ask again whether the nutritive and growth-giving and generative movement belongs by nature to the vegetative, and to the sensitive the movement by impulse.

⟦p22⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ By nature, undoubtedly.

⟦p23⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Then consequently to the intellectual too the self-determining movement.

⟦p24⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ This too he will certainly grant who lays down doctrine consistently with his own starting-points.

⟦p25⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ If then the self-determining movement belongs by nature to intellectual beings, then everything intellectual is also by nature volitive; for the blessed Diadochus of Photice defined self-determination as being volition. ⟦s3⟧ And if everything intellectual is also by nature volitive, and God the Word truly became flesh animated rationally and intellectually, then inasmuch as he is man too, the same one was essentially volitive. ⟦s4⟧ And if this, then the expression "natural will" does not strike the ears of the pious, but those of the heretics.

⟦p26⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s1⟧ I for my part was already persuaded in what went before that the wills in Christ are natural, and concerning this I seek no other demonstration. ⟦s2+3⟧ For no less than the things declared by God, the argument has shown nature itself, the nature of the things that are, bearing witness that the same one was willing correspondingly to his own natures, approving as God, obeying as man; and that the natural wills certainly correspond to the natures: the will without beginning to the nature without beginning, and the will that has begun to the nature that has begun; and that it is not possible for them ever to fall together with each other into one will, even if they are of one and the same, just as neither can the natures, the without-beginning and the begun, the uncreated and the created, the maker and the made, the infinite [0304] and the bounded, the deifying and the deified. ⟦s4⟧ But those in Byzantium, still resisting the natural wills, say that the Fathers said the Lord has the human will by appropriation.

⟦p27⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Since thou thyself wast the guide of this wonderful and brilliant teaching of theirs: by what sort of appropriation do they say this? ⟦s3⟧ Is it the essential, according to which each one, having the things that belong to him naturally, appropriates them on account of the nature? ⟦s3⟧ Or the relative, according to which we affectionately appropriate and cherish one another's things, ourselves neither undergoing nor operating any of them?

⟦p28⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ The relative, obviously.

⟦p29⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Then, before showing the absurdity of this, it would be more just to examine more precisely whether man is volitive by nature or not. ⟦s3⟧ For when this is shown, the blasphemy of such a heresy will become clearer.

⟦p30⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ If it seems good, let us examine this.

⟦p31⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ That natural things are untaught not only those have said who examined nature by reason and stand apart from the many, but also the usage of the commoner sort. ⟦s3⟧ And if natural things are untaught, and we have willing untaught (for no one is ever taught to will), then man is volitive by nature. ⟦s4⟧ And again, if man is rational by nature, and what is rational by nature is also self-determining by nature (for self-determination, according to the Fathers, is volition), then man is volitive by nature. ⟦s5⟧ And again, if in the irrational beings nature leads, but in man, who moves with authority according to volition, it is led, then man is volitive by nature. ⟦s6⟧ And again, if man was made according to the image of the blessed and superessential Godhead, and the divine nature is self-determining by nature, then man too, as being truly its image, is self-determining by nature; and if self-determining by nature, man is therefore volitive by nature; for it has already been said that the Fathers defined self-determination as volition. ⟦s7⟧ Further, if willing is inherent in all men, and is not inherent in some and not inherent in others, and what is contemplated in all in common characterizes the nature in the individuals under it, then man is volitive by nature.

⟦p32⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ It has been shown by many arguments that man is volitive by nature.

⟦p33⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ This having been shown, then, beyond doubt, let us examine, as we proposed above, the absurdity of their premise too.

⟦p34⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ Let us examine it.

⟦p35⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ If man is volitive by nature, as has been shown, [0305] and according to them Christ had the human will by the appropriation that consists in bare relation, they must, if indeed they keep in line with their own starting-points, also say that the other things natural to us, along with it (I mean, the will according to nature), he had by bare appropriation; and the contemplation and initiation of the whole economy will be found by them to be taken as an illusion. ⟦s3⟧ Then, if the sentence of Sergius anathematized not those who say two wills in a certain way, but those who say two wills simply, and these men say two, even if mistakenly, on account of the appropriation, then those who stand for it are pleading for the anathema against themselves. ⟦s4⟧ And again, if, according to the premise championed by them, persons are introduced together with the wills, then, saying two wills, even if mistakenly, on account of the appropriation, as has been said, they will also speak of the persons introduced together with them according to such a premise. ⟦s5⟧ And who will bear the cutting of the one into two persons?

⟦p36⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ What then of the Fathers? ⟦s2⟧ Did they not say that the will which Christ figured in himself is ours?

⟦p37⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Yes, ours.

⟦p38⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ Then by "the human" they did not signify what belongs to him by nature, but what he took on by appropriation?

⟦p39⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Since they said that he likewise took up our nature too, then according to them he will be found having the nature itself too by bare appropriation. ⟦s3⟧ For if that is true, this is too; and if this is false, so is that.

⟦p40⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ What then? ⟦s2⟧ In saying that he figured ours in himself, did they signify what belongs to him by nature?

⟦p41⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Yes.

⟦p42⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ How dost thou say this?

⟦p43⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Since the same one was wholly God with the manhood, and the same one wholly man with the Godhead, he himself, as man, in himself and through himself subjected the human to God and Father, giving himself to us as the best type and pattern for imitation, so that we too, looking to him as the author of our salvation, [n: Hebr. II, 10 et XII, 2.] may voluntarily bring ours over to God, by no longer willing anything beside what he himself wills.

⟦p44⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ Those men said this not from evil intent and villainy
