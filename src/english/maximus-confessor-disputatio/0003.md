⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0308] of any kind, but wishing to make plain the utmost union.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ If this be granted to those from Severus, will they not reasonably say thereafter, so far as the premiss granted goes, this too: that «Not with evil intent, or with any cunning, do we say one nature, but wishing, just as you too do through the one will, to show through it the utmost union»? ⟦s3⟧ For they will use their weapons against them, in the manner in which David too did against Goliath. ⟦s4⟧ See: saying one [for indeed one] will, they coincide with those men in the same concepts and in the same terms. ⟦s5⟧ But this one will, what do they wish to name it? ⟦s5⟧ For they are bound in justice to give its appellation.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ They say it is of inclination.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Then if of inclination, also derivative; and if derivative, inclination, as the prototype, will be an essence.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ Inclination is not an essence.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ If it is not an essence, it is a quality; and a quality will be found from a quality: which is impossible. ⟦s3⟧ What then do they say inclination is?

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ Inclination is nothing other than what the blessed Cyril defined it as, that it is a mode of life.

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ The mode of the life according to virtue, say, or according to vice: is it present to us by selection, or not?

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ By selection, certainly.

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Do we then select willing and deliberating, or without will and without deliberation?

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ Admittedly, willing and deliberating.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Then inclination is nothing other than a volition of a certain quality, holding relationally to some good, either real or supposed.

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ Rightly hast thou made the exegesis of the Father's definition.

⟦p14⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ If the unfolding of the Father's definition has been made rightly, first, it is not possible to speak of a will of inclination. ⟦s3⟧ For how can a will proceed from a will? ⟦s4⟧ And then, saying inclination in Christ too, as the inquiry about it has shown, they teach as doctrine that he is a mere man, disposed deliberatively in our way, both ignorant and doubting, and having opposites; if indeed one deliberates about things doubted, and not about what is beyond doubt. ⟦s5⟧ For we have naturally the appetite for what is simply good by nature; but the experience of the good of a certain kind, through inquiry and counsel. ⟦s6⟧ And for this reason in us inclination is fittingly spoken of, being a mode of use, not a principle of nature; since otherwise nature too would have been converted infinitely many times. ⟦s7⟧ But in the case of the human of the Lord, which subsisted not merely in our way but divinely (for God was he [0309] who for our sake appeared from us in flesh), inclination cannot be spoken of. ⟦s8⟧ For by his very being, that is, by subsisting divinely, he had naturally both kinship to the good and alienation from evil; as the great eye of the Church, Basil, also said, teaching in the interpretation of the forty-fourth psalm: «In this sense thou wilt take also what was said by Isaiah about him, that Before the child knows or chooses evil things, he will pick out the good. ⟦s9⟧ Because before the child knows good or evil, he disobeys wickedness so as to pick out the good. ⟦s10⟧ For the "before" shows that, not having inquired and deliberated in our way, but having subsisted divinely, by his very being he had the good from nature.»

⟦p15⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ What then? ⟦s2⟧ Are the virtues natural?

⟦p16⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Yes, natural.

⟦p17⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ And if natural, why are they not present equally in all who are connatural?

⟦s3⟧ MAXIMUS. [They are present equally in all who are connatural.]

⟦p18⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ And whence in us such inequality?

⟦p19⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s1⟧ From not operating equally the things of nature. ⟦s2⟧ So that, if we all operated equally the natural things for which we also came to be, one virtue would have been shown in all, as the nature is, not admitting the more and the less.

⟦p20⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s1+2⟧ If the natural things come forth to us not from ascesis but from making, and virtue is natural, how do we acquire the virtues, which are natural, by toil and ascesis?

⟦p21⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Ascesis, and the toils that follow on it, were devised by the lovers of virtue only for separating the deceit commixed into the soul through sense; not for bringing in the virtues freshly from outside. ⟦s2⟧ For they lie in us from making, as has been said; whence too, as soon as the deceit is perfectly distinguished, at once the soul shows the brightness of the virtue according to nature. ⟦s3⟧ For he who is not senseless is prudent; and he who is not cowardly or rash is courageous; and he who is not [0312] intemperate is temperate; and he who is not unjust is just. ⟦s4⟧ And reason according to nature is prudence; and the judging faculty, justice; and anger, courage; and desire, temperance. ⟦s5⟧ Therefore by the removal of the things contrary to nature, the things according to nature alone are wont to shine through; just as by the casting off of rust, the iron's casting off, its gleam and brightness according to nature.

⟦p22⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ From this the greatest blasphemy has been shown up in those who say inclination in Christ.

⟦p23⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Nor must this be passed by unremarked: that the name of inclination is of many modes and many meanings, both in holy Scripture and in the holy Fathers, as is plain to those who read carefully. ⟦s3⟧ For sometimes they bring it in the sense of exhortation and advice: as when the Apostle says, Now concerning virgins I have no commandment of the Lord; but I give inclination. [n: I Cor. vii, 25.] Sometimes in the sense of counsel, when the blessed David says, Against thy people they plotted craftily an inclination; which another translator, making it clear, said: Against thy people they plotted craftily a counsel. [n: Psal. lxxxii, 4.] Sometimes in the sense of a decree, when Daniel, the great one among the prophets, says about someone: The shameless inclination went out from the face of the king. [n: Dan. ii, 15.] Sometimes in the sense of opinion, or faith, or way of thinking, when Gregory, named from theology, recounts in the first oration On the Son: «But since to censure is no great thing (for it is very easy, and belongs to anyone who wishes), but to bring in one's own inclination in its place is the part of a man pious and possessed of mind.» ⟦s4⟧ And simply, so that I may not, going through all things one by one, heap a crowd upon my account: having observed it, I found the name of inclination in twenty-eight meanings both in holy Scripture and in the holy Fathers. ⟦s4⟧ For it does not display the character of something common or specific; but the understanding of the speaker is ruled either from what is said before it or from what follows. ⟦s5⟧ Wherefore it is impossible to mark off such a name to one and only one meaning.

⟦p24⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ For how is it possible that what is said in many ways should be allotted to some one thing?

⟦p25⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ In order then that the shame of such a heresy may become more manifest, let us examine such a proposition in another way too.

⟦p26⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ If it please thee, let us examine it.

⟦p27⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Saying one will, whether elective, or of inclination, or authoritative, or if they wish to call it somehow otherwise (for we do not differ about this), they will be forced to say that this is either divine, or angelic, or human. ⟦s3⟧ And principally, whichever of these they call it, they will say it is natural, [0313] if indeed each of them is indicative of a nature; and what they seemed to flee by the method of division, this they were shown to establish by that of analysis. ⟦s4⟧ And consequently, if they call it divine, they have made Christ known as being God alone by nature; if angelic, as neither God nor man, but some angelic nature; if human, they have shown him a mere man and under authority.

⟦p28⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ When they fall into these absurdities, they say the will is neither natural nor of inclination; but they say it is present to us by aptitude.

⟦p29⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Is this aptitude present to us according to nature, or not according to nature?

⟦p30⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ According to nature.

⟦p31⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Then again, by analysis, they will call the will natural; and they will have profited nothing from this circuit. ⟦s2⟧ And since aptitude makes the habit and the readiness from learning, then from learning and progress Christ, according to them, had the habit of the will and the readiness; and he progressed, ignorant before the learning of the things learned. ⟦s3⟧ For what reason then do they turn away from Nestorius, while warmly holding fast to his expressions and concepts? ⟦s4⟧ That, saying one will, they champion his teachings, the Ekthesis championed by them also testifies, having declared that Nestorius professes one will in the case of the two persons fabricated by him.

⟦s5⟧ And further, rejecting that the will is natural, they will say it is either hypostatic or contrary to nature. ⟦s6⟧ But if they say it is hypostatic, the Son in this way will be of other counsel than the Father. ⟦s7⟧ For the hypostatic is characteristic of hypostasis alone. ⟦s8⟧ And if contrary to nature, they teach as doctrine the falling-away of his essences, if indeed the things contrary to nature are corruptive of the things according to nature.

⟦s9⟧ Gladly would I ask them this too: whether the God and Father of all wills as Father, or as God? ⟦s10⟧ But if as Father, his will will be other than that of the Son; for the Son is not Father. ⟦s10⟧ But if as God (and the Son is God, God also the Holy Spirit), then they will grant that the will is of nature, that is, natural.

⟦s11⟧ Further, if according to the Fathers, of whom the will is one, [0316] of these the essence too is one; and according to them the will of Christ's Godhead and of his manhood is one; then they say that the essence of these is one and the same. ⟦s12⟧ And how, being thus impious, do they say that they follow the Fathers?

⟦s13⟧ And again, if according to the same Fathers the difference of nature is not shown in the one will, it is necessary for them either, saying one will, not to say natural difference in Christ; or, saying natural difference, not to say one will; if indeed they direct themselves by the patristic canons.

⟦s14⟧ And again, if according to the same teachers the will is not common to both, namely to the essences, it is necessary for them either not to say that both natures of the same one have one will in common; or, saying this, plainly to fight against the patristic laws and decrees.

⟦p32⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ Very clearly and concisely the account has shown the impiety interwoven with every conception of those on the opposite side. ⟦s3⟧ But what do we say to this, that they try to show this from the Fathers too?

⟦p33⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ If they wish to call Fathers those who divide and those who confuse the economy above nature, we grant it to them. ⟦s3⟧ For all of them held the opinion of one will, although standing diametrically apart in their impiety. ⟦s4⟧ But if those of the Church, by no means will we grant this to them. ⟦s5+6⟧ Otherwise, let them show one only of those who are manifest and known to all, so that we too, on the day of the judgement of our deeds, when we are accused by Christ God, «For what reason did you accept a term that dissolves the whole mystery of my incarnation?», may have a defence: that we revered in all things the repute of the Father.

⟦p34⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ What then of what was said by Gregory the Theologian, «For his willing is nothing contrary to God, being wholly deified»: is it not contrary to the two wills?

⟦p35⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ By no means. ⟦s3⟧ On the contrary, it is more expressive of the two wills than even all the others.

⟦p36⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ How dost thou say this?

⟦p37⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Just as burning brings in with itself the burned and the burning, and cooling the cooled and the cooling, and walking the walking and the walked, and seeing the seeing and the seen, and intellection the intellecting and the intellected (for it is not possible to think or to speak of the relation without the related), so, by consequence, [deification brings in] both the deified and the deifying. ⟦s3⟧ Besides, if the deification of the will is contrary to the two wills according to them, the deification of the nature too will be contrary to the two natures. ⟦s4⟧ For in both cases the Father has set down the same account of deification.

⟦p38⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ The Father's testimony has been shown wholly concurring with the two wills; but one must show Gregory too concordant with it, who [0317] made the Church of the Nyssenes shine. ⟦s3⟧ For they adduce him as thinking one will, from the Father's saying about the Lord: «The soul wills, the body touches; through both the passion flees.» ⟦s4⟧ For they say that the Father said that the Lord's soul wills by the divine will of the Godhead united to him according to hypostasis.

⟦p39⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ Then by the same train he said that the body too touches by the divine touch; and according to them the Godhead too will be tangible. ⟦s3⟧ For what they say about the soul of the Lord, another, transferring it with equal expression to the body, leads them off into the extreme of error.

⟦p40⟧ ⟦s1⟧ PYRRHUS. ⟦s2⟧ Very synoptically hast thou set forth the blasphemy of such an interpretation. ⟦s3⟧ And what do we say also about the testimonies adduced by them from the great Athanasius? ⟦s4⟧ Of which one is this: «The mind of the Lord is not yet Lord, but either volition, or wish, or operation towards something.»

⟦p41⟧ ⟦s1⟧ MAXIMUS. ⟦s2⟧ This too they put forward against themselves. ⟦s3⟧ Wherefore the true account too has used, in all things, their own propositions for the destruction of their own. ⟦s4⟧ For may the truth never be so beggared as to need its own weapons against the opponents. ⟦s5⟧ For if, according to the Father, «The mind of the Lord is not yet Lord», his mind will be wholly other than the Lord; that is, the mind of the Lord is not by nature Lord, that is God; for it is believed to have become his according to hypostasis. ⟦s5⟧ And this is plain from his adding that it is either volition, or wish, or operation towards something; using as a canon for this Clement, truly the philosopher of philosophers, who in the sixth book of the Stromateis defined volition as appetitive mind, and wish as reasonable appetite, or volition about something. ⟦s6⟧ And «operation towards something»
