⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1177] *Of our holy father and teacher Maximus the Confessor.*

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ VARIOUS CHAPTERS ON THEOLOGY AND THE ECONOMY

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *and on virtue and vice.*

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ FIRST CENTURY

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 1. ⟦s2⟧ One is the good that is beyond beginning and beyond essence, the holy Monad of three hypostases, Father and Son and Holy Spirit: an infinite growing-together of three infinites, which has the principle of its being, and of how, and what, and of what sort it is, wholly inaccessible to the things that are. ⟦s3⟧ For it escapes every intellection of those who understand, in no way going out from its hidden inwardness according to nature, and it infinitely extends beyond every knowledge of all knowledges.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 2. ⟦s2⟧ The good that properly is according to essence is that which has neither beginning, nor end, nor cause of being, nor any motion whatever, in respect of being, toward some cause. ⟦s3⟧ But what does not so hold is not properly being either, as having beginning and end and cause of being, and the motion in respect of being toward some cause. ⟦s4⟧ And what is not properly being, even if it is said to be, both is and is said to be by participation, by the wish of what properly is.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 3. ⟦s2⟧ If the Word leads the way before the coming-to-be of beings, there neither was, nor is, nor will be a word higher than the Word. ⟦s3⟧ And the Word is not mindless or without share in life, but endowed with mind and living, as having the begetting Mind [1180] essentially subsisting, the Father, and Life subsisting essentially, coexisting, the Holy Spirit.

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 4. ⟦s2⟧ One God, begetter of one Son, the Father, and source of one Holy Spirit: Monad unconfused, and Trinity undivided; Mind without beginning, alone essentially Begetter of the alone Word without beginning, and source of the alone eternal Life, that is, of the Holy Spirit.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 5. ⟦s2⟧ One God, because one Godhead. ⟦s3⟧ Monad without beginning, and superessential, and without parts, and undivided; the same Monad and Trinity, and so on.

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 6. ⟦s2⟧ If every participation is conceived before those that participate, then plainly the cause of beings lies above all beings, incomparably and in every mode, pre-existing according to nature and conceived before beings: not as essence before accidents, since then the Divine will be shown to be composite, having the hypostasis of beings for the completion of its own existence; but as the superessential before essence. ⟦s3⟧ For if the arts devised the shapes of the things that come from them, and universal nature the species of the things under it, much more did God make the essences of beings subsist out of things that are not, as superessential, and still more as infinitely exalted beyond the positing of superessentiality: he who also yoked the sciences to the arts for the devising of shapes, and gave to nature the activity that produces the species, and made subsist the very being of essences, whatever it is.

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 7. ⟦s2⟧ He who is not participable by beings according to essence, but wishes to be participated in another mode by those who are able, in no way departs from what is hidden according to essence; since even the mode itself in which, willing, he is participated remains perpetually undisclosed to all. ⟦s3⟧ So then, as God, willing, is participated in the mode which he [1181] himself knows, so too, willing, he made the participants subsist according to the principle which he himself understands, through the surpassing power of goodness. ⟦s4⟧ So then, what has come to be by the volition of him who made it could never be co-eternal with him who willed it to come to be.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1+2+3⟧ 8. ⟦s1+2+3⟧ The Word of God, born once for all according to the flesh, is always born willingly according to the spirit, through love for man, in those who are willing; and he becomes a babe, forming himself in them by the virtues; and he shows himself[?] so far as he knows the one who receives him has room, not diminishing out of envy the manifestation of his own greatness, but weighing out by measure the capacity of those who long to see. ⟦s1+2+3⟧ Thus the Word of God, though always appearing in the modes of those who participate in him, always remains, by the excess of the mystery, unseen by all. ⟦s4⟧ Whence the divine Apostle, wisely examining the power of the mystery, says: *Jesus Christ, yesterday and today, the same, and unto the ages*, knowing the mystery to be ever new, and never grown old by comprehension.

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 9. ⟦s2⟧ Christ God is born, becoming man by the assumption of flesh having a noetic soul; he who granted to beings their coming-to-be out of things that are not; bearing whom above nature, the Virgin undid no token of her virginity. ⟦s3⟧ For as he himself became man without altering his nature or changing his power, so he makes her who bore him both mother, and keeps her virgin, at once interpreting wonder by wonder in the same respect, and hiding the one by the other. ⟦s4⟧ For God, of himself, is always according to essence a mystery, bringing himself out of his natural hiddenness so far as to make it, through the manifestation, still more hidden; and again making her who bore him, a mother, a virgin so far as to make the child-bearing an unbreakable bond of virginity.

⟦p14⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 10. ⟦s2⟧ Natures are innovated, and God becomes man: not only the divine nature, stable and unmoved, moving toward the nature that is moved and unstable, that it may stop it from being borne along; nor only the human, without seed, above nature, cultivating flesh brought to completion by the Word, that it may stand from being borne along; but also a star from the east, appearing by day and guiding the magi to the place of the Word's incarnation, [n: Matth. ii, 1 seqq.] to show mystically that the word in the Law and the Prophets surpasses sense, and guides the nations to the greatest light of full knowledge. ⟦s3⟧ For plainly the word of the Law and of the Prophets, understood piously like a star, guides to the full knowledge of the incarnate Word those called according to intent by the power of grace. [n: Rom. viii, 28.]

⟦p15⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 11. ⟦s2⟧ Since I, man, willingly transgressed the divine commandment, and the devil, luring the firmness of my nature with the hope of Godhead, dragged it down toward pleasure, [1184] by which, having made death subsist, he exulted, revelling in the corruption of nature: for this reason God becomes perfect man, passing over nothing of nature save sin, since that was not of nature at all; so that, luring with the bait of the flesh, he might provoke the insatiable dragon, gaping, to swallow the flesh, which would become poison to him, utterly destroying him by the power of the Godhead within it, but to the nature of men an antidote, recalling it to the grace that was from the beginning by the power of the Godhead within it. ⟦s3⟧ For as he, pouring the venom of his vice into the tree of knowledge, destroyed the nature that tasted it, so too he, wishing to devour the flesh of the Master, was destroyed by the power of the Godhead within it.

⟦p16⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 12. ⟦s2⟧ The great mystery of the divine becoming man always remains a mystery: not only because, being manifested in proportion to the power of those who are saved by it, it holds what is not yet seen greater than what has been manifested; but because even what has appeared still remains wholly hidden, known by no account as it is. ⟦s3⟧ And let no one think what is said a paradox. ⟦s4⟧ For God, being superessential, and having risen above all superessentiality, wishing to come into essence, was essentiated superessentially. ⟦s5⟧ Hence also, having for men's sake, as lover of man, truly become man out of the essence of men, he holds for ever undisclosed the mode of how he became man; for he became man beyond man.

⟦p17⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 13. ⟦s2⟧ Let us faithfully contemplate the mystery of the divine becoming man, and without curiosity let us only glorify him who was well pleased that this should come to be for our sake. ⟦s3⟧ For who, trusting in the power of logical demonstration, can declare how the conception of God the Word comes about; how the coming-to-be of flesh without seed; how birth without corruption; how she is mother who remained virgin even after childbirth; how he who is more than perfect advanced in stature; [n: Luc. ii, 52.] how the pure one was baptized; how he nourished, who hungered; how he who toiled bestowed strength; how he who suffered gave healings; how he who died gave life; and, to say last what is first, how God becomes man; and what is still more mysterious, how the Word, who according to essence exists hypostatically whole in the Father, is essentially in flesh according to hypostasis? ⟦s4⟧ How the same is both wholly God according to nature, and has become wholly man according to nature, having altogether denied no nature, neither the divine, according to which he is God, nor ours, according to which he has become man? ⟦s5⟧ These mysteries faith alone contains, being the hypostasis of things that are beyond mind and reason. [n: Hebr. xi, 1.]

⟦p18⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1185] 14. ⟦s2⟧ Adam, having disobeyed, taught that the coming-to-be of nature begins from pleasure; the Lord, banishing this from nature, did not admit the conception that is from seed. ⟦s3⟧ The woman, having transgressed the commandment, showed that the birth of nature begins from pain; the Lord, shaking this off from nature, when he was born did not allow her who bore him to undergo corruption: so that at once he might take out of nature both the voluntary pleasure and the involuntary pain that is due to it, of which he was not the creator, becoming their destroyer; and might teach mystically, by inclination, to make a beginning of another life, which begins perhaps from pain and toils, but ends altogether in divine pleasure and gladness without end. ⟦s4⟧ For this reason he who made man becomes man, and is born as man, that he may save man, and, healing passions by passions, may be shown, being himself the passion of our passions, renewing above nature, in his love for man, our habits according to the spirit by his own privations according to the flesh.

⟦p19⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 15. ⟦s2⟧ He who by divine longing has conquered the soul's disposition toward the body has become uncircumscribed, even if he is in the body. ⟦s3⟧ For God, who draws the aspiration of him who longs, is incomparably higher than all, not allowing him who longs to nail his aspiration to any of the things after God. ⟦s4⟧ Let us then long for God with the whole power of our nature, and make our choice such as no bodily thing can hold, and become in disposition above all beings, sensible and intelligible; and in no way, as to inclination, shall we be harmed at all by our natural circumscription in respect of being with God, who is by nature uncircumscribed.

⟦p20⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 16. ⟦s2⟧ The hardship of the saints is a contest of envy and virtue: of the one, contending that it may prevail; of the other, bearing all things that it may remain unconquered; and of the one, struggling that vice may prosper, through the punishment of those who do right; of the other, that it may keep the good holding the advantage even in misfortunes.

⟦p21⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1188] 17. ⟦s2⟧ The contest of virtue is to struggle in toils, victory bringing, as prize to those who endure, the passionlessness of the soul, according to which, united to God through love, it is separated in disposition from body and world. ⟦s3⟧ For to those who endure, the ill-treatment of the body is strength of soul.

⟦p22⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 18. ⟦s2⟧ Beguiled by the deceit of pleasure from the beginning, we preferred death to the life that truly is. ⟦s3⟧ Let us then gratefully bear its slayer, toil of the body, so that, the death of pleasure having made vanish with itself the death that came about through it, we may receive back, running back to us, the life that was sold for pleasure, bought by small toils of the flesh.

⟦p23⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 19. ⟦s2⟧ If, when the flesh fares well, the tone of sin is wont to grow fat, it is clear that when it suffers hardship the tone of virtue naturally grows young again. ⟦s3⟧ Let us then bravely bear the hardship of the flesh, which cleanses the defilements of the soul and procures the glory to come: *For the sufferings of the present time are not worthy of the glory to come, to be revealed to us.* [n: Rom. viii, 18.]

⟦p24⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 20. ⟦s2+3⟧ Neither do physicians, treating the body, apply one and the same medicine to all, nor does God, healing the diseases of souls, know only one mode of treatment fitting for all; but, assigning to each the suitable one, he works the healings. ⟦s4⟧ Let us then give thanks while we are being treated, even if what is done brings torment; for the end is blessed.

⟦p25⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 21. ⟦s2⟧ Nothing so tests the disposition of the soul as the uprisings of the flesh suffering hardship: if it gives way to them, it will appear to cherish the flesh more than God; but if it remains unshaken by their jolts, it will be shown to honour virtue more than the flesh; through which it will receive as indweller God, who for its sake endured to suffer as we do, crying, as once to the disciples: *Take courage, I have overcome the world.* [n: Joan. xvi, 33.]

⟦p26⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 22. ⟦s2⟧ If all the saints have become partakers of discipline, let us too give thanks, being disciplined with them, that we may be counted worthy to become partakers of their glory. ⟦s2⟧ *For whom the Lord loves he disciplines, and scourges every son whom he receives.* [n: Prov. iii, 11: Hebr. xii, 6.]

⟦p27⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 23. ⟦s2⟧ Adam, accepting the side that held out pleasure, puts the human outside paradise; but the Lord, having this side torn open through toil by the lance, made the thief to be within paradise. ⟦s3⟧ Let us therefore love the toil of the flesh, and hate its pleasure: for the one brings in and restores to good things; the other drives out and sets apart from good things.

⟦p28⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 24. ⟦s2⟧ If God suffers in the flesh, having become man, who that suffers does not rejoice, having God as fellow-partaker [1189] of the passion? ⟦s2⟧ For him, suffering with him becomes the procurer of the kingdom. ⟦s3⟧ For true is he who said: *If indeed we suffer with him, that we may also be glorified with him.* [n: Rom. viii, 17.]

⟦p29⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 25. ⟦s2⟧ If one must toil at all because of the pleasure mixed into nature through the forefather, let us bravely endure the temporary toils, which blunt for us the sting of pleasure, and free us from the everlasting punishments due to it.

⟦p30⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 26. ⟦s2⟧ Love is the end of all good things, since toward God, the highest of goods and cause of every good, it leads and brings near and gathers together those who walk in it, as faithful and unfailing and abiding. ⟦s3⟧ For faith is the foundation of those after it, I mean of hope and love, firmly establishing the true. ⟦s4⟧ And hope is the strength of the extremes, I mean of love and faith, revealing through itself what is to be believed and what is to be yearned for by both, and teaching to make through itself the course toward it. ⟦s5⟧ And love is the completion of these, embracing wholly and whole the last object of appetite, and providing for them rest from the motion toward it, introducing through itself, in place of believing that it is and hoping that it will be present, the enjoying of it as present.

⟦p31⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 27. ⟦s2⟧ The most perfect work of love, and the limit of the activity according to it, is, by a relational exchange of those joined by it, to make the idioms, and the names, befit one another; and to make man god, and God to be called and to appear man, because of the one and invariant wish and motion of both according to will.

⟦p32⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 28. ⟦s1⟧ If we are wholly according to the image of God, let us become our own and God's; or rather, of God alone and whole, and wholly, bearing nothing earthly in ourselves, that we may draw near to God and become gods, receiving from God our being gods. ⟦s2⟧ For thus the divine gifts are honoured, and the presence of the divine peace is welcomed.
