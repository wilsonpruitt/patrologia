⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1216] through which, as Word, he leads up those who do them to the Father, in whom he is by nature.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 85. ⟦s2⟧ A change of life and angelic worship, and the soul's voluntary alienation toward the body, and the coming-to-be of the divine remaking in the spirit: these the mystery of the New Covenant proclaims. ⟦s3⟧ And the word is wont to call spiritual circumcision the cutting-away of the soul's impassioned relation toward the body.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 86. ⟦s2⟧ God, being good and willing to pluck out of us altogether pleasure, the seed of vice, which has robbed the mind of the divine love, permits the devil to bring toils and punishments upon us: at the same time both scraping from the soul, through the toils, the rust of the pleasure that came before, and wishing to produce in us hatred and complete estrangement toward present things, which fawn on sense alone, as things that in use have won nothing more than punishment by way of profit; and to make that one's punishing and man-hating power a cause, by way of distress, of the return to virtue for those who have voluntarily slipped away from it.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 87. ⟦s2⟧ It is fitting and just that those be punished by the devil who gladly received his evil suggestions of voluntary sins. ⟦s3⟧ For the devil is both the sower of pleasure through the voluntary passions, and the bringer of pain through the involuntary.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 88. ⟦s2⟧ The contemplative mind, the mind of knowledge, is often handed over to the devil for punishment for this reason, he justly bringing toils and calamities upon it: that by suffering it may learn to philosophize about endurance and patience in toils, rather than to be proudly and idly vain about things that are not.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 89. ⟦s2+3⟧ He who suffers on account of the grace of God that has been transgressed, if he recognizes the principle of the divine providence that is healing him, both receives the calamity thankfully, rejoicing, and corrects the fault for which he is being disciplined; but he who is insensible to this healing is both justly led away from the grace that was given and handed over to the confusion of the passions, being left to come to the doing of those things for which he inwardly had the aspiration.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 90. ⟦s2⟧ Everyone who, out of awareness of what he has done, voluntarily endures to receive with the fitting thanksgiving the toilsome onsets of involuntary temptations, is not banished from the habit and grace according to virtue, as one who has voluntarily gone under the yoke of the king of Babylon, [n: Jerem. xxvii, 6 seqq.] and who accepts the onsets of torments as paying off a debt; but, remaining in them, he pays to the king of Babylon the violent toils that come from the passible part of the nature, and the assent of the understanding [1217] to them, as being their debtor because of the faults that went before; and to God, offering through true worship, I mean the lowly disposition, the correction of what was neglected.

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 91. ⟦s2⟧ He who does not receive with thanksgiving the calamity brought upon him for correction, by God's permission, through the involuntary temptations, and who does not, by changing his mind, put off the conceit of seeming to be just, as one who resists the divine decrees of the just judgements, and who does not consent to come voluntarily under the yoke of the king of Babylon according to the divine ordinance, is handed over into captivity, and to collars and bonds and pestilence and death and sword, and is altogether exiled from his own land. ⟦s3⟧ For all these things, and more than these, he suffers who is banished from the habit according to virtue and knowledge as from his own land; because out of pride and vain conceit he does not will, paying the penalties for the things in which he did amiss, to take pleasure in afflictions and necessities and distresses, according to the divine Apostle. ⟦s4⟧ For the great Apostle knew that the humility which comes about outwardly in the body through toils is protective of the divine treasures in the soul; and for this reason he endured contentedly, both for his own sake and for those to whom he had been set forth as a type of virtue and faith; so that, even if they suffer as answerable, like the Corinthian who was rebuked, [n: I Cor. v, 5.] they might have, for consolation and for imitation of patience, him who suffers without being answerable.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 92. ⟦s2⟧ He who does not remain in the shapes of visible things because of sense, but seeks out their principles with the mind, contemplating them as types of intelligible things or principles of sensible creatures, is taught that nothing of what is seen is unclean; for all things are by nature *very good*. [n: Gen. i, 31.]

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 93. ⟦s2⟧ He who is not altered together with the motion of sensible things pursues the unadulterated practice of the virtues. ⟦s3⟧ He who has not imprinted his mind with their shapes has received the true opinion of beings. ⟦s4⟧ And he who with his understanding has run past even the very essence of beings, as an excellent theologian, has after it unknowingly drawn near to the Monad.

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 94. ⟦s2⟧ Every contemplative mind, having *the sword of the spirit, which is the word of God*, [n: Ephes. vi, 17.] having slain within itself the motion of the visible creation, has achieved virtue; and having cut off from itself the imagination of sensible shapes, has found the truth in the principles of [1220] beings, in which natural contemplation consists; and having come to be above the essence of beings, receives the illumination of the divine and unassailable Monad, in which the mystery of true theology consists.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 95. ⟦s2⟧ To each, according to the opinion of God that underlies him, God appears: to those who in aspiration have passed beyond material composition, and possess the powers of the soul equal in power to one another according to one and the same ever-moving around God, he shows himself as Monad and Trinity, that he may both point out his own existence and mystically teach its mode. ⟦s3⟧ But to those who have their aspiration moving about material composition alone, and possess the powers of the soul unconnected with one another, he appears not as he is but as they are, showing that they have laid hold with both hands of the material dyad, by which the bodily world consists, as from matter and form.

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 96. ⟦s2+3⟧ The divine Apostle calls the different activities of the one Holy Spirit different gifts of grace, worked, clearly, by one and the same Spirit. [n: I Cor. xii, 4.] If, then, the manifestation of the Spirit is given according to the measure of the faith in each, in the participation of such and such a gift of grace, each of the faithful clearly receives, according to the proportion of his faith and of the disposition of soul that underlies him, the activity of the Spirit measured to it, bestowing on him the habit suited to the activity of this or that commandment.

⟦p14⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 97. ⟦s2⟧ As one receives *a word of wisdom*, another *a word of knowledge*, another *faith*, and another something else of the gifts of grace of the Spirit enumerated by the great Apostle, [n: ibid. 8-12.] so one receives through the Spirit the gift of grace of love perfect and immediate toward God and having nothing material, according to the proportion of faith; another, through the same Spirit, the gift of grace of perfect love toward the neighbour; and another something else, according to the same Spirit, as I said, each having his own gift of grace worked in him. ⟦s3⟧ For he says that every habit of a commandment that is worked is a gift of grace of the Spirit.

⟦p15⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 98. ⟦s2⟧ The baptism of the Lord is the complete mortification of our choice toward the sensible world; and the cup is the denial even of our present life itself for the sake of truth. [n: Marc. x, 38.]

⟦p16⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 99. ⟦s2⟧ The baptism of the Lord is a type of the toils, voluntary and our own, taken up by intent for the sake of virtue; through which, washing off the stains of conscience, we accept the voluntary death of our choice toward the things that appear; and the cup is a type of the involuntary temptations which, from distress and against our choice, rise up against us for the sake of the truth; [1221] through which, preferring the divine longing even to nature itself, we willingly undergo the death of nature that comes by distress.

⟦p17⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 100. ⟦s2⟧ The baptism has this difference from the cup: that the baptism, for the sake of virtue, makes the choice dead toward the pleasant things of life; while the cup persuades the pious to prefer the truth even to nature itself.

⟦p18⟧ ⟦s1⟧ SECOND CENTURY

⟦p19⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 1. ⟦s2⟧ He set the cup before the baptism, because virtue is for the sake of truth, but not truth for the sake of virtue. ⟦s3⟧ Hence he who does virtue for the sake of truth is not wounded by the arrows of vainglory; but he who cultivates truth for the sake of virtue has the conceit of vainglory living with him.

⟦p20⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 2. ⟦s2⟧ By truth he means the divine knowledge; by virtue, the struggles for truth of those who aspire to it. ⟦s3⟧ He, then, who for the sake of knowledge endures the toils of virtue is not vainglorious, knowing that truth is by nature not to be grasped by the toils; for what is first is not by nature circumscribed by what is second. ⟦s4⟧ But he who cultivates knowledge for the sake of the struggles on its behalf is altogether vainglorious, as thinking he has received the crowns before the sweat; not knowing that the toils are for the sake of the crowns, but not the crowns for the sake of the toils; for every method by nature falls out of exercise once that for which it exists has been accomplished, or seems to have been accomplished.

⟦p21⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 3. ⟦s2⟧ He who cultivates only the form of knowledge, which is the bare word, and he who cultivates the image of virtue, which is the bare manner, is himself also a Jew, puffed up with types of the truth.

⟦p22⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 4. ⟦s2⟧ He who sees the whole visible worship of the Law not according to sense, but has examined each of the visible symbols by the approaches of the mind, being taught the God-perfect Word hidden in each, finds God in the Law, groping well with the noetic power, as in a heap of sweepings, in the matter of the legal ordinances, whether he may somewhere find, hidden in the flesh of the Law, the pearl, the Word, which altogether escapes sense.

⟦p23⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 5. ⟦s2⟧ And he too who does not circumscribe the nature of visible things by sense alone, but wisely searches out with the mind [1224] the principle in each creature, finds God, being taught from the magnificence of beings set forth the cause of beings themselves. [n: Sap. xiii, 5.]

⟦p24⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 6. ⟦s2⟧ Since, then, distinction is proper to him who gropes, he who goes through the symbols of the Law in the way of knowledge, and contemplates the visible nature of beings scientifically, distinguishing Scripture and creation and himself — Scripture into letter and spirit; creation into principle and surface; himself into mind and sense — and taking, of Scripture, the spirit; of creation, the principle; of himself, the mind; and uniting them unbreakably with one another, has found God; as having recognized, as is fitting and as is possible, God who is in Mind and Word and Spirit; having been freed from all the things that lead astray and drag down into countless opinions, I mean letter and surface and sense, in which is the differing quantity, opposed to the Monad. ⟦s3⟧ But if anyone intertwines the letter of the Law and the surface of visible things and his own sense with one another and commixes them, he *is blind, short-sighted*, sick with ignorance of the Cause of beings.

⟦p25⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 7. ⟦s2+3⟧ The divine and great Apostle, defining what faith is, says: *Faith is the hypostasis of things hoped for, the proof of things not seen*. [n: Hebr. xi, 1.] And if anyone should define it also as an inward good, or as true knowledge demonstrative of the ineffable goods, he would not have missed the truth.

⟦p26⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 8. ⟦s2⟧ Faith is a relational power, or an efficacious relation, of the perfect, immediate union, above nature, of him who believes with the God in whom he believes.

⟦p27⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 9. ⟦s2⟧ Since, then, man, being of soul and body, is tossed by two laws, I mean of flesh and of spirit; and the law of the flesh has its activity according to sense, and that of the spirit according to the mind; and the law of the flesh is wont to bind to matter, being active according to sense, while that of the spirit, being active according to the mind, effects the union with God immediately: fittingly *he who is not two-minded in his heart* — that is, who has not distinguished in the mind, or cut asunder, the immediate union with God that has come about through faith — as passionless, or rather as one already become god through faith by the union, *will say to this mountain* to move, *and it will move*; [1225] by saying *this*, showing as with a pointing finger the way of thinking and the law of the flesh, which is truly heavy and hard to shift, and, as far as natural power goes, altogether immovable and unshakeable.

⟦p28⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 10. ⟦s2⟧ So deeply has the power of irrationality been rooted in the nature of men through sense, that the many think man to be nothing else than flesh, having sense as a power for the enjoyment of the present life.

⟦p29⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 11. ⟦s2⟧ *All things*, he says, *are possible to him who believes*, [n: Marc. ix, 22.] and is not two-minded, that is, who is not divided from the union with God that has come about for him in the mind through faith, because of the relation of the soul to the body according to sense: all things, that is, that estrange the mind from world and flesh, and make it akin to God, perfected by its achievements. ⟦s2⟧ For this must be understood in addition to the words *All things are possible to him who believes*.

⟦p30⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 12. ⟦s2⟧ Faith is knowledge without demonstration. ⟦s3⟧ And if it is knowledge without demonstration, then faith is a relation above nature, through which we are united to God unknowingly, and not demonstratively, by the union above intellection.

⟦p31⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 13. ⟦s2⟧ The mind that has received the immediate union with God has the power of thinking and being thought altogether idle. ⟦s3⟧ Whenever, then, it looses this, having thought of something of the things that come after God, it has become two-minded, cutting the union above intellection, according to which, so long as it is joined to God, as having become above nature and god by participation, it removes from itself, like an immovable mountain, the law of nature.

⟦p32⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 14. ⟦s2⟧ He who is being introduced to piety, being taught about the works of justice, performs the practice alone, with all obedience and faith, as though eating, like flesh, the visible things of the virtues, that is, the moral instruction; but the principles of the commandments, in which the knowledge of the perfect consists, he yields to God through faith, being unable as yet to stretch himself out along with the length of knowledge.
