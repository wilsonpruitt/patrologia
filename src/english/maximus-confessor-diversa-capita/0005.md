⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1228] virtues, raising up to knowledge according to the mind the motion of the things that come to be according to sense.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 16. ⟦s2⟧ The Lord, saying, *Seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness* [n: Matth. vi, 33.] — that is, before all things the full knowledge of the truth, and so the exercise of the fitting modes — clearly showed that those who believe must make their inquiry concerning divine knowledge alone, and the virtue that adorns it through works.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 17. ⟦s2⟧ Since the things sought by those who believe toward the knowledge of God and virtue happen to be many — release from passions, endurance of temptations, principles of virtues, modes of activities, the unnailing of the soul's disposition toward the flesh, the estrangement of sense's relation to sensible things, the mind's complete withdrawal from all things that have come to be. ⟦s3⟧ And, simply, there are countless others, those that make for abstinence from vice and ignorance, and for the achievement of knowledge and virtue: fittingly the Lord said, *All things whatsoever you ask believing, you shall receive*; [n: Matth. xxi, 22.] saying that the pious must both seek and ask, with science and faith, simply all things — only those that tend toward the full knowledge of God and virtue. ⟦s4⟧ For all these are profitable, and the Lord certainly gives them to those who ask.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 18. ⟦s2⟧ He, then, who for the sake of faith alone, that is, the immediate union with God, seeks all the things that make for the union, will certainly receive; but he who, without this cause, seeks either other things or the things aforesaid, will not receive. ⟦s3⟧ For he does not believe, but as an unbeliever he trades on the divine things for his own glory.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 19. ⟦s2⟧ He who by inclination keeps pure of the corruption that is according to sin corrupts the corruption of the things whose nature it is to corrupt. ⟦s3⟧ For the incorruption of choice is of a nature to keep the corruption of nature incorrupt, according to providence, through the grace of the Spirit that is in it, not permitting it to be corrupted by the contrary qualities.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 20. ⟦s2⟧ Since there is not one and the same principle of nature and of grace, it is not worth puzzling over how some of the saints at times rose above passions and at times rather fell under passions; since we know that the wonder was of grace, and the passion of nature.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 21. ⟦s2⟧ He who through imitation holds the memory of the way of life of the saints puts off the deadness of the passions and takes up the life of the virtues.

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 22. ⟦s2⟧ In the manner that he wills, God, who marked out the life of each before the ages, leads each to the end proper to his life, whether just or unjust.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 23. ⟦s2⟧ The gloomy storm that befell the blessed Paul I judge to be the weight of involuntary temptations; the island, the firm habit of [1229] divine hope, and unshakeable; the fire, the habit of knowledge; the sticks, the nature of visible things, which he gathered with his hand — I mean with the mind's tactile power according to contemplation — nourishing with the concepts drawn from it the habit of knowledge, which heals the dejection rubbed onto the understanding by the storm of temptations; the viper, the wicked and destructive power secretly hidden in the nature of sensible things, which bit the hand, that is, the tactile activity of contemplation according to the mind, but did not harm the clear-sighted mind, which at once destroyed by the light of knowledge, as in fire, the destructive power fastened onto the practical movement of the mind from the contemplation of sensible things.

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 24. ⟦s2⟧ The Apostle was a savour from life unto life, as preparing the faithful, by the example of himself, to be moved through practice toward the fragrance of the virtues; or as a herald carrying over those persuaded by the word of grace from the life according to sense into the life in the spirit. ⟦s3⟧ And a savour of death unto death to those who from the death of ignorance had driven on into the death of unbelief, giving them a sense of the condemnation that awaits them. ⟦s4⟧ Or again, a savour from life unto life to those who are led up from practice to contemplation; and a savour of death unto death to those who, from making dead, by idleness as to sin, *the members that are upon the earth*, [n: Coloss. iii, 5.] pass over into the praised deadness of passionate concepts and imaginations.

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 25. ⟦s2⟧ Three are the powers of the soul: reason, anger, and desire. ⟦s3⟧ By reason we seek; by desire we long for the good that was sought; by anger we contend on its behalf. ⟦s4⟧ According to these powers, then, those who love God, persevering in the divine word of virtue and of knowledge — by the one seeking, by the other longing, by the other contending on its behalf — receive food incorruptible and fattening the mind: the knowledge of the things that have come to be.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 26. ⟦s2⟧ The nature emptied of the knowledge that had been given it, the Word of God, having become man, filled again with knowledge; and, tempering it to unchangeableness, not by nature but by quality, he deified it, stamping it without deficiency with his own Spirit, as one who blends water with the quality of wine for strength. ⟦s3⟧ For it is for this that he truly becomes man, that by grace he may make us gods.

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 27. ⟦s2⟧ God, who created the nature of men, together with giving it being by his wish, fitted to it also a power productive of what is fitting; and by power I mean the motion that is essentially sown [1232] in the nature toward the activity of the virtues, but by inclination is manifested, in use, according to the wish of its possessor.

⟦p14⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 28. ⟦s2⟧ We have a natural criterion, the law according to nature, which teaches us that before the wisdom in all things our aspiration ought to be moved toward the mystagogy of the maker of all things.

⟦p15⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 29. ⟦s2⟧ The well of Jacob is Scripture; the water is the knowledge in Scripture; the depth, the hard-to-penetrate setting of the enigmas of Scripture; the bucket is the learning, through the letters, of the divine word, which the Lord did not have, being the Word himself, and giving to those who believe not the knowledge that comes from learning and study, but bestowing on the worthy the ever-flowing wisdom that comes from spiritual grace, which never ceases. ⟦s3⟧ For the bucket, that is, learning, taking a very small part of knowledge, leaves the whole, grasped in no way. ⟦s4⟧ But the knowledge according to grace has, even without study, the whole of the wisdom attainable by men, welling up variously according to the needs.

⟦p16⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 30. ⟦s2⟧ The tree of life and that which is not such have, from this alone — that the one was named tree of life, and the other not of life, but only of knowledge of good and evil — a great and unspeakable difference. ⟦s3⟧ For the tree of life is certainly also productive of life; but the tree not of life is clearly productive of death. ⟦s4⟧ For what is not productive of life, from not having been called tree of life, would clearly be productive of death; for nothing else is divided over against life by opposition.

⟦p17⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 31. ⟦s2⟧ The tree of life, also as wisdom, has the greatest difference from the tree of knowing good and evil, which neither is wisdom nor is so named. ⟦s3⟧ For proper to wisdom are mind and reason; but proper to the habit set over against wisdom as its contrary are irrationality and sense.

⟦p18⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 32. ⟦s2⟧ Since man came into coming-to-be composed of a noetic soul and a sentient body, let the tree of life be, by one approach, the mind of the soul, in which the very thing that wisdom is resides; and the tree of knowledge of good and evil, the sense of the body, in which clearly resides the motion of irrationality, [1233] which man, having received the divine commandment not to touch it by way of experience through activity, did not keep. [n: Gen. ii, 17.]

⟦p19⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 33. ⟦s2⟧ Both trees according to Scripture, that is, the mind and sense, are able to distinguish certain things: for example, the mind has a power that distinguishes intelligibles and sensibles, temporal things and everlasting; or rather, being a distinguishing power of the soul, it persuades her to cling to the one and to rise above the other. ⟦s3⟧ And sense has a power that distinguishes pleasure and pain of bodies; or rather, being a power of ensouled and sentient bodies, it persuades them to draw pleasure to themselves and to send pain away.

⟦p20⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 34. ⟦s2⟧ Man, if he gives himself to the sensory distinction of bodies alone, according to pleasure and pain, transgressing the divine commandment, eats of the tree of knowledge of good and evil, that is, the irrationality according to sense; having only the distinction that sustains bodies, by which he clings to pleasure as good and holds off from pain as evil. ⟦s3⟧ But if he gives himself wholly to the noetic distinction alone, which distinguishes temporal things from the everlasting, keeping the divine commandment, he eats of the tree of life — I mean the wisdom that subsists according to the mind; having only the distinction that sustains the soul, by which he clings to the glory of everlasting things as good and holds off from the corruption of temporal things as evil.

⟦p21⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 35. ⟦s2⟧ The good of the mind is the passionless disposition toward the spirit; its evil, the passionate relation to sense. ⟦s2⟧ And the good of sense is the passionate motion toward the body according to pleasure; its evil, the disposition that supervenes by privation of this.

⟦p22⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 36. ⟦s2⟧ He who has persuaded his conscience to hold the worst, when it is done, as good by nature, he, stretching out his practical part like a hand, took blameworthily of the tree of life, thinking the worst immortal by nature. ⟦s3⟧ Wherefore God, having naturally placed in man the accusation of evil according to the conscience, set him apart from life, since he had become evil by choice; so that, doing evil, he might not be able to persuade his own conscience that the worst is good by nature.

⟦p23⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 37. ⟦s1⟧ The vine makes wine; wine, drunkenness; drunkenness, ecstasy. ⟦s2⟧ Therefore the active word, which is the vine, cultivated by the virtues, begets knowledge; and knowledge begets the good ecstasy, which puts the mind out of its relation to sense.

⟦p24⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 38. ⟦s2⟧ To the concepts of sensible things the evil one is wont knavishly to join the forms and shapes of sensible things, through which the passions are of a nature to be made about the surfaces of visible things, while, through the sense that lies between, the [1236] crossing of the rational activity within us toward intelligible things comes to rest. ⟦s3⟧ Wherefore he also prevails to plunder the soul and to drag it down into the confusion of the passions.

⟦p25⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 39. ⟦s2⟧ The word of God is a lamp and, at the same time, likewise a light: [n: Psal. cxviii, 105; Prov. vi, 23.] both as illumining the thoughts of the faithful that are according to nature, and as burning those contrary to nature; and as dissolving the gloom of the life according to sense for those who press on through the commandments toward the life that is hoped for, and as punishing with the burning of judgement those who by inclination, through love of the flesh, cling to this dark night of life.

⟦p26⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 40. ⟦s2⟧ He who has not first, he says, been led up to himself through the casting-off of the passions contrary to nature will not be led up to his own cause, that is, God, through the acquisition in grace of the goods above nature. ⟦s3⟧ For he who is truly gathered to God must be separated in understanding from the things that have been made.

⟦p27⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 41. ⟦s2⟧ The work of the written law is release from the passions; of the natural law, equality of honour toward all men according to equality of right; and the perfection of the spiritual law, assimilation to God, as far as is possible for man.

⟦p28⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 42. ⟦s2⟧ The power of the mind is by nature receptive of the knowledge of bodies and of bodiless things; but of that of the holy Trinity it receives the reflections by grace alone, believing only that it is, but not presuming as to what it is according to essence, as the demonic mind does. ⟦s3⟧ For he who has no share of knowledge in no way knows the mode, according to virtue, that cleanses vice.

⟦p29⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 43. ⟦s2⟧ He who loves the lie is handed over to it for destruction, so that he may know by suffering what he willingly cherished, and, coming to experience, learn that unawares he was embracing death instead of life.

⟦p30⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 44. ⟦s2⟧ God has the knowledge of the good alone, as being according to essence both the nature and the knowledge of the good; but of evil, ignorance, as having incapacity for it; for of the things of which he naturally has the power, of these he essentially possesses the knowledge.

⟦p31⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 45. ⟦s2⟧ The breast in Leviticus signifies the better [1237] and lofty contemplation; and the arm, practice, that is, the habit and activity of the discursive part, or knowledge and virtue: since knowledge brings the mind immediately to God himself, and virtue, according to practice, withdraws it from all the coming-to-be of beings; which things the word set apart for the priests, who through all things have acquired God alone as an inheritance and possess nothing whatever earthly.

⟦p32⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 46. ⟦s2⟧ Since those who are wholly qualified in the spirit by knowledge and virtue make the hearts of others, through the word of teaching, receptive of piety and faith, and, withdrawing their practical habit and power from the pursuits that concern the corruptible nature, transfer them to the activity of the incorruptible goods above nature: fittingly the Word commanded that, of the things brought for a sacrifice to God, the breast of the wave-offering — that is, the heart of those who are brought — and the arm — that is, clearly, their practice — be consecrated to the priests.

⟦p33⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 47. ⟦s2⟧ All justice here, compared with that to come, holds the role of a mirror, having the image of the archetypal realities, but not the realities themselves subsisting in form; and all knowledge here of lofty things, compared with that to come, is an enigma, having a reflection of the truth, but not having, subsisting, the very truth that is to be made manifest.

⟦p34⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 48. ⟦s2⟧ Since divine things are held together by virtue and knowledge, the mirror is indicative of the prototypes according to virtue, and the enigma is manifestive of the archetypes according to knowledge.

⟦p35⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 49. ⟦s2⟧ He who has pleased God through practice clearly transfers his mind through contemplation to the region of intelligible things, [n: Gen. v, 24; Hebr. xi, 5.] so that through some imagination he may not behold, according to sense, the death that is in the passions, since he is not found at all by any of those who wish to seize him.
