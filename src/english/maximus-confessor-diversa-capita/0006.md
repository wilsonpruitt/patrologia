⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1240] the cause of nature, as the great Abraham preferred God to Isaac. [n: Gen. xxii, 1 seqq.]

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 51. ⟦s2⟧ He who makes it his practice to pursue virtue, or the study of the divine words, neither for the sake of glory, nor on the pretext of greed, nor for the sake of flattery, and of man-pleasing and display, but who both does and says and thinks all things for God's sake — this man walks in the way of truth in knowledge. ⟦s3⟧ For the divine word is not of a nature to linger in paths not straight, [n: Isa. xl, 3.] even if it finds the way ready in some.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 52. ⟦s2⟧ One fasts, and abstains from the diet that kindles the passions, and does the other things that can contribute to release from vice: this man has prepared the way spoken of; [n: ibid. 4; Luc. iii, 5.] but for vainglory, greed, or flattery, or man-pleasing, or for the sake of some other cause, apart from the divine good pleasure, he practises such manners: this man has not made the paths of God straight; and he endured the toil of preparing the way, but did not have God walking about in his paths.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 53. ⟦s2⟧ *Every valley shall be filled*: [n: ibid.] not simply every one, nor of all; for not of those who have not prepared the way of the Lord and made his paths straight. ⟦s3⟧ When, then, the valley — that is, the flesh or soul — of those who, as I said, have prepared the way of the Lord and made his paths straight is filled with knowledge and virtue, through the presence of God the Word who walks about in them through the commandments, all the spirits of *the knowledge falsely so called* and of vice are brought low, the Word treading them down and subjecting them, and casting down the evil might that exalts itself against human nature, and as it were digging down the greatness and the height of the mountains and the hills, and bringing them to the filling of the valleys. ⟦s4⟧ For the casting-off of the passions contrary to nature, and the putting-on of the virtues according to nature, fills up the soul that has been made a valley and brings low the stirred-up dominion of the evil spirits.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 54. ⟦s2⟧ *The rough ways* (that is, the onsets of involuntary temptations) *shall become smooth ways*, especially when the mind, rejoicing and glad, takes pleasure in weaknesses and afflictions and necessities, removing through involuntary toils the whole dominion of the voluntary passions. ⟦s3⟧ For it has called *rough ways* the occurrences of involuntary temptations, which change into smooth ways through endurance with thanksgiving.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 55. ⟦s2⟧ He who aspires to true life, knowing that every toil, whether voluntary or involuntary, becomes the death of pleasure, the mother of death, will receive with gladness, rejoicing, all the rough [1241] onsets of involuntary temptations; making the afflictions, through endurance, easy and smooth ways, which escort him unerringly to *the prize of the high calling* [n: Philipp. iii, 14.] as he piously runs the divine course in them. ⟦s3⟧ For indeed the mother of death is pleasure; and the death of pleasure is toil, both the elective and that which is against choice.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 56. ⟦s2⟧ Everyone, therefore, who by self-control has untied the many-coiled and many-twisted pleasure, interwoven in many ways with all sensible things together, *has made the crooked straight*; and he who through endurance has trodden down the hard-to-pass and rough onset of toils *has made the rough ways smooth*. ⟦s3⟧ Hence, as a prize of virtue and of the labours undertaken for it, as one who has contended well and lawfully, and conquered pleasure by longing for virtue, and trodden down pain by yearning for knowledge, and through both nobly carried through the divine contests, *he shall see*, as it is written, *the salvation of God*. [n: Isa. xl, 5.]

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 57. ⟦s2⟧ He who loves virtue willingly makes the furnace of pleasures wither; and he who is qualified in mind by the knowledge of the truth is not held back by involuntary toils, since the ever-moving according to aspiration bears him toward God.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 58. ⟦s2⟧ He who through self-control has straightened the crooked things of the voluntary passions, that is, the movements of pleasure, and through endurance has levelled the rough things, the misfortunes of involuntary temptations, that is, the modes of pain, and has set them as smooth ways — such a man shall fittingly see the salvation of God, having become pure in heart; in which, through the virtues and pious objects of contemplation, he sees God at the end of the contests, according to *Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God*, [n: Ibid. 9.] having received in return for the toils for virtue the grace of passionlessness, than which nothing more manifests God to those who have it.

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 59. ⟦s2⟧ Scripture calls cisterns the hearts receptive of the heavenly gifts of grace of holy knowledge, which are hewn out by the firm word of the commandments, and cast off, like callosities, both the love of pleasure toward the passions and the relation of nature to sensible things, and are filled with the knowledge borne from above, that cleanses the passions and gives life to, and as it were nourishes, the virtues in the spirit.

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1244] 60. ⟦s1⟧ The Lord hews cisterns in the desert — I mean in the world and in the nature of men — digging out the hearts of the worthy, and cleansing them of the material weight and way of thinking, and making them spacious for the reception of the divine rains of wisdom and of knowledge, so that they may water the cattle of Christ — I mean those who, through infancy of soul, need moral teaching.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 61. ⟦s2⟧ Scripture calls hill country the lofty contemplation of nature in the spirit, which those cultivate who have died away from the imaginations of sensible things and have crossed over, through the virtues in between, to their intelligible principles.

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 62. ⟦s2⟧ The mind, so long as it has the memory of God living within itself, seeks out the Lord through contemplation; and not simply, but *in the fear of the Lord*, that is, in the practice of the commandments. ⟦s3⟧ For he who seeks out the Lord through contemplation without practice does not find him, because he did not seek out the Lord in the fear of the Lord. ⟦s4⟧ *And the Lord prospered him*; for the Lord prospers everyone who acts with knowledge, both teaching the modes of the commandments and revealing the true principles of beings.

⟦p14⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 63. ⟦s2⟧ The lofty word concerning Godhead is a tower in the soul, fortified by the activities of the commandments. ⟦s3⟧ And this is what it says: *Uzziah* *built towers in Jerusalem*. ⟦s4⟧ For he who is well prospered in the seeking out of the Lord through contemplation, with the fear that befits it — that is, the practice of the commandments — builds towers in Jerusalem, that is, in the simple and quiescent state of the soul, raising high the words concerning Godhead.

⟦p15⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 64. ⟦s2⟧ The principles of particular things, coming over to the universal, make the unions of the things divided; because the more universal comprehend uniformly the principles of the more particular, to which the particulars naturally have their reference. ⟦s3⟧ But also of mind to sense, and of heaven to earth, and of sensibles to intelligibles, and of nature to principle — of these too there is a certain relational principle in the spirit, giving them union with one another.

⟦p16⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 65. ⟦s2⟧ He who has been able to unnail the senses from the passions, and has separated the soul from the relation of the senses, has prevailed to wall off the entry that the devil had made toward the mind through the senses that lie between; and for this reason in the desert — I mean natural contemplation — he has built, as it were secure towers, the pious opinions concerning beings, in which he who takes refuge does not fear the demons who rob in this desert — I mean the nature of visible things — and lead the mind astray through sense and drag it down to the gloom of ignorance. ⟦s3⟧ For he who has acquired the pious opinion concerning each thing does not dread the demons who lead men astray through the things that appear.

⟦p17⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1245] 66. ⟦s2⟧ Every mind that has strength for contemplation is a true husbandman, keeping by its own zeal and care the divine seeds of the good things pure of tares, so long as it has the memory of God preserving it. ⟦s3⟧ For it says: *And he was seeking out the Lord in the days of Zechariah, in the fear of the Lord*. ⟦s4⟧ And the word knows that Zechariah, translated into the Greek tongue, is "memory of God". ⟦s5⟧ Therefore let us always beseech the Lord that his saving memory be guarded for us, lest the very thing that is rightly achieved destroy the soul, lifted up to a height and having dared things above nature, like Uzziah.

⟦p18⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 67. ⟦s2⟧ Unerring contemplation of beings needs a soul released from passions, which is called Jerusalem because of its perfect virtue and its immaterial knowledge; which comes about not only through privation of passions but also of sensible imaginations.

⟦p19⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 68. ⟦s2⟧ Without faith and hope and love, nothing either of evil things is wholly abolished, or of good things achieved at all. ⟦s3⟧ For faith persuades the mind under attack to come over to God, becoming for it an encouragement toward confidence consisting in every equipment of spiritual arms. ⟦s4⟧ And hope becomes for it a most unlying guarantor of divine help, promising the overthrow of the contrary powers. ⟦s5⟧ And love makes it hard to move, or rather wholly unmoved, from divine affection even when under attack, nailing to the divine longing the whole power of its aspiration.

⟦p20⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 69. ⟦s2⟧ Faith encourages the mind under attack, strengthened by the hope of help; hope, bringing before its sight the help that was believed, beats back the onrush of the adversaries; and love makes dead, for the God-loving mind, the assault of the enemies, wholly dimmed by the aspiration toward God.

⟦p21⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 70. ⟦s2⟧ Of the truly divine portion, that is, of true knowledge, the son, first and only, is the word, according to faith, of the divine resurrection within us, with the due economy according to inclination, that is, discernment, nobly bearing the uprisings of both voluntary and involuntary temptations. ⟦s3⟧ For the first resurrection within us of God, who has been put to death in us through ignorance, is faith, well ordered by the works of the commandments.

⟦p22⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 71. ⟦s2⟧ Conversion to God clearly shows through itself the fullest principle of divine hope, without which no assent toward God is of a nature to come about for anyone, in any way, in any respect whatever; since it is proper to hope to bring things to come before sight as present, and to persuade that God the defender is by no [1248] means absent from those warred against by the adverse power, for whose sake and because of whom the saints have their war. ⟦s3⟧ For without some expectation, either hard or easy, conversion toward the good is never of a nature to come about for anyone.

⟦p23⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 72. ⟦s2⟧ Nothing truly so gathers those who are scattered as love does, and creates for them one inclination held fast by concord, whose character is the beauty of equality of honour. ⟦s3⟧ Therefore love is the offspring of the gathering into one, around divine things, and the union of the psychic powers — that is, the rational, the incensive and the desiring; by which those who have already received equality of honour with God through grace, inscribing in memory the beauty of the divine comeliness, hold unforgotten the aspiration of divine love, which records and imprints the undefiled beauty on the ruling part of the soul.

⟦p24⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 73. ⟦s2⟧ Every mind girded with divine strength possesses, as certain elders and rulers, the rational power, from which the faith of knowledge is of a nature to be born, by which it is ineffably taught that God is always present, and through hope it consorts with things to come as present; and the desiring power, by which divine love is constituted, through which, having of its own will nailed itself to the longing for the undefiled Godhead, it holds unloosed its aspiration for what is longed for; and further also the incensive power, by which it clings tenaciously to divine peace, drawing tight the motion of desire toward the divine yearning. ⟦s3⟧ Every mind has these powers working with it toward the overthrow of vice and the establishment and preservation of virtue.

⟦p25⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 74. ⟦s2⟧ Without the rational power there is no scientific knowledge; and without knowledge faith does not subsist, from which hope proceeds as its fair offspring, by which the faithful man consorts with things to come as present. ⟦s3⟧ And without the power according to desire, longing does not subsist, whose end is love; for to yearn for something is proper to desire. ⟦s4⟧ And without the incensive power, bracing desire toward union with what is sweet, peace is in no way of a nature to come about; since peace truly is the untroubled and complete possession of what is desired.

⟦p26⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 75. ⟦s2⟧ He who has not first been cleansed of the passions must not touch natural contemplation, because of the images of sensible things, which are able to stamp toward passion the mind of him who has not been perfectly released from passions. ⟦s3⟧ For the mind that through sense lingers in imagination on the surfaces of sensible things becomes a creator of unclean passions, not being able to cross over through contemplation to the kindred intelligibles.
