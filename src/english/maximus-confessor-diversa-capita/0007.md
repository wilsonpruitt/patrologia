⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1249] having nobly shut the senses, and having utterly thrust away the imagination and memory of sensible things, and having wholly contracted the natural motions of the mind about the search into external things, put to shame, conquering through the divine hand, the wicked and tyrannical dominion that had risen against him.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 77. ⟦s2⟧ When reason becomes mindless, and anger rash, and desire irrational, and ignorance and tyranny and licentiousness rule the soul, then the habit of vice is of a nature to become active in deed, entwined with the varied pleasure of the senses.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 78. ⟦s2⟧ The mind that knows how, in the manner of knowledge, to escape the invisible grapplings must neither pursue natural contemplation, nor do anything else at the time of the assault of the wicked powers than only pray, and subdue the body with toils, and with all zeal make the destruction of the earthly way of thinking, and guard the walls of the city: I mean the virtues that guard the soul, or the methods that guard the virtues; I mean self-control and endurance; lest he who gives the soul a turbid overthrow to drink, [n: Habac. ii, 15.] deceiving through the things on the right hand, should steal the aspiration and turn it away from God, and through things reckoned good should draw down to worse things the understanding that seeks good things.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 79. ⟦s2⟧ He who through reasoned and comprehending self-control and endurance has nobly shut the senses, and through the powers of the soul has walled off the entrances of sensible figures to the mind, easily destroys the wicked devices of the devil, turning him back with shame by the way that he came. ⟦s3⟧ And the way by which the devil comes is the material things that seem to be for the constitution of the body.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 80. ⟦s2⟧ The mind that has joined sense to itself according to nature through reason as a middle term gathers the true knowledge that comes from natural contemplation.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 81. ⟦s2⟧ There are springs outside the city, that is, outside the soul, which Hezekiah stops up: all sensible things; and the waters of these springs are the concepts of sensible things; and the river dividing through the middle of the city is the knowledge gathered by natural contemplation from the concepts [1252] of sensible things, passing through the middle of the soul, as being the boundary of mind and sense. ⟦s3⟧ For the knowledge of sensible things is neither altogether estranged from the noetic power, nor wholly allotted to the activity according to sense; but being, as it were, in the middle of the concourse of the mind with sense and of sense with the mind, it makes through itself their conjunction with one another: by sense, being imprinted according to form with the figures of sensible things; by the mind, transferring the impressions of the figures into principles. ⟦s4⟧ Therefore the knowledge of visible things was fittingly called a river dividing through the middle of the city, as being the border-ground of the extremes, I mean of mind and sense.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 82. ⟦s2⟧ He who at the time of the uprising of temptations abstains from natural contemplation, but holds fast to prayer according to the contraction of the mind from all things toward itself and God, kills the habit that produces vice, and turns back with shame the devil who suggests the said habit, trusting in which he came with his own boastfulness to the soul, exalting himself against the truth through proud thoughts. ⟦s2⟧ This perhaps the great David both knew and suffered and did, he who of all men had most experience of the array of intelligible wars: *When the sinner stood against me*, he says, *I was deafened and humbled, and kept silent from good things*; [n: Psal. xxxviii, 2.] and after him the divine Jeremiah, commanding the people not to go out of the city because of the sword of the enemies that sojourns round about. [n: Jerem. vi, 25.]

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 83. ⟦s2⟧ And so the blessed Abel too, if he had kept guard and not gone out with Cain into the plain, that is, into the breadth of natural contemplation, before passionlessness, the law of the flesh, which both is and is called Cain, would not have risen up and killed him with guile, [n: Gen. iv, 8.] stealing upon him by the things on the right hand in the contemplation of beings before the perfect habit.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 84. ⟦s2⟧ Likewise Dinah too, the daughter of the great Jacob, had she not gone out with the daughters of the natives, that is, with the sensory imaginations, Shechem the son of Hamor would not have risen up and humbled her. [n: Gen. xxxiv, 1 seqq.]

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 85. ⟦s2⟧ It is good for us, before the perfect habit, not to touch natural contemplation, lest, seeking spiritual principles from the visible creatures, we unawares gather passions. ⟦s3⟧ For in the imperfect the apparent figures of visible things prevail more upon sense than the principles of the things that have come to be, hidden in the figures, prevail upon the soul. ⟦s4⟧ Those, however, who have bound their understanding to the letter alone in Jewish fashion expect in this age [1253] the promises of the undefiled goods, ignorant of the goods of the soul according to nature.

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 86. ⟦s2⟧ He who has borne the image of the heavenly hastens to follow through all things the spirit of the holy Scripture, in which, through virtue and knowledge, lies the preservation of the soul; but he who bears the image of the earthy cherishes the letter only, in which consists the worship according to sense directed toward the body, making the passions.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 87. ⟦s2⟧ The might of God is the virtue that destroys the passions and guards the pious thoughts, which the practice of the commandments begets, through which, by the co-operation of God, or rather by the power of God alone, we bring to ruin the wicked powers opposed to the good. ⟦s3+4⟧ And the height of God is the knowledge of the truth, which the toil of the contemplation of the things that have come to be begets, and the sweats over the practice of the virtues, which become fathers of toil; through which we utterly make to vanish the power of falsehood opposed to the truth, humbling and casting down every height of the wicked spirits that exalt themselves against the knowledge of God. [n: ibid.] For just as practice begets virtue, so contemplation begets knowledge.

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 88. ⟦s2⟧ Unforgetting knowledge, having about the divine infinity an unbounded motion according to mind beyond intellection, images through its unboundedness the more-than-infinite glory of the truth; and the self-chosen imitation of the wise goodness that is according to providence brings as its honour the assimilation of the mind to God, most manifest according to disposition, as far as is possible.

⟦p14⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 89. ⟦s2⟧ The tongue is a symbol of the soul's activity of knowledge; and the throat a token of the natural self-love toward the body. ⟦s3⟧ He, then, who has blameworthily glued these to one another cannot remember the peaceful habit according to virtue and knowledge, taking pleasure through zeal in the confusion of the bodily passions.

⟦p15⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 90. ⟦s2⟧ The appetites and pleasures according to nature, bringing no blame upon those who possess them, stand as a necessary consequence of natural appetite. ⟦s3⟧ For pleasure according to nature is produced, even when we do not wish it, by any chance food consoling a want that went before; and by drink, repelling the annoyance of thirst; and by sleep, renewing the power spent by waking; and [1256] by whatever others of our natural things there are, necessary for the constitution of nature, and useful to the earnest for the acquisition of virtue. ⟦s4⟧ These go out together with every mind that flees the confusion of sin, lest through them it remain held fast in slavery to the passions that are in our power, reprehensible and contrary to nature, which have no other origin in us than the motion of the passions according to nature, which are not even of a nature to pass over with us to the immortal and long-lived life.

⟦p16⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 91. ⟦s2⟧ The words of God, spoken barely, in utterance alone, are not heard, not having as their voice the practice of those who speak them. ⟦s3⟧ But if they are voiced by the practice of the commandments, they have both the demons melting away at such a voice, and men, through the progress of the works of justice, readily building the divine temple of the heart.

⟦p17⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 92. ⟦s2⟧ As God according to essence does not fall under knowledge, so neither is his word comprehended by the knowledge that is ours. ⟦s3⟧ For the word of the holy Scripture, even if according to the letter it admits of circumscription, ceasing together with the times of the things narrated, yet according to the spirit, in the contemplations of the things understood, it remains forever uncircumscribed.

⟦p18⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 93. ⟦s2⟧ He who receives the holy Scripture in the manner of knowledge, for the soul, according to Christ, must be exercised laboriously also in the interpretation of the names, being able from it to make clear the whole understanding of what is written, if he cares for the exact understanding of what is written; and not, in Jewish fashion, drag down the height of the spirit to body and earth, and circumscribe the divine and undefiled promises of the intelligible goods by the corruption of the things that pass away.

⟦p19⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 94. ⟦s2⟧ As a vow is a promise of goods brought by men to God by way of promise, so prayer, by the fitting account, will clearly be the request for the goods supplied by God to men for salvation, bearing a return for the good disposition of the things that went before. ⟦s3⟧ And a cry is the increase and augmentation, at the time of the uprising of the wicked demons, of the virtuous modes according to practice and of the objects of contemplation of knowledge according to contemplation; which God hears naturally, not least of all things, [hearing] instead of a great voice the disposition of those who care for virtue and knowledge.

⟦p20⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 95. ⟦s2⟧ The wicked and destructive kingdom [1257] of the devil, typified by the kingdom of the Assyrians, gathering war against virtue and against knowledge, contrives to rout the soul through its innate powers: first rousing desire to an appetite for the things contrary to nature, and persuading it to prefer the sensible things to the intelligible; and stirring up anger to fight on behalf of the sensible thing chosen by desire; and teaching the rational part to devise the modes of the pleasures according to sense.

⟦p21⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 96. ⟦s2⟧ It is proper to the utmost goodness not only to establish the divine and incorporeal essences of the intelligibles as images of the ineffable and divine glory, receiving in proportion to themselves, as is lawful, the whole inconceivable comeliness of the unapproachable beauty; but also to mingle into sensible things too, which fall far short even of the intelligible essences, echoes of its own majesty, able to ferry the human mind that rides upon them unwaveringly to God, as it comes to be above all visible things, as one that has mounted to the summit of blessedness.

⟦p22⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 97. ⟦s2⟧ Every mind crowned with virtue and knowledge, as having been allotted, like the great Hezekiah, to reign over Jerusalem, that is, over the habit that sees peace alone, or the state deprived of passions of every kind (for Jerusalem is interpreted "vision of peace"), has the whole creation under its hand: [creation] through the species that complete it bringing to God through him, as gifts, the spiritual principles of knowledge that are in it, and providing to him, as presents, the modes toward virtue present in it according to the natural law, and through both welcoming him who is able to be held in highest repute in both; I mean the philosophic mind perfected in reason and life through practice and contemplation.

⟦p23⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 98. ⟦s2⟧ He who through practice and contemplation has accomplished virtue and knowledge to the utmost is fittingly lifted above all, having come above the fleshly and reprehensible passions according to practice, and above the so-called natural bodies, I mean those that are under coming-to-be and corruption, and, simply, to speak summarily, above all the species under sense according to contemplation, having passed through in the manner of knowledge all the principles in them; such a one has led up the mind to the kindred and divine things.

⟦p24⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 99. ⟦s2⟧ He who through the toils of practice has been allotted to dwell in passionlessness as in Jerusalem, and is freed from all the annoyance that comes by sin, and both doing and speaking, and hearing and reasoning, peace alone; after receiving through natural contemplation the nature of visible things, bringing [1260] as gifts to the Lord through him the diviner principles in it, and bearing to him, as to a king, as presents, the laws in it, is lifted up before the eyes of all the nations, coming to be above all, namely both the passions according to the flesh through practice, and the natural bodies and all the species under sense through contemplation, having passed over the spiritual principles and modes in them.

⟦p25⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 100. ⟦s2⟧ Practical philosophy makes the practical man above the passions; and contemplation sets the man of knowledge above the visible things, having raised up the mind to the kindred intelligibles.

⟦p26⟧ ⟦s1⟧ THIRD CENTURY

⟦p27⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 1. ⟦s2⟧ He who knows with practice, and practises with knowledge, is the throne and footstool of God: a throne, because of knowledge; a footstool, because of practice. ⟦s3⟧ And if one should say that the human mind too is heaven, cleansed of all material imagination and occupied with, or rather adorned by, the divine principles of the intelligibles, he has not, it seems to me, stepped outside the truth.

⟦p28⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 2. ⟦s2⟧ Every philosopher, who is clearly also pious, guarded by virtue and knowledge, or by practice and contemplation, when he sees the wicked power rising up against him through the passions, as the king of the Assyrians against Hezekiah, has one help toward the release from evils, God, whom he propitiates crying without utterance through the greater intensity in virtue and knowledge; and he receives for alliance, or rather for salvation, an angel, that is, a greater word of wisdom and knowledge, *crushing every mighty man and warrior and ruler and general in the camp*.
