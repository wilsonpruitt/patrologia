⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1261] if there are no foods, there will be no gluttony; and if there is no gold, there will be no avarice. ⟦s2⟧ Therefore of every passionate motion of the natural powers in us the sensible thing is the beginning, or the demon who through it provokes the soul to sin.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 4. ⟦s2⟧ Rubbing makes the activity vanish; but crushing out makes vanish even the very pondering of vice. ⟦s3⟧ For rubbing is the abolition of the passionate action in activity alone; but crushing out is the complete vanishing even of the wicked movements in the understanding.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 5. ⟦s2⟧ Midway between God and men are the sensible and the intelligible things; above which the human mind comes as it advances toward God: not enslaved to the sensible things according to practice, and in no way held fast by the intelligible things according to contemplation.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 6. ⟦s2⟧ Creation becomes the accuser of impious men: through the principles in it proclaiming its own Maker; and through the natural laws in it according to each species, educating man toward virtue. ⟦s3⟧ The principles, then, are known in the holding-together of the permanence of each species; and the laws appear in the identity of the natural activity of each species; and not applying ourselves to these by the noetic power in us, we have both been ignorant of the cause of beings and have clung fast to all the passions contrary to nature.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ 7. ⟦s1+2⟧ The word bids that gifts be offered by us to God, so that it may set forth the infinity of the divine goodness, which, as though it had brought in nothing beforehand, receives from us as gifts the presents, reckoning the whole contribution as ours, showing God's goodness toward us to be great and unspeakable, who receives his own things, offered to him from us, as ours, and confesses the debt for them as for things belonging to another.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 8. ⟦s2⟧ Man, considering the spiritual principles of visible things, is taught that there is a Maker of the things that appear; leaving the notion of what he is like unexamined, as unattainable. ⟦s3⟧ For creation, being seen, clearly furnishes the apprehension that he is Maker, but not of what sort the Maker is.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 9. ⟦s2⟧ The wrath of God is the painful sense of those who are being educated. ⟦s3⟧ And painful sense is the bringing on of involuntary toils, through which God often leads the mind puffed up over virtue and knowledge to contraction and humility, granting it to become aware of itself and conscious of its own [1264] weakness; perceiving which, it lays aside the vain swelling of the heart.

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 10. ⟦s2⟧ The wrath of the Lord is the suspension of the supply of the divine gifts of grace; which comes profitably upon every mind that is lofty and high-flown, and that boasts of the good things given to it from God as of its own achievements.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 11. ⟦s2⟧ Every mind of knowledge and philosophy has both Judah and Jerusalem: Judah as practical philosophy, Jerusalem as contemplative mystagogy. ⟦s3⟧ Whenever, then, through divine grace, the God-loving mind, by both the practical and the contemplative philosophy, having beaten off every power opposed to virtue and knowledge, perfectly binds on itself the might over the spirits of wickedness, and does not render the due thanksgiving to God, the cause of the victory, but is lifted up in heart, reckoning itself the cause of the whole achievement, then, as not having rendered to God according to the recompense which he rendered to him, it receives the wrath of abandonment coming upon it, not only itself, but also Judah and Jerusalem, that is, the habit of practice and of contemplation: passions of dishonour at once, by God's permission, rising up against the practice and defiling the hitherto pure conscience; and false notions being entwined with the contemplation of beings and perverting the hitherto right opinion of knowledge. ⟦s4⟧ For dishonour of passions succeeds him who is exalted over practice; and him who is lifted up over knowledge just judgement permits to stumble regarding the true contemplation.

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 12. ⟦s2⟧ A divine limit and law truly exists in beings according to Providence, permitting those who have appeared ungrateful over better things to be educated through contraries toward gratitude, and to make the experience of contraries a full knowledge of the divine power that accomplishes good things; so that, being permitted by Providence to keep altogether undemolished our conceit over better things, we may not slip down into the God-opposing disposition of pride, thinking the possession of virtue and knowledge to be ours by nature and not acquired by grace; and be found using the good for the coming-to-be of evil, and, through the very things by which the full knowledge of the divine ought to remain unshaken in us, bound the more tightly, through those, as we ought not, falling sick with ignorance of it.

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1265] 13. ⟦s2⟧ We know as a divine limit and law existing in beings the providence that holds beings together, which by just judgement educates toward gratitude, by scarcity of goods, those who over the abundance of goods have appeared ungrateful toward their supplier, leading them through contraries to an awareness of the discernment of him who graciously gives the good things. ⟦s3⟧ For conceit over virtue, remaining uneducated, is of a nature to beget the disease of pride, which bears the disposition opposed to God.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 14. ⟦s2⟧ He who thinks he has grasped the end of virtue will in no way seek any longer the fountain-like cause of good things, having circumscribed the power of aspiration to himself alone, and suffering by his own doing the loss of the very limit of salvation, I mean God. ⟦s3⟧ But he who is aware of his own natural poverty regarding good things does not cease running headlong to him who is able to give the filling of the want.

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 15. ⟦s2⟧ He who has discerned the infinity of virtue never ceases from the course according to it, lest he suffer the loss of the very beginning and end of virtue, I mean God, by fixing the motion of aspiration about himself; and lest he unawares, thinking he has laid hold of perfection, undergo the falling-away from what truly is, toward which every motion of the earnest man presses.

⟦p14⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 16. ⟦s2⟧ Wrath justly comes upon the high-minded mind: that is, abandonment, as has been said; or the permission for it to be troubled by demons both in practice and in contemplation; so that it may take awareness of its own natural weakness, and the full knowledge of the divine power and grace that shelters it and accomplishes the whole of the good things; and be humbled, putting far from itself altogether the alien height that is contrary to nature; so that the other wrath, the taking away of the gifts given, may not come upon it, once it has been humbled and has come to awareness of him who provides the good things.

⟦p15⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 17. ⟦s2⟧ He who has not been chastened by the first species of wrath, that is, of abandonment, so as to come to humility, reckoning it the teacher of fair judgement, clearly receives the other wrath coming upon him, which takes away from him the activity of the gifts of grace and renders him bereft of the power that hitherto guarded him. ⟦s3⟧ *For I will take away*, he says (God speaking of ungrateful Israel), *its hedge, and it shall be for plunder; and I will pull down its wall, and it shall be for trampling; and I will leave my vineyard, and it shall not be pruned nor dug; and thorn shall come up into it as into waste ground, and I will command the clouds not to rain upon it.* [n: Isa. v, 5, 6.]

⟦p16⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1268] 18. ⟦s2⟧ Further, steep is the road to impiety: the insensibility regarding the loss of the virtues. ⟦s3⟧ For he who has accustomed himself, for the sake of the pleasures of the flesh, to disobey God will deny God himself too when occasion calls, preferring to God the life of the flesh, the pleasures of which alone he has held to be stronger than the divine wills.

⟦p17⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 19. ⟦s2⟧ Whenever we suppose that the mind has suffered something in any way whatever, we shall certainly believe that, with such a mind, its practical and contemplative power has suffered with it according to their natural principles. ⟦s3⟧ For it is not possible for the subject to suffer while the things in the subject do not suffer; and I call the mind the subject, as receptive of virtue and knowledge; and the things in the subject, practice and contemplation, which hold toward the mind the rank of accidents. ⟦s4⟧ Therefore in every way they also suffer with it when it suffers, having its qualified motion as the beginning of their own alteration.

⟦p18⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 20. ⟦s2⟧ Every God-loving and virtuous man, like Hezekiah, having girded on in the manner of knowledge the might against the demons, if there comes upon him an assault of wicked spirits, invisibly entwining the war against him in the mind, and through prayer he receives an angel sent to him from God, I mean a greater word of wisdom, and, having crushed out the whole phalanx of the devil, scatters it; and does not ascribe to God as the cause of such victory and salvation, but credits the whole victory to himself, such a one *has not rendered to God according to his recompense*, not having made the multitude of his thanksgiving equal to the greatness of the salvation, nor having measured his own disposition in return against the benefaction of him who saved.

⟦p19⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 21. ⟦s2⟧ Let us illumine the mind with divine concepts; and let us brighten the body with the modes of the diviner principles that have been understood, making it a rational workshop of virtue by the casting off of the passions. ⟦s2⟧ For the innate passions of the body, governed by reason, have no blame; but moved without it, they bring blame. ⟦s3⟧ Of these, then, he says, there must be a casting off: of those whose motion is innate, but whose use, not governed by reason, often becomes contrary to nature.

⟦p20⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 22. ⟦s2⟧ Everyone who has lifted up his heart over the gifts of grace he received, thinking great things as though he had not received them, justly receives the wrath coming upon him, God permitting the devil to grapple with him intelligibly, and to shake the modes of virtue according to practice, and to muddy the clear principles of knowledge according to contemplation; so that, having learned his own weakness, he may recognize the only power, that which wrestles down the passions in us, and be humbled, repenting, casting off the bulk of his conceit, and propitiate God, and turn away the wrath that comes upon those who do not repent, which takes away [1269] the grace that guards the soul and leaves the ungrateful mind bereft.

⟦p21⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 23. ⟦s2⟧ Saving wrath is the divine permission for the high-minded mind to be warred against with the passions by the demons; so that, suffering dishonourably while it boasts over virtues, it may know who is the giver of these; or may become bare of the things not its own, which it thought it had as though it had not received them.

⟦p22⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 24. ⟦s2⟧ Blessed truly is that mind which has praiseworthily died to all beings: to the sensible, by the putting away of the activity according to sense; to the intelligible, by the ceasing of noetic motion. ⟦s3⟧ Note that he calls the praised death of the mind the passing-away, by inclination, from all beings, after which it is of a nature to receive the life that is by divine grace, having received inconceivably, instead of beings, the cause of beings.

⟦p23⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 25. ⟦s2⟧ Blessed is he who has joined the practical part to the good according to nature, and the contemplative part to the truth according to nature. ⟦s3⟧ For every practice is of a nature to come about for the sake of the good; and every contemplation seeks knowledge for the sake of the truth alone; and when these have been accomplished, there will be nothing at all that strikes the practical part of the soul, nor indeed its contemplative part, stinging it through foreign objects of contemplation; since the soul has come to be beyond everything that is and that is understood, and has entered into God himself, the only good and true, who is beyond all essence and intellection.

⟦p24⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 26. ⟦s2⟧ They say that the end of practical virtue is the good. ⟦s3⟧ And this is the completion of the divine activity, to which the rational part of the soul leads, using anger and desire according to nature; in which the beauty according to the likeness is of a nature to shine forth. ⟦s3⟧ And they say that the end of contemplative philosophy is the truth; which is the knowledge, without parts and uniform, of all the things about God, toward which the pure mind is borne, having altogether quenched its own judgement according to sense; in which (in knowledge, that is) the dignity of the divine image is shown unadulterated.

⟦p25⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 27. ⟦s2⟧ No one can truly bless God [1272] unless he has sanctified the body with the virtues and illumined the soul with knowledges. ⟦s3⟧ For the disposition according to virtue is the face of the contemplative mind, lifted up as to heaven toward the height of true knowledge.

⟦p26⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 28. ⟦s2⟧ Blessed is he who knows in truth that God accomplishes in us as in instruments all practice and contemplation, virtue and knowledge, victory and wisdom, goodness and truth, we contributing nothing at all except the disposition that wills the good things; having which, the great Zerubbabel, in addition to the things already said, says, addressing God: *Blessed art thou, who gavest me wisdom, and to thee I confess, Master of the fathers; from thee is the victory, and from thee the wisdom, and thine is the glory, and I am thy servant.* ⟦s2⟧ As a truly grateful servant, he referred all things to God, who had given all things, from whom he had received and held the wisdom, confessing to him as Master of the fathers the power of the goods graciously given, which are the union, as has been said, of victory and wisdom, of virtue and knowledge, of practice and contemplation, of goodness and truth; for these, united with one another, flash forth one glory and radiance of God.

⟦p27⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 29. ⟦s2⟧ All the achievements of the saints were manifestly gifts of grace from God; no one having anything at all except the good given, measured, as from God the Master, in proportion to the gratitude and goodwill of the receiver; and each possessing those things alone which he presents to the Master who gives.

⟦p28⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 30. ⟦s2⟧ The mind that presides over virtue and knowledge, and has willed that the soul be deprived of the evil slavery of the passions, says: *Women prevail, and truth conquers above all*; calling women the deifying virtues, from which the unifying love of men toward God and toward one another consists; snatching the soul out of all things under coming-to-be and corruption, and out of the intelligible essences above them, and entwining it with God himself by a certain commingling of yearning, as far as is possible to human nature, and mystically making the undefiled and divine life-together. ⟦s3⟧ And saying truth, he means the only and one cause of beings, and beginning and kingdom and power and glory, from which and for the sake of which all things have come to be and come to be, and are held together toward being by it and through it; and for the sake of which is all the zeal and motion of the God-loving.
