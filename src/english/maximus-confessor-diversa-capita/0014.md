⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1329] their inclination in equal law, and have made it receptive in activity of the principles of nature according to the whole principle of ever-well-being, on account of the good affection of inclination toward the divine wish, will partake of the whole goodness, according to the divine life shining upon them, as in angels or as in men. ⟦s2⟧ But those who have made their inclination unequal in law with nature throughout, and have shown it dissipative in activity of the principles of nature according to the whole principle of well-being, on account of the antipathy of inclination toward the divine wish, will fall from the whole goodness, according to the kinship of inclination toward ill-being manifested in them; according to which there will be the distance of such men from God, since they do not have, according to the intent of inclination, the principle of well-being brought to life by the activities of good things, according to which the divine life is naturally apt to shine through.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 55. ⟦s2⟧ The balance of each one's inclination at the judgement will be the principle of nature, testing its motion toward ill-being or well-being, according to which participation or non-participation in the divine life naturally comes about. ⟦s3⟧ For according to being and ever-being, God, being present, will hold all together; but according to ever-well-being, only the saints, angels and men, in a proper mode, leaving ever-ill-being to those who are not such, mingled in as the fruit of inclination.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 56. ⟦s2⟧ The mystery of the divine incarnation does not bring in, together with the otherness according to nature of the things of which it is composed, the difference according to hypostasis as well: the one, so that the mystery of the Trinity may not receive an addition; the other, so that nothing may be of one kind and consubstantial with the Godhead according to nature. ⟦s3⟧ For the concourse of two natures came about toward a hypostasis, but not toward one nature; so that both the one according to hypostasis might be shown accomplished in the union out of the natures that ran together with each other, and the difference of the things that came together toward the indissoluble union might be believed according to the natural property, remaining outside all change and confusion.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 57. ⟦s2⟧ We do not speak of a difference of hypostases in Christ, because the Trinity has remained Trinity even when the Word was incarnate, no addition of person having accrued to the holy Trinity through the incarnation; but we speak of a difference of natures, so that we may not hold the flesh consubstantial with the Word according to nature.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 58. ⟦s2⟧ He who does not speak of a difference of natures has nowhere from which to warrant the confession that the Word became flesh without change, not knowing that there are preserved according to nature in the one [1332] hypostasis of the one Christ and God and Saviour after the union that which assumed and that which was assumed.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 59. ⟦s2⟧ If there is a difference according to nature of flesh and Godhead in Christ after the union (for Godhead and flesh are never the same according to essence), the union of the things that came together came about in no way toward the coming-to-be of one nature, but toward one hypostasis, according to which we find in Christ no difference in any mode whatever. ⟦s3⟧ For the Word is the same as his own flesh according to the hypostasis. ⟦s4⟧ For in whatever respect Christ has difference, he cannot be one in every mode; but in whatever respect he admits no difference whatever, the one holds of him always, in every mode, piously, both as being and as being said.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 60. ⟦s2⟧ Faith, through hope, establishes perfect love toward God; good conscience, through the keeping of the commandments, gives subsistence to love toward the neighbour. ⟦s3⟧ For good conscience has no transgressed commandment as its accuser. ⟦s4⟧ And with these the heart alone of those who aspire to true salvation is naturally apt to be entrusted.

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 61. ⟦s2⟧ Nothing is quicker than believing, and nothing easier than confessing with the mouth the grace of what has been believed. ⟦s2⟧ For the one shows the living love of the believer toward the Maker; the other, the God-loving disposition toward the neighbour. ⟦s3⟧ And love and genuine disposition, that is, faith and good conscience, are manifestly the work of the invisible movement in the heart, needing no external matter at all for coming-to-be.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 62. ⟦s2⟧ He who is sick with total immobility regarding the good is altogether easily moved regarding evil. ⟦s3⟧ For to be unmoved in both is impossible. ⟦s4⟧ Whence Scripture is accustomed to call the soul's sloth about good things *stones*, as being insensible of virtues, and its eagerness about evil things *wood*. ⟦s5⟧ And motion according to sense, joined to the activity according to mind, makes virtue with knowledge.

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 63. ⟦s2⟧ Scripture has called, as I think, the natural law of the body a *middle wall*; and the *fence*, [n: Ephes. ii, 14.] the relation in the law of the flesh to the passions, that is, sin. ⟦s3⟧ For the relation alone to the passions of dishonour becomes the fence of the law of nature, that is, of the passible part of the nature, walling off the body from the soul, and not allowing the crossing-over of the principle of the virtues to the flesh, through the soul in the middle, according to practice. ⟦s3⟧ But reason, having come, and having wrestled down the law of nature, that is, [1333] the passible of the nature, abolished the relation in it of the passions contrary to nature.

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 64. ⟦s2⟧ When, robbing by guile through deceit the knowledge of God implanted in the nature, the wicked one appropriates it to himself, he is a thief, attempting to transfer the reverence from God to himself; leading away, namely, the soul's contemplation according to mind from the spiritual principles in the things that have come to be, and circumscribing the noetic power by the sole surface appearance of sensible things. ⟦s3⟧ But whenever, beginning from the natural movements, he drags down sophistically the practical power toward things contrary to nature, and through things supposed good persuasively nails its aspiration to the worse, he swears falsely by the name of the Lord, leading the persuaded soul to other things contrary to the undertaking. ⟦s4⟧ And he is a thief, as robbing for himself the knowledge of nature; and a perjurer, as persuading the practical part of the soul to toil in vain at things contrary to nature.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 65. ⟦s2⟧ Or again, he is a thief who, for the deception of his hearers, practises the divine words forsooth, whose power he has not come to know through works, making the bare utterance a trade of glory, and by the word through the tongue hunting the praise of being thought righteous by the listeners. ⟦s3⟧ And, simply to say, he who has his life discordant with his word, and the disposition of his soul opposed to his knowledge, is a thief, shining not nobly from the goods of others. ⟦s4⟧ To whom the word will fittingly say: *But to the sinner God said: Why dost thou declare my ordinances, and take up my covenant through thy mouth?*

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 66. ⟦s2+3⟧ Likewise he too is a thief who, by apparent manners and character, covers over the unapparent villainy of the soul, covering over the inward disposition with a fiction of gentleness; and stealing, just as that one by the utterance of the words of knowledge [steals] the understanding of the hearers, so he too by the mode of the hypocrisy of character [steals] the sense of the beholders. ⟦s2+3⟧ To whom likewise it will be said: *Be ashamed, you who are clothed in alien garments*; and: *The Lord will uncover their form in that day.* [n: Isa. iii, 17.] For every day, in the hidden workshop of the heart, I seem to hear God saying these things to me, as being plainly condemned on both counts.

⟦p14⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 67. ⟦s2⟧ He is a perjurer, that is, swearing falsely by the name of the Lord, who promises God the life according to virtue, and pursues things alien to the undertaking of his own promise, and transgresses the covenant of the confession of the reverent life through idleness in the commandments. ⟦s3⟧ And, to say it briefly, he who [1336] has chosen to live according to God, and is not perfectly mortified to the present life, is a liar and a perjurer, having sworn to God, that is, having promised the blameless course in the divine contests, and not having fulfilled it, and for this reason in no way praised. ⟦s4⟧ For *everyone who swears by him shall be praised*, [n: Psal. lxii, 12.] that is, everyone who has promised God the life in God, and fulfils through the truth of the works of justice the oaths of his fair undertaking.

⟦p15⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 68. ⟦s2⟧ He who feigns knowledge by the utterance in words alone steals for his own glory the understanding of the hearers; and he who feigns virtue by his character steals for his own glory the sight of the beholders; and both, stealing through deceit, lead astray, the one the understanding of the soul of the hearers, the other the bodily sense of the beholders.

⟦p16⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 69. ⟦s2⟧ If he who fulfils his own promises has praise by all means, as swearing by God and speaking the truth, it is clear that he who becomes the transgressor of his own covenants will have blame and dishonour, as having sworn by God and lied.

⟦p17⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 70. ⟦s2⟧ Not every man coming into this world [n: Joan. i, 9.] is by all means enlightened by the Word; for many remain unenlightened and without part in the light according to full knowledge; but clearly every man who comes by his own inclination into the true world of the virtues [is]. ⟦s3⟧ For everyone who in truth, through the self-chosen birth, comes into this world is by all means enlightened by the Word, receiving an immovable habit of virtue and an infallible science of true knowledge.

⟦p18⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 71. ⟦s2⟧ Not all persons and all things that have the same enunciation will by all means be understood in one and the same mode; but each of the things said must be understood according to the power underlying, namely, the mode of holy Scripture, if we would rightly aim at the scope of what is written.

⟦p19⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 72. ⟦s2⟧ None of the persons, or places, or times, or other things recorded in Scripture, animate and inanimate, sensible and intelligible, understood always in the same mode, has the history or the contemplation consistent with itself throughout. ⟦s3⟧ Hence he who infallibly practises the divine knowing of Scripture must, by the differences of the things done or said, take each of those enumerated differently, and duly assign the contemplation befitting it according to the place or the time.

⟦p20⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 73. ⟦s2⟧ All those in the whole world must be taught to live and conduct themselves by reason alone, and to have so much care for bodies as, through much attention, to cut off [1337] the soul's relation to them, and to give the soul no image of matter at all; the sense being already quenched by reason, the sense which at the beginning thrust reason aside and received the irrationality of pleasure after the fashion of a creeping serpent, and against which death was justly decreed: so that it may cease to provide the devil entry to the soul.

⟦p21⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 74. ⟦s2⟧ Sense, being one in genus, is fivefold in species, through the apprehensive activity according to each species, persuading the erring soul to cherish the cognate sensible instead of God. ⟦s3⟧ Therefore he who wisely follows reason, before the forced death and the one against inclination, votes of his own will the death of the flesh, making a total separation of inclination from sense.

⟦p22⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 75. ⟦s2⟧ Sense, having taken the mind under its hand, teaches it polytheism, through each sense, by its slavery to the passions, worshipping the cognate sensible as divine.

⟦p23⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 76. ⟦s2⟧ He who sits by the letter alone of Scripture has only sense ruling the nature, according to which the relation of the soul to the flesh alone is naturally apt to shine through. ⟦s3⟧ For the letter, not understood spiritually, has only sense circumscribing its enunciation, and not allowing the power of what is written to cross over to the mind. ⟦s4⟧ And if the letter has its intimacy with sense alone, everyone who receives the letter Jewishly, according to the history alone, lives according to the flesh, dying by inclination every day the death of sin on account of living sense, unable by the spirit to put to death the deeds of the body, so as to live the blessed life in the spirit. ⟦s5⟧ *For if you live according to the flesh, you will die*, says the divine Apostle; *but if by the spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live.* [n: Rom. viii, 13.]

⟦p24⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 77. ⟦s2⟧ Let us not then, kindling the divine lamp, that is, the illuminating word of knowledge, through contemplation and practice, put it under the bushel, [n: Matth. v, 15.] lest we be condemned as circumscribing by the letter the incomprehensible power of wisdom; but [let us put it] on the lampstand, I mean the holy Church, which in the height of true contemplation beacons forth to all the light of the divine doctrines.
