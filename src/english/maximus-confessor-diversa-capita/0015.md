⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1340] another lamp has appeared, uttering, as befits the great Apostle, *For we are not ignorant of his devices.* [n: II Cor. ii, 11.]

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 79. ⟦s2⟧ The purification, for those worthy of the purity of the virtues, the Holy Spirit works through fear and piety and knowledge; the illumination of the knowledge of beings according to the principles by which they subsist he gives, through strength and counsel and intelligence, to those worthy of light; and the perfection, through the all-radiant and simple and entire wisdom, he grants to those worthy of deification, leading them up immediately to the cause of beings in every mode, as far as is possible for man, as they are made known from the divine idioms of goodness alone; according to which, knowing themselves from God and God from themselves, there being no middle thing walling them apart (for there is nothing in the middle between wisdom and God), they will have the unalterable unchangeableness, all the middle things having been wholly passed through by them, in which there once lay the danger of erring about knowledge. ⟦s3⟧ And he calls middle things the essence of the intelligible and the sensible things, through which the human mind is naturally led up to God as the cause of beings.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 80. ⟦s2⟧ Fear, piety and knowledge work practical philosophy; strength and counsel and intelligence achieve natural contemplation in the Spirit; and divine wisdom alone grants mystical theology.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 81. ⟦s2⟧ As without oil it is impossible for a lamp to be kept unquenched, so without a habit that nourishes the good things with suitable principles and modes and characters, and again with fitting concepts and thoughts, it is impossible for the light of the gifts of grace to be guarded unquenched. ⟦s3⟧ For every spiritual gift of grace needs a congenial habit, which ceaselessly pours on it, like oil, the noetic matter, that it may remain held fast by the habit of him who received it.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 82. ⟦s2⟧ As without the olive it is in no way possible to find, by nature and in truth, genuine oil; and without a vessel to receive the oil it is not possible for it to be held; and the lamp's light, not nourished with oil, is wholly quenched: so without the holy Scriptures there is in truth no power of concepts befitting God; and without a habit, as a vessel receptive of concepts, a divine concept would in no way subsist; and the light of the knowledge that is in the gifts of grace, not nourished with divine concepts, is not kept unquenched for those who have it.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 83. ⟦s2⟧ The olive on the left I take to signify the Old Testament, as caring rather for practical philosophy; and the one on the right, the New Testament, as the teacher of a new mystery and productive of the contemplative habit in each of the faithful. ⟦s3⟧ For the one provides modes of virtue, [1341] the other provides principles of knowledge to those who philosophize divine things; and the one, snatching the mind away from the mist of visible things, leads it up to what is kindred, made pure of all material imagination; the other cleanses it of material passionate attachment by the tension of courage, as with a hammer knocking out the nails of the inclination that fasten it, by relation, to the body.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 84. ⟦s2⟧ The Old raises the body, made rational, up to the soul through the virtues as intermediaries, preventing the mind from being brought down to the body; the New raises the mind up to God, winged by the fire of love. ⟦s3⟧ And the one makes the body the same as the mind in the movement that is by position; the other renders the mind the same as God by the habit of grace, having such a likeness to God that through it God is known (he who from himself by nature is in no way at all known by anyone), as an archetype from an image.

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 85. ⟦s2⟧ The Old Testament, being a symbol of practice and virtue, prepares the body to agree with the mind in movement; the New, being productive of contemplation and knowledge, makes the mind that mystically holds fast to it bright with divine concepts and marks; and the Old furnishes the man of knowledge with the modes of the virtues, the New grants the practical man principles of true knowledge.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 86. ⟦s2⟧ Of those God is both called and becomes Father by grace who have only the birth in the Spirit by inclination according to virtue; by which, having, as the face of the soul, the character of God who begot them appearing in the virtues throughout their life, they prepare those who behold them to glorify God through the conversion of their manners, offering their own life to them for imitation as a choice exemplar of virtue. ⟦s3⟧ For God is not naturally glorified by bare word, but by works of justice, which proclaim the divine magnificence far more than words.

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 87. ⟦s2⟧ The natural law is left, on account of sense; but it brings the modes of the virtues to reason. ⟦s2⟧ And it makes knowledge active. ⟦s3⟧ The spiritual law is right, on account of the mind; but it mixes with sense the spiritual principles in beings, and makes reasoned practice.

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 88. ⟦s2⟧ He who shows knowledge embodied by practice, and practice ensouled by knowledge, has found the exact mode of true divine working. ⟦s3⟧ But he [1344] who has either of these disjoined from the other has either made knowledge an imagination without hypostasis, or established practice as a soulless idol. ⟦s4⟧ For knowledge without practice differs nothing from imagination, not having the practice that gives it subsistence; and practice without reasoning has become the same as an idol, not having the knowledge that ensouls it.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 89. ⟦s2⟧ The mystery of our salvation makes the life a demonstration of the word, and the word the glory of the life; and practice contemplation put into act, and contemplation practice initiated into mystery. ⟦s3⟧ And, to say it briefly, it makes virtue a manifestation of knowledge, and knowledge a power preserving virtue; and through both (of virtue I speak, and of knowledge) it shows one wisdom constituted: that we may know that the two Testaments agree with each other through all things according to grace, coming together for the completion of one mystery more than soul and body come together with each other by composition for the coming-to-be of one man.

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 90. ⟦s2⟧ As soul and body make man by composition, so practice and contemplation accomplish, by concourse, one wisdom of knowledge; and the Old and the New Testament work out one mystery. ⟦s3⟧ But to God alone by nature belongs the good, from which, by participation, all things are illumined and made good that are by nature receptive of light and goodness.

⟦p14⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 91. ⟦s2⟧ He who understands the visible world contemplates the intelligible; for by imagining he gives the intelligibles a type in sense, and in mind he shapes the principles he has beheld; and he transfers to sense, in many forms, the constitution of the intelligible world, and to mind, with manifold intertwining, the composition of the sensible world; and he understands in the intelligible the sensible, having transferred sense to the mind by the principles, and in the sensible the intelligible, having scientifically bound the mind fast to sense by the types.

⟦p15⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 92. ⟦s2⟧ The first word concerning the Monad, as the beginning [1345] of all virtue, the prophet named head; as in: *My head went down into the clefts of the mountains*; and the purposes of the spirits of wickedness, the clefts of the mountains, by which our mind was swallowed through the transgression; and lowest earth, the habit that in no way perceives divine knowledge, or has any movement whatever of the life according to virtue; and abyss, the ignorance lying upon the habit of vice, on which, as on earth, the seas of vice stand; or by earth he meant the fixed habit of vice; and everlasting bars, the passionate attachments to material things that hold the worst habit together.

⟦p16⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 93. ⟦s2⟧ The endurance of the saints consumes the wicked power that attacks; as persuading its own ministers to pride themselves on the toils for truth, and teaching them to be enlarged in sufferings more than those who care overmuch for the life in the flesh are in comforts, making the natural weakness of the flesh as to suffering an occasion for them of power surpassing according to the Spirit. ⟦s3⟧ For see how the natural weakness of the saints is the occasion of divine power in excess, than which the Lord showed the proud devil weaker.

⟦p17⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 94. ⟦s2⟧ The word of grace, crossing through many temptations to the nature of men, that is, to the Church of the nations, as Jonah crossed through many tribulations to Nineveh the great city, persuaded the reigning law of nature to rise up from its throne (that is, from its former habit, according to sense, concerning evil), and to take off its robe (I mean, to put off the arrogance of worldly glory in matters of character), and to put on sackcloth, namely mourning, and the hard and rough conduct of ill-suffering, befitting the life according to God; and to sit upon ashes. ⟦s2⟧ And ashes is the poverty of spirit, upon which everyone sits who is being taught to live piously and has the scourge of conscience over his offences tormenting him.

⟦p18⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 95. ⟦s2⟧ See how he says that the king is the natural law; the throne, the passionate habit according to sense; the robe, the display of vainglory; the sackcloth, the mourning of repentance; the ashes, humble-mindedness; men, those about reason; cattle, those about desire; oxen, those about anger; sheep, those who stumble about the contemplation of visible things.

⟦p19⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 96. ⟦s2⟧ He who through the virtue of the law forgets the passions in the flesh as left things, and through infallible knowledge is not caught over his right deeds by the disease of puffing-up conceit as by a right hand, has become a man not knowing his right hand, as not yearning for glory that is dissolved, nor his left, as not provoked by the passions of the flesh. ⟦s3⟧ The right, then, as it seems, the word called the vainglory over supposed right deeds; and the left, the licentiousness over shameful [1348] passions. ⟦s4⟧ For the principle of virtue does not know the sin of the flesh as left; and that of knowledge does not know the vice of the soul as right.

⟦p20⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 97. ⟦s2⟧ Knowledge of the virtues according to reason, that is, the true full knowledge in act of the cause of the virtues, naturally produces total ignorance of the excess and the deficiency that lie beside the mean of the virtues on either side, right and left. ⟦s3⟧ For if nothing at all in reason is naturally contrary to reason, clearly he who has been led up to the principle of the virtues will in no way recognize the position of the things contrary to reason. ⟦s4⟧ For it is not possible to behold both opposites at once in the same respect, and to know the one appearing together with the other at once.

⟦p21⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 98. ⟦s2⟧ If there is no principle of unbelief in faith, nor is light by nature the cause of darkness, nor is the devil naturally shown together with Christ, it is clear that what is contrary to reason does not coexist at all with reason. ⟦s3⟧ And if what is contrary to reason cannot be with reason at all, he who has been led up to the principle of the virtues will in no way recognize the position of the things contrary to reason, knowing virtue alone, as it is and not as it is supposed; and for this reason knowing neither the right, through excess, nor the left, through deficiency. ⟦s4⟧ For in both, what is contrary to reason is clearly beheld.

⟦p22⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 99. ⟦s2⟧ He calls unbelief the denial of the commandments; and faith, assent to them; and darkness, ignorance of the good; and light, its discernment. ⟦s3⟧ And Christ he has called the essence and the hypostasis of the good; and the devil, the worst habit, generative of all evils.

⟦p23⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 100. ⟦s2⟧ If reason is the limit and measure of beings, it is equal to irrationality, and for this reason contrary to reason, to be moved beside the limit and beside the measure, or again above the limit and above the measure. ⟦s3⟧ For both alike bring about, for those so moved, a falling away from what truly is: the one persuading them to make the movement of the course unclear and undefined, not having God as goal, through immoderateness of mind, as the end preconceived for them, as they fashion for themselves something more right than the right; the other, beside the goal, persuading them to make the movement of the course toward sense alone, through slackness of mind, thinking the preconceived end to be what is circumscribed for them by sense. ⟦s4⟧ Which things he does not know, not suffering them, who is joined to the principle of virtue alone, and has circumscribed by it the whole movement of his own power according to mind, and for this reason is able to think nothing above reason or beside reason.

⟦p24⟧ ⟦s1⟧ FIFTH CENTURY

⟦p25⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 1. ⟦s2⟧ Reason according to nature, through the virtues as intermediaries, [1349] naturally leads up to the mind him who cares for practice; and the mind leads into wisdom, through contemplation, him who aspires to knowledge. ⟦s3⟧ But passion contrary to reason persuades him who neglects the commandments to be borne down to sense, whose end is that the mind be nailed to pleasure.

⟦p26⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 2. ⟦s2⟧ He calls virtue the most passionless and fixed habit concerning the good, on either side of which nothing stands, since it bears the character of God, to whom nothing is contrary. ⟦s3⟧ And the cause of the virtues is God; and the knowledge of him in act is the alteration, by habit, toward the Spirit, of him who has truly come to know God.

⟦p27⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 3. ⟦s2⟧ If reason has defined the coming-to-be of each thing as it is natural to it, none of beings has naturally either overstepped itself or fallen below itself. ⟦s3⟧ Therefore the limit of beings is the full knowledge of the cause by aspiration; and the measure, the imitation of the cause attainable by beings in act. ⟦s4⟧ And for the aspiration of things moved to be borne above the limit and the measure makes the course profitless, since they do not arrive at God, in whom the movement of all things by aspiration comes to rest, receiving the enjoyment of God as self-subsistent end. ⟦s5⟧ And for the aspiration of things moved to be borne beside the limit and the measure makes the course profitless, since instead of God they arrive at sense, in which, by pleasure, the enjoyment of the passions, which is without hypostasis, has its standing.

⟦p28⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 4. ⟦s2⟧ He who sets his mind toward the cause of beings without relation will be wholly ignorant, beholding no principle in God, who in essence is beyond every principle as regards every cause; toward whom, contracted from all beings, he knows none of the principles from which he has departed, beholding inexpressibly only him toward whom he has come by grace. ⟦s3⟧ For the mind that has run up to God in ecstasy lets go both the principles of bodiless things and those of bodies. ⟦s4⟧ For none of the things after God can naturally be beheld at once together with God.

⟦p29⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 5. ⟦s2⟧ Arrogance is a truly accursed passion, composed by composition of two evils, pride, I mean, and vainglory; of which pride denies the cause of virtue and of nature, and vainglory renders both nature and virtue itself bastard. ⟦s3⟧ For nothing is done by the proud man according to God, and nothing proceeds from the vainglorious man according to nature.

⟦p30⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 6. ⟦s2⟧ It is proper to pride to deny that God is the begetter of virtue and of nature; and to vainglory, to partition nature toward lowering; of which arrogance is naturally the offspring, being a composite habit of vice, which has a willing denial of God and ignorance of the equality of honour according to nature.
