⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1385] that even he himself, the cause of all things, through the beautiful and good yearning for all things, through the excess of his yearning goodness, comes to be outside himself by his providences toward all the things that are, and is, as it were, charmed by goodness and love and yearning; and from being beyond all things and set apart from all, he is drawn down to being in all, according to an ecstatic superessential power, without departing from himself. ⟦s2⟧ For this reason those skilled in divine things also call him zealous, as one whose good yearning toward the things that are is great, and as rousing them to zeal for his yearning aspiration, and as showing himself zealous, for whom the things that aspire are also objects of zeal, and as the things that are provided for being objects of zeal to him.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 87. ⟦s2⟧ Of love and of yearning they say that he, namely God, is the producer, and, they say, the begetter. ⟦s3⟧ For he, having these in himself, brought them forth to what is outside, that is, to the creatures; and according to this it is said, *God is love*; and again, *Sweetness and desire*, [n: Cant. v, 16.] which is yearning; and the beloved and the truly yearned-for is he himself. ⟦s4⟧ By the loving yearning being poured forth from him, then, he himself, its begetter, is said to be moved; and by his being the true object of yearning and beloved and aspired-to and choice-worthy, he moves the things that look toward it, and those in which there is the power of aspiring, proportionately to them.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 88. ⟦s2⟧ Think of God, I pray, as procuring and moving toward the yearning conjunction that is in spirit, that is, as its mediator, and as the one who fits things together toward it, so that he is yearned for and loved by his own works. ⟦s3⟧ And he says *moving*, as moving each thing, according to its own principle, to turn toward him. ⟦s4⟧ And the name of procuring, even if among those outside it signifies a thing not pure, yet here he says it of the mediation that brings about union in God.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 89. ⟦s2⟧ The yearning motion of the good, pre-existing in the good, being simple and unmoved, and proceeding from the good, turns back again to the same, being without end and without beginning; which shows our ever-moving aspiration toward the Divine and [1388] union. ⟦s2⟧ For the loving union with God is set apart from and lies above every union.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 90. ⟦s2⟧ Yearning, whether we should call it divine or angelic or noetic or psychic or natural, let us conceive as a uniting and blending power: moving the higher things to providence for the lower; the coordinate things, again, to a mutual cohesion in communion; and, last of all, the subordinate things to turning toward the better and the superior.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 91. ⟦s2⟧ If knowledge unites the knowers and the known, while ignorance is always the cause of change and of division from itself in the one who is ignorant, nothing will move the one who has believed in truth, according to the sacred Word, from the hearth of the true faith; upon which he will have the permanence of unmoved and unchangeable identity. ⟦s3+4⟧ For he who has been united to the truth knows well that it is well with him, even if the many admonish him as one who is beside himself. [n: II Cor. v, 13.] For it escapes them, as is likely, that he has gone out of error to the truth of the faith that truly is; but he himself truly knows himself to be, not, as they say, raving, but freed, through the simple truth that holds always according to the same things and in the same way, from the unstable and alterable drift about the manifold variety of error.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 92. ⟦s2⟧ The saints became good and loving to man, compassionate and merciful, shown to have one disposition of love toward the whole race; by which, having held firm through their whole life the most excellent form of all goods, humility I mean, that which guards the goods and destroys their contraries, they became in no way at all capturable by any of the troublesome temptations, both the voluntary, which fall under the principle of what is in our power, and the involuntary, which are not in our power: by withering, in the case of the former, their uprisings through self-control, and by shaking off, in the case of the latter, their assaults through patience.

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 93. ⟦s2⟧ The perfect practice of virtue is produced by right faith and unadulterated fear toward God; natural contemplation infallible in its ascent, by firm hope and unblemished intelligence; deification by taking-up, by perfect love and a mind wholly and willingly blinded to the things that are, by transcendence.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 94. ⟦s2⟧ The work of practical philosophy, they say, is to render the mind pure of every passionate imagination; that of natural contemplation, to show it expert in all true knowledge of the things that are, according to the cause by which they subsist; and that of theological mystagogy, to make it, by grace, like God and equal, so far as is attainable, according to habit, so that it is no longer at all mindful of anything [1389] of the things after God, because of the transcendence.

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 95. ⟦s2⟧ What the ether is in the world perceived by sense, that is, the fiery element, this in the world of the understanding is prudence, as a habit illuminating, and receptive of the spiritual principles proper to each of the things that are, manifesting through them without error the cause in all things, and drawing out the soul's aspiration toward the divine; and what the air is in the sensible world, this in the world according to the understanding is courage, as a habit moving, and at once sustaining and effecting the innate life in spirit, and invigorating the soul's ever-moving about the divine; and what water is in the sensible world, this in the world of the understanding is temperance, being a habit productive of life-giving fruitfulness in spirit, and begetting the ever-welling yearning charm about the divine according to aspiration; and what earth is in the sensible world, this in the world of the understanding is justice, being a habit generative, according to form, of all the principles in the things that are, and apportioning to each, according to equality, the life-giving distribution in spirit; and it is the unchangeable establishment of its own base in the good, according to position.

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 96. ⟦s2⟧ Just as, when the flesh is in good condition and grows fat, the soul is tormented and darkened by the passions, as the habit of the virtues and the illumination of knowledge withdraw; so also, when the soul is guarded and made radiant by the divine beauty of the virtues and by the illumination of knowledge, *the outer man is corrupted*, [n: II Cor. iv, 16.] the flesh casting off its natural good condition through the sojourn of reason.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 97. ⟦s2⟧ It was not possible otherwise for man, once he had come to be, to be shown son of God and God by the deification of grace, unless he were first born by choice by the Spirit, because of the self-moved and unmastered power naturally inhering in him. ⟦s2⟧ This deifying and divine and immaterial birth the first man abandoned, by preferring to the goods that are perceived by the mind and as yet unmanifest what is pleasant and manifest to sense; and he is condemned, fittingly, to have the coming-to-be from bodies, which is without choice and material and perishable.

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [1392] 98. ⟦s2⟧ Man is now moved either about irrational imaginations of passions, out of deceit, because of love of pleasure; or about the principles of the arts, out of distress, because of need; or about natural principles, out of the law of nature, because of learning: none of which drew man of necessity in the beginning, fittingly, since he had come to be above them all. ⟦s3⟧ For so it was fitting that he who was from the beginning should be: distracted in no way whatever by any of the things after him, or around him, or alongside him, and in need, for his perfection, of one thing alone, the motion without relation toward the one above him, I mean God, with the whole power of love.

⟦p14⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 99. ⟦s2⟧ The first man had nothing set between God and himself as a screen to knowing, and hindering the self-chosen kindredness that was to come about through love according to the motion toward God. ⟦s3⟧ For, being passionless by grace, he did not admit the deceit of the imagination of passions through pleasure; and, being in need of nothing, he was free of the necessity of distress about the arts because of need; and, being wise, he stood above the contemplation about nature because of knowledge.

⟦p15⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 100. ⟦s2⟧ God, who with wisdom gave subsistence to all nature, and secretly set the knowledge of himself in each of the rational essences according to its power, has given to us men too, as a munificent Master, the longing and yearning for himself according to nature, having naturally interwoven with it the power of reason; whereby we might be able with ease to know the modes of the fulfilment of the longing, and not, slipping aside, to miss what we strive to attain. ⟦s3⟧ Moved, then, by this longing, we are led on to seek concerning the truth itself, and the wisdom and governance that appear in good order in all things, eager through these to attain that for the sake of which we received the longing.

⟦s4⟧ *The end of the divine chapters of Maximus.*
