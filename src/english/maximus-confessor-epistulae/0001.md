⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0364] LETTER 1 — HORTATORY DISCOURSE IN THE FORM OF A LETTER

⟦s1⟧ *To the servant of God, the lord George, the all-praised prefect of Africa.*

⟦s2⟧ No one of those deprived of light in whatever way, as I think, so longs to enjoy the sun's ray, as all we lowly ones, who have been richly filled with thy nobleness, long to behold thee present again and to enjoy thy beauty — thee, stamped, to be sure, with the divine ways of thy companion virtues, in which also, when thou wast formerly resident among us, we know thee to have shone forth with greatness of nature; through which we have been the more clearly taught that virtue is a matter of inclination, and not of rank, and that the imitation of God is a matter of disposition, and not of dignity. ⟦s3⟧ For they in whom — blessed ones such as you — the power of love toward God has been genuinely rooted in the depth of the soul, to these the holding ever the same with regard to the good has without doubt accrued; just as, of course, to those agog with the love of material things, the proneness to fall into a multitude of passions, and to be easily scattered. ⟦s4⟧ And with very good reason. ⟦s5⟧ For he who, by inclination, according to his aspiration, holds fast to the Divine — which is one and alone and impassible, and has nothing at all, from eternity, contemplated together with it or contemplated in addition to it as different in essence; for none of the things that are from it attains to it — he too is one and alone and impassible, having become, by his inclining toward the One, free of relation, one and alone and unchangeable. ⟦s6⟧ For if what is longed for by him is one and alone, and this is properly unchangeable, as being in essence unmoved because of its natural infinity — for the unmoved infinite has nowhere to be moved to — then he too, as being a lover of the One, has in all likelihood become one and alone and [0365] unchangeable, as having made his inclination altogether unmoved from the One. ⟦s7⟧ But he who, in ignorance of the better, by his own inclination has bound the soul's yearning to material things, which are by nature changeable and alterable, and able nowhere at all to stand still, is of necessity changeable and passible and easily altered, having the soul's disposition moved along with, and suffering with, the things that are by nature moved and suffer.

⟦s8⟧ Let none, then, of the things that are, my blessed master, be able to put thee out of this thy good and deifying habit, which keeps thy inclination a fellow-traveller toward God; let not time, changing along with itself in disorder the drift of material affairs, alter the firm footing of thy inclination; let not the threat of men, holding out fear, shift the stability of thy good disposition; let not the speech of flattering men, sweetening the soul's hearing with its utterance, slacken its good tension; let not an appetite for retaliation, from being able, perchance, against someone, wholly corrupt the gentleness of thy manner; let not a longing for the glory that attends ruling diminish the aspiration of love toward the Divine. ⟦s9⟧ For not to rule over men is by no means a lack of glory, either with God or with men, but rather an increase of glory, inasmuch as the soul is set free from every kind of outward turmoil and care — so my account has it, and everyone who piously sees together what is fitting will, as I think, agree. ⟦s10⟧ For every man truly virtuous and God-loving is sufficient to himself for all happiness, needing no provision of outward things to procure it. ⟦s11⟧ For he who has made the reflections of the divine idioms his own distinguishing marks throughout his life has the fullness of all good things entire; through which the exact assimilation to God naturally comes to be in men — with which nothing of the things after God, when compared, is in any way to be set beside. ⟦s12⟧ And, to speak simply, let not sickness, not health, not wealth that is dragged below, not poverty in things that perish, not blame, not praise, not death, not life, not the present, not the future, nor at all any other of the things that are or come to be, be able to adulterate this philosophy, which has nurtured thee and brought thee forward to such renown both with God and with men.

⟦s13⟧ And this will come about — how? ⟦s14⟧ If we entrust to God all that concerns ourselves; and endure to seek nothing at all of the things we were not commanded by God to seek, but seek with all zeal everything whatever we were [0368] enjoined by God to seek; and if, of things, some — as being, after God, under our inclination and power — we will in every manner, and are zealous to give form to through the works themselves, while the others — by every vote, as not needing our inclination for their coming to be and their ceasing to be — we yield to God, with thanksgiving and faith, to lead wherever He wills and wishes, in no way at all at variance with the principles of His providence and governance; by which the universe is borne, unknowably, with ineffable wisdom, toward its own end according to the foreknowledge of God, even if He works the outcome of such principles otherwise, and contrary to our hope. ⟦s15⟧ For most wise is the guardian and protector of our affairs, inasmuch as He has acquired wisdom not as something that has befallen Him, but is Himself, in essence, Wisdom properly, both being it and being called it.

⟦s15⟧ Let us will to keep genuinely one thing only: to love Him, as He said, from the whole heart, and soul, and power, and our neighbour as ourselves; [n: Matth. xxii, 37.] being zealous to devise every manner of bringing this divine ordinance to its end; and the whole divine purpose stands achieved by us, most full in its compass, whatever dignity or rank, whatever life and condition we have — whether rulers or ruled, rich and poor, healthy and sick, or in whatever other state of body; and about the rest, all those things that in no way follow our volition by disposition, let us not be anxious at all. ⟦s16⟧ For what is it to us, that which is established neither according to nature by necessity, nor according to inclination from our own willing? ⟦s17⟧ And perhaps the word, wishing us to consider this, has given us no ordinance about it through a divine commandment; lest, setting our hand, contrary to nature, to the things not in our power, we misuse blameworthily the power of willing; and show it to be weak, as not having the things that can in no way be under it; and do violence to God, thinking the governance of providence to be our own — that governance which wisely uses the things not in our power for the chastisement of the evils in our power, and for the increase and vigorous course of the goods in our power.

⟦s18⟧ Dividing, then, from the things in our power the things not in our power, let us believe the latter to belong wholly to divine providence, and the former to be, with divine providence, the achievement also of our inclination according to volition. ⟦s19⟧ In our power, then, is — that I may speak comprehending it as in a definition — everything whatever that, comprised by the principle of virtue or, on the contrary, by the mode of vice, attends upon our inclination according to our volition. ⟦s20⟧ Not in our power is everything whatever that does not [0369] have our choice as the craftsman of its own existence; and everything that exists according to nature is free of virtue and vice, but by the use of those who have it becomes this or that — or, to say it more properly, appears so.

⟦s21⟧ All the goods in our power, then — and these alone — as works of our choice with God, and as things that are of our inclination, let us both will vehemently and make much of; and so indeed we shall be colts of divine grace, obedient and docile to the rein, receiving the Word as He mounts, and as He guides aright for us, in good order, the course of the virtues. ⟦s22⟧ But the things not in our power — as being not, through themselves, by nature vice or virtue, but instruments of choice, bringing the hidden impulse of the principle that is in our power into the open — let us not will at all; lest by misuse we corrupt the natural power of willing, when it wishes to lay hold of things impracticable, and that cannot be achieved by it. ⟦s23⟧ But let us entrust them to God, and receive with thanksgiving every change of times and of affairs, which comes about to our advantage according to the saving wish of God; so that not only through the same things, but also through the things not the same, the good of virtue may be achieved by us in the same way, and through the uneven, evenly. ⟦s24⟧ For no one ever falls from good hope who yields and entrusts to God the governance and providence, which rests with Him, of the things that do not rest with himself. ⟦s25⟧ And true is the Wise man who said somewhere in his words: Look to the ancient generations, and see: who believed the Lord and was put to shame? ⟦s25⟧ Or who called upon Him, and He overlooked him? [n: Eccli. ii, 11.]

⟦s26⟧ Since these things are so, as the account, running with truth, has shown — and has set forth clearly the property of things willed and things not willed, distinguishing from one another by definition the things that are not commixed — let us neither, when rule is taken from us, lay claim to it as suffering the deprivation of some good — for rule does not altogether follow us when we have willed it, lying far from the principle that is in our power; just as neither do wealth and health, nor whatever of the things that lie in the middle are given form by us toward virtue or vice — nor again, when it is given, thrust it away as something wholly evil, since it can perhaps be an instrument of virtue for the God-loving, who have chosen to offer everything to God with all zeal: in addition to all the things in their power, also the things not in their power that are permitted by economy to befall them. ⟦s27⟧ For what is perhaps not good or evil by nature through itself is, with reason, neither to be yearned for nor to be fled. ⟦s28⟧ For the things without which we can become and be and remain good and virtuous — and again evil, and bereft of all virtue — of these the possession is not in any way necessary to us at all, nor indeed the lack of possession, for the coming-to-be and the preservation of any good or evil. ⟦s29+30⟧ Since also some, deprived of power and of wealth and [0372] of health, have acquired many treasures of everlasting goods; and others, on the contrary, having power and wealth and health, have by their own inclination become outcasts from all the divine goods. ⟦s31+32⟧ And the poor man Lazarus bears witness, and the rich man of his story: the one, deprived of all material power, of health and of abundance, receiving the blessed rest in the bosom of Abraham; the other, laden to the extreme with these material things, shut off from the holy ones by the chasm, and condemned to have the chastising fire as his portion. [n: Luc. xvi, 19 seqq.] And again David and Job, the great contenders for the truth, proved through both lives, set off from one another diametrically, in opposition, preserved their virtue growing through contraries. ⟦s31+32⟧ For virtue is not the work of wealth and health and power, nor again of the lot opposed to these, so as to suffer change toward contraries, altered along with affairs; but of a divine disposition, and of an inclination qualified to the extreme by the good, and fixed unchangeably according to God, and keeping its own identity invariant among contraries.

⟦s33⟧ Let us, then, be content with rule when it is given, and accept it as an instrument, given us by God, of help toward virtue. ⟦s34+35⟧ And again, when it is taken away, let us not seek it, as laying down a great weight of fearful vexation, and as it reveals the purpose of Providence toward us — of Providence, which dispenses to each, lovingly, what is to his advantage. ⟦s34+35⟧ For, Seek not, says the Wise man, to become a judge, lest thou be unable to remove injustices; lest perchance thou stand in awe before the face of a mighty man, and put a stumbling-block in thy uprightness; and sin against the multitude of a city, and cast thyself down in the crowd, and bind sin twice. [n: Eccli. vii, 6-8.] Except, indeed, that if thou shouldst wish to appear even from these, blessed one, thou hast come off less than none of those of thy station. ⟦s36+37+38+39⟧ For the eyes of the Lord, as the same Wise man said, have looked upon thee for good, and He has raised thee up from thy lowliness, and lifted up thy head, and many have marvelled at thee; [n: Eccli. xi, 13.] and they tell to peoples and nations thy virtues. ⟦s36+37+38+39⟧ And what the Holy Spirit proclaimed beforehand of the apostles through the great David — Into all the earth has gone out the sound — of thy right deeds; and to the ends of the world the fragrant spreading of thy virtues has come. [n: Psal. xviii, 5; Rom. x, 18.] For thou didst not eat thy bread alone, but didst give the orphans a rich share of it, and from their youth didst nurture them as a father, and from their mother's womb didst guide them toward [0373] righteousness. ⟦s36+37+38+39⟧ Thou didst not overlook the naked perishing and leave them unclothed; and all the powerless have blessed thee, for thou didst warm their shoulders with the fleece of thy lambs. ⟦s40⟧ Thou didst not set thy gold upon thy dust — that is, upon the enjoyment of thy flesh — but didst send all thy wealth ahead into heaven, for the preparing of the goods of the soul. ⟦s41+42⟧ Thou wast not deceived by perishing wealth, and when it flowed thou didst not set thy heart upon it. [n: Psal. lxi, 11.] With mockers thou didst not lodge, whose life is shame and whose living is filled with stains. ⟦s43+44⟧ Thy foot did not hasten to deceit; nor didst thou rejoice at the fall of enemies, and thy heart say: Well done. [n: Job xxxi, 5.] The earth did not groan over thee; for thou didst not eat its strength alone without price, nor crush the souls of its masters. ⟦s45⟧ From unjust gifts thou didst draw back thy hands; and thou didst rescue the poor man from the hand of the mighty, and didst weep over everyone powerless; and seeing a man in straits, thou didst groan. ⟦s46⟧ The orphans who had no helper thou didst help; and the mouths of widows have blessed thee. ⟦s47⟧ For thou didst put on righteousness, and clothe thyself with upright judgement like a double cloak. ⟦s48⟧ Thou becamest an eye of the blind, and a foot of the lame, and a father of the powerless. ⟦s49+50⟧ Thou didst break the millstones of the unjust, and from the midst of their teeth didst wrench the plunder. [n: Job xxix, 12, 13; xxx, 25.] And, to say it in sum: thou feedest the hungry, and givest drink to the thirsty, and takest in strangers, and clothest the naked, and visitest the sick, and ministerest to those in prisons; [n: Matth. xxv, 35.] or rather, through all these thou art well-pleasing to God; through which things thou hast judged it more glorious than all else readily to suffice for all these.

⟦s51⟧ For this reason God has justly kept thee, and His lamp shines over thy head [n: Job xxix, 3.] — that is, the law of the commandments shines through brightly in thee, for all, by the practice of the virtues; and by His light thou walkest in darkness — that is, by the knowledge of the pious contemplation of the things that are, thou passest unharmed through the deceit and the ignorance of this age, hastening toward the shadowless light. ⟦s52⟧ For to thee alone in truth, beyond all men, beloved, it has belonged not to corrupt virtue by the weight of rule, nor in any way to bow the law of Christ to gold; but so far to enslave authority to virtue as to be known from these alone — virtue, I mean, and reason — and to become heard of by all, everywhere, through the renown of righteousness.

⟦s52⟧ But if, declining — as is likely — the hardship that is according to the flesh, we think to procure its comfort through authority, let us know that the comfort of the flesh the transgression of the divine commandment invented for life, and man, preferring the temporary to the everlasting, brought it in; but the hardship of the flesh the keeping of the divine commandment, and God, who for this reason became man, showed forth and legislated through Himself; and that the one is the cause of men's estrangement from paradise and from the goods there, and the other of the return into paradise, and of the taking-up into heaven; and — what is the most wonderful of all —
