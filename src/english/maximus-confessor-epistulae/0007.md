⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ LETTER 5

⟦s1⟧ *Of the same, to Constantine.*

⟦s2⟧ If my blessed master truly rejoices, as he has written, in meeting me through my letters as though I were present, it is clear that he rejoices altogether because he accepts my words. ⟦s3+4⟧ Now acceptance of words is the disposition, conformed to the words, of him who accepts the words; and the exact demonstration of this disposition is the deed of the power carried in the words. ⟦s5⟧ Let my master, then, furnish me, as a most trustworthy pledge of encouragement to speak, his own eagerness to do the things that are spoken; and I will never, as it is written, restrain my lips [n: Psal. xxxix, 10.] from speaking to him the things that can profit both him and me and others — so far as I am able to receive the grace of understanding wisely and of speaking piously what has been understood, from God who gives to all simply, and does not reproach. [n: Jac. i, 5.]

⟦s5⟧ [0421] Let us become, then, beloved, eager doers of the works of the commandment; and let us flee the error of this age, and bind the soul to none of the things in it that are reckoned delightful. ⟦s6⟧ For the world passes away, and all the things in it wither, being by nature the leavings of corruption. ⟦s7⟧ And the eagerness for divine things of those who aspire to salvation is attested not only by hatred toward sin, and the life free of affairs; and by estrangement from wicked men, and the soul's detachment of disposition from all things that perish; and by contempt of the flesh for the sake of virtue, and, toward all the things that through the senses deceitfully fawn upon the soul, a firm and unyielding self-mastery — through which we wither all licentious appetites and lull to sleep the disordered movements of anger, not allowing our thought, enslaved, to be carried off along with them, and, like a charioteer snatched away by hard-mouthed horses, to be borne down over precipices, there being no one to marshal and able to check the irrational impulse of the beasts yoked beneath.

⟦s8⟧ But also by endurance and patience toward all involuntary temptations; by which, even when we suffer bodily, we remain dispassionate in soul and unconquered, cast down by none of the things that shake us. ⟦s9+10⟧ Proved by both of these — I mean self-mastery over the passions that are in our power, and endurance of those that are not in our power — we put forth, as from a most divine sowing of the commandments, comely and ripe fruits, the virtues: for instance, prudence of knowledge, against both ignorance and superstition; courage, against both cowardice and headlong rashness; temperance, against licentiousness and stupidity; justice against injustice, I mean against taking more than one's share and taking less; arming ourselves with humble-mindedness against pride; putting forward condescension against elation; moderation against conceit; long-suffering against faint-heartedness; holding fast, again, to gentleness toward all, and to meekness, and to cheerful sharing with those in need; caring laboriously for hospitality and brotherly love and love for man; pursuing peace and love, in which, as in a kind of foundation of good things, all the good things given to men from God are gathered and kept.

⟦s11⟧ These things, my blessed master, everyone who yearns for the kingdom of heaven, and hastens to flee the trial of the everlasting punishments threatened against sinners, studies by all means to do and to keep as far as he can; having, as the helper of the keeping of such things, the unforgetting memory of death and of the uncertainty [0424] of its hour; the thought of the soul's desolation after its departure, which brings nothing else along with it except the conscience branded for the things it has done ill, and of the fearful search of it that will be made by the evil spirits in this air; the reflection on the bitter and lightless confinement of souls now in Hades; the expectation of the shame before the just Judge and the heavenly Powers at the manifestation of hidden things — a shame heavier than all the punishments threatened against sinners. ⟦s12⟧ These things, and more than these, I have both written already as far as I could, and I will not cease writing, so far as I am able, since I have my longing for my beloved master as the one who draws such words out of me.

⟦s13⟧ But let us be sober, and keep watch over the guarding of ourselves, blessed one, lest we be condemned the more, as speaking and hearing divine things only idly and in vain. ⟦s14⟧ For for these words too we shall render an account to God in the day of judgement [n: Rom. xiv, 12.] — both I who speak them and those who hear them — if we leave them altogether idle and fruitless. ⟦s14⟧ But may it rather come to pass that we all exult in all the words of God, and be shown doers of the divine and saving wills in them; by the prayers and intercessions of our all-glorious and all-holy Lady, the Theotokos and Ever-Virgin Mary, and of all the saints. ⟦s15⟧ Amen.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ LETTER 6

⟦s1⟧ *Of the same, to the most holy and most blessed archbishop John, on this: That the soul is incorporeal.*

⟦s2⟧ Having received the honoured letter of your perfection in virtue, adorned with every magnanimous moderation, I both admired and was amazed: how, being the same in thought in both, thou hast stood like thyself — neither unapproachable by thy loftiness, nor despised for thy lowliness. ⟦s3⟧ For having wisely blended for thyself out of both one form of virtue, thou makest, paradoxically, the other to be contemplated in each: [0425] in the lowly, the lofty; in the lofty, conversely, humility. ⟦s4+5⟧ Therefore indeed, servant of God, I receive without excuse thy injunction, which bids me demonstrate more from nature, without the testimony of Scripture and of the Fathers, whether the soul is an incorporeal creature; taking courage once more from the drops of your teaching, with which thou hast bedewed me in various ways when I was parched with ignorance.

⟦s6⟧ But who are these that affirm there to be no incorporeal creature? ⟦s6⟧ And with what non-existent fabrications are these opposing the truths of your words? ⟦s7⟧ And how, unless they are more contentious than is fitting, were they not put out of countenance by your argument, which stands so very far from every opposing account? ⟦s8⟧ And so that my account of this may proceed rather in due order, I shall make your wise argument itself, as an unconquerable ally of the truth, the first-fruits of my own; priding myself on what is yours as provisions for the road.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 1. ⟦s2⟧ If body is by its own nature unmoved, and the soul too is body, even if the most fine-grained, as they say, then insofar as it is body it will not be moved. ⟦s3⟧ And if this is so, whence have we movement? ⟦s4⟧ And so on to infinity, until we come to an incorporeal creature. ⟦s5⟧ But if they say that we are moved by God in the first account, then — since I know most of our movements to be out of place and shameful — they will of necessity declare the Divine the cause of these too.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 2. ⟦s2+3+4⟧ Another. ⟦s2+3+4⟧ And again: if every bodily existence that is in any way at all, according to the true account, being bounded by quantity and quality, in bulk and form, and surface and figure, sets the limit of the contemplation of itself, as being bounded by necessary limits — for he who searches it out can imagine nothing outside these — and the soul too is body, then it is altogether circumscribed either as from these, or as these, or as some of them, or as some one of these, or by these, or by some, or by some one of these. ⟦s2+3+4⟧ But in fact it is circumscribed neither as from these, nor as these, nor as some, nor as some one of these, nor by these, nor by some, nor by some one of these. ⟦s2+3+4⟧ Therefore the soul is not body.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 3. ⟦s2⟧ Another. ⟦s3⟧ If, then, it is not doubted by them that the soul is; and, being, it is none of the things the mind grasps, out of which, or which, bodies are — not colour, not figure, not resistance, not weight, not magnitude, not extension in three dimensions; and, simply, it is altogether none of the things grasped in bodily nature: inasmuch as it is none of these, as the argument has shown, it will be altogether incorporeal, if indeed it is.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0428] 4. ⟦s2⟧ Another. ⟦s3⟧ Further: if every contraction and expansion and cut belongs to bodies, and what is free of all contraction and expansion and of any cut whatever is altogether incorporeal, then the soul is incorporeal, as being wholly unreceptive of all these.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 5. ⟦s2⟧ Another. ⟦s3⟧ If, all bodily essence being divided into the animate and the inanimate, the soul too is body, it will altogether be either animate or inanimate. ⟦s4⟧ But if animate, it will be ensouled altogether by something that ensouls it — either an essence, or a power, that is, an accident. ⟦s5⟧ But to say that a soul is ensouled, as also that light is lit, or that fire is heated, is wholly laughable. ⟦s6⟧ And if we say that an essence ensouls the soul, we shall again call that either body or incorporeal. ⟦s7⟧ And if body, the account of it will meet always the same absurd approaches of thoughts, out of absurd ones, until it is agreed together that the soul is incorporeal. ⟦s8⟧ But if we say that a power, that is a quality, ensouls the soul, then what is without essence and without hypostasis will be that which gives life and movement to the essence. ⟦s9⟧ Besides this, the difference that consists in ruling and being ruled will be reversed, and contrary to nature; if indeed the soul, fashioned by nature to rule the body as an incorporeal essence, will — being body according to them — be ruled by what does not exist according to essence, I mean the power according to quality: she who exists and subsists according to essence, as is the common preconception of all. ⟦s10⟧ But if we call it inanimate body, it will be without sense and without imagination, irrational and without understanding. ⟦s11⟧ But in fact all these both are about the soul and in the soul, and are seen there. ⟦s11⟧ Therefore the soul is not body. ⟦s12⟧ For that it has this body, which is of another essence, we who know well do not decline to say; but to declare that it is body we altogether shun, as most rash and at the same time most uneducated, not being ignorant that being differs greatly from having.

⟦s13⟧ But if, because they call the Divine incorporeal, they will not admit to call the soul incorporeal, fearing a fear where there is none, lest they bring it to equality with God, then let them cut away the rest of ours as well, so that their contest of folly may be complete: all the names that we have been called from God, as participating; or that God has been called from us, as operating and giving or imparting — or rather, was called God from us. ⟦s14⟧ What, then, do I mean by these? ⟦s15⟧ Being, living, being illumined, being made good, reasoning, understanding: the ones on account of the essence; the others on account of the movement, that is, inclination and disposition. ⟦s16⟧ For we are called beings, and living, and lights, and good, and intellectual, and rational. ⟦s17⟧ And God likewise we name being, and living, and light, and good, and mind, and reason; either as being these [0429] and being called these from him as from a cause, or as being operated upon, calling the Divine these from what is ours. ⟦s18⟧ What account, then, have they, that the soul both is and is named all these things, besides many others, but no longer incorporeal, because the Divine is? ⟦s19⟧ As though it were needful either that we not be called these things because the Divine is, or that God not be named these things because we too are. ⟦s20+21⟧ But in fact we both are these things and are named them — not as being, on account of them, the same as God, but as the caused to the cause, or the participant to the participated, or existence to the cause; nor comparatively or synonymously, as with things consubstantial, but homonymously, and, so to speak, by participation — the things standing apart from one another in nature infinitely; unless it please anyone to say, as is truly so, that the Divine is above both nature and thing.

⟦s22⟧ Where, further, according to them, if it be granted that the soul is body, will the principle of the "according to the image" be preserved for us, unless we say that the soul has the likeness to the archetype through all things? ⟦s23⟧ For just as we name the image that is known in these things intellectual, as the image of the intellectual, and so of the immortal, incorruptible and invisible; so too, calling it incorporeal as the image of the incorporeal — as escaping all bulk and likewise all measurement by extension — we yet call it again something other than that, according to the property of nature. ⟦s24⟧ For otherwise it would no longer be an image, but an invariant identity. ⟦s25⟧ And in the very things in which that is beheld in the uncreated and unchangeable and unoriginate nature, in the same things we clearly hold the created rational nature to be shown — not in the same way, but as the disclosure of a word in the last echoes of a voice. ⟦s26+27⟧ And just as the most philosophic mind, through the things that are accomplished among beings in the ordering of the universe, transferring the powers of its conceptions to the pure and immaterial, distinguishes the mover from the things moved; and, grasping it as one and alone and simple, existing from itself, holding ever the same, and a making cause in the abiding of its own identity, knows it to be by nature unapproachable to the others: unchangeable, because unmoved — for there is nothing toward which it will be moved, that which both makes and fills the things that are, and is above all the things that are — and comprehensive, as Creator, knowing all things even before their coming-to-be by the infinity of its power: in the same mode of contemplation, through the varied and skilful movement of the members and parts of the small world — I mean man — reckoning the moving cause, it knows it to be something other in essence than the organic existence of the body; and not as a body in a body, which is the view of the new wise men and the dogmatists of what is undemonstrated, but [0432] it contemplates it as simple and unitary in things scattered, and unbounded in things contracted: changeable, as moved; and moved, as having something toward which it will be moved; and of the change in movement it knows the cause to be not the nature, but the judgement, whenever this happens to be mistaken.

⟦s28⟧ Change, then, does not belong, as I think, to the very essence of the soul — for then it would change infinitely often, and would not remain the same according to essence — but to the movement that is in our power, carried along with the sovereign will. ⟦s29⟧ Hence for the opponents to assert and declare that even the soul's ever-moving about the Divine has the power of change seems to me to belong not to men who speak the truth, but to men who seek pompously the clever, and not reverently the exactness of the account. ⟦s30+31⟧ For who that shares in reason, and is not without portion in the love of virtue, does not know that the soul's ever-moving about the good is nothing other than natural operation, accomplished toward the cause for which and on account of which it came to be; whereas change is a movement contrary to nature, bringing in the failure of this cause? ⟦s32⟧ For change, to speak in my own way, is nothing other than weakness, and a falling away from the operations according to nature. ⟦s33⟧ Deign, then, to say to the word-chatterers: No longer dare to compare God and creation. ⟦s34⟧ For the Divine, properly speaking, is neither body, nor incorporeal, nor essence, nor any of the things that are understood or spoken — not to enumerate everything in order — but, being understood, it remains ungraspable, and being spoken, ineffable.

⟦s35⟧ Let these things, then, be said by us by way of inquiry and not by way of dogma. ⟦s35⟧ And if they are sufficient for the subject, so far as is possible in a short letter, and do not fall much short of your greatness of mind, thanks be to God, and to you who urged me on. ⟦s36⟧ But if you wish, in case the opponents are not persuaded, that we grapple with them more combatively, we are ready, by the grace of God, to let loose upon them the cloud of divine witnesses and testimonies that surrounds us; [n: Hebr. xii, 1.] so that, laying aside the weight of all laborious inquiry into this, and the easily-besetting word-battle of the contentious, we may hold undisturbed the true knowledge of God and of his creatures. ⟦s37⟧ But if these things have been put forward deficiently — which must quite necessarily have happened — and not successfully, as coming from an unlearned understanding and a weak instrument, forgive me, beloved, who dared to say these things through obedience alone, as God who searches the hearts [n: Psal. vii, 10.] knows; and correct gently him who does not wish to be ignorant [0433] of any of the things that are profitable, and lead by the hand with sympathy him who desires to walk aright.
