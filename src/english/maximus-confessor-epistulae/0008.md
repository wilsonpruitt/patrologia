⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ LETTER 7

⟦s1⟧ *Of the same, to John the presbyter, on the soul's having its intellectual operation even after death, and being deprived of no natural power.*

⟦s2⟧ On the second of the present month of August, of the present first indiction, our common friend delivered to me the honoured letter of your paternal holiness; on reading which I was comforted, as was fitting, at you, my masters and, after God, the causes of all my good things, being in health. ⟦s3⟧ For the Holy Spirit of God has been united with you so wholly that, even beholding you absent with the eyes of the mind alone, I order both life and speech according to the power that is in me; as both seeing and revering God, made manifest in memory through you. ⟦s4⟧ But I was grieved not moderately at the doctrine about the soul which, as you have written, is preached there by certain men without fear, considering how much strength and freedom of speech such an evil has here too. ⟦s5⟧ And what fills me the more with dejection here, and casts a weight like a cloud upon my understanding, is the new-fangled doctrine about the resurrection now championed by almost everyone, and especially by the supposedly eminent monks; so that even one who shares in reasoning in any way at all no longer supposes by conjecture that the coming of the Antichrist is being prefaced, but rather believes and affirms that it is present, having the very [absurdity] of what is said bearing witness to such an evil.

⟦s6+7+8⟧ For they say — they who sharpen their tongue fearlessly against all things, and reckon nothing fearful of what is impiously done or said — that bodies at the resurrection will again be held together for living by phlegm and blood, by yellow and black bile, by the drawing of air and by sensible food; nothing at all new beyond the present life being shown forth through the resurrection, except not being able to die again. ⟦s6+7+8⟧ I know not how, toward all the things in the holy Scripture about the soul and the resurrection of the dead, spoken through prophets and apostles, and through the very Word of God who conversed with us through the flesh, and especially those set forth in a particular manner to the Corinthians through the divine Paul concerning the resurrection, [n: I Cor. xv, 44.] they have willingly stopped their hearing, and shut the eye of the soul to understanding — things so clear, and plain to hear, and needing no interpreter to be known; and, having nature itself, the nature of beings, teaching them no less than the things divinely declared — nature, which knows how to lead even the barbarians to knowledge of truth, [0436] putting itself forth as a manifest demonstration of the things sought — they are not abashed before her.

⟦s6+7+8⟧ For who, unless he is wholly deprived of the grace of reason, does not know that every nature is essentially bounded by its own idioms, and by this shows both the mode of its own existence and the difference by which it is marked off without mixture from the other natures; and that when it is bereft of the idioms that hold together its essence, either it will not be at all, or it has become what it was not — if indeed one would consent at all, by way of accommodation, to say that it is, upon the removal of its idioms? ⟦s6+7+8⟧ For every subject is of a nature to be corrupted together with the natural idioms when they are corrupted. ⟦s9⟧ For what, or where, will an ox be, or a horse, or a lion, and such things, if the idioms holding together the nature of each are not? ⟦s10⟧ Or who, being free of madness, would dare to say that these things are without those, I do not see. ⟦s11⟧ If, then, it is true that none of the things that are can be or be known without the things that naturally and properly characterize them, then those who take away from the soul what is innate in it according to essence — that is, the rational and intellectual, in that it ever is, and operates understanding and reasoning — plainly take away together its being too, even if they do not say so; and according to them it will be corruptible and mortal, not having being after the death of the body: than which what is more absurd?

⟦s11⟧ But if they suppose that it lives, in word at least if not in fact, wary of the refutation from all, then it is altogether also moved. ⟦s12⟧ For every life of things that have come to be is shown in movement. ⟦s13⟧ And if it is moved, it altogether also operates; for every movement is made manifest through operation. ⟦s14+15⟧ And if it operates, being moved altogether naturally, and not by imposition or by accident, it will operate — for not in a circle, or by change of place, or, to put it briefly, bodily, but intellectually and rationally. ⟦s16⟧ And if it both lives and is moved and operates intellectually and rationally, it altogether reasons and understands and knows. ⟦s17⟧ But if it does not know and reason, neither does it operate, nor is it moved, nor does it live. ⟦s18⟧ For it is not possible for there to be life without the innate movement; nor for natural movement to be shown forth when the connatural operation is not.

⟦s19⟧ And besides: the soul is rational and intellectual either through itself, or through the body. ⟦s20⟧ And if through itself, that is, through its own essence, it is rational and intellectual, it is altogether also self-subsistent. ⟦s21⟧ And if it is self-subsistent through itself by nature, it will operate both by itself and with the body, understanding and reasoning according to nature, and never ceasing from the intellectual powers that belong to it naturally. ⟦s22⟧ For the things that belong by nature to what in any way is cannot be taken away so long as it is and subsists. ⟦s23⟧ The soul, then, ever being since it came to be, and subsisting through God who [0437] fashioned it so, ever understands and reasons and knows, both by itself and with the body, through itself and its own nature. ⟦s24⟧ No account, therefore, will be found that can estrange the soul, after the body's dissolution, from the things that belong to it naturally, and not on account of the body. ⟦s25+26⟧ But if the soul is rational and intellectual on account of the body, first, the body will be more honourable than the soul that came to be on account of it; then, too, it will have the intellectual and rational from it, as having come to be on account of it. ⟦s27⟧ For if without the body the soul can in no way understand or reason, understanding and reasoning will belong to it altogether from the body. ⟦s28⟧ And if the soul has acquired understanding and reasoning from the body, as being unable, according to those who say so, to have these without it, it will altogether not be self-subsistent either; for how could it be, which without the body does not have by itself what characterizes it? ⟦s29+30+31⟧ And if it is not self-subsistent, it is clearly not an essence either. ⟦s29+30+31⟧ And not being a self-subsistent essence, it will be an accident, inhering only in the body that subsists naturally, and after its dissolution able in no way to be anything at all. ⟦s29+30+31⟧ And there will be nothing more, for those who are so senseless as to have taken away the immortality of the soul, than the vain labour of Epicurus and Aristotle, with whom, as is likely, these noble fellows even pride themselves on being ranked. ⟦s29+30+31⟧ And on this, so much.

⟦s29+30+31⟧ But as to what is said by them about the resurrection, who that cares for virtue and piety, hearing it, will not gain dispassion for himself through a passion — I mean indignation against them? ⟦s32⟧ For if, as they say, the body will rise according to the image of the present life, qualified invariantly by the warring humours, and will be fed at the fearful coming of God after the consummation of this world, I do not think that through these things — to speak briefly, cutting short the many things that might be said — they champion anything other than eternal death and corruption without end. ⟦s33⟧ For if death is the corruption of bodies so constituted, and the body that is constituted [0440] through the influx of foods is ever corrupted, dissipated by the flux on account of the natural antipathy of the humours in it, of which it is also constituted, then by the very things through which they suppose the body to be constituted after the resurrection they proclaim death preserved forever. ⟦s34⟧ Whereas one ought to believe that the body itself rises according to essence and form, but incorruptible and immortal, and, to speak apostolically, spiritual instead of psychic, [n: I Cor. xv, 44.] in that it carries nothing of the constitutive property, alterable according to corruption, as God knows who will temper it anew toward the impassible and honourable; and to busy oneself about nothing more.

⟦s35⟧ Seeing the many rejoicing and delighting in these things, I was dumb, and was humbled, and kept silence from good things, [n: Psal. xxxviii, 3.] judging silence more profitable than speech for every prudent man; and I go mourning and with a sad countenance by myself, reckoning how great a death of souls such words, if neglected, will work, darting everywhere like sparks; and there is no one able or willing to come to the help of the word in its danger, on account of the wickedness of the time that prevails. ⟦s36⟧ Wherefore I groan the more at my separation from you, not having close at hand the all-powerful oversight of your paternal holiness, which guarded me and delivered me from harmful things, and more frequently propped up by word my many slips.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ LETTER 8

⟦s1⟧ *Of the same, to the same.*

⟦s2⟧ The longing according to the flesh is of a nature to wither with time, departing along with those who are separated in place, who have their conjunction with one another according to it. ⟦s3⟧ For it has its constitution in sense, which can in no way lay hold of the things not present. ⟦s4⟧ But the longing according to the spirit ever holds intellectually present to one another those joined by it, even if they are separated bodily from one another, not admitting circumscription by time or place. ⟦s5⟧ For it has its existence in the mind, which is in no way divided along with, or shut in along with, the bodies divided from one another in place. ⟦s6⟧ Having been deemed worthy from the beginning to have this longing toward you most holy ones, [0441] I seem ever to see you present, and to perceive you conversing; and there is no time or place that can remove me from the memory of you, which shows you constantly present spiritually, and you driving away all the foul-smelling thoughts in me, which do not bear the great fragrance of the divine grace in you. ⟦s6⟧ And I am persuaded indeed that memory does not merely imagine you most holy ones, but perceives you truly present; making what comes to pass an exact assurance of your presence. ⟦s7⟧ For the effective power in you according to the grace of God, driving away together with the memory the demons that trouble me, provides the clearest showing of your presence. ⟦s8⟧ And it is nothing wondrous if the God of signs and wonders makes those absent bodily to be present intellectually — as he himself knows, and those who are operated by him in such things in a manner befitting God — and more than bodies are present to one another in place. ⟦s9⟧ And if, merely remembered, honoured Fathers, you furnish so great a penalty to my dishonourable thoughts, visiting invisibly in the spirit, how much more when present before my eyes, and sanctifying my hearing with divine words by a living voice, and teaching virtue brilliantly by your own manners?

⟦s10+11⟧ Therefore I go mourning and with a sad countenance, longing for and seeking thee, the good shepherd and teacher, who knowest how to make me, the wandering sheep, dwell in a place of green pasture [n: Psal. xxii, 2.] — I mean the habit of virtue through practice, which drives back the burning heat of the passions — and who art able to nourish me beside the water of rest — I say the grace of knowledge that waters the mind through contemplation; who turnest my soul from vice to virtue by divine words and examples, and leadest me in the paths of righteousness, showing the saving modes according to piety; and who wisely comfortest me with rod and staff — that is, by the memory of the everlasting punishments, leading the beginner away from vice as with a kind of rod, and by the encouragement of the good things to come, supporting through hope, like a staff, the one who is advancing toward virtue; which, I think, the Lord and Saviour too hinted symbolically, pouring wine and oil on the wounds of him who fell among robbers. [n: Luc. x, 34.] For the longing for the kingdom of heaven, mixed with the fear of the Gehenna of everlasting fire, keeps the soul unhurt, binding it fast to the love of the Creator, once it has been scoured of every passionate thought through the astringency.

⟦s12⟧ But fill now, honoured Father, thou too, the reed of teaching with the divine Spirit, [0444] and call, like a shepherd and with love for man, him who wanders unshepherded on the mountains of ignorance and the hills of vice; and put him under thy right hand, and bind up with sympathy him who has been torn by many wicked bites of the wolves of Arabia [n: Habac. i, 8.] — that is, of the West; for the name of Arabia, as they say, when interpreted into the Greek tongue, signifies "West". ⟦s13⟧ And I understand — and perhaps not mistakenly — according to the principles of anagogy, by Arabia this flesh, tyrannous in those like me over the divine laws of the spirit; which nourishes, in very truth, many shameless and savage wolves; which is truly a West, and has become one through sin, and is truly so called; on which there rides, [n: Psal. lxvii, 5.] as good and loving man, the good Shepherd, he who shepherds Israel, God and the Word of God; he who sits upon the Cherubim, having clearly given heed and beheld our danger; and therefore having shown himself, and roused up his own might, and come in the flesh to save the lost, and to slay the wicked beasts nurtured in it, and to make it a land of peace, and a pasture of rational sheep led up to God through practice. ⟦s13⟧ Of which I have not yet passed by the territory unharmed through spiritual contemplation, as Israel passed Mount Seir; nor have I passed through its desires dispassionately, through a practice of knowledge, as those who drew their race from Jacob passed, through Moses, their brothers the sons of Esau who dwelt in Araba; but I still dwell in Araba, like the sons of Esau — the West, I mean the flesh — expecting, on account of the fleshly pleasures, no other life after the present one. ⟦s14⟧ Which flesh every true Israel, one who sees God, does not consent even to sojourn in, pressing on to be away from the flesh through the virtues, and aspiring to be at home with the Lord [n: II Cor. v, 6.] through knowledge — him who mystically both is and is named the true land of promise of ineffable good things, that flows with milk and honey. ⟦s15⟧ For the Lord is nourisher of those who are infants according to him, and the delight of those who are growing to manhood according to him; nourishing with virtues through practice, as with milk, those who fear him, and sweetening with mystical knowledges, as with honey through spiritual contemplation, those who love him.

⟦s16⟧ Therefore do not cease, honoured Father, to shepherd me with sympathy by the words of truth — me, the prey of beasts — and do not stop ransoming me [0445] from the passions in the flesh, together with the spirits of wickedness that stir them up, which wickedly devour and consume my inner man; and call me to thyself, and make me dwell under thy wings, if indeed all fear of the sensible barbarians, on account of whom I came across such stretches of sea as one who loves life, is truly gone. ⟦s16⟧ About which I especially beseech you most holy ones, after the longed-for notice of your all-radiant health according to God, to inform me more exactly; and to show me by letter the whole state of affairs prevailing there at present, as it is. ⟦s17⟧ For being weak in thought and very rotten, and scarcely able even in peace to be sober in some small part, and to gather together my mind that is scattered about many things through cowardice, I wish to make the voyage with safety; as being imperfect in understanding, and not able to enter into the principles of the providence that wisely governs the whole; lest, bending at the knee in thought through ignorance in what befalls, and through weakness yielding in the contests of endurance, I be deprived of the crowns that come from them, having become a deserter in the trial of what is required. ⟦s18⟧ By the letter, as if present, I greet you most holy ones, and all those with you; asking, through your holy and acceptable prayers, to be commended to Christ, the God and Saviour of all.
