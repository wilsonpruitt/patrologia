⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0489] …especially certain of those who, having strayed from the true doctrines of the Church concerning the soul — telling the marvel that the soul pre-exists its own body, in the Greek manner, or exists after it, in the Jewish manner, and supposing a beginning neither good nor God-pleasing to precede the fashioning of visible things — are disposed in antipathy toward such an account; whose unsound and discordant problems on this head I have judged it not to belong to the present occasion and discourse to refute, and I shall return to what lies before us.

⟦s2⟧ Since, then, as has been set out, every composite nature is so disposed, none of those who have learned to think piously about divine things would ever dare to say that Christ is one composite nature; lest, choosing to say such things, by a certain natural train and consequence of necessity — supposing him wholly created and from things that are not, circumscribed and passible, and not consubstantial with the Father; and either the flesh coeternal with the Word, or the Word, being of one time with the flesh, for such is the principle of every composite nature — he should make himself liable to fearful charges. ⟦s3⟧ For what is of a composite nature is also, clearly, composite according to nature. ⟦s4⟧ And what is composite according to nature could never be connatural and consubstantial with what is simple according to nature. ⟦s5⟧ It is not right, then, for the pious to say that Christ is one composite nature, not only because of the absurdity that is gathered from such an expression, but because none of the God-chosen Fathers has ever been shown to have said this; but rather to confess one composite hypostasis of Christ, and two natures; so that he may be known as consubstantial and connatural with the Father according to the Godhead, and the same be recognized as consubstantial and connatural with us according to the flesh; and that one and the same, toward both the Father and us, be believed to be mediator of God and men.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *On composite hypostasis.*

⟦s2+3⟧ But if those who speak against us should say that we too — since every composite hypostasis is shown to have its own parts of one time both with itself and with one another, and we do not deny saying that Christ is a composite hypostasis — cannot shake off the reproach on this point, as caught by our own words: we say it would truly be so, if we supposed it to be composite as having a species predicated of it. ⟦s4⟧ But as it is, not saying this, we incur no reproach of any kind whatever, knowing clearly that every composite hypostasis that falls under some species is composite not on its own account, but on account of the nature that contains it, when that nature is composite, and of the predicated species under which it is naturally brought; since the particular, that is the specific, holds in itself the universal — that is, the common and generic — whole and most fully, while the proper imparts nothing at all of its own principle to the generic: which, in the case of Christ, no one at all of those who from of old down to us [0492] have been expounders of the sense of the divine sayings has ever proclaimed; in whose case no one will be able to find either genus or species as predicated of Christ. ⟦s5⟧ For not by the principle of nature did the thearchic Word sojourn among us through flesh; but by a mode of economy, having united our nature to himself according to hypostasis without deficiency, he renewed it. ⟦s6+7⟧ So they have profited nothing from their great cleverness — to take up the sequence of what was examined a little before — since the clever device has turned back upon them again into the quantity they fled, so that they cannot say that even the difference in quality itself is without quantity. ⟦s8⟧ For it is altogether necessary that the qualities whose difference they suppose there to be be many, or at the least two, as has been said before. ⟦s9⟧ For that a single quality, holding itself altogether unmixed with respect to another, differs from itself, no one who has a mind would ever say.

⟦s10⟧ It remains, then, either that they desist even from bare difference itself, having learned that it is wholly indicative of a quantity of certain things that differ; or that they accept along with us the confession of the truth — with us who, by the principle of the difference and of it only, for the knowledge that the things which came together have remained unconfused, piously take up the number in accordance with the Fathers. ⟦s11+12+13⟧ Otherwise, let them tell us, who wish lovingly to ask — if there is in them a word of wisdom not ignoble — how, saying that the things which ran together into union are two, and not denying that these same have remained unconfused even after the union, as neither has departed from its natural definition and principle, even if both together make up one person of the Son and one hypostasis, they do not confess as two the things that are preserved even by their own account, as they say, if they speak the truth? ⟦s11+12+13⟧ And what is their account of the diminution, and for what cause, and how have both the natures become one? ⟦s11+12+13⟧ Or, if the union has remained pure of diminution, what is the account of the things preserved without diminution not being spoken of? ⟦s11+12+13⟧ Then we readily acquiesce in the privation of the one, and admire the vigour of their erudition, as of those alone shown to us capable of the knowledge of things unspeakable. ⟦s11+12+13⟧ But they will never be able to produce the argument that persuades of this.

⟦s11+12+13⟧ *A concise solution of Severus's difficulty against the expression "In two".*

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1+2+3+4+5⟧ Even if, above all, they flee at last to that unsound and empty invention of Severus — the one he carries about everywhere and sings as something clever — saying that it is ridiculous to say that from two natures two natures are again displayed from the union, drawing the likely conclusions as from a premise granted by us: from whom of those instructed in the divine doctrines of the Church did they hear that the union from two natures produced two natures? ⟦s1+2+3+4+5⟧ But they will by no means be able to say that they have heard it, if they care for truth. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5⟧ We say that the union came to be from two natures; but that the one [0493] hypostasis of Christ brought to completion from them is composite, keeping and preserving those very natures from which it was composed — as a whole its own parts — without deficiency, with their natural idioms, and without change; and kept and preserved in them, as a whole in parts. ⟦s6⟧ From two natures, then, we say that Christ is not two natures, but one composite hypostasis; of these as of its own parts; comprehensive of the natures, and containing these as its own and completing parts; and in them, as in parts, both being and being recognized. ⟦s7⟧ For as long as the things which came together are believed to have remained unconfused — and they remain unconfused always — it is impossible for them not to be two in respect of their naturally being what each naturally is according to essence; just as, then, for these not to be one by the principle of the hypostasis, as long as the union exists — and it exists always, according to the pious confession of the faithful — is also impossible.

⟦s8⟧ Therefore, saying that the same is one and two, we do not say both of the same and one thing in the same respect, but in one respect and in another. ⟦s9+10⟧ For instance, according to the principle of the nature of the things from which the union is, the "two": for we do not know God the Word to be the same as his own flesh according to nature; but according to the principle of the hypostasis, the "one": for we know God the Word to be the same as his own flesh according to hypostasis. ⟦s11⟧ Neither, then, do we senselessly fuse the natures into one nature, by saying that the natural difference in Christ has been abolished, lest we introduce a change of the Word and of the flesh; nor again do we madly divide them into two self-subsistent beings, by positing a difference also according to the principle of the hypostasis, lest we deny our own salvation: as did those who, from diametrically opposite sides, parted the impiety between them, Apollinarius together with Eutyches, and Nestorius — the former, not knowing a natural difference in Christ; the latter, adding to it a personal one as well — and fell from the truth. ⟦s12⟧ But knowing that even in the union according to hypostasis the natural property is unchangeable, we turn away from confusion and from division alike, making neither the union confusion, as though ignorant of the things united, nor the difference division, as though knowing the things that differ to subsist by themselves in partition.

⟦s13⟧ This, then, as I think, the great Theologian Gregory too appears to teach in the great Apologetic, saying «One from both, and both through one»; as though he said: For just as one from both — that is, from two natures the one, as a whole from parts, according to the principle of the hypostasis — so also through the one according to hypostasis, as through a whole, both, by the principle of nature, the parts: that is, the two. ⟦s14⟧ Which, going through it more clearly in the second oration on the Son, he says: «For even if the combination is one, yet not [0496] in nature, but in concourse.» ⟦s14⟧ And this very thing the blessed Cyril too is shown to think in his letters to Succensus, in which, on the example of man, he says: «So that the two are no longer two, but through both the one living being is brought to completion»; all but crying out explicitly in these words that, for the things from which the composition is — both in the man that we are, who is from soul and body, and in the mystery of Christ, which is from Godhead and manhood — their being two according to the essential principle has not been abolished; since otherwise not even otherness of essence would still remain. ⟦s15⟧ But that they are two by themselves, in monad and monad, self-subsistently, the principle of piety does not admit at all; lest we divide the one man, or living being, into two men or living beings, and the one Christ into two Christs, or two Sons. ⟦s16⟧ For if the blessed Cyril had not known well to think and to hold and to teach this everywhere, he would not have said that the difference of the natures was not abolished through the union; he would not have taught that the one Christ is one thing and another, and other and other, and this and that, and both, and that the natures have remained unconfused. ⟦s17⟧ He would not have held that the Word did not pass over into the nature of the flesh, or that the flesh was not converted into the nature of the Word himself. ⟦s18⟧ He would not have known that the God-speaking men make the evangelical and apostolic sayings about the Lord some common, as of one person, and others divided, as of two natures. ⟦s19⟧ He would not have declined to say that the whole, that is Christ, is one nature, if he had known this to be pious. ⟦s20⟧ He would not have defined human nature as nothing other than flesh intellectually ensouled, affirming through this that the nature of manhood too is perfect in Christ. ⟦s21⟧ He would not have said that by adding "Incarnate" the signification of our essence is brought in. ⟦s22⟧ He would not have determined that to say two natures united without division is significant of right belief, when the expression is not mischievously put forward in the mode Nestorius held. ⟦s23+24⟧ He would not have been seen saying such things — no, countless others — as is clear to the diligent from his writings.

⟦s25⟧ Whence, then, and from which of the teachers, will they demonstrate that the natures in Christ, from which he also is — preserved after the union, as they say, even by their own account — ought not to be piously confessed by the faithful as two natures? ⟦s26⟧ For no one at all of the approved teachers is found to have decreed this; on the contrary, all both said it, and taught the faithful through their own words to say it; unless indeed, fashioning from their heart, as is their custom, what seems good to them, they should put forward the fiction as from one of the Fathers, for the deception of the more simple. ⟦s27⟧ But these men, as it seems, deeming it a shame to be persuaded, and preferring their attachment to the men who deceived them from the beginning [0497] to the divine love, and honouring contentiousness above piety, make the acceptance and honour from God for the concord of the faith lower than being honoured as priests by men — to whom they bring the deception by lying against us of things that are not — and than receiving evil in return for evils, I mean the perishable things as the wage of deception; and they do not cease from warring against the truth and attempting to draw away from it as many as they can.

⟦s28⟧ Therefore I, the least, exhort you — being genuine children of the holy apostolic Church, in which, illumined through the regeneration of the divine washing, you were deemed worthy to be adopted to God in the Spirit — to withdraw from such men, remembering the holy Apostle Paul writing to the Romans in this manner: I exhort you, brethren, to watch those who make the dissensions and the stumbling-blocks contrary to the teaching which you learned, and turn away from them. ⟦s29+30⟧ For such men do not serve our Lord Christ, but their own belly; and through fair speech and blessing they deceive the hearts of the innocent. [n: Rom. xvi, 17, 18.] And again, concerning these same men, accustomed to stir up troubles in the Church of God, writing to the Corinthians thus: For such are false apostles, deceitful workers, transforming themselves into Apostles of Christ. ⟦s31⟧ And no marvel; for Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light. ⟦s32+33⟧ It is no great thing, then, if his ministers also are transformed as ministers of righteousness; whose end shall be according to their works. [n: II Cor. xi, 13-15.] And the deceitfulness of the inclination of these very men, and the stealth of their manner, the Lord in the Gospels, hinting at it, proclaimed beforehand, as it were securing the faithful, saying: Beware of the false prophets, who come to you in sheep's clothing, but inwardly are ravening wolves. ⟦s34+35⟧ By their fruits you shall know them. [n: Matth. vii, 15, 16.] And concerning these very men the holy Evangelist John too, in his first catholic epistle, making the pious more sober, says: Beloved, believe not every spirit, but test the spirits, whether they are of God; for many false prophets have gone out into the world. ⟦s36+37⟧ Hereby know the Spirit of God: every spirit that confesses Jesus Christ come in the flesh is of God. ⟦s38⟧ And every spirit that does not confess the Lord Jesus Christ to have come in the flesh is not of God; and this is ⟨that of the Antichrist, of which you have heard that it comes, and⟩ even now is in the world. [n: I Joan. iv, 1-3.]
