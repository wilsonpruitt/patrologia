⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0565] — for it is impossible that a difference be known that is wholly without share in quantity — taking up number for the sole indication, but not the division, of the quantity conceived together with the difference, does not depart from the truth, signifying through number the quantity conceived of necessity with the difference; since, as I said, with every difference spoken of in whatever mode a quantity is wholly conceived together, of which we have been taught that number is by nature indicative, but not divisive. ⟦s2⟧ And if he who signifies through number the quantity conceived of necessity together with the difference, for the establishing of the mystery, cares rather for the truth, that he may not uphold a confusion of the things united: how must one not think it just, according to some, that for this cause the quantity according to essence of the things preserved without confusion in Christ after the union be confessed, since number, as the argument has shown at length, is by nature to make indication of difference, but not of division?

⟦s3⟧ If, then, the principle according to essence of each of the things united remained unaltered after the union, not falling over into the definition of the essence of the other, manifestly the Godhead of Christ has remained according to essence Godhead, not migrating into the essence of flesh; and again the manhood of the same has remained according to essence manhood, not suffering change into the nature of the Godhead. ⟦s4⟧ And if neither the Godhead of Christ nor the manhood have migrated into each other on account of the union according to hypostasis, but the natures have remained unconfused, according to the great Cyril, clearly the difference after the union is of the natures preserved in Christ, and no account will gainsay it. ⟦s5+6+7+8+9+10⟧ And if the difference after the union is of the things preserved without confusion in Christ, and it is impossible that a difference be known wholly separated from quantity, let the quantity of the things differing be spoken of for the indication of the difference, that we may not be suspected of upholding a difference somehow bare and not true. ⟦s5+6+7+8+9+10⟧ For the account knows every difference to be bare that does not have the truth of the things whose difference it is existentially underlying it.

⟦s5+6+7+8+9+10⟧ We, then, never saying properly that hypostasis and nature are the same, according to the teaching of the Fathers, as the argument demonstrated above beforehand, piously uphold in Christ the identity and the difference: the one, by the principle of the one hypostasis, according to which we confess God the Word the same as his own flesh, lest the all-praised Trinity receive a personal addition, becoming a tetrad; the other, by the principle of the otherness according to essence of the things from which he is, according to which the parts of Christ in no way fall over into one another. ⟦s5+6+7+8+9+10⟧ For Godhead and manhood could never become the same according to essence, that nothing created may be, by union, connatural and consubstantial with the Godhead. ⟦s5+6+7+8+9+10⟧ Since to say that nature is consubstantial with nature we know to belong to a raving understanding. ⟦s11⟧ For [0568] nature could never be connatural and consubstantial with nature. ⟦s12⟧ For the connatural and consubstantial is by nature said only of the individuals of the same species referred to one essence.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *That he who does not say nature and hypostasis are the same piously speaks of the union and the difference in Christ, believing the one to be according to hypostasis, the other according to nature.*

⟦s2⟧ Holding this confession, we neither deny the difference of the natures after the union, believing that the natures from which the one Christ was composed have remained unconfused; nor are we ignorant of the union according to hypostasis, confessing Christ one of the holy and consubstantial and worshipful Trinity. ⟦s3⟧ But we also make manifest through number the difference of the things from which Christ is, preserved in him in truth without cut and confusion after the union, for the destruction of the confusion of Apollinarius and Eutyches; and, since we know the union according to hypostasis, with a piercing voice we put forward the one nature of God the Word incarnate, by flesh having the intellectual soul, against the division of Nestorius; fleeing equally both the confusion of Apollinarius, who denies the natural difference of the things united after the union, and upholds that the flesh is the same as the Word according to nature on account of the union; and the division of Nestorius, who proclaims a hypostatic difference in Christ, and cuts asunder the identity according to hypostasis of the flesh with the Word.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *That Severus, saying nature and hypostasis are the same, makes the union confusion and the difference division; by which he is shown both distending the Word of the Trinity into a tetrad of persons, and cutting the mystery of the Monad into a dyad of Godheads, and blasphemously casting Christ out of all essential existence.*

⟦s2⟧ But Severus, saying essence and hypostasis, and nature and person, properly the same in the matter of the divine incarnation, neither knows the union as unconfused, even if he pretends to say so; nor the difference as undivided, even if he goes through this too boastfully; but he works the union into confusion after Apollinarius, and the difference into alienation after Nestorius. ⟦s3⟧ For if hypostasis and nature are the same, in no way, according to him, will the flesh be the same as the Word or other. ⟦s4⟧ For, saying the difference in Christ after the union as in a natural quality, he too will be found dividing the union along with Nestorius, introducing the difference according to hypostasis of the flesh toward the Word after the union; since according to him the difference as in a natural quality cannot be conceived otherwise than as difference in a hypostatic quality, if indeed nature and hypostasis are the same. ⟦s5⟧ For, saying the difference as in a natural quality, he is compelled to bring in besides the difference as in a hypostatic quality too, that he may show the hypostasis being the same as the [0569] nature. ⟦s5⟧ And if he does not speak of the difference according to hypostasis after the union, fearing the division, let him not speak of the natural difference after the union either, if indeed he has resolved to be consistent with himself, and is eager to keep the canons he himself defined for himself; since he knows nature and hypostasis to be the same as each other in definition and principle.

⟦s6⟧ And if according to both — I mean the nature and the hypostasis — the flesh has no difference toward the Word, as Severus thinks, let the same flesh clearly be both consubstantial and of one hypostasis with the same, according to the drivel of Apollinarius. ⟦s7⟧ And if the flesh is consubstantial with the Word, it will be consubstantial with the Father and the Spirit too, and the Trinity will appear become a tetrad; since things consubstantial do not admit concurrence according to hypostasis with one another. ⟦s8⟧ And if the flesh is of one hypostasis with the Word, it will be of other essence toward him. ⟦s9⟧ For things of one hypostasis have the principle of essence toward one another in every way and wholly different. ⟦s10⟧ And if the flesh, as being of one hypostasis with the Word after the union, is of other essence toward him, Christ will be found cut into two natures, according to Severus, after the union; if indeed, according to him, number wholly possesses the power of division. ⟦s11⟧ Thus everything that fights against the truth is easily turned round upon itself and easily trips over itself.

⟦s12⟧ And if, saying one composite nature of Christ after the union, he thinks to attach to it the difference as in a natural quality: first, insofar as it is a composite nature, Christ will be consubstantial with nothing at all of the things that are; if indeed Christ is one and alone according to essence and nature. ⟦s13⟧ For nature, as has been said, could never at all be consubstantial with nature. ⟦s14⟧ And not being consubstantial with any of the things that are, the same will be neither God at all, nor indeed man; or, if God, he who upholds this will be a polytheist, saying the Father and the Holy Spirit God of an incomposite nature, as simple, and saying Christ God of a composite nature, as not simple; and he will be shown proclaiming two Godheads, one simple and one composite.

⟦s14⟧ Then, saying the difference as in a natural quality: if he says that the natures whose qualities they are underlie the different qualities, he too will be shown making indication of two natures after the union, becoming, even unwilling, the advocate of those against whom he contended. ⟦s15⟧ But if he says that the difference is of bare qualities apart from the things, let him say that the union too is of these. ⟦s16⟧ For of whatever the difference is after the union, of these manifestly is the union; and in a few syllables he has brought in the spontaneity of Epicurus and the deceitful [0572] phantasy of Mani, Christ not existing for him in truth in the things, but in empty qualities; and Christ will be God by quality alone, and not in reality. ⟦s17⟧ For immortality and mortality, of which he says the difference after the union is, belong to natures, but are not natures. ⟦s18⟧ For where is mortality, if that which dies does not exist; or immortality, if the nature which death does not touch does not exist? ⟦s19⟧ But truly wickedness is a thing unreasoning, and seeming to be wise is by nature the craftsman of great folly. ⟦s20⟧ And for the prudent it is more profitable to embrace silence toward such men than to be thought, by refuting them, themselves too to make sport of the mystery. ⟦s21⟧ Severus, then, holding such views about Christ, has died together with his own doctrines.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ *A concise exposition, with the right advocacy of the true confession of the Fathers.*

⟦s2⟧ But we do not think thus, we do not believe thus, *nor is this the portion of Jacob*, says he who said it; but, confessing that the one Christ, that is his one hypostasis, was composed from two natures that are perfect according to the proper principle of their being — Godhead, I mean, and manhood — we believe the natures to have remained unconfused, and without any cut whatsoever, after the union. ⟦s3⟧ For, believing Christ to be, we have confessed that the natures from which he was composed are preserved after the union. ⟦s4⟧ And for this reason we proclaim in him a natural difference, looking, namely, at the parts from which he was composed, because Godhead and manhood are not the same according to essence; but a hypostatic identity; for the flesh is manifestly the same as the Word according to hypostasis. ⟦s5⟧ Knowing, then, a real difference after the union of the natures from which Christ was composed, we are not ignorant of the identity according to hypostasis; since the parts of Christ, while differing naturally from one another, do not differ according to the hypostasis of the whole composed from them; because the completive parts of the whole are by nature not to differ from one another at all by the principle of the whole.

⟦s6⟧ Confessing, then, the same to be two natures, we have recognized the same to be at once God and man; if indeed we do not put forward the names in his case as bare and lying upon nothing. ⟦s7⟧ And believing him to be in two natures undividedly and unconfusedly after the union, we have proclaimed the same perfect in Godhead and the same perfect in manhood, as a whole in parts. ⟦s8⟧ For in the parts from which he was composed, in them, as a whole, he is recognized after [0573] the union; because Christ has no other indication of his being and being preserved and being properly spoken of than the natural abiding in being, after the union, of the parts of which he was composed. ⟦s9⟧ For not only from these, but also in these, and, to speak more properly, these is Christ. ⟦s9⟧ And we number after the union the things from which Christ is, not partitioning — God forbid — the natures, but making only the indication of their difference preserved after the union.

⟦s10⟧ And we confess a concourse by natural union, that is true and real, according to the holy Cyril, and we uphold the all-holy Virgin as Theotokos properly and in truth; not simply, but as having truly conceived God the Word himself, begotten ineffably of God and Father before all the ages, and having given birth to him, incarnate ineffably from her. ⟦s11⟧ And we say that both the miracles and the passions are of the same, Christ being evidently one, who operates the divine things and the human: the divine in a fleshly way, because through flesh not without share in natural operation he put forth the power of the miracles; and the human in a divine way, because without natural compulsion, by authority, willing it, he accepted the experience of the human passions. ⟦s12+13⟧ Of the same we say the cross and the death and the burial and the resurrection and the ascension into the heavens; whence he came down without flesh, not passing over by changing places, he who in no way is contained by the things that are; for he is free of all circumscription; but he appeared through flesh, as by nature loving man, becoming, by a true birth from a woman, containable among our things, as he willed. ⟦s14⟧ Wherefore we bring to him, incarnate too, one worship with the Father and the Spirit.

⟦s15⟧ If, then, with soul and mouth we call the holy Virgin properly Theotokos, and confess the union according to hypostasis, and say that a natural concourse by union has come about, and uphold one Lord and Christ and Son, and one nature of God the Word himself incarnate; and believe Christ to be one of the holy Trinity, worshipped together and numbered together with the Father and the Spirit: how are we reviled as dividing by the fearless and by those who dare to say all things falsely, on account only of the bare word of number, put forward by us, as has often been said, for the sole indication of the difference of the natures preserved after the union? ⟦s16⟧ If, then, number of necessity wholly makes a division of the quantity of things, let someone show this, and we accept the calumny as true. ⟦s17⟧ But if they only abuse, slandering those who proclaim the true faith of the mystery, on account of the love of glory with which they are sick, they themselves will give account to God in the day of judgement, says the divine Apostle, [n: Rom. xiv, 12.] on behalf of those whom they have swept away into error — those, that is, who have completed the long course of the present life. ⟦s18⟧ But those [0576] who survive, may they have God changing them, and leading them to the knowledge of the truth now warred against by them. ⟦s19⟧ For so it is fitting to speak, on account of the commandment that bids us pray for those who curse. [n: Matth. v, 44; Luc. vi, 28.]

⟦s20⟧ I, then, have gone through these things for thee in epitome, concerning what thou didst ask, servant of God, for the sake of thy full assurance; not holding one thing in soul, as some of those who rejoice in Severus noise abroad, and speaking another to those I happen upon — do not think this — but as I was taught, so I both think and believe, and what I received from the Fathers, I speak. ⟦s21⟧ And, to say it more properly, I put forward my very understanding embodied in words. ⟦s22⟧ And if my word is false, may I not attain the blessedness there; but may I carry off, as the fruit of falsehood, alienation from the good things promised. ⟦s23⟧ And do thou, for the sake of my ready obedience, deign to repay me with thy prayers; that Christ our God, the salvation of those who fear him, may heal the wounds of my soul by the power of the mystery of his life-giving sufferings for us; who alone with the Father and the Holy Spirit is glorified unto the ages. ⟦s24⟧ Amen.
