⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ LETTER 20

⟦s1⟧ *Of the same, to Marinus the monk.*

⟦s2⟧ I, knowing the multitude of my own offences, and seeing the condemnation that, as is likely, will receive me for them after the departure from this life; and considering that great and dread tribunal as though already assembled in my soul, before which each of us shall receive, according to desert, the requitals of what we have done, had resolved to keep silence as my housemate, sparing in every way the divine words that are above me, as it seems to me at least; lest I be condemned more, and more justly, as bringing forth words of God put to death by me through my inaction in good works, and therefore unable to furnish to the hearers the life that is in grace, and unable to display the manners of the virtues in the conduct of me who speak; and lest I become liable to the threat to come, as not only having myself, within myself, murdered through my evil manners the life-giving words of God, but also presenting to the hearers the whole evil conduct of my life as a living image, plain to all, of contempt for those words. ⟦s3⟧ I then, as I said, both knowing and fearing these things, shook off my eagerness for speaking and writing the divine words, judging it the greatest injustice, and a ruinous loss, to carry off vain praises from the hearers for things of which I have not the working. ⟦s4+5⟧ But since the most holy abbot, great in prudence and in all things precious to me, has overcome the verdict of my silence with the many charms of divine thoughts — he whom I could not thrust away, because of the beauty of the virtues that shines in him according to divine intelligence; and he has compelled me to engrave this modest syllable to thy godliness, having related some parts of thy achievements according to God, and having profited me exceedingly — I thought it needful to say in summary those things that are profitable and saving to thee and to me, and to all who wish to hear.

⟦s4+5⟧ [0600] David, the great prophet and king, showing those who have resolved to love God, and have chosen the divine salvation above all things on earth, the way of the divine virtues that is truly of knowledge, and opening the door of knowledge that lies in practice, says: The beginning of wisdom is the fear of the Lord, and a good intelligence to all who do it. [n: Psal. cx, 10.] If, then, the fear is the beginning of wisdom, the end of wisdom, clearly, is by nature the good intelligence, for those who do it. ⟦s6+7⟧ And if these things are so, let us be zealous with all our power to acquire the fear of God, and the good intelligence: the fear, that we may flee the defilements of sin, shaking them out of the soul by the expectation of the everlasting punishments; the intelligence, that we may be shown skilled doers of all the divine wills. ⟦s8⟧ For he who fears the penalties for transgressions of the divine commandments is not caught by the passions that defile his soul; and he who has inwardly the good intelligence, which distinguishes, by the reason that is due, the temporal from the everlasting, becomes luxuriant with all the divine goods, contriving that the knowledge in him be shown in no way idle. ⟦s9⟧ For as fear, in those who truly fear God, becomes the destroyer of the unclean passions, so also the intelligence of the truly intelligent becomes the craftsman of all the virtues. ⟦s10⟧ Let us then fear God, and do nothing of what he does not wish; and let us love his intelligence, and neglect to do nothing of what he wishes. ⟦s10⟧ For he who fears God hates all lawlessness, and he who has made the good intelligence his house-guest loves all righteousness. ⟦s11⟧ For as hatred of lawlessness signifies the one who fears, so also love of righteousness makes manifest the intelligent man.

⟦s12⟧ Let us not, then, put on fear in the bearing of dejection alone, nor show our reverence as going no further than the manners that appear; but by a disposition of soul and a humility of heart made fast by frequent groanings in the depth, and struck invisibly in the conscience by the scourge of the memory of former sins, and therefore, by its contraction upon itself, not bearing at all to look at the stumblings of others. ⟦s13⟧ For he who feigns the fear of God by bearing alone differs not at all from the ape, which imitates the bearing and the postures of men. ⟦s14⟧ For this animal among the four-footed, standing by nature far from human nature, seems to those who watch it, by its imitation of bearing, to be a man, though it possesses by nature nothing human; just as he too who imitates the bearing of those who truly fear the Lord, to deceive those who see him, but has not the disposition of inclination [0601] imbued, equally with them, by the divine fear, is another Sadducee or Scribe, both being one and being called one: keeping the outward form of reverence, with seemliness from without, but falling altogether short of the reality, because of the unclean friendship for the passions that lies somewhere in the depth of the soul.

⟦s15⟧ Nor again let us display divine knowledge by the mere utterance of words polished for beauty; nor define the good intelligence as going no further than the bare being able to speak, without doing; but by a disposition of soul imbued through practice with the virtues, and therefore brightening by practice the splendour of knowledge, and by knowledge making radiant the tension of practice; so that fear, coming together through deeds with the longing for God that accords with the good intelligence, may work the divine wisdom perfect in us: beginning in the abstention from evils that accords with the divine fear, and brought to perfection in the practice of the commandments according to the good intelligence. ⟦s16⟧ For he who defines intelligence as lying in the mere bare utterance of the divine words is like a parrot mimicking human words. ⟦s17⟧ For this animal, alone among the birds, being taught, feigns human words, though it has by nature nothing at all human; just as he too who, going no further than bare utterance, mimics the words of the truly intelligent to astonish the hearers, but has not the habit of knowledge imbued with the practice of the virtues, is truly another Pharisee, or a blameworthy priest, both being one and being called one; feigning intelligence by the mere utterance of words, but falling altogether short of the wisdom itself that truly has its being in deeds; and therefore greatly puffed up, and, by the boastfulness of the conceit that nourishes the pride of his mind, standing against the word of the truth; not knowing, it seems, that knowledge without practice, in those who are greatly puffed up over it for love of glory, is manifestly a theology of demons, having Jesus expressly rebuking it [n: Luc. iv, 33.] — the one of whom it would speak as God; even if such a man, held fast by the passion, does not perceive it.

⟦s18⟧ Therefore let us fear God with fear, and free ourselves from every defilement of flesh and spirit, not allowing the law of the flesh, through the praised mortification of the members, to rise up against the law of the spirit; nor let us circumscribe the magnificence of the divine fear by the manners that appear in bearing alone, but let us make the composure of our bearing a herald of the good disposition within, in the soul, that we may flee the coming threat of the fire; and let us adorn the good intelligence with the practice of the commandments, that we may not, like the vainglorious, carry about dead words, bereft of the life that lies in the practice of the commandments; but let us [0604] make the divine words effective in practice, pursuing peace with all, and love; caring for kindness and meekness, and long-suffering, and forbearance in all things toward all; bearing what befalls with patience, and with thanksgiving; and receiving afflictions unshaken as divine gifts; in nothing resisting the principles of the providence that, through these things, conducts our life profitably; so that we may attain everlasting life with all the saints, delighting in the goods to come, in the abiding of the blessedness of everlasting things, hearing God say to us: Come, you blessed of my Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world; [n: Matth. xxv, 34.] and may we rejoice, enjoying those ineffable goods which eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor have they entered into the heart of man, which God has prepared for those who love him. [n: I Cor. ii, 9.]

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ LETTER 21

⟦s1⟧ *Of the same, to the most holy bishop of Cydonia.*

⟦s2⟧ Having come to know, through pious inquiry into the mystery of his economy toward us, that God himself is equal to himself in height and in depth, my God-guarded master, having marvelled how the limit and the limitlessness — contraries and unmixed, joined together in him — had their manifestation through one another, the infinity being ineffably limited together with the limit, and the limit being extended, beyond nature, together with the infinity; while he himself, about whom these things are, is in both the same with himself, having in no way at all lowered himself as regards the infinite, as being by nature unlimited, and by economy both being and being known as incomprehensible; having the height of the divine glory beyond thought, and the depth of the condescension of the economy uncontainable — wisely submitted himself, pliantly, like wax, to God as to a seal; and receiving him impressed wholly into his depth, he made himself a conspicuous imitation of the divine blessedness. ⟦s3⟧ For, wisely exchanging himself into the divine modes, with the lowliness of nature he blended, through virtue, the eminence of dignity; and with the height of dignity, conversely, he raised up together, through knowledge, the humility of nature; and he has made each to be contemplated, paradoxically, in the other. ⟦s4+5⟧ For in both he has kept himself the same with himself, having lowered nothing of his solemnity in either; showing very clearly, through the things by which he acquired the unadulterated imitation of God, that God put forward the priesthood on earth, ordaining it in his own stead, so that he, being seen even bodily, may not fail to make his mysteries apparent to those able to see.

⟦s4+5⟧ [0605] For this reason my master, bearing himself in a manner like God's, by word together with manners, has wrought much salvation in me. ⟦s6+7⟧ For as the Lord besought men to be reconciled to God and Father as though they had been wronged rather than had done wrong — as the divine Paul testifies, saying, We are ambassadors for Christ, as though God were beseeching through us: we pray you on behalf of Christ, be reconciled to God [n: II Cor. v, 20.] — so that he might shame them, by the greatness of the benefit, into coming to an awareness of their own hardening; so also my master, through a voluntary lowering, skilfully overpowering me, by seeming to wish to learn from me through letters the things he has been appointed to teach because of his dignity, has made me, though late, aware of my own ignorance. ⟦s8⟧ I confess, then, my thanks to my master; and I glory in the manner by which he gently thrust away the cloud of my conceit; and I ask him not to cease setting his servant in order by such words and manners.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ LETTER 22

⟦s1⟧ *Of the same, to Auxentius.*

⟦s2⟧ If, trusting in the Spirit — inasmuch as you have love clasped about you indissolubly, needing nothing for renewal — you have decided to neglect letters to your friends, I both praise the rule and approve of you, as entrusting the treasure of love to him from whom and into whom it is wont both to begin and to come to rest. ⟦s3⟧ But if, making light of the fitting manner of friendship, you excuse yourselves from writing out of sloth, I shall not cease to do the contrary, not knowing how to stand in awe of friends because of the greatness of their standing in the world. ⟦s4⟧ And if neither of the two is the cause of not writing, receive from the absent, and give readily to the absent, as nature has taught, converse through letters — or rather, as God, making nature wise, has consoled it. ⟦s5⟧ For speech shaped in letters goads the memory when it is at rest, and rouses it when it nods, to see and to take the impress of the faces of those who are loved; through which longing, like a fire that has been quenched, is fanned up again from a small spark.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ LETTER 23

⟦s1⟧ *Of the same, to Stephen the presbyter.*

⟦s2⟧ It is proper to spiritual love not only to do good to those in need when present, but also to console them when absent; and not to allow souls to be cut apart along with bodies; nor indeed the power of reason, through which the soul bears the image of the Creator, to be [0608] circumscribed by place; but either, when present, to converse with the beloved by voice, face to face, on fitting things, or, when absent, to speak with them through letters. ⟦s3⟧ For nature, by the grace of God, wisely devised this manner, for an unseparated union of those who stand far apart from one another bodily by local distance. ⟦s4⟧ For so speech, sounding in us the more frequently by voice and by letters, sets in the soul a memory hard to wash out, so as to see continually those who are ever present in the spirit by love, and to embrace them, and to be called back from all grievous things. ⟦s5⟧ Do not, then, forget me, your child and disciple, precious Fathers, but, as being both disciples and teachers of love, feed my starving soul with the word of the virtues, and enlighten my mind, pressed down by the gloom of ignorance, looking to the reward laid up for you in heaven for this; even if what is done should perhaps draw you away for a little from a better and more precious leisure. ⟦s6⟧ For I know that it is not equal in good repute to converse with God without intermediary and to provide with sympathy for the lesser. ⟦s7⟧ Yet neither is this alien to the things held lawful with God.
