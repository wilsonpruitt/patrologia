⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0669] showing both to be identical with each other; and declaring that each is, for the other by alternation, what each is established as being for itself: the nave a sanctuary in potency, consecrated by the reference of the mystagogy to its end; and conversely the sanctuary a nave in activity, having it as the beginning of its own mystagogy; it remains one and the same through both. ⟦s2⟧ So too the whole world of the things that are, brought forth from God by coming-to-be, divided into the intelligible world, filled out by noetic and bodiless essences, and this sensible and bodily world, magnificently woven together from many forms and natures, being as it were another church not made by hands, is wisely shown forth through this one made by hands; and it has, as its sanctuary, the world above, allotted to the powers above; and, as its nave, the world below, given over to those whose lot is to live through sense.

⟦s3⟧ Again, the world is one, not divided along with its own parts; on the contrary, it circumscribes even the difference of the parts themselves that comes from natural property by their reference to its own unity and indivisibility; and it shows them to be identical with itself and with one another, without confusion, by alternation, and the one wholly entered into the other as whole into whole; and both, as parts, filling it out as one whole, and as parts filled out uniformly and wholly according to it as a whole. ⟦s4⟧ For the whole intelligible world appears, to those able to see, mystically typified in the whole sensible world by symbolic forms; and the whole sensible world is present in the whole intelligible world, made simple by way of knowledge, according to mind, in its principles. ⟦s5+6+7+8⟧ For in that world this one is in its principles; and that one in this, in its types; and their work is one, as it were a wheel within a wheel, says Ezekiel, the wonderful seer of great things, speaking, I think, of the two worlds. ⟦s5+6+7+8⟧ And again: *For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made*, says the divine Apostle. [n: Rom. i, 20.] And if the things that do not appear are clearly seen through the things that appear, as is written, much more will the things that appear be understood through the things that do not appear by those who devote themselves to spiritual contemplation. ⟦s5+6+7+8⟧ For the symbolic contemplation of the intelligibles through the visibles is the spiritual science and intellection of the things seen through the invisibles. ⟦s9⟧ For things that are indicative of one another must surely have true and very clear reflections of one another, and an unimpaired relation upon them.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0672] CHAPTER 3

⟦s2⟧ *That the holy church of God is also an image of the sensible world alone.*

⟦s3⟧ And again he used to say that the holy church of God is a symbol of the sensible world alone by itself, as having the divine sanctuary as heaven, and possessing the beauty of the nave as earth. ⟦s4⟧ And likewise that the world is a church, having heaven like a sanctuary, and the ordering of the earth like a nave.

⟦s4⟧ CHAPTER 4

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1+2+3+4+5⟧ *How and in what manner the holy church of God symbolically images man, and is itself imaged by him as a man.*

⟦s1+2+3+4+5⟧ And again, by another mode of contemplation, he used to say that the holy church of God is a man: having the sanctuary as soul, and the divine altar as mind, and the nave as body; as being an image and likeness of man, who came to be according to the image and likeness of God; and through the nave, as through a body, putting forward moral philosophy; through the sanctuary, as through a soul, expounding natural contemplation spiritually; and, as through a mind, through the divine altar, entering upon mystical theology. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5⟧ And conversely, that man is a mystical church: as through the nave, the body, making the practical part of the soul shine virtuously by the activities of the commandments according to moral philosophy; and as through the sanctuary, the soul, bringing to God through reason, according to natural contemplation, the principles of sense, purely cut away in the spirit from matter; and as through the altar, the mind, calling forth the silence, much-hymned in the inner shrines, of the unseen and unknown great-voicedness of the Godhead, through another silence, talkative and many-voiced; and, as far as is attainable for man, consorting with it according to mystical theology; and becoming such as one must be who has been deemed worthy of God's sojourning and has been marked with his all-radiant splendours.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ CHAPTER 5

⟦s3⟧ *How and in what manner, again, the holy church of God is an image and type of the soul understood by itself.*

⟦s4⟧ And he taught that the holy church is able to be an image not only of the whole man, who is constituted, I mean, of soul and body by composition, but also of the soul itself, contemplated by itself in reason. ⟦s5⟧ For he used to say that the soul as a whole consists of a noetic and a vital power; and that the noetic is moved with authority according to wish, while the vital remains as it is, according to nature, without choice. ⟦s6⟧ And again, that to the noetic belong the contemplative and the practical; and the contemplative, he said, is called mind, and the practical reason; and that of the noetic power [0673] the mover is the mind, and of the vital the provider is the reason; and that the one is and is called wisdom (I mean the mind), when it keeps its movements toward God altogether unchanging; and that the reason likewise both is and is called prudence, when, having soberly joined to the mind by its activities the vital power governed by it in providence, it shows it undifferentiated, bearing the same and like reflection of the Divine through virtue as the mind; a power which he said is naturally shared between the mind and the reason; so that the soul is, and is shown primarily, rather to consist of mind and of reason, as noetic and rational; the vital power being contemplated, clearly, equally in both, mind and reason, I mean (for it is not lawful to conceive either of these as without share in life), and being divided between both; through which the mind, which we said is called wisdom, unfolding by the contemplative habit according to ineffable silence and knowledge, is led to the truth through unforgetting and unceasing knowledge; and the reason, which we called prudence, by the practical habit, bodily, according to virtue, comes to its end in the good through faith; from both of which is constituted the true science of divine and human things: the truly unerring knowledge, and the end of all the most divine philosophy of the Christians.

⟦s6⟧ And to speak more clearly about these things: of the soul, he said, one part is contemplative, as has been said, and the other practical; and the contemplative he called mind, the practical reason, as the first powers of the soul; and again the mind he called wisdom, and the reason prudence, as the first activities. ⟦s7⟧ And again, going through it in detail, he used to say that there belong to the soul, according to the noetic, the mind, wisdom, contemplation, knowledge, unforgetting knowledge, and that the end of these is the truth; and according to the rational, the reason, prudence, practice, virtue, faith, and that the end of these is the good. ⟦s8⟧ And the truth and the good, he said, signify God: the truth, when the Divine seems to be signified from its essence, for the truth is a simple and sole and one and identical and partless and unchangeable and impassible thing, and one never escaping notice, and altogether without interval; and the good, when from its activity. ⟦s9⟧ For the good is beneficent, and provident for all things that are from it, and their guardian: from 'exceedingly' and 'being', or 'having been set', or 'running', according to the opinion of the etymologists, bestowing on all the things that are to be and to endure and to be moved.

⟦s9⟧ [0676] The five pairs, then, understood concerning the soul, he said, are engaged about the one pair that signifies God. ⟦s10⟧ By a pair I now mean the mind and the reason; wisdom and prudence; contemplation and practice; knowledge and virtue; unforgetting knowledge and faith. ⟦s11⟧ And the one significant of the Divine, the truth and the good; by which the soul, moved in progression, is united to the God of all, imitating the unchangeableness and the beneficence of his essence and his activity, through the firm and immovable habit in the good according to choice. ⟦s12⟧ And, that I may mingle with these a small fitting contemplation, perhaps this is the divine decad of the strings of the intelligible psaltery of the soul, [n: Psal. cxliii, 9.] which has the word sounding beneath it to the Spirit through the other blessed decad of the commandments, and intelligibly produces the complete and harmonious, the melodious notes by which God is hymned: that I may learn what is the principle of the decad that sings and of the decad that is sung; and how decad, mystically united and joined to decad, brings back to himself Jesus, my God and Saviour, filled out through me who am saved, him who is ever most full and can never go out of himself; and restores me, man, wonderfully to myself, or rather to God, from whom I have received being and have it, and toward whom I hasten, longing from afar to receive in addition well-being. ⟦s13+14⟧ Which he who has been able to know from suffering the things said will surely know, having already come to know clearly by experience his own dignity: how the 'according to the image' is restored to the image; how the archetype is honoured; what is the power of the mystery of our salvation, and for whom Christ died; and how again we can abide in him and he in us, as he said; [n: Joan. xv, 4.] and how *The word of the Lord is right, and all his works are in faith*. [n: Psal. xxxii, 4.] But let us bring the discourse back to the sequence of the discourse, content with this much about these things.

⟦s15⟧ For he used to say that the mind, moved through wisdom, comes to contemplation; through contemplation to knowledge; through knowledge to unforgetting knowledge; and through unforgetting knowledge to the truth; around which the mind receives the limit of its movement, its essence and power and habit and activity being circumscribed.

⟦s16⟧ For he said that wisdom is the power of mind, and that the mind itself is wisdom in potency; contemplation its habit; knowledge its activity; and unforgetting knowledge, the unceasing and habitual ever-moving of wisdom and contemplation and knowledge, that is, of power and habit and activity, about [0677] the knowable that is beyond all knowledge; whose limit is the truth, as a knowable never escaping notice; which is indeed worthy of wonder: how the unforgetting ceases by being circumscribed; or is it, clearly, as being bounded by God, the truth? ⟦s17⟧ For God is the truth, about whom the mind, moving unceasingly and unforgettingly, can never cease from its movement, not finding a limit where there is no interval. ⟦s18⟧ For the wonderful greatness of the divine infinity is something without quantity and without parts and altogether without interval; and it has no comprehension of any sort that reaches it, so as to know what at all it is in essence. ⟦s19⟧ And what has neither interval nor comprehension in any respect is limitable by nothing.

⟦s19⟧ And likewise that the reason, moved through prudence, comes to practice; through practice to virtue; and through virtue to faith, the truly firm and unfaltering full assurance of divine things; which the reason, having it first in potency according to prudence, afterwards displays in activity according to virtue, through its manifestation in works. ⟦s20⟧ For faith without works is something dead, as is written; and no one in his right mind would ever dare to say that anything dead and without activity is to be numbered among the good things. ⟦s21⟧ And through faith to the good, about which the reason receives its end, ceasing from its own activities, its power and habit and activity being circumscribed.

⟦s22⟧ For he said that prudence is the power of reason, and that the reason itself is prudence in potency; practice its habit; virtue its activity; and faith the inward and unalterable fixity of prudence and practice and virtue, that is, of power, habit and activity; whose last limit is the good, about which the reason, ceasing from its movement, comes to rest. ⟦s23⟧ For God is the good, in whom every power of every reason is of a nature to be bounded. ⟦s24⟧ But how and in what manner each of these is accomplished and brought into activity, and what is contrary to each of these, or akin to it, and to what extent, to divide and to say is not of the present subject; except so much as to know that every soul, when through the grace of the Holy Spirit and its own diligence and zeal it has been able to join these to one another and to weave them together, the reason, I mean, to the mind, and prudence to wisdom, and practice to contemplation, and virtue to knowledge, and faith to unforgetting knowledge, none being lessened in respect of the other or exceeding it, all excess and deficiency being cut away from them; and, to speak summarily, to make its own decad a monad, then it too, to God
