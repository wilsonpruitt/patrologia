⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0680] … true and good, and one and only, it shall be united; beautiful and magnificent, and become like him [as far as may be attained], by the completion of the four general virtues: which manifest the divine decad in the soul, and contain the other, blessed decad of the commandments. ⟦s2⟧ For the tetrad is in potency a decad, being composed from the monad in a sequence by procession. ⟦s3+4+5+6+7+8⟧ And again the same is a monad, containing the good singularly by concourse, and showing the simple and partless of the divine activity partitioned in itself without being cut; by which virtues the soul vigorously kept what is its own unassailed, and manfully struck away what is alien as evil: as having a well-reasoned mind, and prudent wisdom, and active contemplation, and virtuous knowledge, and upon these the unforgetting knowledge, most faithful at once and unchanging; and as having brought to God, as effects joined to their causes, the activities temperately joined to the powers; and as having received in return for them the deification that makes simplicity.

⟦s3+4+5+6+7+8⟧ For the activity and manifestation of the mind is reason, as of a cause its effect; and of wisdom, prudence; and of contemplation, practice; and of knowledge, virtue; and of the unforgetting knowledge, faith; from which is made the inward relation toward the truth and the good, I mean God; which he said is divine science, and unerring knowledge, and love and peace, in which and through which is deification: [science] as the completion of all the knowledge about God and divine things attainable by men, and the unerring compass of the virtues; [knowledge] as having truly set foot upon the truth, and furnishing a lasting experience of the divine; [love] as partaking wholly, by disposition, of the whole delight of God; and [peace] as undergoing the same things as God, and preparing those counted worthy to come into it to undergo them. ⟦s3+4+5+6+7+8⟧ For if the Divine is wholly unmoved, as having nothing whatever to trouble it (for what is there that even reaches its watchtower?), and peace is a stability unshaken and unmoved, and besides, an untroubled gladness, does not every soul too that has been counted worthy to obtain the divine peace undergo the divine things? as having passed beyond the bounds not only of vice and ignorance, of falsehood and wickedness, the vices opposed to virtue and knowledge, to truth and the good, which subsist parasitically upon the movements of the soul contrary to nature, but now also, if it is lawful to say, of virtue itself and knowledge, and again of truth and goodness as known to us; and as having laid itself to rest, ineffably and unknowably, in the more-than-most-true and more-than-good bed of God, according to his most unlying promise; so that it has nothing left, of the things that are of a nature to trouble it, which reaches its hiddenness in God; in which blessed and [0681] all-holy bed that awesome mystery of the oneness beyond mind and reason is accomplished; through which God with the church, the soul, and the soul with God, shall become one flesh and one spirit. ⟦s3+4+5+6+7+8⟧ O how shall I marvel at thee, Christ, for thy goodness — for I will not dare to say hymn thee — I who have not even the power sufficient to marvel worthily! ⟦s3+4+5+6+7+8⟧ *For the two shall become one flesh; this mystery is great, but I speak of Christ and of the church*, says the divine Apostle. [n: Ephes. v, 32.] And again: *He who is joined to the Lord is one spirit*. [n: I Cor. vi, 17.]

⟦s3+4+5+6+7+8⟧ So then, when the soul has become uniform, and has been gathered both to itself and to God, there will no longer be the reason that divides it into many by conception, since it has been crowned on the head with the first and only and one Word and God; in whom, according to one simplicity beyond conceiving, all the principles of beings both are and subsist uniformly, as in the creator and maker of beings; gazing intently on whom, who is not outside it but whole in it whole, it too will know by a simple apprehension the principles and the causes of beings, by which perhaps, before it was espoused to the Word and God, it was led along by divisive methods; being borne through them savingly and harmoniously toward him who contains and makes every principle and every cause.

⟦s3+4+5+6+7+8⟧ Since, then, these things belong to the soul, as we said — which has in the mind wisdom in potency, and from wisdom contemplation, and from this knowledge, and from knowledge the unforgetting knowledge, through which it is led to the truth, as being the limit and end of the goods of the mind; and has in reason prudence, and from this practice, and from practice virtue, and from this faith, according to which it comes to rest in the good, as the blessed end of the rational activities; through which the science of divine things is gathered, by concourse of the union of these with one another — the holy church of God, likened to the soul by contemplation, clearly fits all these things: signifying through the sanctuary all the things that have been shown to be in the mind and from the mind by procession, and making plain through the nave all the things that have been shown to be in reason and from reason by expansion; and gathering all things to the mystery accomplished on the divine altar; into which he who has been able to be initiated prudently and wisely through the things accomplished in the church has established his own soul as truly a church of God, and divine; for whose sake perhaps, and of which, the church made by hands, being by the variety of the divine things in it wisely [0684] a pattern by symbol, was handed down to us for guidance toward the better.

⟦s3+4+5+6+7+8⟧ CHAPTER 6

⟦s3+4+5+6+7+8⟧ *How and in what manner holy Scripture too is called a man.*

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6+7⟧ And just as, by the contemplation according to anagogy, he said that the church is a spiritual man, and man a mystical church, so too he said that the whole of holy Scripture, taken together, is a man: having the Old Testament as body, and the New as soul and spirit and mind. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6+7⟧ And again, that of the whole of holy Scripture, Old I mean and New, the letter according to history is the body; and the mind of what is written, and the aim to which the mind is directed, the soul. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6+7⟧ Hearing which, I especially admired the exactness of the likening, and I fittingly hymned, as far as I could, him who distributes gifts of grace to each according to worth. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6+7⟧ For as this man of ours is mortal according to what appears, but immortal according to what does not appear, so too holy Scripture, having the letter that appears as passing away, but the spirit hidden in the letter as never ceasing to be, establishes the account of the contemplation as true. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6+7⟧ And as this man of ours, mastering by philosophy the passionate appetite and impulse, withers the flesh, so too holy Scripture, understood spiritually, circumcises its own letter. ⟦s8+9⟧ For the divine Apostle says: *As much as our outward man is corrupted, so much the inward is renewed day by day*. [n: II Cor. iv, 16.] Let this be understood and said of holy Scripture too, understood figuratively as a man. ⟦s10⟧ For as much as its letter recedes, so much the spirit gains; and as much as the shadows of the temporary worship pass by, so much the truth of faith, all-shining and wholly bright and shadowless, enters in; according to which and for the sake of which it principally both is and has been written, and is called Scripture, being engraved on the mind through spiritual grace; just as this man of ours too, because of the rational and noetic soul, principally and most of all both is and is called man; according to which and through which he is image and likeness of God who made him, and is by nature set apart from the other living things, having toward them no reflection whatever of a relational power.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ CHAPTER 7

⟦s3⟧ *How the world is called a man, and in what manner man too is called a world.*

⟦s4⟧ And again, following this image in apt imitation, he suggested that the whole world too, constituted from visible and invisible things, [0685] is a man; and again, that man, constituted from soul and body, is a world. ⟦s4⟧ For he said that the intelligible things hold the ratio of soul, as the soul too holds that of the intelligibles; and that the sensible things hold the figure of body, as the body too that of the sensibles. ⟦s5⟧ And that the intelligibles are soul of the sensibles, and the sensibles body of the intelligibles. ⟦s6⟧ And that, as soul is in body, the intelligible world is in the sensible world; and the sensible in the intelligible, as body knit together with soul; and that one world is from both, as also one man is from soul and body, neither of these, which have grown together with each other by union, denying and dismissing the other, because of the law of him who bound them together; according to which the principle of the unifying power has been sown in them, not allowing their identity according to hypostasis upon the union to be ignored because of the natural otherness; nor the property that circumscribes each of these to itself to be shown more powerful toward severance and partition than the friendly kindredness placed in them mystically by union; according to which the universal and single mode of the invisible and unknown presence in all things of the cause that holds beings together, being present in all variously, constitutes all things, both in themselves and in one another, uncommixed and undivided; and shows them to be, by the unifying relation, rather of one another than of themselves; until it please him who bound them to loose them, for the sake of a greater and more mystical economy, at the time of the hoped-for universal consummation; at which the world of the things that appear, as a man, will die, and will rise again new from having grown old, at the resurrection expected presently; when this man of ours too, as a part with the whole and a small one with the great, will rise together with the world, having obtained the power of being no longer able to be corrupted; when the body will become like the soul, and the sensibles like the intelligibles, in comeliness and glory, one divine power shining forth upon all by a manifest and active presence, proportionately to each, and through itself preserving the bond of the union unloosed unto the infinite ages.

⟦s7+8+9+10+11⟧ If anyone, then, wishes to have both a life and a word dear to God and pleasing to God, let him make much of the better and more honourable parts of these three men — the world I mean, and holy Scripture, and the man that we are. ⟦s7+8+9+10+11⟧ Let him take care, as far as he can, of the soul, which is immortal and divine and is to be deified from the virtues; and let him despise the flesh, which is subject to corruption and death, and is able, when it is neglected, [0688] to soil the dignity of the soul. ⟦s7+8+9+10+11⟧ *For a corruptible body*, it says, *weighs down the soul, and the earthy tent burdens the mind of many cares*. [n: Sap. ix, 15.] And again: *The flesh lusts against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh*. [n: Galat. v, 17.] And again: *He who sows to his own flesh shall of the flesh reap corruption*. [n: Galat. vi, 8.] And toward the bodiless and noetic powers let him move his emulation in the mind through intellection, leaving the things present and seen: *For the things seen are temporary*, it says, *but the things not seen everlasting*; [n: II Cor. iv, 18.] in which, because of the abundance of their habit of peace, God rests. ⟦s7+8+9+10+11⟧ And let him be borne up temperately to the Holy Spirit through prudent study of holy Scripture, having passed beyond the letter; in whom is the fullness of good things, and the hidden treasures of knowledge and of wisdom; within which if anyone shall appear worthy to come, he will find God himself written on the tablets of the heart through the grace in the Spirit, beholding as in a mirror with unveiled face the glory of God, by the taking away of the veil of the letter. [n: Coloss. ii, 3.]

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ CHAPTER 8

⟦s3⟧ *Of what things the first entrance of the holy synaxis, and the things done after it, are symbols.*

⟦s4⟧ Our discourse has now come, after the brief exposition of the contemplations about the holy church spoken by the blessed elder, to make, as far as possible, a still briefer narration of the holy synaxis of the church too. ⟦s5⟧ The first entrance, then, of the high priest into the holy church at the sacred synaxis, he taught, bears the type and image of the first coming through flesh into this world of the Son of God and our Saviour Christ; through which, having freed and redeemed the nature of men, which had been enslaved to corruption, and had been sold by itself to death through sin, and ruled tyrannically by the devil — having paid all the debt on its behalf as one liable, he who was not liable and was sinless — he led it back again to the grace of the kingdom that was from the beginning, giving himself as ransom for us and exchange, and giving in return for our corruption-working sufferings his own life-making passion, a healing remedy, and saving for the whole world; after which coming, his ascent into heaven and to the throne above heaven, and his restoration, are symbolically figured through the entrance of the high priest into the sanctuary and his ascent to the priestly throne.
