⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ [0009] OPUSCULUM 1 — TO MARINUS

⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ *Of our father among the saints Maximus the Confessor, to Marinus the most holy presbyter.*

⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ Having set thyself as thine aim to journey toward the all-hymned friendship with God, God-honoured Father, thou runnest the course toward it in good order: by reason circumscribing the nature of beings for the coming-to-be of virtue; and by mind, for the establishment of an unerring union [knowledge], bounding the movement of every age and time; with which the longing for God enters in more warmly, both extending the movement of thee who longest alongside the infinity, and by the incomprehensibility of the one longed for making thine aspiration incomprehensible; whose end is God himself, who becomes for the worthy the fulfilment of longing, as self-subsistent enjoyment of good things; of which reason knows experience alone as its comprehension, inasmuch as it is clearly beyond intellection, a union, as of what falls under no intellectual type in any way whatever.

⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ For this reason life has become for thee the person of reason, and reason the nature [0012] of life; which together delineate the new man truly according to Christ, bearing by good imitation the image and the likeness of God who created him; which one must now take to be the truth and the goodness: the one being the end of contemplation, the other of practice; and the one the opponent of falsehood, the other of wickedness.

⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ Hence to thy achievement of the virtues was joined the comprehension of the divine doctrines, held together by the abundance of knowledge and by the power of demonstration; according to which, having discerned and thrust aside what was put forward without exactness by some concerning wills — who say that will and wish and counsel and choice and inclination, and authority and opinion and prudence, are the same as one another, differing from one another in names alone; and that there will come to be one will of God and of the saints; and who for this reason affirm and insist that Christ has one elective will — thou wast not content with thyself for the confirmation of what had been well judged, because of the poverty of spirit that has been bred up with thee; [n: Matth. v, 3.] but thou didst deign to take me too, who was brought up in unlearnedness, as one casting his vote with thee for what thou hast well recognized. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ This I come to do, even if it is rash, conquered by the wage of obedience.

⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ Of each of these I shall make the writing in sequence, concise and as it were definitional; not engraving words of my own — for how could I, who am poor in these things? — but gathering together what others have laboured over concerning these, that we may know that these differ from one another in both: I mean in name and in reality.

⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ *Of natural will, that is, volition.*

⟦s7⟧ Natural will, that is, volition, they say is a power appetitive of what is according to nature, and holding together all the idioms essentially present to the nature. ⟦s8⟧ For the essence, naturally held together by this, has appetite for being and living and being moved according to sense and mind, aspiring to its own natural and full true being. ⟦s9⟧ For the nature is constituted volitive of itself and of all the things that are naturally apt to make up its constitution, being appetitively attached to the principle of its own being, according to which it both is and has come to be.

⟦s10⟧ Wherefore [0013] others, defining this natural [will], say that will is a rational and vital appetite; and choice a deliberative appetite for things in our power. ⟦s11⟧ Volition, then, is not choice; if indeed volition is a certain simple appetite, rational and vital, while choice is a concourse of appetite and counsel and judgement. ⟦s12⟧ For having appetite, we first deliberate; and having deliberated, we judge; and having judged, we choose, in preference to the worse, what has been shown by the judgement to be better. ⟦s12⟧ And the one depends only on natural things; the other only on things in our power and able to come about through us. ⟦s13⟧ Choice, then, is not volition.

⟦s13⟧ *Of wish.*

⟦s14⟧ But neither is choice wish. ⟦s15⟧ For wish is an imaginative appetite for things in our power and not in our power, that is, one formed by the understanding alone. ⟦s16⟧ And the imaginative appetite belongs only to the power of understanding, without the deliberative reason about things in our power; or [it is] a certain qualified natural volition. ⟦s17⟧ But choice is a deliberative appetite for things to be done that are in our power.

⟦s18⟧ The account of the description alone, then, having shown the difference of these, would have sufficed to stop from contentiousness those who are superfluously fond of words, and who plainly contradict — I do not know how to say it in seemly terms — what has been well recognized. ⟦s19⟧ But since those who are eager to learn long somehow to dwell on what is inquired into through more words, we shall say it in another way too. ⟦s19⟧ Those who have treated of these things say: Not to all to whom choosing is fitting is wishing also altogether fitting. ⟦s20⟧ For we say that we wish to be healthy and to be rich and to be made immortal; but we do not say that we choose to be rich and to be healthy and to be made immortal; since wish is both of things possible and of things impossible, while choice is of possible things alone, and of those able to come about through us. ⟦s21⟧ And again, wish is of the end; choice, of the things toward the end. ⟦s22⟧ They say, then, that the end is the thing wished, for example health; and toward the end, the thing deliberated, for example the mode of health. ⟦s23+24⟧ The same proportion, then, that the thing wished has to the thing deliberated, they say [0016] wish has to choice; since we choose only those things which we think can come about through us; but we wish also things that cannot come about through us. ⟦s25⟧ It has been shown, then, that choice is not wish either; and it will be shown again that it is neither deliberation, that is, counsel.

⟦s26⟧ *Of counsel, that is, deliberation.*

⟦s27⟧ Counsel, that is, deliberation, they say is an inquiring appetite that comes about concerning some one of the things to be done that are in our power. ⟦s28⟧ And the thing chosen is what has been judged out of counsel. ⟦s29⟧ It is clear from this, then, that deliberation is about things still being inquired into, while choice is about things already judged beforehand. ⟦s30⟧ And this is clear not only from the definition but also from the etymology. ⟦s31⟧ For the thing chosen (προαιρετόν) is the one taken (αἱρετόν) before (πρό) another. ⟦s32⟧ And no one prefers anything without having deliberated; nor does he take without having judged. ⟦s33⟧ Counsel, that is, deliberation, then, is not choice.

⟦s34⟧ *Of choice.*

⟦s35⟧ Choice, they say, is a deliberative appetite for things to be done that are in our power. ⟦s36⟧ For choice is something mixed and blended of many things, composed of appetite and counsel and judgement. ⟦s37⟧ For choice is none of these considered by itself: it is neither appetite alone by itself, nor counsel, nor judgement; but blended of these, just as the man in our condition is composite of soul and body. ⟦s38⟧ Since choice comes about, and the thing chosen, at that moment when that which has been preferred out of counsel, which choice is about, takes appetite to itself. ⟦s39⟧ Necessarily, then, choice is about these things after the judgement, about which counsel is before the judgement. ⟦s40⟧ For we choose those things about which we deliberate.

⟦s41⟧ *About what things we deliberate.*

⟦s42+43⟧ We deliberate about things in our power and able to come about through us, and having an end not evident. 'In our power', then, has been said, since we deliberate only about things to be done; for these are in our power. ⟦s44⟧ For [we do] not [deliberate] about self-subsistent wisdom. ⟦s45⟧ For neither about God, nor about things that come about of necessity and always in the same way, such as the cyclic movement of the times; nor about things that are not always, yet come about always alike, such as the rising and setting of the sun; nor about things that are by nature, yet come about not always alike [0017] but for the most part, such as about the sixty-year-old's going grey, or the twenty-year-old's growing a beard; nor about things that are by nature, but come about now one way, now another, indeterminately, such as about rains and droughts and hail. ⟦s46⟧ For these reasons, then, 'in our power' has been said. ⟦s46⟧ And 'able to come about through us', since we deliberate neither about all men [all things human], nor about every matter; nor yet about all the things in our power and coming about through us; but there must be added 'and having an end not evident'. ⟦s47⟧ For if it be manifest and agreed, we no longer deliberate about it, even if it be in our power and come about through us. ⟦s48⟧ And it has been shown that counsel is not about the end, but about the things toward the end. ⟦s49⟧ For we deliberate not about becoming rich, but how and by what means we shall become rich. ⟦s50⟧ And to speak concisely, we deliberate only about those things the doing of which is equally contingent; and that is equally contingent which we are able [to do], both it and what is opposed to it. ⟦s51⟧ For if we were not able [to do] both, we would not deliberate about both. ⟦s52⟧ For about things agreed and manifest, or about things impossible and not contingent, no one deliberates. ⟦s53⟧ And if we were able [to do] only one of the opposites, this would be agreed, as unambiguous, and the opposite impossible. ⟦s54⟧ For example, when bread and a stone are set before one, no one deliberates which he will make his food, the one being agreed, the other being clearly impossible. ⟦s55⟧ These, then, we choose: the things equally contingent, about which we also deliberate.

⟦s55⟧ *Of inclination.*

⟦s56⟧ But neither is choice some inclination, even if the many readily think so; but it is of inclination; if indeed they say that inclination is an inward appetite for things in our power, from which choice comes; or a disposition toward things in our power that have been appetitively deliberated. ⟦s57⟧ For appetite, having been disposed toward the things judged out of counsel, has become inclination; after which, or to speak more properly, out of which, comes choice. ⟦s58⟧ Inclination, then, holds the ratio of habit to operation with respect to choice.

⟦s59⟧ *Of authority.*

⟦s60⟧ But neither is choice authority. ⟦s61⟧ For choice, as I have often said, is a deliberative appetite for things to be done that are in our power; but authority is lawful lordship over things to be done that are in our power; or unhindered lordship over the use of things in our power; or unenslaved appetite for things in our power. ⟦s62⟧ Authority and choice, then, are not the same; since we choose according to authority, but we do not exercise authority according to choice; and the one only selects, while the other uses the things in our power and the things that bear on the things in our power, that is, choice and judgement and counsel. ⟦s63⟧ For according to authority we deliberate, [0020] and judge, and choose, and are impelled, and use the things in our power.

⟦s64⟧ *Of opinion.*

⟦s65⟧ But neither is choice some opinion. ⟦s66⟧ For opinion is twofold, rational and irrational; but choice is changeable. ⟦s67+68⟧ They say, then, that irrational opinion is a simple and ready knowledge, as when someone puts forward another's opinion, not knowing its cause; for example, saying that the soul is immortal, and not giving a demonstrative cause of its immortality. ⟦s67+68⟧ And rational opinion they say is knowledge constituted by the termination of the understanding, as when someone, having reasoned something out, also renders its cause scientifically. ⟦s69⟧ For understanding is that which passes through and operates by intervals, beginning from a premise up to a conclusion. ⟦s70⟧ For example, someone proposes to show the soul immortal, and says: Since the soul is self-moved; and the self-moved is ever-moving; and the ever-moving is immortal: the soul therefore is immortal. ⟦s71⟧ Or again: Since the soul is incomposite; and the incomposite is indissoluble; and the indissoluble is immortal: the soul therefore is immortal. ⟦s72+73+74+75+76⟧ For it is proper to understanding to traverse some road leading to the knowledge of the matter; and it travels from the premises through the syllogisms to the conclusions, and makes rational opinion. ⟦s72+73+74+75+76⟧ And again, opinion is not only of things in our power, but also of things not in our power; but choice is only of things in our power. ⟦s72+73+74+75+76⟧ And we call opinion true and false; but choice we do not call true and false, but good and bad. ⟦s72+73+74+75+76⟧ And opinion is of universals; choice, of particulars; for choice is about things to be done, and these are the particulars. ⟦s72+73+74+75+76⟧ Through more arguments, as I think, it has become manifest that choice is not opinion. ⟦s72+73+74+75+76⟧ For if opinion, constituted according to the termination of the understanding, is knowledge, while choice is a deliberative appetite for things to be done that are in our power, choice is not the same as opinion.
