⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0021] a habit opposed to both folly and over-subtlety; and choice, as the account has variously rendered it, a deliberative appetite for things in our power. ⟦s2⟧ And they say that prudence comes about in this manner: for example, the first movement of the mind they call intellection; and the intellection concerning something they call concept; which, having persisted and stamped the soul toward what is understood, is called pondering; and pondering, having remained in the same and having tested itself, is named prudence; and prudence, having been broadened, makes reasoning, which is named the inward word among those skilled in these matters; which, describing it, they say is the fullest movement of the soul, coming about in the reasoning faculty without any utterance; and from it, they say, the uttered word proceeds.

⟦s3⟧ And again they say that prudent thought is the knowledge of the thing prudently considered, which arises from prudence in the one capable of prudence; for they speak of prudence, and the object of prudence, and the one capable of prudence, and prudent thought. ⟦s4⟧ And prudence is the relation; the object of prudence is what is prudently considered; the one capable of prudence is what considers prudently; and prudent thought is the knowledge of the thing prudently considered, which has come about from prudence in the one capable of prudence. ⟦s5⟧ If, then, this is so, but choice is not so, therefore choice is not prudent thought.

⟦s5⟧ It has been shown, then, clearly, as I think, that the things put forward are not the same as one another, as those hold who think so. ⟦s6⟧ But if they should say that they are altogether the same as one another, because appetite, that is, the appetitive power of the nature, is predicated equally of all as a genus, let them say also that all the species are the same as one another, the land animals and the winged and the aquatic, because the living being is predicated equally of all as a more generic genus; and the ordering of the universe will be gone for them, being not a hypostasis of different realities, but only a display of bare names.

⟦s7⟧ *That the will of the saints toward one another and toward God will not be in every way one after the resurrection, even if what is willed is one for all, as some say.*

⟦s8⟧ So then choice, having taken to itself impulse and use upon the things in our power, is the limit of our rational movement according to appetite. ⟦s9⟧ For what is rational by nature, having as natural power the rational appetite, which they also call the volition of the intellectual soul, has appetite and reasons; and having reasoned, it wishes. ⟦s10⟧ For wish, they say, is not the simply natural [volition], but the qualified, that is, the volition concerning something; and [0024] wishing, it inquires; and inquiring, it considers; and considering, it deliberates; and deliberating, it judges; and judging, it chooses; and choosing, it is impelled; and being impelled, it uses; and using, it ceases from the appetitive movement toward that thing. ⟦s11⟧ For no one uses without first having been impelled; and no one is impelled without choosing; and no one chooses without having judged; and no one judges without having deliberated; and no one deliberates without having considered; and no one considers without having inquired; and no one inquires without having wished; and no one wishes without having reasoned; and no one reasons without having appetite; and no one has rational appetite without being by nature rational. ⟦s12⟧ Man, then, being by nature a rational living being, is appetitive, and capable of reasoning, of wishing, of inquiry, of consideration; elective, and capable of impulse and of use.

⟦s13⟧ If, then, along with the rest man is also by nature an elective living being; and choice is of things in our power and able to come about through us and having an end not evident; and in our power is both the principle of the virtues, which is the law of the powers according to nature in operation, and the mode according to the misuse of those same powers, which makes the passions contrary to nature subsist parasitically: therefore everyone elective by nature is receptive of opposites and judging of them. ⟦s14⟧ And if judging of opposites, altogether elective too; and if elective, since the movement toward both lies under him, he is not unchangeable by nature. ⟦s15⟧ So then, since counsel and judgement and choice are of things ambiguous, as being in our power, when the ambiguous things are no more, the self-subsistent truth having been shown manifest to all, there is no choice moving through the things that are intermediate and in our power; since there is no judgement either, making the marking-off of opposites, of which we make the better chosen before the worse. ⟦s15⟧ And if then, according to the law of nature now prevailing, there is no choice, all ambiguity of beings having passed away, there will be only an intellectual appetite in act for those who are thus appetitive according to nature, laying hold ineffably of the mystical enjoyment alone of what is by nature the object of appetite, toward which it had its movement through the things enumerated; whose satiety is the infinite intensification of the very appetite of those who enjoy; each partaking supernaturally as much as he longed; and toward the very thing by nature longed for there is an unmediated co-mingling. ⟦s16+17+18+19+20⟧ And if as much as each has longed, so much [0025] will he partake of what is longed for, by the principle of nature the volition of all will be shown to be one, but by the mode of movement, different.

⟦s16+17+18+19+20⟧ And if by the mode according to movement the volition of all men is not one, never will there come to be in every way one volition of God and of those being saved, as it seemed to some; even if what is willed is one for God and for the saints, the salvation of those being saved, which is the divine aim, as the end foreconceived before all the ages; about which there will come about an agreement in volition of those being saved with one another and with God who saves; God having passed wholly into all generically and into each one specifically, he who fills all things by the measure of grace, and is filled in all, like members, according to the proportion of the faith in each, those who have grown together with him. [n: Ephes. i, 23.] For if the will of God is by nature saving, and that of men by nature saved, what saves by nature and what is saved by nature would never be the same; even if the one aim of both is the salvation of all, put forward by God, chosen by the saints.

⟦s16+17+18+19+20⟧ And if the divine and the human will are to be the same and one in number, according to those who say so, since God made all the ages subsist by will, the choir of the saints will also be creator by the same will, whether natural or of inclination; and, in short, by that by which they are naturally apt to be identified with the divine; which is absurd, and the work, as I think, only of a wandering understanding. ⟦s16+17+18+19+20⟧ For as long as not all are creators, or only apostles, or only prophets; and these again either Peter only or Moses only; and there are not one but many abodes with the God and Father for the saints; [n: Joan. xiv, 2.] and the human [will], moved by imitation toward the will good by nature, is not the same with it in natural quality and quantity — [a sameness] which is impossible (for how could what is by nature participable be the same as what by nature participates?) — it is impossible that there be in every way one inclination of God and of the choir of the saints; even if, as I said, what is willed is one for both, the salvation of all; about which is the union of the wills.

⟦s21⟧ But, as it seems, not knowing what an account of absurdity springs up for them from what they say, they let their own understanding be carried aimlessly where it ought not. ⟦s22⟧ For if there is no will, however it is understood or said, that is without relation and has not the genus 'relative' predicated of it, it is a quality, but not of those that are by themselves, as being, namely, considered in another. ⟦s23⟧ And if the will is of those considered in another, it is altogether an accident; and if an accident, it will be characteristic either of essence or of hypostasis (for [0028] there is nothing intermediate between these considered by itself, either partaking of neither, or composite from both). ⟦s23⟧ And if of essence, they will predicate one nature of God and of the saints, all having undergone, through the one will, coalescence into one essence. ⟦s24⟧ For what is predicated generically of certain things equally is indicative of the essence under which they fall. ⟦s25⟧ And if of hypostasis, there will be one hypostasis of all, of God and of the saints, all having been fused into the same with one another. ⟦s26⟧ For what is specifically innate in someone according to hypostasis, being considered invariantly in others, confounds all with one another, and makes the principle of the how-to-be of each one altogether indiscernible.

⟦s27⟧ *That it is not possible that one will be said of Christ, whether it be called natural or elective, as it seemed to some.*

⟦s28⟧ So then, if their account was weak in these matters, neither when it passes to our Lord and God and Saviour of all, Christ, and constructs one will said of him in whatever way, would it ever be strong. ⟦s29⟧ For if they should say that this will of Christ is natural, they will be shown sick straightaway with the Manichaean fantasy, as maintaining one nature of Christ, which is not and will not be, revealed by a corresponding will; and they will be caught at the same time emulating the madness of Arius and the essence-fusion of Apollinarius, as alienating Christ according to essence from the God and Father and from the immaculate Mother. ⟦s30⟧ For the natural will characterizes nature; and no account will contradict this. ⟦s31⟧ And if it characterizes nature, it is clear that those who say this proclaim Christ a nature. ⟦s32⟧ And if Christ is a nature, he is neither God by nature nor yet truly man by nature; if indeed the Father is not truly Christ by nature, nor the Mother truly Christ by nature. ⟦s33⟧ And if Christ is God by nature inasmuch as he is by nature Christ, he who says this is a polytheist, maintaining one nature of the Father as God, who is not by nature Christ, and another nature of Christ as God, who is by nature Christ; and the end of such portent-mongering for them will be condemnation for polytheism.
