⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0029] contrary to nature, and it will show the mode of the use of the things that lie with him as having become destructive of the principle of nature; for such is choice contrary to nature of the things to be done that lie with the one choosing, making the worse chosen before the better. ⟦s2⟧ For when the judgement of the one choosing, according to deliberation, about the things to be done that lie with him has been set right or has slipped — of which judgement choice is, as it were, the vote — either the principle according to nature subsists for him through good use, or the mode contrary to nature subsists parasitically through bad use: the one becoming the messenger of choice according to nature, the other of choice contrary to nature.

⟦s2⟧ And if the choice of Christ be according to nature, not only shall we again blame them for the same things, as rashly improvising another nature of Christ, intermediate between Godhead and creation; but we shall also laugh at them as triflers, making Christ, like a mere man according to Nestorius, receptive of opposites by nature according to choice. ⟦s3⟧ And if contrary to nature, I pass over the blasphemy in silence, lest unawares I defile my tongue with the refutations against them. ⟦s4⟧ For in saying there is an elective will in Christ, they have altogether indicated that the hypostasis of Christ is able to be moved according to nature and contrary to nature. ⟦s5⟧ For such, as I said before, is choice; and if it is characteristic of the hypostasis of Christ, they have marked him off, by this very will, from both the Father and the Spirit as of other counsel and of other inclination. ⟦s6⟧ For what is considered specifically in the Son according to hypostasis is altogether incommunicable to the Father and the Spirit according to hypostasis. ⟦s7⟧ As many, then, of the holy Fathers as made mention of choice in the manhood of Christ indicated the appetitive power of the nature essentially — which is the same as saying the natural volition — or our own choice, present in God incarnate by appropriation.

⟦s8⟧ Having considered this especially, I, thy servant and disciple, in what was set out to my most holy lord and teacher Thalassius concerning the difficulties of holy Scripture, said "choice"; knowing that if the maker of men has become man for us, he has clearly also achieved for us the unchangeableness of choice, as creator of unchangeableness; [0032] receiving essentially and by authority the passions of our penalty through the experience itself, while taking upon himself those of dishonour by appropriation, in his love for man; of which he has made the appropriation the cause to the race of the elective dispassion, and has given the experience as a faithful pledge of the natural incorruption that will follow. ⟦s9⟧ For the human of God was not moved by choice as we are, making the discernment of opposites through counsel and judgement, lest it be thought changeable by nature according to choice; but having received its being together with the union with God the Word, it had its natural movement according to appetite, that is, volition, undoubting, or rather stable; or, to speak more properly, it had its rest unmoved in him, according to the most unalloyed essentiation, altogether deified by God and Word; which rest he, naturally shaping and moving it as his own and natural to his own soul, has fulfilled without fantasy the great mystery of the economy on our behalf; having diminished nothing at all natural of what was assumed, except sin, of which no principle at all has been sown in any of the things that are.

⟦s10⟧ The difference of the names, then, having become manifest to us through the description distinctly fitted to each, let us not be troubled when we hear those who use the names without distinction for the confusion of the realities understood; since for us the truth is in realities, and not in names; and let us not think what is not the same to be the same; but believing piously, let us confess Christ truly perfect God and truly perfect man, the same being so in reality, properly, in truth, and not called this by bare name alone; and for this reason let us proclaim two natures, of which he is hypostasis, if indeed we do not proclaim him fleshless; and two natural wills, according to the Fathers, if indeed we boldly declare that he is not soulless and mindless; and let us not dare to innovate a word for which we have no refuge in the authority of the Fathers. ⟦s11⟧ For one will of Christ, natural or elective, those zealous to be pious fear to say, not only because of the absurdity shown, [0033] but because no one of the holy teachers has ever been found to have said it; knowing truly that choice belongs to things able to be moved to both — I mean good things and bad; which in the case of Christ, the very essence and source of good things, even to conceive, not to say to speak of, is fullest of all impiety. ⟦s12⟧ And concerning these things, so much.

⟦s13⟧ Concerning the one operation lying in the seventh chapter of the Difficulties of the great Gregory, the account is clear. ⟦s14⟧ For describing the future state of the saints, I said there was one operation of God and of the saints, the one deifying all the saints, of the hoped-for blessedness: belonging to God by essence, but having come about for the saints by grace. ⟦s15⟧ Or rather, I added "of God alone"; since the deification of the saints by grace is the effect of the divine operation alone, of which we do not have the power sown in our nature. ⟦s16⟧ And of what we do not have the power, of these neither do we have the practice, which is the completion of natural power. ⟦s17⟧ Practice, then, depends on power; and power on essence. ⟦s18⟧ For practice is from power; and power from the essence and in the essence. ⟦s19⟧ These three, then, as they say, depend on one another: what is able, power, the possible. ⟦s20+21+22+23+24⟧ And "what is able" they say is the essence; power, that according to which we have the movement of being able; and the possible, what is naturally apt to come about by us according to power. ⟦s20+21+22+23+24⟧ And if what is naturally apt to come about by us we have according to natural power; but deification is not of the things naturally apt to come about by us according to power, not being of the things in our power — for there is no principle in nature of things above nature — therefore deification is not the practice of our power, of which we do not have the power according to nature, but of the divine power alone; being not a requital to the saints for just works, but a demonstration of the bounty of the one who made them; who makes the lovers of good things by adoption that which he himself will be shown to be by nature, according to the principles which he himself knows; that he may both be perfectly known, and remain altogether incomprehensible. ⟦s20+21+22+23+24⟧ I did not, then, destroy the natural operation of those who will undergo this, by making it manifest that it has ceased from what it is naturally apt to accomplish, and undergoes only the enjoyment of good things; but I indicated [0036] that the superessential power alone is productive of deification, having come about by grace for those deified.

⟦s25⟧ And let no one suspect that by these things one operation of Christ is proclaimed. ⟦s26⟧ For we do not proclaim Christ a deified man, but God perfectly become man, and having made manifest, through flesh operative by nature, intellectually ensouled, the infinite — infinitely-many times, infinitely more than infinite — power of his own ineffable Godhead; the same wholly God and truly man; the same perfect and most full in both by nature, and having without deficiency all that is naturally in both, except sin, of which there is no principle. ⟦s27⟧ For of the natures of which he was hypostasis, of these he also naturally received the essential principles. ⟦s28⟧ And if he received the essential principles of those of which he was hypostasis, he also fittingly had the natural ensouled operation of the flesh, whose essential principle is sown in the nature. ⟦s29⟧ And if he had as man natural operation, whose principle was constitutive of the nature, he clearly also had as God natural operation, whose principle was manifestive of the superessential Godhead. ⟦s30⟧ For being perfect God and the same perfect man, he operated naturally according to each of those of which he was hypostasis, not being divided. ⟦s31⟧ And if he operated naturally according to each of those of which he was hypostasis, not being divided, it is clear that with the natures of which he was hypostasis he also had their essential and constitutive operations, of which he himself was the union; operating congruously with himself, and through the things he operated confirming the truth of those from which, and in which, and which he was.

⟦s32⟧ One, then, is Christ from two natures, Godhead and manhood; only-begotten Word and Son and Lord of glory; in which he is known, and which he is truly believed to be; having two natural and generic movements, that is, operations, constitutive of those from which he was; whose results were the effects of operation in detail, both put forth from him and perfected by him, without cut of those from which he was, and apart from any confusion whatever. ⟦s33⟧ For he does not undergo cut or confusion who is never subject to changes, and who provides to all the things that are the permanence and constitution both of being and of how-to-be.

⟦s33⟧ [0037] These things, by thy command, sanctified Father, let it be said, have been set out in brief; that thou mayest have, if they bring anything useful, the means to beat off the chatter of the talkative, which has taken licence from the difficulty of the times. ⟦s34⟧ And do not overlook thy servant, who is poor; but enrich him with the divine Spirit, repaying his obedience with thy strong prayers; and do not cease commending [me] to Christ the great God and Maker of all, who is worshipped by all creation, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, unto the ages. ⟦s35⟧ Amen.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 1. ⟦s2⟧ By reason the nature of beings is circumscribed for the coming-to-be of virtue, by way of procession; which is the complete denial of nature according to virtue. ⟦s3⟧ And by mind the movement of every age and time is bounded, for the establishment of an unerring union, by way of ascent; which is the surpassing of the things in which nature is — I mean place and time, in which is the hypostasis of beings. ⟦s4⟧ And the restoration by grace toward him from whom are all things, and through whom, and to whom, as the limit of all, comes about by way of taking-up; which is a passion of the one taken up, but an operation of the one taking up.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 2. ⟦s2⟧ Person, that is, hypostasis; nature, that is, essence. ⟦s2⟧ For essence is the "according to the image", reason; and the "according to the likeness", life, is hypostasis; out of both of which virtue has been completed. ⟦s3⟧ For they say that virtue is the hypostasis of wisdom, and that wisdom is the essence of virtue. ⟦s4⟧ Wherefore the unerring manifestation of wisdom is the mode of the conduct of the contemplative; and the base of virtue is the principle of the contemplation of the practical. ⟦s5⟧ And the most unlying character of both is the unswerving gazing toward what properly is.
