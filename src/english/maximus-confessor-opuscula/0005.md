⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ OPUSCULUM 3 — FROM THE SAME TREATISE, CHAPTER 51

⟦s1+2⟧ *Of the same. ⟦s1+2⟧ From the same treatise, chapter 51.*

⟦s3⟧ *That the Fathers, saying two wills in Christ, signified the natural laws, not the inclinations.*

⟦s3⟧ But let no one call the account to account for forbidding the duality of wills of inclination, when he finds almost all the renowned teachers saying two wills; nor again, on the other side, let him on this account, after Severus, shift his pious mind to one will, lest he make one evil the successor of another: I mean confusion the successor of division. ⟦s4⟧ For the inspired Fathers did not speak in Christ of a quantity of wills of inclination, but of natural ones, rightly naming wills the essential and natural laws and principles of the things united. ⟦s5⟧ For having understood with the movements of the mind the natural appetite of the flesh intellectually ensouled, and not the appetite of inclination of some particular man, an appetite having the natural power of aspiration for being, moved naturally and formed by the Word for the fulfilment of the economy, they wisely named it will; without which it is impossible for human nature to be. ⟦s6⟧ For natural will is the appetitive power of what is according to nature, and the power [0048] that holds together all the idioms essentially present to the nature; according to which, in what is by nature volitive, being naturally fitted to will is ever inborn.

⟦s6⟧ But being naturally fitted to will and willing are not the same, as neither are being naturally fitted to speak and speaking. ⟦s7⟧ For what has the faculty of speech is ever naturally fitted to speak, but does not ever speak; since the one is of essence, held together by the principle of nature, and the other of counsel, formed by the inclination of the one speaking. ⟦s8⟧ So that the ever being naturally fitted to speak is of nature, and the speaking in this or that way is of hypostasis; just as also with being naturally fitted to will, and willing. ⟦s9⟧ But if being naturally fitted to will and willing are not the same (for the one, as I said, is of essence, and the other belongs to the counsel of the one willing), then the incarnate Word had as man being naturally fitted to will, moved and formed by his divine will. ⟦s10⟧ «For his willing», says the great Gregory, «is nothing contrary to God, deified wholly.» ⟦s11⟧ But if it had been deified, it had been deified, plainly, by growing-together with what deifies; and what deifies and what is deified are altogether two, but not one and by nature the same. ⟦s12⟧ Since what deifies and what is deified are among relatives; and relatives are naturally fitted to be introduced together with one another, and the one to be conceived along with the other.

⟦s13⟧ So then, as being naturally fitted to it, the Saviour is signified as willing, as man, in a fleshly way, with the other passions, the contraction before death too, showing the economy pure of every phantasy, and redeeming the nature from the sufferings to which it was condemned on account of sin. ⟦s14⟧ And again he shows the impulse, having put death to death by the flesh, so that he might both show in himself, as man, what is naturally fitted being saved, and display, as God, the ineffable and great counsel of the Father being bodily fulfilled. ⟦s15+16+17⟧ For not in order to suffer, but in order to save, did he principally become man. ⟦s15+16+17⟧ Therefore he says: «Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; yet let not my will but thine be done»; showing, together with the contraction, the impulse of the human will, formed and coming to be in its growing-together with the divine, according to the interweaving of the natural principle with the mode of the economy; since the incarnation is a manifest demonstration of nature and economy: of the natural principle, I mean, of the things united, and of the mode of the union according to hypostasis, the one being confirmed, and the other making the natures new, without change and confusion. ⟦s15+16+17⟧ But he was not asking that the same thing, in the same respect, should be both annulled and brought into operation, which is absurd; since the will of the Son by nature is that of the Father. ⟦s15+16+17⟧ The Saviour therefore had as man a natural will, formed by his divine will, not opposed to it. ⟦s18⟧ For nothing natural is at all opposed to God, when not even what is of inclination is, in which too [0049] the personal division is contemplated, if indeed it be according to nature; since otherwise the maker must be charged with the blame, as having made what by nature fights against himself.

⟦s18⟧ But how did the Word, being incarnate, truly become man, if he was destitute of what most characterizes the nature as rational? ⟦s19⟧ For what is deprived of the appetitive movement by aspiration would also have no share in any vital power. ⟦s20⟧ And what does not have vital power from nature has not, plainly, any soul whatever, without which not even flesh could ever subsist. ⟦s21⟧ So then he made a phantasy of the economy by a bare shaping of flesh; and the Word, incarnate according to hypostasis, did not fulfil it by a nature of flesh intellectually and rationally ensouled, not having as man, according to Severus, a natural will. ⟦s22⟧ For if as man he was truly lacking in natural will, he did not truly become perfect man; and if he did not truly become perfect man, he did not become man at all. ⟦s23⟧ For what existence is there of an imperfect nature, of which there is not even a principle?

⟦s24⟧ The aim, then, of Severus and of those about him is, through some natural deficiency at all events, to thrust out the nature assumed in ineffable union, and to ratify the abomination of the phantasy of Manes, and of the confusion of Apollinarius, and of the co-essentiation of Eutyches. ⟦s25⟧ For I remember, when I was staying in the island of the Cretans, certain false bishops of the party of Severus disputing with me; and I heard them say that it is for this reason that we do not confess in Christ two operations, according to the Tome of Leo, on account of the wills that follow them, with which a duality of persons is of necessity introduced together; but neither again one, since it cannot be contemplated as simple; but one will, and every divine and human operation, we say, proceeds, according to [0052] Severus, from one and the same God the Word incarnate.

⟦s26+27⟧ Against whom one might bring, complaining, that part of the prophecy: «O, o, flee from the north; be saved into Sion, you who inhabit the daughter of Babylon.» [n: Zach. ii, 7.] For truly the north is the understanding of Severus, which has become a gloomy place, and deprived of the abiding of the divine light; and the daughter of Babylon is the confused teaching of the false doctrines, evilly begotten from the most wretched habit that grew upon him; which those inhabit who have turned away from the light of knowledge, and are not willing to be saved by conversion to Sion, I mean the Church. ⟦s28+29+30⟧ For the account of Severus, put to the test, fights at once against theology and against economy.

⟦s28+29+30⟧ For if according to him wills are naturally fitted to follow operations, and persons to be introduced with wills, as things caused with causes — not to say: what is the demonstration? — then with every person, plainly, according to him, there is a will; and with this, altogether, an operation suited to it will be introduced together. ⟦s28+29+30⟧ For the principle of relatives, having an indissoluble relation, makes accordingly an equal reciprocation of the things related.

⟦s31⟧ So then, since what is said simply has many meanings: if the wills introduced by reciprocation together with the persons be natural, the blessed Monad will, according to Severus, also be a Trinity of natures. ⟦s32⟧ But if of inclination, it will altogether be at faction with itself, not concurring in its wills, as a Trinity of persons; or altogether, if there is one will of the superessential Trinity, the Godhead will be of one person, three-named.

⟦s32⟧ And again, if according to the premise of Severus a will altogether follows the operation, and with this a person is introduced, then of necessity, when the operation is destroyed, the will that follows it is also destroyed together with it, and the person introduced with this. ⟦s33⟧ But if with the operation the will, and with the will the person, is destroyed together, Christ will be, according to Severus, without hypostasis together with the operation: on account of the will destroyed together with it, the person introduced with the will having been destroyed together with it.

⟦s34⟧ And again, if according to him wills altogether follow operations, and persons are introduced together with wills, and he says that every divine and human operation proceeds from one and the same God the Word in- [0053] carnate, then every will too according to him (that is, divine and human), as following the operations, will altogether be brought forth together, with the persons introduced together with them in equal number, from one and the same Word incarnate; and no argument will gainsay it.

⟦s35⟧ Christ, then, will be according to Severus, by the destruction of the natural operations, without essence; and by the setting aside of the one, again without counsel and without hypostasis; and by the bringing forth of all divine and human operation, of many counsels and many persons; or, to say it more properly, of infinite counsels and infinite persons. ⟦s36⟧ For he who said «every operation» made a declaration of a numberless quantity.

⟦s37⟧ So then, by the reciprocation of the premise of Severus, his account of theology has collapsed, as he introduces Arian polytheism, and Sabellian atheism, and, in the Hellenic manner, a nature of Godhead fighting with itself; and by the premise, the account of the economy has plainly been adulterated for him, as he advocates the one Christ without essence, without counsel, without hypostasis, and again the same of infinite counsels and infinite persons; than which what is more impious?

⟦s38⟧ Do you see where the canon of Severus carries and leads away those who are persuaded by it? ⟦s39⟧ For such is every account that does not have the truth as its unconquered foundation. ⟦s40⟧ But saying one will of Christ, my good man, how and of what kind dost thou say it is? ⟦s41⟧ If as natural to Christ, thou hast estranged him by nature from Father and Mother, as united to neither according to essence. ⟦s42⟧ For Christ is by nature neither of the two; and how, saying this, wilt thou escape the danger of polytheism? ⟦s43⟧ But if of inclination, it will be characteristic of his hypostasis alone. ⟦s44⟧ For what is of inclination is distinguishing of person; and he will plainly be shown, according to thee, of other counsel than the Father and the Spirit, and fighting against them. ⟦s45⟧ But if of his Godhead alone, the Godhead will be subject to passion, aspiring contrary to nature after food and drink. ⟦s46⟧ But if of his manhood alone, it will not be by nature effective. ⟦s47⟧ For how, if indeed it is human? ⟦s48⟧ And the display of the miracles will plainly be shown to be portentous.

⟦s49⟧ But if it is common by nature to both, how will the will be [0056] common by nature to those whose nature is different? ⟦s50+51⟧ But if as composite of a whole, besides the new myth and fiction — for what composition of will is there? — thou hast again estranged him from the Father, characterizing by a composite will a composite hypostasis alone. ⟦s52⟧ So, then, the word, coming on, uproots every planting which the Father did not plant; [n: Matth. xv, 13.] since it is not naturally fitted to make a strange tillage its own.

⟦s53⟧ But, as it seems, Severus destroyed the natural will of the manhood of Christ, not knowing that the most proper and first idiom of every nature, above all a rational one, is its movement by aspiration; which the Fathers having examined, splendidly confessed in Christ a difference of natural, but not of inclinational, wills. ⟦s54⟧ For they would never have spoken of a difference of wills of inclination in Christ, lest they should proclaim him of two inclinations and two counsels, and, so to say, fighting against himself by the faction of thoughts, and on this account of two persons; they who knew that by this difference alone of the wills of inclination there came about in life both the entry of sin and our standing apart from God. ⟦s55⟧ For evil consists in nothing else but only in the difference of our will by inclination from the divine will; with which, opposed, quantity is altogether introduced together, and the number indicative of it, showing the antipathy of our will of inclination toward God.

⟦s56⟧ There is, then, one aim for Nestorius and Severus as regards impiety, even if the mode is different. ⟦s57⟧ For the one, fleeing the union according to hypostasis on account of confusion, makes the essential difference a personal division; the other, on account of division not saying the essential difference, works the union according to hypostasis into a natural confusion; whereas one ought to confess in Christ neither confusion nor division, but union of the things differing according to essence, and difference of the things united according to hypostasis; so that both the principle of the essences and the mode of the union may be piously proclaimed. ⟦s57⟧ Broken away from both of which, the one ratifying the union of the qualities of inclination only, the other the difference of the natural ones only after the union, both fell away from the truth of the things, daringly condemning the mystery, the one of division, the other of confusion.
