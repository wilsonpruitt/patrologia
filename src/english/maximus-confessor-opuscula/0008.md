⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0081] and as one possessing a natural, that is a human, will — bending it down by way of economy and driving it together into union with the paternal will — that he said to the Father himself, Not my will be done, but thine, it is plain that he said this as God by nature; and from this, then, he showed himself as not possessing the same will as the Father and an equal one, but another, and different according to nature; and, subjecting it, he asked that the paternal alone be done. ⟦s2⟧ And if he possessed a natural will other than the Father's, it is plain that he had also an essence varied from his. ⟦s3⟧ «For of one essence there is indeed also one will», according to the most wise Cyril. ⟦s4⟧ And the natural will being different, the nature is different in every way and altogether.

⟦s4⟧ One of two things, then: either as man he had a natural will, and for our sake, by way of economy, refused death, willingly, and again rushed against it through his perfect convergence with the paternal will; or, not having as man a natural will, he suffered as God by nature the passions of the body in his own essence — an essence contracting naturally before death and possessing a natural will other, according to essence, than the Father's; which also he sought and besought in prayer should not be done. ⟦s5⟧ And what God fears death after the nature of flesh, and on this account begs that the cup pass, and possesses a natural will other than the paternal?

⟦s6⟧ Ridding our souls, then, of this absurdity, let us hold fast the pious confession of the Fathers; and when he says, «Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass», as the great Athanasius says in his discourse On his Incarnation and on the Trinity, «nevertheless not my will be done, but thine; the spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak, we understand that he shows here two wills: the one human, which is of the flesh, the other divine. ⟦s7⟧ For the human, because of the weakness of the flesh, refuses the passion; but his divine will is willing.» ⟦s8⟧ In this way also the great Gregory the Theologian, in the second discourse On the Son, plainly teaches, saying: «For his willing is in nothing contrary, being wholly deified.» ⟦s9⟧ So he had a human willing, according to this divine teacher, yet in no way contrary to God; because this was not at all of inclination, but was properly natural, always shaped and moved by his Godhead according to essence towards the fulfilment of the economy; and wholly, throughout, deified by its convergence and growing-together with the paternal will, and properly divine by the union, but not by nature, both truly become so and called so; in no way, by being deified, departing from what is according to nature.

⟦s10⟧ By saying, then, «wholly deified», the teacher, having presented the union of his human will with his divine and paternal [0084] will, drove entirely out of the mystery of Christ all opposition, and those who will contrary things; and by saying «For his willing», showing both the implanted movement of his human will and its essential and natural difference from his divine and paternal will, he removed confusion together with phantasm entirely. ⟦s11⟧ For knowing that this, and the division equal to it in impiety, make war on the principle of the economy, both this divine Father and the holy teachers of the catholic Church who are like him taught as doctrine, with a loud voice, both the difference and the union against each of them: the one as preserved really and perfectly in the natural principle, the other again as kept firmly and hypostatically in the mode of the economy, for the confirmation of the realities that are essentially in the one and only Christ God by the indissoluble union, and of all the natural things that belong to them.

⟦s12⟧ For all of them, just as they taught as doctrine one nature and another — divine, I mean, and human — and a twofold nature in the case of the same and one, so they proclaimed clearly to all one will and another — divine, I mean, and human — and two wills; and one operation and another, divine and human; and again a twofold operation, that is, two. ⟦s13⟧ For so too they plainly gave voice to the signification of the essences in number. ⟦s14⟧ For the same appears as man, though being according to nature God, willing by way of economy that the cup pass, and up to this point typifying what is ours, as the wise Cyril has taught us; that he might drive from our nature every contraction before death, and steel and rouse it manfully to impulse against it — against death, I mean. ⟦s15⟧ And he appears again as God, although being man according to essence, willing to fulfil the economy paternally and to work the salvation of us all; and he is shown also as man, being God by nature, operating humanly and voluntarily accepting the experience of the sufferings for our sake; and he is shown again as God, though being according to nature man, operating divinely and putting forth naturally the divine signs; from which it was known that the same was both God and man at once, possessing naturally wills and operations equal in number to the essences from which and in which he was, for the confirmation of the perfect existence of the things which he properly was.

⟦s16⟧ But if anyone, to set these aside, puts forward in contradiction into the midst of the argument the theandric operation of Dionysius the revealer of God, and the kindred operation, shown forth through both, of the most wise Cyril, let him know that he will gather from this no strength for himself against the pious confession. ⟦s17⟧ For the theandric, uttered by the teacher with a fitting word, signifies [0085] periphrastically, to be sure, the twofold operation of him who is twofold in nature. ⟦s18⟧ For he plainly proclaimed a divine and a manly — that is, a human — operation by the conjunction in the utterance; but not simply; whence he did not name it by number either, even if he taught it in the singular, marking the union of the natural operations; a union which does not harm their natural difference, just as the indissoluble union of the essences does not harm the difference of the essences itself; since, according to those who say, *If he had one operation, and this theandric* — if the incarnate Word possessed this as one natural operation, how will the one operation according to nature be partitioned into two? ⟦s18⟧ And by what principle will the one operation, in the same respect, constitute and mark off the different essences, making each have invariance according to essence towards things of the same genus, and again difference according to essence towards things of another genus? ⟦s19⟧ And I pass over saying that he has fallen from natural kindredness with the Father and with us, since neither he at all, nor we, possess the implanted and essential operation as theandric. ⟦s20+21⟧ But if again it is hypostatic — besides the novelty of the opinion (for who is it that says an operation is hypostatic?) — then too such an account declares him alien from the Father in operation, if he has an operation hypostatic and not natural, other than the Father's. ⟦s22⟧ For it is by the hypostatic idioms that the Word plainly possesses his difference from him.

⟦s22⟧ And «the kindred and one operation shown forth through both», again, according to the celebrated Cyril, was said by the teacher not for the destruction of the essential difference of the operations according to nature — of the natures from which and in which the one and only Christ God is — but for the establishing of their utmost union. ⟦s23⟧ And he seems in some way, through other words, to imitate Dionysius the revealer of God. ⟦s24⟧ For, that he might seem to be not bare God, nor again mere man; neither God operating by himself bodilessly, nor man by himself doing the things according to inclination; but God incarnate and perfectly become man for our sake, the same operating divinely and humanly at once, he said that not by word alone and by commands befitting God is the Saviour found operative in raising the dead and healing every disease and every infirmity, but he hastened to take his holy flesh as a kind of co-worker for this especially: doing this, on the one hand, as God by his all-working command, and again giving life also by the touch of the holy flesh united to him according to hypostasis, to show that this too is able to give life through his essential operation, to which properly belong the touch, the voice, and all such things. ⟦s25⟧ Then, when he had shown that the natural operations of him — of Christ God composed of both — are preserved perfectly, that of his Godhead through the all-working [0088] command, and that of his manhood through the touch, he presented them as united throughout by their growing-together and interpenetration with each other; so that the operation of the Word himself and of his all-holy flesh is shown to be one through the union: not natural, not hypostatic — for the teacher said no such thing — but kindred to the parts; through which, as has been said, it was shown forth both by all-working command and by touch of hand.

⟦s26⟧ It has been shown, then, from the sacred teaching of the wise man itself, that he declares the difference of the natural operations, as also of the natures themselves whose they are, to be kept even after the union, and again defines the union itself: the one in saying *command*, *all-working* and *touch*; the other in saying *one* and *kindred*; by which he magnificently sends away from the mystery of Christ all coalescence and division. ⟦s26⟧ But those who do not take it so, but understand the kindred operation as one of the Word and of the flesh, teach as doctrine the co-essentiation of Eutyches and Apollinarius. ⟦s27⟧ One must therefore in all things preserve the principle unharmed and keep the mode of the economy uncorrupted, so that the wicked pair — division, I mean, and confusion — may have no entry against the truth.

⟦s28⟧ In accordance with this, then, the pious understanding must be taken of the other singular expressions of the holy Fathers too, if any should be found; and let us in no way receive them as in contradiction to the dual ones of the saints; knowing that these are strong for the difference against confusion, and those secure for the union against division; but let us embrace both, these and those, joyfully with soul and mouth, and confess them in orthodox fashion; and through the expressions that seem somehow contrary in utterance, let us wisely rout those who are contrary in understanding alike to themselves and to one another and to the truth, and manfully drive them out of our fold, that is, the catholic and apostolic Church of God; and let us not give at all any passage whatever against the orthodox faith to those who scheme, like robbers, to remove the paternal boundaries, [n: Prov. xxii, 28.] by throwing away any of the pious weapons and doctrines through which their overthrow and destruction is wont to come about.

⟦s29⟧ But this too one must know: that in the case of natures and natural operations we find singular expressions of the holy Fathers besides the dual ones, as «the one incarnate nature of God the Word», and «the theandric operation», and «the kindred one, shown forth through both»; but in the case of natural wills it is not [0089] possible, so far as I know, to find any singular utterance at all, but only the dual significations, in pronouns and in number. ⟦s29⟧ How then, and by what reason, should we even consent to examine at all whether one or two wills must be said in the case of Christ God, when the legislation and teaching of the Fathers bids us confess and uphold two natural wills in him, as also the natures themselves and their natural operations, so far as to recognize the difference?

⟦s30⟧ These things having now been said by me by way of digression to thy God-honoured holiness, I beseech thee to grant me pardon and correction together for them, whether said unfittingly or not well understood, with the good-loving affection and imitation of fatherly compassion; and to commend me to Christ God, who with the Father and the Holy Spirit is glorified unto the ages of ages. ⟦s30⟧ Amen.
