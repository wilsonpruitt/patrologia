⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ OPUSCULUM 9 — TO THE FATHERS, MONKS AND ORTHODOX LAITY OF SICILY

⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ *To the holy fathers sojourning in this Christ-loving island of the Sicilians, abbots and monks, and to the orthodox peoples, Maximus, lowly and a sinner, an unworthy servant.*

⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ «Great peace have they that love thy law, and [0113] there is no stumbling-block for them», [n: Psal. cxviii, 165.] said David, the ancestor of God, to God; making known from this to all, and declaring to those who aspire to salvation, that, attending with a most divine love to the utmost to the last object of appetite by nature, they should by this love pursue with joy, indissolubly, the good of peace toward one another and toward all, if possible; removing from all every stumbling-block, through pious and utmost like-mindedness toward one another. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ For which reason I too, your servant, have been eager to set down readily in writing what I had earlier established by tongue, for the security and full assurance of you most holy ones, removing by this defence every suspicion that has arisen in you from others about our humility. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ «For I do not bear», as Gregory the Theologian says, «that any of those who carefully watch all our affairs, whether they stand well or otherwise, should be struck in me; for it is good, so far as is possible and reason allows, to set no obstacle or stumbling-block before the many, either by sinning or by being suspected; if indeed we know how inexorable and most heavy is the punishment from him who does not lie for those who cause one of the little ones to stumble.» [n: Matth. xviii, 6.]

⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ I make known, then, to you most holy ones, that never at any time has the supposition of counterpoised and contrary doctrines been held by me, as the insinuation of those who slander me has it. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ For neither did I ever confess, or put in writing at all, one and at the same time two (that is, according to them, three) volitions and operations in the same and one Christ God. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ For the one is overturned by the other, [0116] having no fixity from natural reasonings; besides that such a doctrine is also unwitnessed by those of the truth, that is, by the holy Fathers. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ For if one, how also two; and if two, how also one? ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ And what is this, and how is it called? ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ And with whom, according to it, is the incarnate Word of one operation, that is, consubstantial?

⟦s7⟧ For if they say with the Father, they introduce from this a double divine operation; if indeed they confess the third operation fashioned by them not for the destruction of the two operations of the same according to nature, I mean the divine and the human. ⟦s8⟧ For if not for destruction, it is plain that they champion two divine operations of the same: a natural and a hypostatic. ⟦s9⟧ And if two, then altogether different; and from this [they make] their own supposition polytheist, since the divine operation in the Father and the Son is known to them in otherness and not in natural identity. ⟦s10⟧ And not only polytheist [do they make] their opinion, but already atheist too; as they contrive that the Son has no divine operation, inasmuch as they commix nature and hypostasis with each other, along with the idioms of each; and are known to hold the same as Sabellius, describing the Son as having communion with the Father in each, I mean both nature and hypostasis, which is alien to the Catholic Church; not to say that [they make] the Father too passible by nature and incarnate, that is, become man, by his oneness of operation with the Son, in the miracles and the sufferings alike. ⟦s11⟧ If indeed they say that the Son operates nothing other than the miracles and the passions according to the third operation fashioned by them, as has been said; in which they suggest that the only-begotten Word also has communion with the Father, besides the divine operation according to nature.

⟦s12⟧ But if, hard pressed by these arguments, they define that the Son according to this third operation has communion not with the Father who is without beginning, but with the immaculate Mother, here too they furnish him with two created, that is, human operations, a natural again and a hypostatic; alienating him, that is, in each from the God and Father; and being carried along with the dissection of Arius, by the perfect estrangement, according to them, of the Only-begotten from the Father in both operations. ⟦s13⟧ Whereby they render us too wonder-workers, [0117] and not only passible by nature; if indeed they say that by the third operation, according to them, or, as they like to call it, the operation of the whole, that is, of the hypostasis, Christ preserves his kindredness with us; according to which they fittingly recognize him as willingly not only admitting the experience of the sufferings, but also displaying the power of the miracles; which is impossible.

⟦s14⟧ For it is not possible that the same and one operation, or volition, or nature at all, should be at the same time divine and human; or uncreated and created; or without beginning and having a beginning; or composite and incomposite; so that by the same and one the Son should preserve kindredness and invariance by nature with each, I mean the Father and the Mother; or should do both according to the same, I mean both the things divine by nature and the human things. ⟦s15⟧ For this not even those who fashion goat-stags would conceive.

⟦s16⟧ But if it is impossible that the same and one operation, or volition, or nature, should be at the same time divine and human by nature; or that the Son should have communion according to the same and one with both, I mean Father and Mother, or should do both, the divine and the human things; but rather fittingly, as has been said: by the divine, the divine things, and by the human that is his, the human things, yet with the communion and growing-together of each with the other, and not dividedly; therefore the third operation, according to them, is also nameless, as without existence, ratified by no divine word, and by no Father at all.

⟦s17⟧ «And what», they say, «do we furnish to the whole, if we do not give the one operation to the whole as one, on account of the union?» ⟦s17⟧ But those who bring these things forward have not known what they are saying. ⟦s18⟧ For thinking that the whole is something other besides its own parts, from which and in which it consists, they somehow insist that one must, not from demonstration but from assertion, attach to the whole as whole something else besides what is present according to nature in the parts: I mean the one operation. ⟦s19⟧ Let them then show first that the whole has another existence besides that of its own parts; and then thus assign to it, as whole, another operation again, besides the essential operations present in the parts. ⟦s20⟧ But if not another existence according to nature, then plainly they will not render to the whole another operation according to nature besides those of the parts either. ⟦s21⟧ For Christ, being both according to nature (for the same is God by nature and man), has according to nature what is proper to each nature: I mean the divine volition and operation, and the human volition and operation; but not one only, for the destruction of the two operations according to nature; or another beside the two according to nature, that is, three operations and volitions.

⟦s22⟧ Each, then, as essence, so also each essential volition and operation, we assign according to nature to the same [0120] and only Christ. ⟦s23⟧ For even if, contemplating the difference of his parts, we fittingly give to his Godhead the divine things and to his manhood the human things; conversely, on account of the union, we attach by exchange what is proper to each nature to the other; from which we say both that God is passible against sin, and that the flesh is «assumed and become what that which anointed it is; and I dare to say, co-divine», according to Gregory the Theologian; «neither doing the divine things as God», for he did them through flesh, intellectually ensouled and united to him according to hypostasis, and not by bare Godhead, as before; «nor the human things as man», but with an authority of infinite power, and not liable to necessity. ⟦s24⟧ For the passion of the incarnate Word was not a paying of penalty, as with us, but a self-emptying for us. ⟦s25⟧ Nevertheless, as to a whole, the same and one and only Christ according to nature, we give all the things of the natures from which he is, sin alone excepted; wherefore we confess the same passible and impassible; uncreated and created; circumscribed and uncircumscribed; earthly and heavenly; seen and understood; containable and uncontainable according to nature.

⟦s26⟧ But if, hemmed in again by these, they say that the effect of his two operations according to nature, that is, the divine sign done by the Saviour God toward others [men], such as the resurrection, or the taking-up, or the cleansing, or some such of the wonders, is one and the same operation, not even so does their account have good grounds. ⟦s27⟧ For we know not one, but many and infinite divine signs of the Saviour, about which the most theological John says: «If they should be written every one, I suppose that not even the world here itself could contain the books that should be written.»

⟦s28⟧ Then too, from the things that come about outside, we do not at all recognize the essential existence of any other of the things that are, not to say of the very Word of God who is above all the things that are, incarnate for us; but from the natural idioms by which it is itself characterized in itself, which is the same as to say, from natural volitions and operations. ⟦s28⟧ Or else in this way they would define the carpenter too as an animal that works wood, but not rational, nor mortal, recognizing his nature from the stool, perhaps, or the couch, and not from the idioms that essentially [0121] constitute it; which even to conceive is ignorant. ⟦s29⟧ Whence we properly discern the same to be God and man by nature, not from elsewhere than from the innate idioms that characterize him divinely and at the same time humanly: the divine volition and operation, as I said, and the human volition and operation of the same; by which and through which he seals what he was and has become, working wonders willingly as God above us, and the same suffering willingly as man for us; as being known not as something other besides those from which, in which, and which he is, like some bastard and intermediate thing, that is, a third result, after the example of mules, as the myth-fashioning of the empty-talking heretics tells its wonder-tale, having strayed from the pious proclamation of the holy Fathers; but being in truth properly the very things from which and in which he in truth properly is. ⟦s30+31+32⟧ For being from Godhead and manhood, and in Godhead and manhood according to nature, Christ is God by nature and man; and nothing else at all.

⟦s30+31+32⟧ «And how», they say, «does Cyril the wise declare that one should think a certain middle order of the expressions about Christ, writing thus to Acacius: "For some of the expressions are as much as possible befitting God; others again in the same way befitting man; and others hold a certain middle order"?» ⟦s30+31+32⟧ And what could this ever contribute to them toward their own supposition? ⟦s33⟧ For the teacher does not in these words hand down to us that from a conversion or a loss of the extremes (I mean of Christ's Godhead and manhood, or of the divine and human volition and operation of the same) there comes some other middle, that is, intermediate result, having communion with neither nature of those from which he is; or again that he is, according to them, or has, some composite operation; but that he properly is these, from which he is; yet he is not named these dividedly, but each of them together and in the same, as being one and only Christ. ⟦s34⟧ And he himself bears witness about these things, defending the Eastern bishops, and writing to Acacius thus: «But the brothers at Antioch partition the things united in no way at all; but they insist that only the expressions about the Lord are divided; and that some befit his Godhead, and others again his manhood. ⟦s35⟧ For the same is God and man; and they say that there are also others made common in a certain way, and looking as it were to both, I mean Godhead and manhood. ⟦s35⟧ What I mean is something like this: some of the expressions are as much as possible befitting God, others in the same way again befitting man; and others hold a certain middle order, displaying the Son of God as being God and man together and in the same.» ⟦s36⟧ The teacher has therefore shown us that there is a middle order of expressions, not signifying that Christ is something other besides those from which, in which, and which he is, as some have supposed; but that the same [0124] is at the same time God and man according to nature.

⟦s37⟧ And he brings forward the divinely inspired Scriptures in proof of these things, saying thus: «When he says to Philip, Have I been so long time with you, and hast thou not known me, Philip? ⟦s37⟧ Believest thou not that I am in the Father, and the Father is in me? ⟦s38⟧ He that hath seen me hath seen the Father also; [n: Joan. xiv, 9, 10.] and, I and the Father are one, [n: Joan. x, 30.] we affirm that the expression is most befitting God. ⟦s39+40+41+42+43+44⟧ But when he rebukes the crowds of the Jews, saying this: If ye were children of Abraham, ye would do the works of Abraham; but now ye seek to kill me, a man that hath told you the truth: this did not Abraham, [n: Joan. viii, 39, 40.] we say that such things are spoken in a manner befitting man. ⟦s39+40+41+42+43+44⟧ Yet both those befitting God and indeed the human ones are of the one Son. ⟦s39+40+41+42+43+44⟧ And we affirm that those are middle expressions, such as when blessed Paul writes, Jesus Christ the same yesterday and today, and for ever. [n: Hebr. xiii, 8.] And again: One God the Father, of whom are all things, and we of him; and one Lord Jesus Christ, by whom are all things, and we by him. [n: I Cor. viii, 6.] And again: Whose are the fathers, and of whom is Christ as concerning the flesh, who is over all God, blessed for ever, amen. [n: Rom. ix, 5.] For behold, behold, he who named Christ Jesus says that he is the same yesterday and today and for ever; and that all things came to be through him; and him who is of the Jews according to the flesh he names God over all; and indeed blessed for ever, amen. ⟦s39+40+41+42+43+44⟧ Do not thou then divide in these the expressions about the Lord. ⟦s45⟧ For they have in the same what befits God and what is human; but rather apply them as to one Son, that is, to God the Word incarnate.» ⟦s46⟧ By these words of the Spirit, then, the Teacher has shown us that the middle expressions themselves signify nothing whatever else in Christ than what befits God and what is human, in the same; whence he exhorts that we must apply them to him indivisibly, as to the same who is God and man by nature; and nothing else at all, as I said.

⟦s47⟧ «And yet», they say, «he himself also confesses one operation in Christ. ⟦s48⟧ For thus, interpreting the holy evangelist John, he sets out: "And so, when he raised up the girl of the ruler of the synagogue, saying, Maid, arise, he took her by the hand, as it is written; [n: Luc. viii, 54.] giving life as God by the all-working command, and giving life again also through the touch of his own flesh; showing one and kindred operation through both."» ⟦s49⟧ But this indeed has the meaning of the teacher very much uncovered: that he does not define one operation of both the essences from which the one and only Christ is, as some might suspect; if indeed he himself declares to Hermias, saying: «For to operate alike …
