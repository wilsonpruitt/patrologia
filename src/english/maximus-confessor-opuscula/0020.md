⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1+2+3+4+5⟧ OPUSCULUM 16 — ON THE TWO WILLS OF THE ONE CHRIST OUR GOD

⟦s1+2+3+4+5⟧ *Of Maximus the great, the confessor: On the two wills of the one Christ our God.*

⟦s1+2+3+4+5⟧ Different shapes and forms have been devised by art by different men, for safety and adornment together; and by these they think to make a fine show in what appears, having grown weary, through folly, of what is hidden. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5⟧ But for thee, sanctified Father, who hast already transferred thy thought to the life without interval, virtue unartful, surpassing every form and shape, has sufficed for beauty and for guard; adorning, besides what is [0185] unseen, what is seen by all as well, so greatly as thou hast preferred to what is dissolving and to the world [worldly] a longing that stands firm and never has satiety. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5⟧ Or rather, showing thee, as is right, so far made akin to God and like him, as it has declared thee wholly estranged from matter and the things about it in relation, and unlike them. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5⟧ Hence too the being uncapturable by the things that act ill is thine. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5⟧ For he is not of a nature to suffer at their hands who by inclination has suffered the good things, and is wholly held fast by them, and has received the fulfilment of longing, the perfect victory in himself of what is longed for, and the cessation of movement, the ever-moving rest; according to which the death that long ago prevailed over nature vanishes, nature being no longer worsted by it through transgression. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5⟧ For thou no longer undergoest it again, because of the prevailing grace of the Spirit, by which thou art held fast through holding fast to it in prayers; and, accomplishing its paths with knowledge, thou showest in operation which are royal and divine for those who wish to walk straight on the unerring path of piety, and markest off the turnings-aside from these on either side; and for the one thou showest the threatened griefs, for the other the promised holy recompenses, so that by fear and longing thou mayest lead them up to the perfection that is in God.

⟦s6⟧ And admiring thy work, I fittingly praise thine understanding; or rather, struck by thy vigour in both, glorifying God who empowers thee and makes thee wise, and entreating thee to use the same methods toward those lacking in wits, and not to grow weary — for virtue has no weariness, being the accomplisher of all good things — until thou put into them the mind of Christ, [n: I Cor. ii, 16.] and train them to search out the depths of the Spirit, [n: ibid. 10.] whose very manifest things circumscribe all the wisdom and power of beings, and who reveals the wisdom spoken in a mystery to those who, like thee, have attached themselves to him and have judged nothing more precious than living with him.

⟦s7+8+9⟧ But since, possessing the virtue that is first of all and likewise last, as one that leads forward and as a seal shining round — I mean humility — thou hast deigned to inquire of thy servant and disciple, on account of the definition of natural will which the devout monk made, whether it is well or not, where he said that it «is a power appetitive of what is according to nature, and holding together all the idioms that belong essentially to the nature»: I suppose that it is well, and that this is one will and that of inclination another, and not out of tune with the teaching of the God-approved Fathers; even if it was thought by some not to be so, who [0188] insist that the Fathers define will as volition, and as the thing willed or what is willed.

⟦s7+8+9⟧ And who defines it so, and which of the Fathers, I would gladly ask them. ⟦s7+8+9⟧ For if so, what else is this but a circular demonstration, which does not distinguish the principle of each by itself, but sets down, against all likelihood, the same principle of each through the other, and puts it forward; which is what it is to define will by volition, and this again by that? ⟦s7+8+9⟧ For the things defined convert with their own definition. ⟦s10⟧ For that they call will volition too is plain. ⟦s11⟧ Yet they do not say this as a definition of will. ⟦s12⟧ For who can show it? ⟦s13⟧ Since then wish will be a definition of purpose, and movement in turn of motion, and manhood of man, according to those who say so. ⟦s14⟧ But whether they signify what is signified in this way or in that, they in no way harm its existence, whether what is indicated be a movement or an essence. ⟦s15⟧ For if this harmed it, the existence of all universals together with particulars would altogether collapse. ⟦s16⟧ For in all things we make use of different significations.

⟦s17⟧ And this too I say: that even if they call will volition, yet in no way do they call it the thing willed or what is willed. ⟦s18⟧ For how are aspiration and the object of aspiration the same? ⟦s19⟧ For if so, toward what will it be moved, being itself properly that very thing toward which it is moved, and not being in nature other than that? ⟦s20+21+22⟧ For it is a middle relation between the extremes, uniting them through itself, but not concurring with them in existence. ⟦s20+21+22⟧ And witness is the divine and great Gregory, who does not at all bring the thing willed and the thing begotten into identity with volition and generation, but through these, as natural relations, refers them back to the one begetting and willing. ⟦s20+21+22⟧ For if he did not even consent to make the reference of the thing willed or begotten to the volition and the generation — though volition is middle according to nature — «For it does not altogether follow, he says, how was he going to bring them into identity, and declare these to be one?»

⟦s20+21+22⟧ Besides, if they say that will is the thing willed, and we are by nature a thing willed and what is willed — for we are God's creature — then God has by nature no other will at all but us alone, coming to be and passing away, as we do; and before we were, he was bereft of all will. ⟦s23⟧ And the same will be concluded in our case too: a will proper to us and natural will not belong to us; only the thing willed from outside, being or not being, good or not such, will be wrapped around us. ⟦s23⟧ Moreover, the thing willed is something among the things outside. ⟦s24+25+26+27+28⟧ For it is called a thing willed and what is willed because of the one moved toward it by volition, and not because of itself. ⟦s24+25+26+27+28⟧ So that if he who has natural volition wills none of the things that are, there is for him no thing willed or what is willed. ⟦s24+25+26+27+28⟧ What account, then, have those who insist that the thing willed is will, or who declare that it is so called by the holy Fathers?

⟦s24+25+26+27+28⟧ [0189] But let it be said so, according to them; for our discourse is not about a difference of names, but about pious concepts. ⟦s24+25+26+27+28⟧ But let them answer this to us who fittingly ask: whether the divine will is other according to nature in relation to the human, and this other in relation to that, even if it be called and named in ten thousand ways. ⟦s24+25+26+27+28⟧ Let them teach this, and make firm our understanding, which is shaken over what is unknown; and let us not be much vexed at their impositions of names, but even contribute something of our own to them. ⟦s24+25+26+27+28⟧ For if it is not other and other according to essence, it is plain that it is one by nature and the same. ⟦s29⟧ And if this, the nature too is of necessity one and invariant. ⟦s30⟧ For where there is no natural otherness of wills, neither is there at all otherness of essence and nature. ⟦s31⟧ But if it is other and other by nature, they are altogether different; and if different, how must one not confess their number in quantity? ⟦s32⟧ For the denial of this works the utter destruction both of the difference itself and of the things that differ, as not being. ⟦s33⟧ For how is it possible both to be and not to be numbered, and for what cause? ⟦s34⟧ Or how are the wills, being according to nature, not numbered in the same way, if indeed the natures themselves would reasonably not cease from being numbered? ⟦s35+36⟧ For what is good in the case of those, since they are innate in the things and constitutive of them, ought to hold also for the things themselves; and either let them remove number from these too, as injurious according to them, or, as in no way harmful, let it be included in the case of those too, for the indication of the innate difference present in them, on account of which we use it even with the essences; or let them show how they do not altogether work the destruction or the confusion of the Saviour's natural will as man, by their refusal of number.

⟦s37⟧ And how is the Word incarnate perfect man, apart from natural will? ⟦s38⟧ For being deified by the union with God does not remove it from its true being according to essence, as neither the very nature of the flesh animated rationally and intellectually; just as the utmost and thoroughgoing commingling and union with fire does not remove iron from its own; but it undergoes the things of fire, since it has become fire by the union; yet it is heavy again according to nature, and cuts, because it has not suffered damage to its own nature, nor departed at all from natural operation; though it exists with the fire according to the same and one hypostasis, and does without interval both the things according to its own nature — I mean the cutting — and in turn the things according to the union, which is the burning. ⟦s39⟧ For the burning belongs to it, as the cutting also belongs to the fire, because of the utmost interpenetration and exchange of these into one another; and neither its nature — I mean the iron's — even though it is seen with fire, are we [0192] at all prevented from naming or numbering, nor its natural operation, even though it is [?] with burning, and has no interval toward it, but shows through and is known uniformly with it and in it. ⟦s39⟧ What reason, then, is there not to number what belongs naturally to the nature, that is, the will? ⟦s40⟧ And thereby to attest an imperfection in what is ours to God the Word made flesh, more than perfect and before perfection, although, by the good pleasure of the Father and the co-operation of the Holy Spirit, for our salvation, he shared equally in all the things of the nature, according to the union without interval, as indeed in the nature itself perfectly, save only sin; which was not even of the nature, but of an inclination, the nature having been moved contrary to its principle and its law: a deviation and a slip; [?]

⟦s40⟧ And to me the said monk seems fittingly to have attended to this above all, having distinguished the will of inclination from the natural. ⟦s41⟧ For the one, according to his definition, which is well made, is a power appetitive of what is according to nature. ⟦s42⟧ For every being whatever, and above all every rational one, naturally has appetite for what is according to nature, and, having received from God the power for this, has it according to essence, for its own constitution. ⟦s43⟧ For if it has not the appetite for what is according to nature, could it have it for what in no way is — whence and how — when that has no existence or movement at all? ⟦s44⟧ For only non-being is inert and without essence; and in no way is there any power of it at all among beings, or movement. ⟦s45⟧ But the other is self-chosen impulse, making turnings-aside to either side; and it is not distinguishing of nature, but properly of person and hypostasis.

⟦s46⟧ And having discerned this, the divided and man-worshipping Nestorius impiously taught a union of inclinations; so that he might confirm each hypostasis through the other, and the mere man might be preserved for him, having made the union with God the Word by a certain inclination and self-chosen movement, as he himself says; from which he also establishes the identity of will, or, to speak more properly, the multiplicity of will; inasmuch as, according to him, the union goes forward part by part at each movement of the will of inclination, from the imperfect to the complete, being borne along and advancing; and not from the ineffable conception itself, the Godhead of the Word having been united whole, perfectly, with all that is its own, to our nature whole and all that pertains to it, according to one and the same hypostasis.

⟦s47⟧ So then, that the said monk might set out precisely the completeness of the nature in God incarnate, and cast away what is opposed: the natural will he [0193] defined in him; the will of inclination he marked off, giving neither room to division, through its being at strife in this, nor entry to phantasy, through that natural will's not existing. ⟦s48⟧ But if someone perhaps insists that it is not possible for the two wills to be spoken of in his case otherwise than by opposition: if as different according to essence, I too accept it; but if as opposed, the account is false. ⟦s49⟧ For not whatever is different is altogether also opposed. ⟦s50⟧ For what is opposed is the work of an inclination moved, plainly, contrary to reason; but what is different, of a nature governed by reason; and the one is seditious of nature, the other manifestly constitutive. ⟦s51⟧ Essential difference, then, is for the constitution of the things in the nature; by no means opposition, which is for their dissolution. ⟦s52⟧ For nature does not have what is contrary to nature; nor is there any principle of what is at strife.

⟦s53⟧ Besides, if two wills are altogether contrary and opposed, how is it that in the case of rational beings divided by nature and hypostasis this does not happen everywhere and altogether? ⟦s54⟧ For we converge sometimes by these wills, though they are of inclination, both with God, and with angels, and with one another; but not for this have we by now fallen away from inclination and from the will according to it. ⟦s55⟧ For if we fell away from these, we would fall away also, plainly, from convergence with the others; since, as long as we have this, we hold to the other things of inclination too. ⟦s55⟧ If then this is so here, and no opposed array is seen at all, although the wills of inclination are preserved in existence and number, and keep the friendly disposition in relation: how, in the case of the same and one God the Word incarnate, who became man perfectly for us, will there be opposition in the wills that belong naturally to him according to essence? ⟦s56⟧ And how do they say these are contrary: because of number itself, or because of existing itself? ⟦s57⟧ If because of this, let them destroy the nature of the flesh as well, as opposed to the Word because of its existence. ⟦s58⟧ For the subject, as is plain to all, is much stronger toward opposed array than what is in a subject. ⟦s59⟧ But if because of number alone, let them make the natures bereft of this too, and let them not number even one of them; and let them refuse it again, for the same cause, in the case of the three hypostases of the one Godhead without beginning as well, lest that too be at strife with itself. ⟦s60⟧ But it is absurd to allot so great a strength to what is nothing at all (not essence, not quality, not any other of the things that are); and more absurd to blame the very existence of the natural will, and to contrive its destruction from that; and this, though among the others in the
