⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1+2+3⟧ OPUSCULUM 17 — ON THE DEFINITIONS OF THE DEMARCATIONS

⟦s1+2+3⟧ *Of the same. ⟦s1+2+3⟧ On the definitions of demarcations.*

⟦s1+2+3⟧ Every demarcation of the things that are comes about either according to opposition, or according to being set against, that is, being divided or being destroyed. ⟦s1+2+3⟧ For instance, sensibles and intelligibles have opposition as being of other essence, but they are not set against one another. ⟦s4⟧ For these are not destructive of one another; and for this reason they admit toward themselves the demarcation according to opposition, that is, division. ⟦s4⟧ But life and death have a demarcation, yet not according to opposition, but that of being set against, that is, the destructive. ⟦s5⟧ For the positing of the one becomes the removal of the other; and the existence of the one is the non-existence of the other. ⟦s6⟧ If then anything is antecedently set against another, it is consequently altogether also opposed to it; but if anything is antecedently opposed to another, it is consequently in no way set against it. ⟦s6⟧ And to say it more clearly: the essences, through the differences that are in them, admit the demarcation toward one another according to opposition, as soul and body, and all things that differ according to essence; but the things about the essence, that is, qualities, have the demarcation according to being set against, as life and death, and anything of the sort. ⟦s7⟧ For these are manifestly destructive of one another.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0213] SCHOLION

⟦s1⟧ By opposition understand division; by being set against, think destruction. ⟦s2⟧ For the one of them is in the essences; the other is inborn in the things about the essence. ⟦s2⟧ And in the latter both run together wonderfully, destruction and division again; but the former, again, avails to divide, not to destroy.

⟦s3⟧ *Definitions of unions.*

⟦s4⟧ Union according to essence: in the case of hypostases, that is, of individuals.

⟦s5⟧ Union according to hypostasis: in the case of essences, that is, of soul and body.

⟦s6⟧ Union according to relation: in the case of inclinations, into one will.

⟦s7⟧ Union according to juxtaposition: in the case of planks.

⟦s8⟧ Union according to fitting: in the case of stones.

⟦s9⟧ Union according to blending: in the case of liquids, wine and water, and the like.

⟦s10⟧ Union according to commixture: in the case of dry things and liquids, flour and water.

⟦s10⟧ Union according to confusion: in the case of meltable things, wax and pitch, and the like.

⟦s11⟧ Union according to heaping: in the case of dry things, wheat and barley, and the like.

⟦s12⟧ Union according to fusion: in the case of things drawn off [0216] and restored again; as a torch going forth from fire and restored to it again.

⟦s13+14⟧ Union according to essence is in the case of things of other hypostasis.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ Union according to hypostasis is in the case of things of other essence.

⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ OPUSCULUM 18 — THE DIFFICULTIES OF THEODORE OF BYZANTIUM, AND THE FIRST SOLUTION

⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ *First difficulty of Theodore, deacon of Byzantium, rhetor and synodicarius of Paul, archbishop of Constantinople.*

⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ If the Fathers predicated ignorance also of Christ, according to the same principle as the will, it is necessary that those who say that the will is contemplated in Christ not according to appropriation also say that he is ignorant. ⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ And how is he God who does not know the things to come? ⟦s5⟧ Rather, they must also introduce him as a mere man, according to the already condemned heresy of the Agnoetae. ⟦s6⟧ But if, shrinking from the absurdity that follows from this, they say the ignorance is according to appropriation, as also the forsakenness and the insubordination, let them in like manner say the will too. ⟦s7⟧ For the Fathers ranked ignorance together with the will, keeping for it the same principle; as Athanasius says in his book against the Arians, and Gregory the Theologian in the first oration on the Son, and others in other writings.

⟦s8⟧ *Second difficulty of the same.*

⟦s9⟧ If every expression and word not spoken by the Fathers [0217] is manifestly innovation, one of two things is necessary: either to show that it is a word of the Fathers, to speak of natural wills in Christ; or, not being able to show it, let them know that under the name of the Fathers they are innovating their own teaching. ⟦s10⟧ But if, understanding other words of theirs toward their own aim, they make such a coinage of names: if the second is blameworthy, then the first is too.

⟦s11⟧ *Of Maximus the monk, to Marinus the presbyter: Solution of the difficulties set out above by Theodore, deacon and rhetor. ⟦s11⟧ Solution 1.*

⟦s12⟧ The rhetor's inquiries, most holy and God-honoured father, do not so much have the reasonableness of a difficulty as display the unreasonableness of the difficulty, both from his falsehood and from his strife for the overthrow of the divine incarnation of the Only-begotten. ⟦s13⟧ For not according to the same principle as the will, as he says, did the Fathers predicate ignorance of Christ. ⟦s14⟧ For who is he that can show this, even if these men, as is their custom, not only fabricate things that in no way are, but also boldly bring them forward against themselves? ⟦s15⟧ Or else in this way the principle of ignorance and of will will be the same, bringing into the same things that are in every way properly incompatible with one another; since the one presents the destruction of what is, the other the positing of what is; which is plainly impossible, as habit and privation are not at all the same with one another.

⟦s16⟧ For it is not because the divine and great Gregory counts this too among the words put forward by the Arians (I mean ignorance) that he has thereby declared it the same as the will. ⟦s17+18+19⟧ For he did not even expressly mention the will at all in the place where he goes through these things, saying: «But do thou count for me against these the words of their ingratitude: 'My God and your God'; [n: Joan. x, 36.] 'greater'; 'he created'; 'he sanctified'», and what follows. ⟦s17+18+19⟧ But if, because he only took it along with these, they perhaps attempt to bring ignorance into the same with the will, then surely 'My God and your God', and 'greater', said of the Father, and to create and to sanctify, will also be the same as ignorance according to them, because of the ranking-together, since he ranked these too together with ignorance. ⟦s17+18+19⟧ But if this is a perversion of understanding, that is still more an aberration of those who reason [0220] thus, let alone of those who attempt to say or write it.

⟦s17+18+19⟧ Besides, if the principle of ignorance and of will is the same, either the things that will by nature are altogether also ignorant, or the things that are ignorant by nature altogether also will. ⟦s17+18+19⟧ So then God too, willing according to nature, will fall into the passion of ignorance; and all inanimate things, ignorant according to nature, will be moved by a natural will. ⟦s20⟧ And if this is so, Christ himself too, who subsisted from Godhead and manhood, having, as they say, both the ignorance and the will according to appropriation, will surely have by nature, in respect of the divine will, the ignorance as well. ⟦s21⟧ And I keep silent about saying that, even if erroneously because of the appropriation, they were nevertheless shown to be advocates of that which they fought against, themselves also constituting two wills, the one according to nature, I mean, and the one according to appropriation; which are manifestly two, with ignorances equal in number; which is also absurd, to opine a twofold ignorance in Christ, who has not even one, but altogether makes every ignorance vanish, if indeed he is the wisdom and power of God. [n: I Cor. i, 24.]

⟦s22⟧ And what appropriation, then, do they mean? ⟦s23⟧ The essential, according to which each thing makes its own what belongs to it naturally, as having it through its nature; or the relational, according to which we naturally love and make our own one another's things, while we ourselves neither suffer nor operate any of them? ⟦s24⟧ But if the first, they rather declared the incarnate God a mere man, making a doctrine of him as naturally ignorant. ⟦s25⟧ But if the second, not a man at all, but only God without flesh, as having in a mere relation the things of the flesh, and not as making his own naturally the natural things of a man, together with being and remaining God. ⟦s26⟧ Since in the place where he puts ignorance, the teacher also clearly enumerates all the natural things of God incarnate: such as sleeping, hungering, thirsting, toiling, weeping, being in agony, shrinking back, the cross, death; and with these the rising too, and the taking-up, by which he also saved us, having willingly accepted these things according to nature for us, and suffered. ⟦s27⟧ If then they opine that these took place in Christ according to the mere appropriation in relation, and do not hold them to be natural to the incarnate Word as man, what is left for them to leave to Apollinarius and Manes? ⟦s28⟧ For they have surpassed Severus in impiety, shouting louder in blasphemy, since he did not rise up so much against the truth, [0221] but made use of the expression of the natural idioms, even if he sets aside the nature of the flesh, squeezing the different natures into one.

⟦s29+30+31⟧ For these things will follow those who mix the unmixable, and who thrust away and steal aside the mind of the Fathers, which sets all things distinctly in fitting order. ⟦s29+30+31⟧ Whence the all-praised and God-bearing teacher, in the second oration on the Son, goes through these things about ignorance, defining as by a rule and pattern, though these men at first brought them forward to us uninterpreted, snatching away the hearer: «Is it not plain to all, that he knows as God, but says he is ignorant as man, if one separates what appears from what is understood?» ⟦s29+30+31⟧ He lays down, then, that ignorance is to be taken only in the demarcation of the essences in conception; but that the natural wills, and everything else natural except sin alone, are to be known in the hypostatic union of the natures of the incarnate Word himself, unto the most proper confirmation of those from which and in which he is, and which he is.

⟦s32⟧ If then we contemplate ignorance in the demarcation in conception, it is not properly his; it is what is not ours either according to nature as constitutive, even if it is said to be ours because of the ancient transgression, such as ignorance, forsakenness, disobedience, insubordination. ⟦s33⟧ For what principle of nature is there for us in these, or what is constitutive of the essence? ⟦s34⟧ Wherefore we do not predicate these of Christ either, as being at all Christ's, nor anything else, as I said, of the things known in the demarcation in conception; and very reasonably. ⟦s35⟧ For if we do not know the incarnate Word in demarcation, nor imagine him in bare conception, but believe him to be in the utmost hypostatic union of the natures, it is clear that nothing of the things taken in division in conception is his at all; but those things alone we hold properly his, whatever are natural together with the nature, and are outside the defilement that comes by sin, being preserved really, in the unity of person and of his one hypostasis, and confessed by the pious.

⟦s35⟧ So then, if the will is not natural and constitutive of our essence, let them prove it. ⟦s36⟧ But if it is natural (for man is volitive by nature), either it too, along with the other things of the incarnate Word, both is and is known according to essence, as of one who became man and is volitive according to both the natures from which he is composed; or the other natural things too, along with it (I mean the will according to nature), will be known in the division in conception, barely, but not properly and really; and according to them the contemplation and initiation of the whole economy, or rather the incarnation itself, properly, will be taken by them according to division and imagination, [0224] nothing natural being confessed by them in Christ according to union, nor the nature itself at all. ⟦s37+38⟧ For where there is nothing natural, neither will there ever be a nature; just as, where properly there is the nature, there are the things according to it without omission, except sin alone. ⟦s39⟧ Wherefore no pretext at all will they have against the truth. ⟦s40⟧ For every conception, and through all things, has been taken away, so that they may, if indeed they should wish, gladly welcome the truth and sincerely confess with us, and not lean on the arguments, spun as from a spider, of their feeble understanding: with us, those who believe in him orthodoxly, that from what Christ is, in these Christ is; and in what Christ is, these Christ is. ⟦s41⟧ Christ, then, is God and man in the same; and he is both in Godhead and in manhood; and he was united both from Godhead and from manhood.
