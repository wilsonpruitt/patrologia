⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ OPUSCULUM 20 — DOGMATIC TOME TO MARINUS THE PRESBYTER

⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ *Of Maximus, the most holy monk and confessor.*

⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ Those whose reason is adorned with virtues have their mind illumined with graces of knowledge, which remove it from all forgetfulness, as those [virtues] remove the reason from all deceit, and which lead it to initiation into the hidden so far as they lead it back to the imitation of him who was manifested for our sake. ⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ This thou hast shown forth in the Holy Spirit, in the things in which thou thyself, contending with diligence in holy manners and holier practices, God-honoured Father, surpassest praise as far as thou art able to bring vigour to bear: making the stretching-forth the end of the course, and the ever-moving ascent to the Word the end of reason, so that thou mayest pass beyond nature, and cast away all types, and converse purely with the most pure, according to the withdrawal of thyself from all things and toward all, and the perfect passing-over in relation; which procures the unbounded good flow by grace, a flow that circumscribes and passes beyond everything definable, and that [0229] prepares the one who ascends with him from glory to glory practically through virtue, and passes through the heavens from knowledge to knowledge scientifically, [n: Hebr. iv, 14.] and in the hidden-mystery and most-voiced silence, in the failing of all intellectual operation, meets unknowingly with the Father of spirits, [n: Hebr. xii, 9.] to advance tenaciously into the Holy of holies, where Jesus, he who is above us, entered as forerunner on our behalf in what is ours.

⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ To him, having been led up above nature by grace and by the blessed zeal for sacred rites, thou leadest up those like me who lie below, stretching out a hand with compassion through thy letter, and a word through the meaning hidden in it; and persuading [us] by zeal to withdraw from the things that remove [us] from God, and, without relation, [to honour, to practise, to prefer] from the whole heart only the love that is his and that unites to him; according to which, as simple and formless, thou exhortest [us] to beat off all doubleness, and the covering of figure both of the understanding itself and of what appears, and to achieve the virtue that is truly blessed and reveals the blessed things; which may I be able to accomplish, supported by thy most holy prayers, having put off the sin that so easily besets. [n: Ibid. 2.]

⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ But since, rousing my thought again to work and wishing to cleanse the capacity of my soul of all ignorance, thou hast bidden me search out the discourse against the Diaetetes of the most divine and great Teacher of the holy Church of the Theopolitans, which is the same as to say, of the whole inhabited world, who bears the name of the resurrection—or rather, to speak properly, against the Diaeretes, inasmuch as he separates entirely from God and from us, through utter conversion and commixture, God who became man for our sake, the offspring consubstantial with the Father, our Lord Jesus Christ—how he says the operation in him is one, although he teaches the operations as natural: I put forward the man himself as the wise interpreter and teacher of this phrase as his own, who defines it to be nothing other than the indissoluble union of the innate operations, and the result from them—I mean the work and the action—as being indicative and declarative of them; [an action] which has shared in the name of operation, if anything has, since it is properly something both particular and universal. ⟦s1+2+3+4⟧ For inasmuch as it is produced from the essential operation that is according to natural property, it holds the rank of a particular; just as, again, that [operation], producing [it], [holds the rank] of a universal. ⟦s5⟧ For it is natural that specific things share in the name of the generic, and that universal things be predicated of the [0232] existence of the particulars, yet not share their name, lest through this they be made specific somehow and reckoned in the rank of a particular.

⟦s6⟧ So the teacher, naming the result, as I said, of the two innate operations themselves, that is, the action, from the name that belongs to them, since it has gathered both together according to their union, called it one operation, because nothing divine or human is accomplished separately, but is produced from one and the same at once by growing-together and in a united way, according to the single interpenetration in these. ⟦s6⟧ Yet he did not for this reason call his essential operation one according to natural property; just as [he did not call his] essence and nature one on account of the very singleness of the one person, [an essence] sharing in neither of those from which he was composed. ⟦s7⟧ For even in the union of the natures the difference according to essence is preserved, preserving properly together with itself that also of the things that belong essentially to the natures.

⟦s8⟧ And declaring this in the very discourse he laboured over against the Diaetetes, after first demarcating very scientifically concerning natural property, which is fitly disposed toward operation, and concerning operation itself, which proceeds from the fitness into work and action, he goes through it thus: «Therefore one operation in Christ»—and he adds the cause, because nothing divine or human is accomplished separately—«we too say.» ⟦s9⟧ And when he had shown in this way the principle of the union, adding that of the essential difference, he says: «But not his property one, God forbid; for the property of Godhead and of manhood is not the same»—that is, the property fitly disposed toward natural operation, according to him. ⟦s10⟧ And going on again: «And otherwise, to say the same universally: that the operation of any natures whatever that have come together is one, by the communion, namely, of the union and of its completion, is wholly necessary; but to call their property one, without confusion—perhaps even in those where room for confusion is altogether inconceivable.»

⟦s11⟧ So the Father has most plainly made clear his own meaning by his own words, saying that the operation in Christ is not one essential [operation], lest confusion and mixing be brought upon his parts; nor hypostatic at all, lest separation and distance of the extremes happen to him—I mean of the Father without beginning and of the undefiled mother. ⟦s12⟧ For he is clearly distinguished from them by hypostasis and by the hypostatic [marks]. ⟦s13⟧ But what does he say? ⟦s14⟧ One by the communion of the union and of its completion, which he defined above as work and action; giving no entry of any kind either to those who wish to commix or to those who wish to separate; but driving off the former by making known what is principally and according to nature proper to his manhood, and what to the superessential Godhead; and routing the latter by setting apart or separating no operation in the one and the same.

⟦s15⟧ For the name of operation, taken in common of simple movement alike and [0233] of movement considered in relation, or of the result itself, brings no mixing upon the realities, so long as the clarification of the things understood is made distinct; by which we keep without division the things of either of the natures that have essentially run together, and recognize without confusion the things proper to the one as [the things] of the other, because of the union, and [the things] of both as [the things] of the whole from them, because of [its] existence; neither commixing [them] as to this [whole], nor separating [it] as to those [natures]; but holding the difference by the definitions of the essences, and binding tight the union by the principles of one and the same hypostasis. ⟦s16⟧ Thus then, and for these reasons, this man, and every other approved and divine Father, if there is any, [says], as I seem already to have written, that the operation in Christ is one and two: the one, looking to the union of the natural operations, as also of the natures; the other, to their essential difference. ⟦s17+18+19+20⟧ So much for this.

⟦s17+18+19+20⟧ As for the interpretation of the passage of Gregory, the theologian and great herald of the [truth of the] Church, the passage that says «the word is formed from the man, not from him who is understood in the Saviour; for his willing is not even opposed to God, being wholly deified», I determine it to depend on a very pious understanding, for which and out of which the industrious man laboured, but to be lacking a little in precision of wording; against which those who are facile and gross in everything contrive to make raids, or, to say it more truly, onslaughts. ⟦s17+18+19+20⟧ And nothing is so earnestly sought by them as to search out and find fault with even the things very securely guarded on every side and opening no room to them against the account; let alone when they have seized from somewhere upon a bare word, and that one put forward from an unfeigned and pure understanding: that this God-possessed Father, for confirming and establishing the essential and natural will of the ensouled flesh assumed from us (that is, of the human [element] in the Saviour), says: «For his willing is wise and circumspect», from which its essential difference from his will that is by nature divine and paternal is shown, a difference allowing no confusion at all to grow upon the mystery of Christ.

⟦s17+18+19+20⟧ But to pronounce *wholly deified* with paroxytone accent, as from certain copies, and not rather with oxytone, for fear lest the one will be brought in by the opponents, gives a handle against us, as though we were hinting at a relational and hypostatic union, as it were by grace and worth, in that the saints too are [0236] principally moved and operated by God, because of their whole convergence and disposition toward God and divine things. ⟦s17+18+19+20⟧ For the oxytone expression of *deified* neither brings into identity with the essential and natural will the willing of the Saviour as man (for who is able to show it?), and it presents the utmost union and growing-together. ⟦s21⟧ For *deified*, being among the relatives, as also *ignited* and *illumined* and all such, by all means brings in with itself also that to which it has the reference of its relation, that is, the deifying, and the igniting, and the illumining; so that the principle both of the difference and of the utmost union is made firm no more from that side than from this. ⟦s22⟧ For not to be opposed is not sufficient for union; for everything that is natural and blameless is not contrary, but neither is it by all means also united to God. ⟦s23⟧ But what is deified is united in every way and wholly, and in no way displaces the essential difference, inasmuch as it is unconfused in the union.

⟦s24⟧ But if someone will say: "And if nothing natural is opposed, how is it that the Father, making mention of our innate will—if indeed it was of that, and not perhaps of some other—did not say that it follows God by all means, but that it is «resisting for the most part and wrestling against»? ⟦s25⟧ For either it is not natural, as being adverse, or it is not adverse, as being natural. ⟦s26+27+28+29⟧ And then it stands as other in natural quality from the willing of the human [element] in the Saviour, if this is in no way [opposed], but that is opposed": we say that insofar as it is natural, it is not opposed; but insofar as it is not moved naturally by us, it is clearly opposed, and resisting for the most part, whereupon sinning also follows. ⟦s26+27+28+29⟧ For it is in the mode of the movement according to misuse, and not in the principle of the power according to nature, that what is contrary to reason and law subsists; since, when it is shaped and moved suitably, even if it does not have being-united to God, it has at least concurring and not resisting. ⟦s26+27+28+29⟧ For as there is no principle in nature of what is above nature, so neither of what is contrary to nature and in revolt. ⟦s30⟧ Hence the teacher did not declare that it does not follow in every way and wholly, but tempered it, saying: «not by all means», and «for the most part»; to which is understood "sometimes, and seldom", because of the difficulty the many have in being led up to virtue.

⟦s31⟧ For the willing of the human [element] in the Saviour, even if it was natural, was yet not bare as ours is, just as neither was the human itself, being deified above us to the uttermost by the union, upon which sinlessness also properly depends. ⟦s32⟧ But ours is manifestly bare and in no way sinless, because of the swerving that happens this way and that: a swerving that does not alter nature, but turns aside movement, or rather, to say it more truly, exchanges its mode. ⟦s33⟧ And this is plain from our doing many things irrationally, and yet in no way passing over into an irrational essence from the innate rational [essence] that is in us.

⟦s33⟧ [0237] So the human [element] in us is not one thing and that in the Saviour another; nor is the willing other, according to the principle of nature at least, even if [it is] otherwise [in him], above us; for in a divine way he subsisted, and it was shaped, through the utmost union with the divine. ⟦s34⟧ But again, to fit, quite suitably indeed, each of the things in us that are blamed—such as opposition, or opposed array, and whatever belongs to this list—even if, separating the natures inseparably in conception, they go on to the next nature: yet to take the manhood of Christ along with them in these is not lawful. ⟦s35+36⟧ For if not even in us are they considered as suitable [to nature], but as contrary to nature and reason, how can one attach them to that [manhood] even by conception or by after-conception, so to speak? ⟦s35+36⟧ But only by appropriation, out of pity, as to the head of the whole body, just as to a physician [are attributed] the passions of the sick man, until God, who became man for our sake, frees us from them; yet consuming and wiping them out of us completely by the power of his embodiment.

⟦s37⟧ For the account of passions is twofold: one of penalty, the other of dishonour; and the one characterizes our nature, the other counterfeits it throughout. ⟦s38⟧ The former, then, he accepted essentially, willing as man for our sake, at once confirming the nature and loosing the condemnation against us; the latter, again, as lover of man, he appropriated by way of economy, it being known in us and in our insubordinate mode, so that, as fire wax, or as the sun the vapour of the earth, consuming it utterly out of us, he might make the communication of his own things, and prepare us to be dispassionate from now, and incorruptible according to the promise. ⟦s39⟧ We must, then, welcome, as is fitting, above all the zeal and at the same time the labour of him who has been earnest in such things for piety's sake, but urge him toward a more exact diligence with the words of the Fathers, because of the abusers of good things, who out of excessive folly do not concede even that which they could not destroy even if they wished.

⟦s40⟧ As for Honorius, the pope of the [Church] of the Romans, I do not think that in the letter written to Sergius he speaks against the duality of the innate wills in Christ by saying *one will*; rather he speaks for it and, as is likely, establishes it, saying this not to the setting-aside of the human and natural will of the Saviour, but [meaning] that no will of the flesh or passionate thought in any way went before his seedless conception and incorrupt birth. ⟦s41⟧ For only the divine and paternal volition, through the only-begotten Son, who himself worked his own incarnation, and by the concurrence of the Holy Spirit, wrought it. ⟦s42⟧ And that he holds to this meaning is plain from this. ⟦s43⟧ For having said that because of the ineffable
