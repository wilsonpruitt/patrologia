⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [0240] union of the human and divine nature, both God is said to have suffered, and manhood to have come down from heaven with the Godhead, and having in this way shown the utmost exchange, by interchange, of the things that belong naturally to each nature of the one Christ and Son, he adds, saying: «Whence also we confess one will of our Lord Jesus Christ.» ⟦s2⟧ How, he says? ⟦s3⟧ «Since manifestly our nature, not sin, was assumed by the Godhead»—that is, not from sin; all but speaking in unison with the great Athanasius, who writes these things against the impious Apollinarius: «He was born of a woman, raising up for himself the form of man from the first moulding, in a display of flesh without carnal wills and human thoughts, in an image of newness; for the volition is of the Godhead only, since the whole nature also is of the Godhead.» [n: Galat. iv, 4.]

⟦s4⟧ For since the procession of the Word's birth according to the flesh for our sake came about above us—for no passionate volition or thought of flesh went before it, as is seen in our case, because of the pleasure that has prevailed over the race since the deceit, but the volition of the Godhead only, through the Son, who, as I said, himself worked his own embodiment by the good pleasure of the Father and the co-operation of the all-holy Spirit, making new in himself and through himself the mode of birth brought in upon the nature, and making his own conception without seed from the holy Theotokos and Ever-virgin Mary—considering this ineffable principle of his birth, the one said «only the volition of the Godhead in him», the other «one will of the Lord Jesus Christ, since manifestly, he says, our nature, and not sin, was assumed by the Godhead: without carnal wills and human thoughts», as the divine Athanasius says; not, however, [meaning] that he did not also have, as man, together with being God by nature, a human and natural will, just as also [he had] the divine and paternal.

⟦s5⟧ And he hints at the same thing in what follows, saying: «He was conceived without sin of the Holy Spirit and of the holy, undefiled and Ever-virgin Theotokos Mary, and without defilement was born of her according to the flesh.» ⟦s6⟧ And he brings forward the divine Scripture making mention of the flesh [0241] both in praise and in blame, not suggesting, God forbid, that we understand the Lord's flesh to be other than ours in nature and in essence—he who knew that it was assumed from our essence, that is, from the all-holy womb of the Ever-virgin and Mother of God, connatural with us—but other in respect of sinning, and in having in no way arrayed against [it], as we [have] in our members, the law from transgression against the law of the spirit. ⟦s7⟧ «For the flesh corrupted by sin, which wars against the law of his mind, was not assumed by the Saviour,» he says. ⟦s8⟧ For of him whose birth the law according to sin, through seed, did not precede, of him that law is not present at all in the members either; but [there is] a law of divine righteousness, shown forth to us as a pattern, and wholly wiping out the [law] brought in upon the nature from transgression. ⟦s9⟧ For the sinless one came, he says, to seek and to save what was lost, [n: Luc. xix, 10.] that is, the nature of the human race that had sinned. ⟦s10⟧ «For there was no other law in his members, or will different from or contrary to the Father.» ⟦s11⟧ Showing by this, not that he did not have a human and natural will—for he does not appear to have said this—but that as man he possessed neither in the body, through the members, any operation contrary to nature whatever, nor in the soul any contrary or irrational movement of will, as we do, «since he was also born above the law of human nature.»

⟦s12+13⟧ And he presents it more clearly in what follows, that his account was to mark off from the Saviour only the passionate will, and not the natural; and that, even if in his natural and human [will] he concurred with the paternal and divine, having no difference from it arising from counter-action, yet, giving himself to us as a pattern, he willingly subjected his own, and commended the paternal, so that we too, imitating him and setting aside our own, may fulfil the divine with all zeal; saying thus: «Even though it is written, I came not to do my own will, but [the will] of the Father who sent me; and, Not what I will, but whatever thou [wilt], Father: [n: Joan. vi, 38.] these are not of a different will»—that is, of a contrary and counter-acting [will]—«but of the human economy assumed», [an economy] making our things its own with compassion. ⟦s12+13⟧ «For he said these things for our sake, to whom the Teacher of piety has given an example, that we may follow in his footsteps; [n: I Petr. ii, 21.] and that each of us may prefer in all things not his own will, but rather the Lord's.» ⟦s14⟧ So, as I said, he makes no destruction of the natural and human will, but of the passionate one and that contrary to nature; and, to speak in sum, [0244] he attests that God, incarnate for our sake, is free from all sin in what is ours.

⟦s15⟧ And to put it briefly: by *the one will* I think he signifies that the divine volition alone went before his birth according to the flesh; and by «there is no difference of will», that he did not have the contrary or counter-acting [will], but that which concurs throughout and is united. ⟦s16+17+18⟧ Hence, when he says that our nature was assumed by the Godhead, he makes mention of one will; but when he sets forth in his discourse the saying *I came not to do my own will*, dropping the number he says: «These are not of a different will»—that is, of a contrary and opposed [will]—from which it is plainly concluded that there are two wills according to nature in the Saviour. ⟦s16+17+18⟧ For if he did not have a contrary [will], he had a natural one as man. ⟦s16+17+18⟧ For what is not contrary is wholly natural, and no one will gainsay it; for nothing in nature or in the things according to nature is contrary at all. ⟦s16+17+18⟧ It is likely, then, that they [?] lay upon him what he did not properly write in his own words, and that those of the opposing side contrive to make the man's writings a covering for their own opinion, since it is not the best, misinterpreting them in another [sense] against his aim. ⟦s16+17+18⟧ For he has his account speaking in his defence, driving off every onslaught of the abuser.

⟦s19⟧ And so I take his mind to stand, pure of all suspicion. ⟦s20⟧ And the most holy presbyter, the lord abba Anastasius, returning from elder Rome, has made it surer for me: a man adorned, if any other is, with divine virtue and prudence. ⟦s20⟧ And he said that much discussion had been raised by him with the most holy men of the great Church there, on account of the letter written by them to Sergius, asking for what reason and how *the one will* was set in it; and he found them grieved at this, and making defence; and besides them the one who dictated it in Latin at his bidding, the lord abba John, a most holy fellow-labourer, maintaining that they had in no way made mention in it of one will by number at all, even if this has now been fabricated by those who translated it into the Greek tongue. ⟦s21⟧ Nor [had they intended] any annulment or [0245] expulsion of the natural will of the Saviour as to the human, but the complete removal and destruction of the [will] that is ours and blameworthy, by which also the war of those of one race against one another is set up, wishing to show that the assumed flesh is pure of all sin, according to the tradition of the most sacred oracles and of the Fathers' teachings. ⟦s22⟧ And by such words they appear somehow in accord with what was just now explained by my nothingness, and as it were setting a seal on the defence made on behalf of Honorius.

⟦s23⟧ So, learning that they had made this defence, I greatly admired their exactness, just as I was also struck at the villainy of those who dare everything for the one purpose of being impious to no end, and who wish, as is their custom of old and now, by certain pilferings and misinterpretations, to draw over to themselves, against all likelihood, those who contend ardently against them, and to usurp their mind, which in no way follows them. ⟦s24⟧ Of necessity, then, having learned this too, I have made it known to thee, God-honoured Father, so that, fortified on every side, thou mayest have the means to beat off the phalanxes of the opponents, striking vigorously with reason and overcoming mightily by faith, having from this the glory of the Only-begotten for a proclamation, and for a diadem his communion and union by grace.

⟦s25⟧ But, O most sacred head, and honoured by me, make these things known to him who presides hierarchically over our blameless and orthodox faith, under whose wings all of us, both those near and those far off, shall rest in holiness, having as the sole foundation of holier doctrines his blessed illumination, his own and [given] through him, by which we are led by the hand and led up to the shadowless and paternal light in the Holy Spirit; looking to him as the author of our salvation, after him who is so by nature and first, and directing our paths piously, hastening to the life that is not dissolved by corruption but constituted by incorruption; in which, as here we share in hope through his God-possessed prayers and God-wise teachings, so there too may we be counted worthy to share in experience through his divine-working mediation and leading-in, according to the abiding and perfecting in the Word himself and God, our Lord Jesus Christ.
