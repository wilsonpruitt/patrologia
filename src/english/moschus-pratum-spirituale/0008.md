⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ CHAPTER 65

⟦s1⟧ Abba John, surnamed Molybas, told us about the elder — I mean abba Stephen — saying that he was gravely ill with sickness and disease, and the doctors compelled him to take meat. ⟦s2⟧ Now this blessed man had a brother, a layman, very devout and living according to God. ⟦s3⟧ And it happened, while the priest was eating the meat, that his brother the layman came; and when he saw him he was scandalized and much grieved, because, he said, after so much ascesis and self-control he had taken meat at his end. ⟦s4⟧ And at once he was in an ecstasy, and he saw someone saying to him, "Why wast thou scandalized at the priest because thou sawest him eating meat? ⟦s5⟧ Dost thou not know that he took it out of necessity and out of obedience? ⟦s6⟧ Truly thou oughtest not to have been scandalized. ⟦s7⟧ But if thou wouldst see in what glory thy brother is, turn round behind thee, and look." ⟦s8⟧ And turning round, he said, he saw the priest crucified, as Christ was; and the one who had appeared, he said, said, "See in what glory he is. ⟦s9⟧ Glorify, then, him who glorifies those who love him in truth."

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [2917] CHAPTER 66

⟦s1⟧ Abba Antony, the superior of the laura of the Aeliotes, who built it, told us about abba Theodosius the Solitary, saying: "Before I took up stillness," as he used to tell it, "I was in an ecstasy and saw a young man whose form was beyond the sun. ⟦s2⟧ And he took me by the hand," he said, "and said to me, 'Come, for thou hast to wrestle.' And he brought me into a theatre whose size I cannot tell. ⟦s3⟧ And I saw the theatre filled with men, one part of them dressed in white, the other Ethiopians. ⟦s4⟧ So when he had brought me into the arena of the theatre, I saw an Ethiopian man, huge beyond measure, whose head was up among the clouds, powerful and hideous. ⟦s5+6⟧ Then the young man who had appeared to me said to me, 'With this one thou hast to wrestle.' And when I saw the man I was afraid and began to tremble, and to be dismayed, and to beg the one who was leading me, saying, 'Who that has the mortal nature of men can wrestle with this one?' [?] 'Go in, then, with all eagerness. ⟦s7+8⟧ For as soon as thou comest to grips, I will judge the bout, and give thee the crown of victory.' So when I went into the arena, and we began to grapple with each other, at once the good umpire came, gave his award, and handed me a crown. ⟦s9⟧ And the crowd of the Ethiopians howled and vanished. ⟦s10⟧ But the other part, those in white, sent up praises to him who had granted and given me the fair victory."

⟦s1+s2⟧ CHAPTER 69

⟦s1+s2⟧ We visited in Alexandria, I and the lord Sophronius the sophist, before he renounced the world, abba Palladius, a God-loving man and a servant of God, who had his monastery at the Lithazomenon; and we begged him to tell us a word of profit. And the elder began to say to us: "Children, the time is now short. Let us strive here a little and toil, so that we may enjoy great good things for ever. Look at the martyrs, look at the holy ones, look at the ascetics, how they endured like brave men. Time past knows them, but we always marvel at their endurance. And each of those who hear bears witness, I suppose, with great amazement, to the patience of the blessed martyrs, which is beyond all the nature of men: how some had their eyes gouged out, and others their legs cut off; and some were maimed in their hands, others in their feet; and how fire consumed some at once, and others slowly; and how rivers drowned some, and the sea others; and how savage beasts tore some apart alive like criminals, while others, after dying first by novel devices, birds of prey devoured. And in a word, if one were to tell all the kinds of evils devised against them, all that the enemy demon brought [b: C] against the God-loving martyrs and ascetics: how they endured, how they strove, overcoming the weakness of the body by the courage of the soul, and having expected the good things they hoped for to be worth more than their present toils, and receiving them. This showed their faith firm on both counts: on the one, that having toiled a little they enjoy great good things for ever; on the other, that they bravely endured the strange torments of the body which our enemy the devil brought on them. So if we endure afflictions and hold fast with God, we are found God-lovers indeed, and God will strive alongside us, and lighten our toils for us all the better. So, children, knowing what sort of work the time calls for, let us come to know ourselves through stillness. For in this time we must make use of a good repentance, so that we may be called temples of God. [2921] [b: A] For it is no ordinary honour that comes to us in the age to come."

⟦s1+s2⟧ He said again: "Let us remember him who had nowhere to lay his head." The elder said again: "Affliction, according to Paul, works patience; so let us make our mind able to receive the kingdom of heaven." He said again: "Children, let us not love the world, nor the things in the world."

⟦s1+s2⟧ The elder said again: "Let us guard our thoughts, which is the medicine of salvation."

⟦s1+s2⟧ The same abba Palladius was asked by us: [b: B] "Do us a kindness, Father: tell us from where, and from what thoughts, thou camest to the monastic life." The elder was a Thessalonian, and he told us, saying: "In my country, outside the wall of the city, about three stades off, there was a recluse, a Mesopotamian by race, named David, very virtuous and merciful and self-controlled. He spent about seventy years in his enclosure. Now since, because of the barbarians, the walls of the city were guarded at night by the soldiers, those who guarded the wall on that side, the side where the elder's enclosure was, saw one night fire coming out through all the windows of the recluse's cell. So the soldiers thought the barbarians had set fire to the elder's cell. When morning came the soldiers went out and found the elder unharmed and the cell undamaged, and they were amazed. Again on the following night they saw the same fire at the elder's cell; and indeed it appeared for a long time. This was plain to the whole city and that country, so that many kept vigil on the wall at night for the sake of seeing the fire. For it went on happening until the elder's death. Having seen this wonder not once, nor twice only, but many times, I said to myself: If in this world God grants such glory to his servants, [2924] [b: A] what glory then in the age to come, when their faces will shine like the sun? This, children, was the reason for my coming to this monastic habit."

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ The elder told us this too: that with abba David there came another monk too, named Adolas, a Mesopotamian as well; and he shut himself up on the other side [?] of the city, in the hollow foot of a plane tree. ⟦s1+2⟧ He made a small window in it, through which he would meet those who came to him. ⟦s3⟧ So when the barbarians came and plundered the whole country, it happened that they passed through that place. ⟦s3⟧ And one of the barbarians saw the elder peering out, drew his sword, and raised his hand to strike him; and he stayed with his hand stretched out and unmoving. ⟦s4⟧ When the rest of the barbarians saw this they marvelled, and they fell down before the elder and begged him. ⟦s5⟧ And the elder made a prayer and healed him, and so sent them away in peace.

⟦s5⟧ CHAPTER 71

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1+s2+s3+s4+s5+s6+s7+s8+s9⟧ The same abba Palladius told us again, saying: "In the Arsinoite (it is a city of the Thebaid) a man was arrested for committing a murder, and after being tortured a great deal he received sentence to be beheaded. So as he was being led out of the city six miles (for that was where he had committed the murder), a monk followed behind him, as though he had to see how he was beheaded. On the road, as he was going to be beheaded, he saw the monk following and said to him, 'Truly, lord abba, hast thou no cell or handiwork?' The monk answered, saying, 'Truly, brother, I have both a cell and handiwork.' He answered and said to him, 'Then why dost thou not sit in thy cell and weep for thy sins?' The monk said to him, 'Yes, brother, I neglect myself badly; and that is why I am coming to watch how thou diest, so that even so I may come to compunction.' Then that man said to him, 'Go, lord abba, sit in thy cell, and give thanks to God who saved us. For since he became man and died for us, man no longer dies the eternal death.'"
