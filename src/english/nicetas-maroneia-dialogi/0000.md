[0169] PROEM. The aim of the discourse is an inquiry and joint examination concerning the procession of the Holy Spirit, and a resolution of the ambiguity between us and the Latins over this question, looking toward the removal of the estrangement between the two that has arisen from it. Its character is dialogic, befitting the fine-grained treatment of questions that require more exact joint consideration, and most fitted of all to the joint examination of ecclesiastical doctrines, and for the finding of the truth most useful and most necessary to those who have no wish to wrangle; wherefore some of the great teachers also made use of this form of discourse. The persons of the dialogue are the Latin and the Greek; and the speech of each of the two is fashioned equally poised and equally matched: partly on account of the underlying subject matter, since the question has never before been examined and decided synodically, and hence has no small force on either side; and partly [0172] because it belongs to the character of the Latin not to endure seeming to have the weaker argument, but to yearn, as it were, to conquer, and where victory is not easy for him, to carry his neck high even so, as one exulting in his speech; and because he is lavish in ambitiously displaying his prowess in argument, lest he seem to fall short of the Greek in wisdom; and partly because the Greek in his turn, for a display of modesty, willingly yields on many points, ordering his words with the more equitable judgment and discernment, and showing that his discourse is not aimed at strife, but that his zeal is for truth; and he imitates those skilled in the wrestling-schools, who have often defeated their opponents by feigning to sink at the knees and to be on the very point of falling, and, by pretending to be entangled, have rather entangled and thrown them.

Yet at the first each sets out his own principles: and the Greek attempts to lead the Latin's argument into absurdity, and through many seeming absurdities undertakes to show that his opinion is absurd; while the other hastens to dissolve the absurdities, and shows either that the things alleged are not absurd, or that they do not follow from his own doctrine. Then both plunge into the picturing of the matter through examples; for this manner of discourse too is customary with the theologians: and the one from images and analogy points toward the things of the Spirit's procession; the other finds fault with some of the images and sets against them others supposedly more apposite; and the Latin in his turn imitates the Greek, and finds fault with some, and shows his own rather to be the more natural. Upon this they make their discourse still more aporetic, and pose difficulties to one another in return, being unable to resolve the impasses, and showing that the difficulties on either side arise not through any absurdity or implausibility or impossibility in the problem (for then they would not arise on both sides), but through the surpassing nature of the thing theologized; and at the last they grapple with one another through testimonies, the one bringing forward these and the other those, as though they were in conflict, and they present the contrary voices of the holy teachers as the cause of their strife and estrangement from one another. Then they bring the testimonies into accord; and so they too themselves, as now minding one and the same thing, are thereafter persuaded each by the other: the one having learned from the holy Fathers that it must of necessity be that "from the Son" no longer be pronounced with the holy creed; the other persuaded no longer to call him heterodox, since the preposition ἐξ among the Latins and the διά among us holds no difference in respect of the Son.

1. GR. Come then, O Latin: casting far from ourselves the contentious and quarrelsome spirit as a hindrance to the finding of the truth, let us take counsel together, with the Holy Spirit, upon the question of the procession of the Holy Spirit, over which we are also divided. For it is needful, if we would be true worshippers and adorers of the Holy Spirit, both in all other things to call upon the Holy Spirit itself and to have it as guide toward the finding and grasping of the truth, and most of all in the discourses that concern this very One — both because the difficulty is greater in inquiries concerning God, and the Spirit is true God, worshipped together with Father and Son as of one nature with them and glorified with them; and because, knowing the things that are its own — itself, and it alone, with Father and Son — and searching the depths of God, it knows how to unveil them to whom, and when, and in what measure is needful. But this shall not be for those who choose to gainsay contentiously; for far from contentious gainsayings is the illumination that comes from the Spirit.

LAT. Come, O Greek — in love of truth, which is the same as to say, in love of God; for God is true, and God is truth, and from God is the truth for all. For I long for the inquiry into these things, and have long been in travail with this joint examination. And may the Paraclete come to us, the Spirit of truth, and prosper the discourse, turning the crooked into straight, as an upright Spirit being renewed in the inward parts of the faithful and filling up the wanting ravines of knowledge, and making the rough ways smooth for us.

GR. Common now to us both is the prayer, and one the aim: the seeking and finding of the truth in the consideration set before us. It remains to begin the undertaking.

LAT. But first let us treat briefly, as in a preliminary survey, of the things held and spoken as doctrine among us concerning the holy Trinity; for these will perhaps carry no small weight toward the prospering of the discourse, and the more so if they should be confessed by us in common; for in this way both what accords with our common confessions and what is discordant will be the more easily discerned in the discourses that follow. For what is said must agree with the principles; and what agrees, this [0176] is to be reckoned genuine, but what is discordant, to be dismissed as spurious and contrary.

GR. You say well; for this belongs to the best order, and contributes not a little to the discernment of the truth. For what follows from true principles and premises is assuredly itself also true; but what conflicts with them and opposes them is, on the contrary, false. And so let it be done.

2. LAT. Tell me, then: do you say that there are certain essential and natural things in the Trinity?

GR. I do; for there are both essence and nature, even though its being is above essence and nature.

LAT. And do you hold essence and nature to be the same, or different?

GR. The same altogether; for so the holy Fathers teach.

LAT. And what then? Do you not say that certain hypostatic and personal things also exist in the Trinity?

GR. These too I affirm; for there are both hypostases and persons, even though the holy Trinity is nameless and beyond name; for above every thing and every thought and every name is the existence above all existence of the super-divine Godhead — or, to speak more properly, super-existence; yet nonetheless the one and selfsame and unvarying Godhead of the holy Trinity is called both essence and nature; and again, the Father and the Son and the Spirit are hypostases and persons of the Godhead.

LAT. And do you say that these and those are the same, or different?

GR. Which do you mean? Say on.

LAT. Are essence and hypostasis the same, or different? And are nature and person the same, or not the same?
