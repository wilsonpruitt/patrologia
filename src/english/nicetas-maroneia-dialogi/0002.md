For the Father, being unbegotten, begets the Son; and in begetting the Son, through the mediation of the Son he at the same time also puts forth the Spirit; and the Son, being begotten, also puts forth the Spirit; and the Spirit again, proceeding mediately from the Father — inasmuch as the Son mediates in the procession — at the same time proceeds also from the Son, no longer through any other mediating. So that we lay down as proper to the Father the unbegotten, the generative, and the mediately putting-forth: the unbegotten, because he is not from anyone; the generative, because he begets the Son; the mediately putting-forth, because through the mediation of the Son he puts forth the Spirit also. And to the Son, the begotten and the immediately putting-forth: the one because he himself is begotten from the Father, the other because immediately from himself he sends forth the Spirit, being himself intermediate between the Father and the Spirit. And to the Spirit, the proceeding mediately from the Father, and proceeding immediately also from the Son, inasmuch as the Son is contemplated between the Father and the Spirit, but between the Son and the Spirit no one, at least as regards the going-forth. And these are the properties of each of the three, holding untransposably and unmovably and unalterably [0184] and unchangeably.

As, then, it is not because the Father has not been begotten that he therefore neither begets nor puts forth — but both those things and these belong to him, and he is both unbegotten and also begetter and putter-forth, the one immediately from himself, the other mediately through the Son — so also the Son: it is not because he has been begotten that he therefore does not also put forth the Spirit. And the Spirit: it is not because it proceeds from the Father that it therefore does not also proceed of the Son; but its procession is immediate from the Son, and mediate from the Father. At all events the property is kept for each person unmoved and unconfounded; thus is the Trinity in a Monad, and undivided the Monad in a Trinity. For the Father and the Son and the Spirit are both a Monad and a Trinity: the one according to the nature of the Godhead, which is one and the same and undivided and unalterable; the other on account of the properties, which hold unmovably, as we said just above.

But the properties being kept unchangeable for each, the Spirit could not be said to proceed from itself, because this is not a property of the Spirit; but proceeding from the Father through the Son, it proceeds also from the Son, yet not through any other. And where is the confusion of Sabellius? For if we said, after him, that the Father and the Son, or the Spirit as well, are one, as one hypostasis or person, then in asserting that what proceeds from the Father proceeds also from the Son we should be of one mind with Sabellius, or altogether half-Sabellians, just as certain others are half-Arians. For in saying that the three are one hypostasis and one, thus after Sabellius, we should be making the Spirit proceed from the Father and from the Son no more than from itself; but also the Father as begetting and being begotten and proceeding, and each of these no more from another than from himself; and the Son not only as begotten but also as proceeding, and besides these as begetting too; and the Spirit again not only as proceeding, but also as begetting and being begotten. For such are the drivellings of Sabellius, who assigns to one and the same person different names and different properties.

And if we asserted that the Father and the Son alone are one person, and laid it down as doctrine that from this one the Spirit comes forth, in this way too we should be half-Sabellians, saying that what proceeds proceeds from the Father and from the Son. But as it is, we, holding the Father to be one hypostasis of the Godhead and the Son another — of the same nature, that is — and the Spirit likewise another, and then laying down that what proceeds from the Father proceeds also from the Son, say it otherwise.

Those, then, who say that the Father and the Son are one person, and then assert that the Spirit is proceeding from the Father and from the Son, have the absurdity following upon them: not because they say simply "from the Father and from the Son," but because they say it as of one from both; and because they lay down that both, the Father and the Son, are one, not according to the force of the Lord's utterance, but otherwise than as he says: I and the Father are one. But we, saying that the Spirit is both from the Father and from the Son, do not say this [0185] as from one, but as coming forth, of one nature, from two persons of one nature, in a certain manner unknown and unspeakable. To say, then, that the Spirit is from both as from one is impious, for it is Sabellian; but to say that it is from both, which have not their difference in essence nor their distinction in a doubleness of names, but in hypostatic properties, is far from the Sabellian impiety. So that, since such a doctrine is other than the opinion of Sabellius, do not account it Sabellian and to be cast away. But rather prove that the absurdity stands otherwise.

5. GR. Let these things be, and let it not be Sabellian to say that the procession of the all-holy Spirit is from the Father and the Son; but that at any rate is absurd. For if it is from both, then the property of its hypostasis is henceforth composite and not simple, and the hypostasis is composite, because it proceeds both immediately and mediately, the one from the Father, the other from the Son. And thus the property of the Son too is found composite, having not only the being begotten from the Father, but also the putting forth of the Spirit from himself immediately, as being intermediate.

LAT. By this reasoning there would be composition in the Father also, and the more so, because he is not unbegotten only, but also begetter and putter-forth. But if in the Father's case these things did not introduce composition, neither then will those in the case of the Son and of the Spirit. Or what is this arbitrary allotment, that the Father is not composite out of the unbegotten and the generative and the putting-forth, while the Son is composite because of the begotten and the putting-forth, and the Spirit because of proceeding from two sources and in two ways — that is, from the Father and from the Son, mediately, namely, and immediately? And yet even if we grant each a simple property, by your reasoning in this way too, as you say, a doubleness will be brought in, and composition will follow, and each will be composite. For indeed in the Father there is both the essence of the Godhead and the property of begetting; and in the Son, together with the essence, there is also the property of being begotten; and in the Spirit, together with the same essence, the property of the procession. But there is no composition there, but simplicity above all simplicity, because these are parts of the Godhead [lat: the Latin column reads *quia nec partes Divinitatis ista sunt*, "because these are not parts of the Divinity"] (for it is partless above all partlessness), but properties contemplated about the one essence and about the hypostases of the one essence, and each of them characterizing each hypostasis.
