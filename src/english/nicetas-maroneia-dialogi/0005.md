But if the one is perfect and the other imperfect, which of the two would you say to be either this or that? First, that one person is imperfect from which the imperfect procession comes. Then, what need has the perfect of the imperfect? But if the procession is simple: if it is perfect from the Father, why also from the Son? And if from the Son, why also from the Father? For in this way it seems superfluous that the Spirit should come forth from the other as well.

LAT. But perhaps someone says that the procession is not one and another — the one from the Father, the other from the Son — but one and the same and simple from the Father and from the Son, the Father putting forth the Spirit through the Son as intermediate. For from the Father is the Son, and from the Son the Spirit, wherefore also from the Father; so that the same procession is immediately from the Son, but mediately from the Father. And I say "mediately" not as though some time intervened (for he does not first beget the Son and then through the Son put forth the Spirit), but because [0197] in begetting the Son he puts forth the Spirit also through him, and the putting-forth of the Spirit is not without the Son.

GR. By this reasoning one might say that he begets the Son also through the Spirit which proceeds, and that the generation of the Son is not without the procession of the Spirit; and the Son too would be begotten from the Father and from the Spirit.

LAT. But there is also a certain order in the Trinity which we must keep — not one that alters nature, but one that displays the difference of hypostases and presents the property of each hypostasis as unfailing. The Father, then, in begetting the Son puts forth the Spirit also through him; and the Son, being begotten from the Father, puts forth the Spirit also from himself; and the Spirit, proceeding from the Father, proceeds also from the Son, as through the Son; but it is said to proceed from the Father also, in that, proceeding immediately from the Son, it proceeds from him who is begotten from the Father. And accordingly the one and the same procession belongs to the Spirit, mediate from the Father and immediate from the Son, as we have often said. Yet whether you wish to call it simple or not, it is perfect from either side or from both, that is, from the Father through the Son; because it is both from perfect ones, and perfect, and perfectly; and there is nothing superfluous in the perfect Trinity.

And if you inquire for what cause the Spirit, proceeding from the Father, proceeds also from the Son, and what the need is, I too will ask you: what need has the Father to beget the Son or to put forth the Spirit, and why does he not either beget both or put forth both, and what is the reason of the Word's generation or of the Spirit's procession? For there was no word higher than the Word, nor than the Spirit. Neither, then, do I tell you what the reason is of the procession from the Father and from the Son, nor why or how the procession is from both. For the existence of the holy Trinity lies above the how and the why, and above such inquiries.

And just as neither the generation is imperfect because it is not without the procession — even if it is distinguished by the difference of mode — nor the procession, because it is not apart from the generation, but each of the two is perfect, and each is other than the other, and neither of them is apart from the other: for as the Father does not first beget the Son and then, as you say, the Spirit proceeds from the Father, but both are always at once, both the generation and the procession, and each is perfect both by itself and together with the other, so also the proceeding from the Father and from the Son, even if it should not say one and the same and simple thing but one thing and another, still each of the two is perfect both by itself and together with the other, and not apart from the other. For it is not because in the same fire there is always both heat and light that on that account the one of them is imperfect, [0200] but because each is with the other, and on that account it is perfect. For fire is perfect, both as perfectly illuminating and as perfectly heating, that is, burning; for it has each in the highest degree. And it is perfect because it also works both at once and altogether. For the perfect is twofold: the one according to each property, the other according to the connaturality with the other. So also in the Trinity. For the Trinity is perfect both in that it is a Trinity, not a personal monad or a dyad; and it is perfect in that each hypostasis also, contemplated by itself — even if they are not separated from one another — has the perfect and above-perfect in every way.

And according to these same things the procession too is perfect from the Father, and perfect from the Son, and perfect from either, and from both; and each of them is above reason, even if you should call it neither simple nor one and the same. And in this way the difficulty has been resolved for you, and no absurdity follows upon the argument. And if you should even call it one, there is nothing to the contrary. For just as if someone should ask, Why does the Father beget? Why is the Son begotten? Why does the Spirit proceed? — we should have nothing else to say than that each of these is a property of each of the divine hypostases: so also if you yourself ask, Why does the Father beget the Son and put forth the Spirit with the Son mediating, while the Son himself also is begotten from the Father and puts forth the Spirit with no other any longer mediating, and the Spirit proceeds both from the Father and from the Son, and from the one mediately and from the other immediately? — you will hear nothing else than that the matters of the properties stand thus. You see that the things you say follow upon the argument as absurdities are shown either not to follow, or not to be absurdities, but only to appear absurd.

8. GR. And yet I see another absurdity following upon the procession of the Spirit from the Father and from the Son.

LAT. Say this too. For perhaps I shall be furnished with some argument against it as well, if the Holy Spirit should grant it.

GR. If the Spirit is from the Son, the Son would be father of the Spirit, and the Spirit son of the Son and grandson of the Father, and the Father grandfather of the Spirit; but these things are ridiculous and alien to truth and to piety.

LAT. Against this argument it was open to reply that the following of these ridiculous and impious things does not attach to our doctrine; since one would have said — were he not scrupulous, in discourses concerning God, about uttering whatever comes to hand and things which the divinely inspired oracles do not grant, as you yourself say — that if the Spirit also is from the Father, it would be son of the Father and brother of the Son…
