And if this opinion shall be on both sides, see how great the peace is. And observe: he did not say greater, but — what was more — he says, excelling yourselves. «Let not each look on his own things.» [1279] For when each man leaves his own advantage and becomes a man of his brother's advantage, so that you look to my advantage and I to yours, such a life is no longer human but angelic.

For let this be minded in you which was also in Christ Jesus, who, being in the form of God, counted it not a seizure to be equal with God, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, being made in the likeness of men; and being found in fashion as a man, he humbled himself, becoming obedient unto death, even the death of the cross.

He is about to treat of lowliness of mind. For what was minded in Christ and through Christ? The utmost lowliness of mind. — [ΦΩΤ.] Do you wish to know that lowliness of mind in no way harms you, nor damages you, nor brings you down from the height that belongs to you, but rather becomes itself a proof of it? Consider Christ, he says. For most of all is he shown in this to be God by nature: in being humbled and in bearing the man. For he knew that from this humbling he would suffer no loss of the height of the Godhead that belongs to him by nature. But if he had had it as a thing acquired, he would have feared to humble himself, lest he fall from a dignity hardly acquired by him. The humbling, then, is a proof of the height of his Godhead which is by nature. If, therefore, anyone desires to be lofty in virtue and great, let him be lowly of mind; for it is lowliness that shows and bears witness to the height of virtue. Let him imitate Christ: he will lose nothing by being humbled, but rather will gain greatly. He who is poor in virtue cannot be humbled, for he fears lest he fall from a counterfeit and pretended height. But let him who is rich and lofty in virtue be humbled. For in this he the more shows himself to be truly lofty: that he does not fear to be humbled, and knows that he stands firm. «Who, being in the form of God.» See how, out of these few words of the Spirit, all the heresies are undone. For first, in saying Being, he has declared his coeternity with the Father, and has thrown down Arius; then Marcion of Pontus as well. For if he was in the form of God, how do you not say that he began from Mary? Along with him Paul of Samosata too is undone, who said that the Lord was a mere man, wrought upon by God. For we would say to him: As the form of a servant is the nature of a servant, and not an operation, so also the form of God is the nature of God, and not an operation. And Nestorius, who thinks the same things, is undone along with them. Among these also Marcellus the Galatian, and Sophronius and Photinus, have been overthrown. And see Sabellius too falling. «He counted it not, he says, a seizure to be equal with God.» For "equal" is not said of one person, but of two and more. How then do you say that they are bare names, spoken of one hypostasis — the name of Father, and of Son, and of the Holy Spirit? See Arius struck a second time. For we would say to him: What is, He took the form of a servant? It is plain that he was becoming man. [1281] Therefore, being said to have also the form of God, he was God; for form is used of the two alike. But what says Arius? Thus, he says, the Son is inferior, in that he did not seize being equal — he says — to the great God; for a man seizes that which he does not have, he says. But what have we to say to him? First, nowhere do the Scriptures introduce to us a great or a small God. Next, he who by your account is small — how should he have seized becoming great? For he could not have, even if he had wished. Next, Paul brought in an example of lowliness of mind. He would not, then, have said: Since the small one did not rise up against the great God, you ought to submit to one another. But having shown the equality of Father and Son, he said that out of lowliness of mind he bore flesh. For not to rise up against the greater — what sort of lowliness of mind is that? Rather that was weakness and want of power. But for one who has what is equal and of equal power to be humbled and to become man — this is of lowliness of mind. And look at the book itself; for here it is written as in a riddle: «He counted it not a seizure.» That is, by way of seizure. «To be equal with God.» When a man has seized something, he does not even wish to lay it aside, fearing its loss. But when a man has something natural and his own, he lays it aside readily; for he knows that whenever he wills, he takes it again. What then does he say? The Son of God did not fear to come down from the dignity. For he counted not his Godhead a seizure — that is, he did not know himself to have by seizure his being equal to the Father, but by a natural dignity, he says. Therefore he emptied himself as far as the form of a servant, being made in the likeness of men. And what is the likeness of a man? Plainly, another man. And why did he say the likeness of a man? Because he had certain things above man: his being God also, and his being born without intercourse. As to the words, Being found in fashion as a man, the Eutychians say: Behold, he was not by nature a man. But we say that this was said lest the Godhead be thought to have been turned into manhood. And by fashion, he says, he means as it were in flesh; for it is proper to flesh to be fashioned as a man. For he was not one of the many, but had things differing also. Or the words, as a man, he uses for, such as a man. For of a king too, when he comes forth in glory, we say, He went forth as a king. And the Evangelist also says, «And we saw his glory as of an Only-begotten from the Father,» that is, such as an Only-begotten's. — [ΦΩΤ.] For Christ our God looked not to his own, but to what was profitable and salutary for all. Therefore he humbles himself through the incarnation, and exalts us all through his own humbling. He, then, who humbles himself becomes an imitator of Christ — and that though he himself is God by nature and lofty according to essence. But those who are lofty among men have acquired the loftiness in them by seizure, by covetousness; since indeed the nature of all men is common and equal and of like honor. If, then, he who is God by nature and lofty by nature humbled himself for us, ought not we [1284], who are by nature of like honor and alike lowly, to be humbled to one another, and to be conscious of our own measures? And if we are humbled, God exalts us also above measure, granting us freely what eye has not seen and ear has not heard and has not come up upon the heart of man. «Blessed, he says, are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of the heavens.» And yet, even if this were not so, it is of itself lofty and great to be counted worthy to become imitators of Christ. «But himself.» Where are they who say that by necessity and by the Father's command he willingly became man [lat: the Latin gives *invitus est incarnatus*, "unwillingly was he made man"]? «He emptied.» For since he knew that he had his being equal with God not by seizure, but from nature and a coeternal dignity, therefore he confidently emptied himself — that is, he humbled himself and became man, without casting away his being God. Whereas if he had known that he had his being equal with God by seizure, he would never have become man, being afraid lest he be brought down. «Taking the form of a servant.» See Paul's exactness.
