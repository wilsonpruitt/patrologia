For those others belong to such as shall die the deathless death. Do you see, even from this, at once, the recompenses of virtue? «For a boast to me.» For this brings a boast not to you only, but to me also, that I trained such men as these. «That I did not run in vain.» What boast have you, O blessed soul? That I did not run in vain, he says, nor labor. That is, that my teaching toward you has not proved vain and unprofitable.

But if I am poured out as a libation — that is, even if I die, [1289] he says, and am about to offer myself to Christ as it were a sacrifice and a libation (for to die for Christ's sake is to offer oneself a sacrifice to Christ) — I rejoice, he says. And he did not say this as though he were now about to die; rather he expounds beforehand what shall be afterward, and consoles his disciples in advance, showing that one must not grieve over his end — which is brought to Christ as a libation and a sacrifice — but even rejoice.

But if I am even poured out as a libation upon the sacrifice and ministration of your faith, I rejoice and rejoice with you all. And do you also in the same way rejoice and rejoice with me.

[ΦΩΤ.] This passage must be taken either as those before us expounded it, or in this way as well: But if I am indeed sacrificed because of the sacrifice and ministration of your faith, which I offered in sacred rite to God, I do truly rejoice — for a teacher's joy is the perfecting of those he has taught; and I rejoice with you, because you also, being perfected, enjoy the greatest joy. So then I for my part rejoice on this account and rejoice with you; and you too ought to rejoice, because you are coming to be in perfection, and to rejoice with me, because you have gladdened your teacher. But perhaps the words, «But if I am even poured out as a libation,» seem to introduce the thought that, had he not been poured out upon their sacrifice, he would have been grieved; for this is what the turn of the phrase suggests. And we say that neither is this absurd. For if he himself said above that he held it more needful to remain in the flesh for the advancement and the perfecting of the disciples, he would certainly have been grieved to be sacrificed without having yet perfected them, and without having their faith to bring to Christ as a sacrifice and a ministration. — And in another way also. He would not simply have chosen to be sacrificed, but on this ground, that he had become a diligent and fervent herald of the faith, and on this ground, that he had so equipped his disciples that their faith could be brought to God as a sacrifice and a ministration. For he who is sacrificed for these things — he it is who truly rejoices and exults in being sacrificed. For no one chooses to be slaughtered for nothing. — And it may be taken thus as well: I am poured out now by the trials and the afflictions. For elsewhere too he says, «I die daily.» But even if I am sacrificed, he says, by the trials and the afflictions — for bonds lay about him — yet still I rejoice upon the sacrifice and ministration of your faith, and so on. — Or in this way as well: Yet still, because upon the sacrifice — that is, for your sake and for the preaching to you — I rejoice, and so on. «Upon the sacrifice.» Even if I come to my end, he says, and am poured out as a libation, I rejoice and rejoice with you upon the sacrifice and ministration of your faith; that is, I rejoice because I offer you also to Christ as a sacrifice and a ministration — or rather, not you, but your faith do I offer as a sacrifice and a ministration. I rejoice, then, because I myself am poured out; and I rejoice with you also, because I am about to offer your faith to Christ as a sacrifice and a ministration. «Rejoice and rejoice with me.» Upon your [1292] faith rejoice; but for those things for which I am about to be poured out, rejoice with me.

CHAPTER 4. Concerning Timothy and Epaphroditus, whom he sent to them.

But I hope in the Lord Jesus to send Timothy to you shortly, that I too may be of good heart when I know the things concerning you. For I have no one like-souled, who will genuinely care for the things concerning you. For all seek their own, not the things of Christ Jesus. You know the proof of him, that as a child with a father he served with me for the Gospel. Him therefore I hope to send, as soon as I shall have seen the things concerning me, straightway. And I trust in the Lord that I myself also shall come shortly.

The very sending of Timothy he refers to the purpose of Jesus Christ. «That I too may be of good heart.» Even as you, he says, are of good heart on learning the things that concern me — both that the Gospel has come to advancement, and that the enemies have been put to shame, their contrivances having been turned about to my good — so may I too be of good heart and of good cheer, when I know the things that concern you. «For I have no one like-souled.» I could indeed, he says, have sent another; yet one who would so take your part or care for the things that concern us, I have not even one other. For it belongs to like-souledness that Timothy also should care for them as Paul does. «For all seek their own.» That is, their own repose. For no one, he says, would readily choose, for what seems good to Christ and for your advantage, to undertake so long a journey abroad as the one that reaches to you. And this he says not to revile the others, but to praise Timothy, and to teach us not to fall into the like. Yet observe that he who seeks ease, or only his own advantage, does not seek the things of Christ. «That as a child with a father.» You have no need, he says, to learn from me that, besides being subject to me, he served for the preaching. And with reason does he commend him, and this too for their advantage, that holding him in honor they may hear the more and be the more profited. «As soon as I shall have seen the things concerning me.» When I see, he says, to what end the things concerning me will come. Straightway. Immediately. «And I trust in the Lord.» It is not as one who is not coming, he says, that I send Timothy, but that in the meantime I may be of good heart, when I know the things concerning you.

But I thought it necessary to send to you Epaphroditus, my brother and fellow-worker and fellow-soldier, but your apostle and minister to my need; since he was longing after you all and in distress, because you heard that he was sick nigh unto death, but God had mercy on him — and not on him only, but on me also, that I might not have grief upon grief.

[1293] This man too he sends with praises, as he did Timothy, for the reason written there. «And my fellow-soldier.» This is more than fellow-worker. For it is possible to work together even in slight things; but the fellow-soldier works together in dangers and trials. «But your apostle and minister to my need.» For it was through him that they had sent him the things for his need. Therefore he calls him their apostle, and minister of his own need. «Since he was longing after you.» His love he puts forward by way of commendation. «And in distress.» He was in distress, knowing that he was loved by you, and therefore aware that you would be grieved at his sickness. «For indeed he was sick.» He shows that it was not through neglect that he was slow to send anyone to them, but that he was constrained. For Epaphroditus was ill, and Timothy — the one who was also able to come — he had alone; how then could he have sent him? «But God had mercy on him.» What will the heretics say, who call the world evil? For behold, the Apostle calls it a mercy of God that Epaphroditus was suffered to remain in this life.
