⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ But see how he lays hold of them without naming them, fearing lest he make them more shameless [lat: the Latin gives *veritus ne nimium pudore ipsos afficeret*, fearing lest he affect them with too much shame]. ⟦s2⟧ «Circumcised the eighth day.» ⟦s3⟧ What was held in honor among them he set down first — circumcision. ⟦s4⟧ And see how great a bodily nobility he alleges, lest the Jews suppose that he says what he has said as one deprived of these things. ⟦s5⟧ For otherwise they were put to shame, when he blamed the things that were theirs — he who had all of them in the highest degree; and he says, Circumcised the eighth day. [1300] Meanwhile he shows that he was not a proselyte. ⟦s6⟧ «Of the race of Israel.» ⟦s7⟧ He shows that he was not even of proselyte parents. ⟦s8⟧ «Of the tribe of Benjamin.» ⟦s9⟧ Of the tribe held in repute, he says, among the Jews. ⟦s10⟧ «A Hebrew of Hebrews.» ⟦s11⟧ From my forefathers, he says, I am of the Jews of repute (for it is possible to be of the race of Israel and yet not a Hebrew) — taking the word «Hebrew» as having to do with the knowledge of the Hebrew tongue. ⟦s12⟧ For after they had been scattered among the nations, in their great ignorance of the ancestral tongue they had no exact knowledge of the scriptures current among the Jews, nor of the law itself. ⟦s13⟧ «As touching the law, a Pharisee.» ⟦s14⟧ This was the sect of repute among the Jews. ⟦s15⟧ For now he speaks also of the virtue that comes of deliberate choice; for the first things were not of choice. ⟦s16⟧ Of choice is the zeal; but not of choice are circumcision, being of the race of Israel, of the tribe of Benjamin, and the rest. ⟦s17⟧ «As touching zeal.» ⟦s18⟧ For it is possible to be a Pharisee, and yet not a zealot. ⟦s19⟧ As touching the righteousness which is in the law. ⟦s20+21+22+23⟧ Since the Jews too persecuted the Church, not for zeal of God but for love of rule, he makes this point secure as well. ⟦s20+21+22+23⟧ For he who was blameless as touching the righteousness in the law — how should he have persecuted for love of rule, and not for zeal of God?

⟦s20+21+22+23⟧ But whatsoever things were gains to me, these I have counted loss for Christ's sake. ⟦s20+21+22+23⟧ Nay rather, I even count all things to be loss, because of the surpassing worth of the knowledge of Christ Jesus my Lord, for whose sake I suffered the loss of all things, and count them to be refuse, that I may gain Christ, and be found in him, not having my own righteousness which is of the law, but that which is through faith in Christ.

⟦s20+21+22+23⟧ [ΦΩΤ.] By gain he means the things gathered to him from the law. ⟦s24+25⟧ So then he does not speak ill of the law, as the followers of Marcion babble. ⟦s24+25⟧ And if he does count these things loss, he does not say it simply — which would be the mark of one speaking ill of the law — but he calls them loss by the comparison with Christ; and this tends the rather to the law's praise, that he does not dare to call it loss otherwise than by the setting of it beside Christ and the comparison with him. ⟦s26⟧ «These I have counted.» [ΤΟΥ ΑΥΤΟΥ] If you count all things loss, O Paul — even the very things of the law that are kept after the flesh — because of the surpassing worth of the knowledge [ed: our Greek source breaks off here and resumes in mid-argument; the parallel Latin column shows the extent of what is missing — see the crux] it is not to cast off a loss (for this is rather to make gain, namely to be set free of loss), but rather signifies the casting away of certain profitable things [lat: the Latin column gives *quarumdam vilium rerum abjectionem*, "the casting away of certain worthless things"]. ⟦s27⟧ How then, when you put away the things you have counted loss, do you say, That I suffered loss? ⟦s28⟧ And we say that he did not count these things loss simply, but by comparison with the more perfect knowledge that is according to Christ, since in itself he reckoned them gain. ⟦s29+30⟧ Therefore the words, I count it loss, are by comparison with the better things; but the words, I suffered loss, are said in themselves, with regard to the nature of the law. ⟦s31+32⟧ Or this too must be said: that the divine Paul takes no care for the choice of Greek words; he looks only to make plain the matter in hand. ⟦s33⟧ For just as elsewhere, wishing to make plain the power of death [1301] over us, he was in no way exact about the wording, but instead of saying, it played the tyrant, or it did violence, said, it reigned — signifying by a word of good omen its ill-omened tyranny — so now too, the casting away of harmful things, which is a thing of good omen, he indicated by a word not of good omen, saying, I suffered loss. ⟦s34⟧ «Loss.» ⟦s35+36⟧ Certain of the heretics say: Behold, the law is loss and refuse; how then do you say that it was given by God? ⟦s37⟧ But observe: he did not say, It is loss, but, I have counted it loss. ⟦s38⟧ For to those who sit beside the letter and stand away from Christ it becomes the procurer of loss — not by its own nature, but by the inexperience of those who use it ill. ⟦s39⟧ For if gold and lead lie before a man, and there is need to take one of the two, and he chooses the lead, he has suffered loss — the lead not being loss by nature, but by the deprivation of the better. ⟦s40⟧ So then the law also, if a man use it as he ought, is gain; for it conducts to Christ. ⟦s41+42⟧ For Moses the lawgiver, speaking of Christ — that He will raise up for you a prophet like me (like me, that is, both in respect of the flesh and of the mediation) — said that they must hearken to him. ⟦s43⟧ But if a man will sit beside the letter, it is an occasion of loss. ⟦s44⟧ These things you may understand of the words, I count them to be refuse, as well. ⟦s45⟧ And first it may be said that he did not say Refuse of the law, but of worldly things as well. ⟦s46⟧ And if of the law also, you will understand it according to the sense given for loss; and this too is well. ⟦s47⟧ For the straw of the wheat is called refuse. ⟦s48⟧ Yet so long as the wheat has not yet appeared, the straw is necessary, as preserving the wheat that is to be; but when it has appeared, the straw is useless to men. ⟦s49⟧ So also the law. ⟦s50+51⟧ So long as Christ had not become man, it was needful, for it conducted men to him; but when he had appeared, it is superfluous, so far as concerns the keeping of the things in it after the flesh. ⟦s52⟧ And in another way too the law was needful before Christ. ⟦s53⟧ For it had more of philosophy than the doctrines of the Greeks, but was grosser than the philosophy that is in Christ. ⟦s54⟧ Set therefore midway between idolatry and the faith of Christ, it profited, leading men little by little out of idolatry and out of the grossness that came of it, and making them fitter to believe in Christ when he appeared. ⟦s55⟧ Since, then, it has fulfilled its own office and has conducted men to Christ, it is henceforth superfluous for being kept after the flesh — as the child's dress and training are to a boy who has come to man's estate. — [ΟΙΚΟΥΜΕΝΙΟΥ] You may also understand the law to be refuse in another way. ⟦s56+57⟧ For refuse is fodder for beasts; and the law accordingly, after the faith, is under observance among the more irrational sort of men. ⟦s58⟧ But why at all have you counted it loss and refuse, O Paul? ⟦s58⟧ Because for a long time it deprived me of Christ, while I sat beside the letter — not that he was now suffering loss from it. ⟦s59⟧ For he knew how to use it spiritually; but [1304] he speaks of that loss which was before his believing. ⟦s60⟧ For even after the loss that damaged us has passed by, we still, in remembering it, call it loss. ⟦s61⟧ For see how safely he spoke: I count it loss, because of the surpassing worth of the knowledge of Christ — as though he said, By nature it is not loss; but since, while I supposedly kept it, I did not recognize Christ, it became a loss to me. ⟦s62⟧ «Nay rather, I even count.» ⟦s63⟧ Since the word, I have counted, carried the force of past time, he says that even now I count. ⟦s64⟧ For indeed, he says, for a long time it deprived me of Christ — not by its own will, as one might say, but by my relation toward it, which was not according to knowledge. ⟦s65⟧ «That all things are loss.» ⟦s66⟧ For if, he says, one should set the bodily observance of the law over against the knowledge of Christ, he will find the law to be loss. ⟦s67⟧ So great, he says, is the difference made by the knowledge of Christ.
