But the words are those of one urging them on. And by true he means the things of virtue; for vice is falsehood. By seemly, all that lies in bearing and in words and in gait and in deeds. By pure, as by way of contrast with him who minds earthly things. By lovely, either to God or to the faithful. «If there be any virtue, and if any praise.» He wills them so to live as to please men also. (Without offence [1320] be ye, he says.) Not looking to praise, but living praiseworthily. «These things consider.» Do you see that he wills to banish even the evil thought out of our souls? «What things ye also learned.» Through teaching ye learned, and received, and heard; but through works ye saw. And that he became for them a pattern, this is the meaning of, Ye saw in me. For since it was not possible to write about all things — about bearing, about gait, about words, about laughter, and all the rest — in one word he taught all, in saying, And ye saw in me. «And the God of peace shall be with you.» For if ye do these things, he says, ye shall be free from fear, in peace, in security. For if we through virtue are at peace toward God and toward men — «For with them that hated peace, he says, I was peaceable» — God also shall be with you. And God being with you, what need is there to busy oneself about the rest?

But I rejoiced in the Lord greatly, that now at length ye have flourished again in your thought for me; wherein ye were indeed minded, but ye lacked opportunity. Not that I speak in respect of want; for I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, to be self-sufficient. I know also how to be humbled, and I know also how to abound; in every thing and in all things I have been initiated, both to be filled and to hunger, both to abound and to suffer want. I am strong for all things in Christ who empowers me. Nevertheless ye did well, in that ye had fellowship with my affliction.

«But I rejoiced in the Lord greatly.» Greatly, for this reason: because the joy was not fleshly, but in the Lord. And he rejoiced, not because he himself received, but because those who gave were benefited. «That now at length.» The phrase, Now at length, indicates a long time in which they had neglected to do this, namely to send him the things needful. And the phrase, Ye have flourished again, is as of fruits that had been dried up and afterwards budded once more. For thus, he says, ye also, having withered away in almsgiving, have flourished again. The phrase, «Your thought for me,» showed that they had been negligent only in the things that concerned him, and not in the things that concerned all men — which was the act of one recovering the force of his rebuke. For the thought for me, he says — that is, the remembering of me, as ye always used to remember me, he says. Then, as though making apology for their remissness toward him, he says: «But ye lacked opportunity.» That is, Ye did not neglect me out of remissness, but ye had no opportunity of sending, either because ye were not in plenty, or because ye had not one to carry the things. But why does he who always boasts in not receiving (for it is good for me rather to die, he says, than that anyone should make void my boasting) now receive? And we say that there, among the Corinthians, it was on account of the false apostles that he did not receive. For, he says, that wherein they boast, they may be found even as I. Since he did receive, not so much for his need as that those who supplied might be benefited. For whence had he wherewith even to be fed in a foreign land, neither carrying anything with him, nor giving his leisure to handiwork, but to the preaching [1321]? «Not that I speak in respect of want.» Since he had gently rebuked them by writing, Now at length, he says: The things I said, I did not say for this reason, as being in want, but for your advantage, he says, I spoke, that ye be not without fruit — which he says lower down. — [ΦΩΤ.] I said that I rejoiced in the Lord greatly, that ye have flourished again in your thought for me. But do not suppose, he says, that I say this because of the want I had. For I, he says, have learned, and so forth. I am trained, he says, in all things, and not even when in want am I put out. Yet I rejoiced nevertheless over you, that ye did well. For having made yourselves partakers of my affliction, ye shall be partakers also of the prizes. For this reason I rejoiced greatly. Since he had said, I rejoiced greatly, because ye did well in becoming fellow-partakers of my affliction, he reminds them that from the beginning he had laid up for them this grace and this benefaction — namely, to receive from them the things needful, and thereby to make them partakers both of the afflictions borne for Christ's sake and of the prizes laid up for these. For do not suppose, he says, that it is because I am now far away, and in a place where the preaching has not been spread abroad as among you, nor are there men who so readily supply the things needful — that for this reason I accepted your supply and counted you worthy to become partakers both of my affliction and of the recompense. For indeed, when I was still near, and had many who would have supplied the cost had I been willing, I preferred you. For when I went forth from Macedonia, and at Thessalonica, I counted you worthy to be the suppliers of my need. But do not suppose that I say these things and extol this deed of yours in order that ye may supply again and again, and satisfy my necessities. For so far am I from saying them for this reason, that behold, I say, I have all things — that is, I am discharged as regards all the things that pertain to need; and I am discharged in this sense, that I no longer have need of them. Then, that he may not grieve them as though disdaining them: Not so, he says, but that I abound, and so forth. «For I have learned, in whatsoever state I am.» The thing, then, is a matter of teaching and of discipline. And what didst thou learn, O divine soul? In whatsoever state I am. That is, to be content, he says, with what is present, and to be self-sufficient. «I know also how to be humbled.» Perhaps someone will say: To know how to bear hunger belongs to virtue; but to be filled — of what virtue is that? Of very great virtue indeed. For as straitness, so also freedom from care knows how to breed many evils. For many, coming to be in plenty, have become more careless, and have not been able to bear a belly that is filled. For indeed Israel bore not the fulness, but kicked against it. But not so Paul. «To be humbled.» That is, to suffer hunger. «To abound.» That is, to be sated. In every thing and in all things I have been initiated. In every matter, he says, and in [1324] all the things that befall, I have been initiated. That is, I have experience of all things. «I am strong for all things in Christ who empowers me.» That he may not seem to speak boastfully, and moreover as being thankful, he refers all to Christ. What has been said, he says, is no achievement of mine, but of Christ who supplies me this strength. «Nevertheless ye did well.» And these things being so, he says, and I being strong to bear all things, ye did well, he says, in becoming fellow-partakers of my affliction. For lest they should become more careless on hearing that I need nothing, he handles the argument in another way. For by what went before he abated their conceit, but by what he says now he makes them more eager. He did not say, Ye gave and stopped my grief, but — what was more venerable — «Ye had fellowship with my affliction.» And what a thing it is to become a partner of Paul, and that in the affliction borne for Christ's sake!
