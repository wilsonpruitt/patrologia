⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [2816] mightest thou fittingly obtain? ⟦s1⟧ For small beside these are both Aristides and Pisistratus, in the things for which each was admired: Pisistratus, who, having laid upon the Athenians a tribute of a tenth of the produce of the country, when, passing along Hymettus, he saw an old Attic man working the rock and worn out with his toils, was moved, as was natural, on his account; and sending to him he asked what fruits the rock brought him; and the old man, groaning deeply, said: "Pains, and a multitude of ills; and of these the tenth must go to Pisistratus," not knowing that the tyrant was present; and the word came to Pisistratus. ⟦s2⟧ And he, admiring his endurance, and thinking to give some great gift, released the rock to the farmer free of tax; and, as though in memory of virtue, from that time the place was named Tax-free. ⟦s2⟧ But why did it occur to me to speak of Pisistratus, and one old man, and that unhappy plot, beside the freedom of all thy subjects, whence every artisan too prospers beyond hope, and the farmer at his ploughlands sings thy deeds as a solace for his toils?

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 15. ⟦s2⟧ Consistent with these, thou didst furnish those things too, which conquer every report by their wonder. ⟦s3⟧ For formerly the cities used to hold certain inhuman spectacles. ⟦s4⟧ For unhappy men were handed over to the beasts in the midst of the people, having for spectators those who possessed kinship of nature with them; and a man took pleasure, I know not how, watching a man torn apart and his body not even hidden by earth, but filling, instead of a tomb, the bellies of the beasts.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 16. ⟦s2⟧ Another spectacle inclined wholly to the opposite. ⟦s3⟧ For male boys, as though exchanging their own nature for women's, wished to be women in their bearing, and were broken in their limbs, moving the hand instead of the tongue, and maddening a whole people toward a wanton sight; whence men, inflamed to strife and madness, were divided by hatred, and thrust against one another. ⟦s4⟧ These things, then, thou didst scare away like some stain from thy subjects; and in both respects nature is temperate in men; and the cities were saved.

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 17. ⟦s2⟧ But that I may not go far beyond the occasion, I shall put an end to these, and now proceed with the speech to what remains. ⟦s3⟧ For thou didst not think it enough to extend thy humanity as far as the common [2817] [b: A] need, but, as though ever longing to do good, thou lookest out for those who need to be done good to, and whatever in particular grieved the cities. ⟦s4⟧ And knowing these things thou didst add what was lacking, and didst see fit to change them for the better; and they were changed. ⟦s5⟧ Passing over most of these (for they are too great, and the occasion prevails over the need), I make, as it were from things sacred, a fitting beginning for the speech.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 18. ⟦s2⟧ There is a sacred city among those that lie toward the rising sun, populous, bearing its token from its piety, and set before the others by its divine rites; whence there come to it Indians and Persians and Phoenicians and the races of the Scythians, and the august of Hellas, and all Ionia; and one might call it, as it were, the common native land of the race of men. ⟦s2⟧ This city, by its lack of water, grieved along with its inhabitants those too who came from everywhere. ⟦s3⟧ For it had as much as the fortune of the rains supplied; and in place of anything else they had made treasuries of water; and devising against the want, they barely held out so against the hardship. ⟦s4⟧ But until thee this too still grieved it; for thou didst think that the city ought to be honoured as befitted it; and thinking it dreadful that through this one city all men should make trial of hardships, raising channels of water on high, and bringing to one level with the heights the ground that did not rise to them, and tracing through uneven and rugged land a straight road for the stream, thou didst join springs from far off by it; and in these they send streams clear and abundant, conquering the eye by the swiftness of their course; so that those who arrive keep festival at their ease, and all dwell there with pleasure.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 19. ⟦s2⟧ Again to our own parts. ⟦s3⟧ When the city that bears Caesar's name had a harbour that had given out with time, laid open to every threat of the sea, no longer preserving its designation in its works, but holding from its ancient fortune only the bare name, thou didst not overlook her as she begged and ever lamented her merchantmen, which, often escaping the open sea, suffered shipwreck in the harbour. ⟦s4⟧ And those who needed the cargoes had a more pitiable lot; for they saw perishing the things of which they were in need, and had a profitless sight of them. ⟦s5⟧ But since thou didst will it she grows young again, and boldly receives the ships; and the city is full of necessaries.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 20. ⟦s2⟧ A like suffering had all but overtaken the city of Alexander too. ⟦s3+4⟧ For a tower exceedingly high, the work of an ancient prosperity, rising at the tip of the harbour, raises a flame of safety for those coming from the open sea, and comforts those at sea, announcing the city beforehand. ⟦s5⟧ Looking toward it the helmsman steers his vessel, and keeps his ship untried by the dangers that lie close by; and the sailors, delighted at the sight, at once clap their hands, and one points it out to another, [2820] and they proclaim that they have the city; and, to speak simply, it bestows pleasure and safety together upon those who arrive. ⟦s6⟧ This tower the sea, striking against it from behind and laying it bare little by little of its defences, now come close, was shaking, and had all but brought it down to the ground. ⟦s7⟧ But thou didst outrun the suffering with the cure. ⟦s8⟧ For, walling it about with unbreakable breakwaters, able to fight the sea that is hard to fight, thou didst give back to the city its possession, deathless.

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 21. ⟦s2⟧ And why need I count up one by one walls and harbours, and the beauties of buildings, and abundance of money lavished in rivalry upon the provision of food? ⟦s2⟧ And what of the greatest wall, beyond all conception, raised high and great and led through the whole of a part of Thrace? ⟦s3⟧ For it is drawn from sea to sea, shutting off the approach to the barbarians and thrusting back every assault of enemies; so that small beside it is even that of Themistocles, for all its breadth and its carrying both the wagons, even when they went in opposite directions to each other upon the wall. ⟦s4⟧ For I think that if Homer were alive and became a spectator of such works, he would have let go the wrath of Achilles and one ship set on fire, and called upon the Muses here many times, that in poetic grandeur of voice he might not fall too far short of the worth of the works.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 22. ⟦s2⟧ For besides the rest, thou lendest a humane ear to him who approaches thee. ⟦s3⟧ And a man converses without fear about what concerns him, seeing that the loftiness of thy rank displays nothing haughty; the very thing that lifted Pausanias the Spartan tastelessly into arrogance, when he was convicted by Persian luxury. ⟦s4⟧ But thou both speakest humanely and hearest those who speak; but a dread look and a great spirit are kept for the barbarians and the wrongdoers. ⟦s5⟧ And thou judgest for us kindly, and legislatest what is fitting. ⟦s6⟧ And through the lawful fear of thee contracts are just, marriages are chaste for the subjects, the families legitimate, and the children not doubtful to their fathers through suspicion.

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 23. ⟦s2⟧ But truly that golden race, sung of in words alone, time has at last shown us in deeds. ⟦s3⟧ For not only dost thou legislate for us the best things through [2821] [b: A] the laws, but thou hast also shown thine own life as a living law and a pattern of temperance to the ruled; for the subject is always somehow apt to liken itself to the mind of its rulers. ⟦s4⟧ For thou hast made one woman thy consort, a partner of life and counsels, whom fortune gave thee together with the empire. ⟦s5⟧ And if any other seems beautiful to some, and much talk is stirred about her, she is wholly unknown to thee, along with Helen. ⟦s6⟧ For it seems to thee dreadful to master so many subjects, yet to be slave to pleasures with the strangest slavery of all, and to make power the stuff of wantonness.

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 24. ⟦s2⟧ But I confess that I have suffered something strange, and from delight in thy noble deeds do not measure my words by my power, but speak at length, as though recounting thy deeds among those who do not know them. ⟦s3⟧ Yet to you too it is ever most sweet to hear about these things which report has often made known to us who longed for them. ⟦s4⟧ For they say that lovers too, though they know their darlings better than at any time, the more they strive to know them, yet take no pleasure in speaking or hearing anything that does not hold the memory of them. ⟦s5⟧ Now the illustrious men of old, for my part, I have often admired on hearing of them, and I rise up at the report, and I have always prayed that such men might rule affairs, and that our life might never be bereft of a crop of good men. ⟦s6⟧ Nevertheless, I find such men priding themselves, one on one thing and another on another. ⟦s7⟧ And if a man has but one, he is admired as though he bore all; but thou, gathering the virtues of all, and adorning what each lacked by the addition of another's, showest [b: C] through thine own one self the images of those who went before.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1+2+3⟧ 25. ⟦s1+2+3⟧ The Persians, then, admire Cyrus, calling him benefactor and father together; and their judgment I shall not so far fault; for they gave the title to the king from the things in which they were fortunate; but not from the wrongs the Medes had suffered, and Astyages' lamenting — he who was mother's father to Cyrus, and had his grey hairs made captive through him, and, the greatest thing toward misfortune, from the things in which he himself was unfortunate, was calling the third from himself, his child's child, at once child and master. ⟦s1+2+3⟧ And thus by unjust longing and domestic tragedies he received the rule. ⟦s4⟧ And that done, he [2824] fell with iron and battles upon those who loved freedom, leading them into slavery and laws they were not used to; and out of his own kin he crowned himself with Asia. ⟦s4⟧ But thou, receiving the empire through no lust, but by a just vote, hast become to us all what Cyrus was to the Persians alone; and, contrary to him, didst free us from the customary tributes. ⟦s5⟧ And now it seems to all the one freedom to be under thee willingly, and to obey a humane nod.

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 26. ⟦s2⟧ The Spartans hymn Agesilaus, and some of them are proud in him; and I too, with them, am his lover, and have admired him as alone of all the kings of Sparta who ever were in knowing well how to be ruled first, and so receiving the rule. ⟦s3⟧ For one could see a good man, breathing something Laconian, and teaching the barbarians to be temperate, and to be well persuaded what a thing, after all, the nature of Hellas was. ⟦s4⟧ And he put an end also to what was sick and seditious in his native land, and made Sparta one in its counsels. ⟦s5⟧ But we shall allow the Spartans no more pride than this, now that we have the new Agesilaus, through whom the barbarian is at rest, having hung up his shield, and embraces peace, though it is no friend of his; and whatever in the empire was disorderly has been changed into men who are temperate and know how to bear a lawful rule. ⟦s6⟧ But to stand by friends unjustly, and to contend beyond what is right — for which Sparta then first saw an enemy's fire, and lost her flower at Leuctra — let the Agesilaus of old keep these; we are far from them.

⟦p14⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 27. ⟦s2⟧ As for Alexander son of Philip: when I learned that he showed a mind worthy of so great a kingship, holding out to his subjects a hand flowing with gold, and mastering the pleasures of the body, and even becoming of a higher nature, formerly I disbelieved it, and this seemed to me a mere fable and a fiction. ⟦s3⟧ But now I see in deeds what I used to marvel at hearing in words. ⟦s4⟧ So much I know about this: if he was such as thou thyself hast come forth to us in experience, I am persuaded that he conquered in all things, and was the son of Zeus, and that Philip was deceived.

⟦p15⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 28. ⟦s2⟧ And thus thou hast come forth to us, hiding all others by the wonder. ⟦s3⟧ Whence our cities are happy, busied with dances and festivals; for peace blossoms upon our affairs, and Ares and faction wander far from what is ours. ⟦s4⟧ The sea is sailed by merchants without danger; the land is tilled upon light-hearted hopes; helmet and spear and sword have been refashioned into ploughs and friendly names; and women bear children like their parents.

⟦p16⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 29. [2825] [b: A] And the seasons keep their order, and all things go forward according to nature. ⟦s1⟧ For the Divine is gracious, holily honoured with pious works; and the cities are bright, each priding herself on one thing or another; and all have put forth a common adornment, setting up thy images for thy benefactions; and we with them; for whose dedication we do not gather theatres of the people, things utterly cast aside by thee also, a temperate judge; for in these the delight is vulgar, and one may see a market crowd making merry. ⟦s2⟧ Wherefore these we bid farewell; but words honour thy images, and contests of words, and through these the Muses.

⟦p17⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 30. ⟦s2⟧ But what, then, shall we inscribe upon them? ⟦s2⟧ What will hold to their worth? ⟦s3⟧ Or surely this: "The city to its benefactor, through whom I now [b: B] lift a proud neck and am a city"? ⟦s3⟧ But may it be that thou govern the rule of the Romans for ever, and have very many such good men set over affairs; and that the cities weave crowns for their good fortune, and dedicate paintings, and sing in hymns; and that the sons of poets and orators, moving their tongues about thee, may ever have abundance, and glory in thy trophies.
