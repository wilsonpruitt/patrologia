⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ ON HAGIA SOPHIA

⟦s1⟧ [b: A] Men of the herd, once, and the swept-together rabble, rising up against the emperor Justinian in Byzantium, brought about the riot called Nika; which I have related, without concealment and with precision, in my books on the wars. ⟦s2⟧ And showing that it was not against the emperor alone, but no less against God, that, accursed as they were, they had raised their arms, they dared to set fire to the church of the Christians (the Byzantines call the church Sophia, having made the name most fitting for God). ⟦s3⟧ And God allowed them to accomplish the impiety, foreseeing into what beauty this sanctuary was to be changed. ⟦s4⟧ So the church then lay all burnt to charcoal; but the emperor Justinian, not long after, turned it out such that, if anyone had asked the Christians whether it was their wish that the church be destroyed and become such as this, showing them some model of what is now to be seen, they would, it seems to me, have prayed as quickly as could be to see the church suffer it, so that it might be changed for them into its present form. ⟦s5⟧ So the emperor, taking no thought for money of any kind, set himself with zeal to the building, and gathered all the craftsmen from every land. ⟦s6⟧ And Anthemius of Tralles, most learned in what is called the science of the master-builder, not only of all those of his own time but by far of those who had lived before him, [2829] [b: A] served the emperor's zeal, ordering the works for those who were building, and preparing beforehand images of what was to be built. ⟦s7⟧ And with him was another master-builder, Isidore by name, a Milesian by birth, both intelligent in other respects and fit to serve the emperor Justinian. ⟦s8⟧ And this too, then, was part of God's honour toward the emperor, that He had set ready beforehand those who would be most useful to him for what was to be done; and one might fairly admire the mind of the emperor himself as well, for this very reason, that out of all mankind he was able to choose, for the most serious of his affairs, the most fitting men.

⟦s9⟧ So the church has become a spectacle of surpassing beauty: to those who see it, extraordinary; to those who hear of it, altogether past belief. ⟦s10⟧ For it is lifted to a heavenly height, and, as though riding at anchor among the other buildings, it leans out over them, lying above the rest of the city; adorning the city, because it is part of it, and glorying in itself, because, being part of it and mounting above it, it stands so high that from it the city is viewed as from a watch-tower. ⟦s11⟧ Its breadth and its length have been so fittingly turned out that it may be found, not unreasonably, both very long and altogether broad. ⟦s12⟧ And it is made majestic by a beauty past telling; for it is refined both in its mass and in the harmony of its measure, having nothing either too much or too little; since it is both more stately than the buildings we are used to and much more seemly than the immoderate. ⟦s13⟧ And it abounds exceedingly in light and in the gleaming of the sun. ⟦s14+15⟧ You would say that the place is not lit from without by the sun, but that the radiance is born within it: such an abundance of light is poured round about this sanctuary.

⟦s16⟧ And the face of the church (that would be the parts toward the rising sun, where they perform for God the ineffable rites) has been wrought in this way. ⟦s17⟧ A building rises from the ground, not made in a straight line, but drawn in little by little from the sides and receding at the middle, and, passing into the shape of a half-circle, which those skilled in such things call a half-cylinder, it stands up to a sheer height. ⟦s18⟧ And the summit of this work ends in a quarter of a sphere; and above it another crescent-shaped structure is lifted upon the adjoining parts of the building, [2832] wonderful in its comeliness, but by the precariousness of its composition seeming to be something altogether fearful. ⟦s19⟧ For it seems somehow not to be raised aloft securely, but to hang perilously over those who are there; and yet it is set fast with singular firmness and safety. ⟦s20⟧ On either side of these, columns stand upon the floor, they too not standing in a straight line, but drawing back inward in the shape of a half-circle, as though making way for one another in a dance; and above them a crescent-shaped structure hangs. ⟦s21⟧ Opposite the east a wall has been raised that holds the entrances, and on either side of it both the columns and the work above them stand in a half-circle, very like what has been described. ⟦s22⟧ In the middle of the church rise four man-made hills, which they call piers: two toward the north, two toward the south wind, facing and equal to one another, each pair having between them about four columns. ⟦s23⟧ The hills are made, built up of stones of great size, chosen out, and fitted to one another with skill by the masons, and they reach to a great height; seeing them you would liken them to sheer crags. ⟦s24⟧ Upon these, four arches rise in a square; and their ends, meeting one another two by two, are set firm upon the summit of these hills, while the rest, lifted up, hang aloft to a boundless height. ⟦s25⟧ Of the arches, two stand over empty air, toward the rising and, I suppose, the setting sun; the remaining two have beneath them some building and quite small columns. ⟦s25⟧ Above them a circular structure is lifted in a round; whence the day always first smiles through. ⟦s26⟧ For it rises, I think, above the whole earth, and the building is broken at short intervals, left open on purpose, just so far that the places where the masonry happens to be divided may be conduits of ample light.

⟦s27⟧ But these things, I think, are beyond the power of a tongue that lisps and is thin of voice. ⟦s28⟧ The interweaving of the arches being worked out in a square, the work between them has been finished off into four triangles. ⟦s29⟧ And the base of each triangle, pressed together by the setting of the arches into one another, makes the lower angle sharp; but rising with them and widening for the rest of the way in the space between, it ends in the circle which rests there; and the remaining angles it makes there. [2833] [b: A] Upon this circle a dome of enormous size, spherical, rising upon it, makes it singularly handsome. ⟦s29⟧ And it seems not to stand upon solid masonry, because of the openness of the masonry, but, hung from heaven by the golden chain, to cover the place. ⟦s30⟧ All these things, fitted to one another aloft beyond all expectation, hung from one another, and resting only on what is nearest them, make a single, most extraordinary harmony of the work; but they do not allow those who view them to let their gaze linger long on any one of them. ⟦s31+32⟧ Rather, each draws the eye after it and carries it over most easily to itself; and the change of the sight is ever quick to turn, since the beholder can nowhere choose what he would admire more than all the rest. [b: B] But even so, though they look all round, knitting mind and brows upon everything, they are unable to grasp the art; but they always go away from there struck with astonishment at the helplessness of their sight. ⟦s33⟧ So much, then, for these things.

⟦s34⟧ By many devices the emperor Justinian and Anthemius the master-builder, with Isidore, contrived that the church, raised so high, should be in safety; of which all the others it is beyond me to know and impossible to express in words. ⟦s35⟧ One alone I shall set down at present, by which one may judge the whole strength of the work. ⟦s35⟧ It is thus. ⟦s36⟧ The hills I mentioned just now are not made in the same manner as other buildings, but in this way. ⟦s36⟧ A course of stones has been laid in a square, hard by nature but smooth in their working; cut, if they were to make the projecting parts of the pier's sides, with angles; if they had been allotted the space between, made rectangular. ⟦s37⟧ And what joined them was not lime, which they call asbestos; not bitumen, Semiramis's pride in Babylon; not any other such thing; but lead, poured into the hollows, and passing everywhere between, settled into the joints of the stones and binding them to one another. ⟦s38⟧ That, then, was worked in this way. ⟦s39⟧ Let us go on to the rest of the church.

⟦s40⟧ The whole roof has been overlaid with unalloyed gold, blending its splendour with its beauty; yet the gleam from the stones wins, [2836] flashing back against the gold. ⟦s41⟧ And there are two colonnades on either side, not separated from the building of the church in any way, but even making the measure of its breadth greater, and reaching with it in length all the way to the end, though lower in height. ⟦s42⟧ And these too have a dome for a roof, and gold for ornament. ⟦s43⟧ Of these two colonnades, the one is allotted to the men at prayer; and the other is set apart for women doing the same. ⟦s44⟧ They have no difference, nor do they differ at all from each other; but their very equality reaches to the beauty of the sanctuary, and their likeness adorns it.

⟦s45⟧ Who could be the interpreter of the upper galleries of the women's quarter? ⟦s46⟧ Or who describe the multitude of colonnades and the colonnaded courts with which the church is surrounded? ⟦s46⟧ And who could reckon up the beauty of the columns and stones with which the sanctuary is adorned? ⟦s47⟧ One would think one had come upon a meadow in its bloom. ⟦s48+49+50⟧ For one would rightly marvel at the purple of some and the green of others; at those on which crimson blooms, and those from which white flashes; and still more at those which nature, like some painter, varies with the most contrary colours. ⟦s48+49+50⟧ And whenever anyone [goes there to pray, he perceives] [?] at once that it is not by human power or art but by the inclining of God that this work has been turned out; and his mind, lifted up to God, walks the air, thinking that He is not far off, but delights above all to dwell in those whom He Himself has chosen. ⟦s48+49+50⟧ And this happens not only to one seeing it for the first time, but the very same thing seems so to each man continually, as though the sight were beginning there. ⟦s51⟧ Of this spectacle no one has ever had his fill; but men who are present in the sanctuary rejoice in what they see, and when they go away they pride themselves on their conversations about it. ⟦s52⟧ Moreover, the treasures of this church, the gold vessels and the things in silver and precious stones, which the emperor Justinian dedicated here, it is impossible to tell in full, even for one speaking with precision. ⟦s53⟧ By one alone I permit those who read this to judge. ⟦s54+55+56⟧ For the part of the sanctuary that is most especially unprofaned, and accessible to priests alone, which they call the altar, carries forty thousand pounds of silver.

⟦s54+55+56⟧ So the [church] of Constantinople,[?] which they are accustomed to call the Great, to speak summarily, counting it off on the tip of the finger, and to tell in the briefest word the most notable of the facts, was built in this way by the emperor Justinian. ⟦s54+55+56⟧ And the emperor built it not with money alone, but also with the toil of his mind and the rest of the virtue of his soul, as I shall presently show. ⟦s57⟧ Of the arches which I mentioned just now (the master-builders call them *loroi*), one, the one which is toward the rising sun, had already risen on either side, but had not yet been wholly [2837] [b: A] completed in the middle, but was still waiting. ⟦s58+59+60+61+62⟧ And the piers, upon which the building was going up, not bearing the size of what lay upon them, somehow suddenly cracking, looked as though they would before long be broken up. ⟦s58+59+60+61+62⟧ So Anthemius and Isidore and those about them, terrified at what had befallen, brought the matter before the emperor, having come to despair of their art. At once the emperor, driven by what I do not know, but by God, I think (for he is no master-builder), bade them bring the curve of this arch round to its completion. "For this," he said, "resting upon itself, will no longer need the piers beneath it." ⟦s58+59+60+61+62⟧ And if the account were without witnesses, I know well that it would have seemed flattery and wholly past belief; but since there are many witnesses present of what was done at that time, we need not hesitate to go on to the rest of the account. ⟦s58+59+60+61+62⟧ So the craftsmen did what was commanded; and the whole arch hung aloft in safety, setting the seal of experience on the truth of the conception. ⟦s63⟧ That, then, was worked in this way. ⟦s63⟧ But with the other arches, which face the south and the north wind, this is what came about. ⟦s64⟧ The so-called *loroi* had been raised aloft; and everything beneath, weighed down by them, laboured; and the columns there threw off small flakes, as though scraped. ⟦s65⟧ And again the master-builders, disheartened by what had befallen, reported their plight to the emperor. ⟦s66⟧ And again the emperor devised this counter-measure. ⟦s67⟧ He ordered that the tops of these labouring parts, as many as touched the arches, be taken down at once, and set in again much later, once the damp of the masonry had well left them. ⟦s68⟧ And they did accordingly; and the structure has continued safe thereafter. ⟦s69⟧ And the emperor carries off a certain testimony of the work, such as this.
