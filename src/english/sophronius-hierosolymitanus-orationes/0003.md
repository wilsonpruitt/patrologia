⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Whence, travelling the King's highway, the middle way, we both abhor the confusion and loathe the cutting; and with our soul we embrace only the union of Godhead and manhood that is at once unconfused and impartible, which alone the natural concourse according to hypostasis knows how to recognize.

⟦s2⟧ For this union Godhead and manhood, united with one another, have had, so that they should neither undergo change nor suffer division. ⟦s3+4+5+6⟧ For the account of the union — of the natural union, I mean, and hypostatic (for I know no other union in Christ beside this) — is not ignorant of the difference, but removes the division through and through, and keeps the things that have run together into union unchanged, and does not admit partition of the things united. ⟦s3+4+5+6⟧ And for this reason, naming Christ from Godhead and manhood, and from two natures, we proclaim the same both God and man, both of two natures and twofold according to the natures. ⟦s3+4+5+6⟧ Likewise also perfect in Godhead [?]; wherefore we also teach as doctrine that he is in two natures, and we record the same as consubstantial with the Father as God, and say that the same is consubstantial with his mother and with us as man: the same visible and invisible, the same created and likewise uncreated, the same body and bodiless, the same tangible and intangible, the same circumscribed and uncircumscribed, the same earthly and heavenly, the same flesh rationally ensouled and Godhead, the same recent and eternal, the same lowly and most high, and whatever is found to belong impartibly to a twofold nature; even if the one he always was, as having an eternal nature, and the other he became for us in the last times without change, as having assumed the human nature. ⟦s3+4+5+6⟧ For if the union was unchangeable and impartible, as it both is unchangeable and remains impartible, and the two are signified as unalterably different, and shine together impartibly in otherness, these are natures and essences and forms, out of which the ineffable union has come to be, and in which one and the same Christ is beheld; [3168] and the one too remains one, the result that has come about from them, no longer divided in two, and displaying without cutting and change the things from which it is. ⟦s7⟧ This is a hypostasis and a composite person, constituted out of an unconfused blending, and knowing no partition of the coming-together, and having obtained to be one and to remain one, undivided; and it is neither two, inasmuch as it has become one; nor does it confuse, and bring into one oneness and a natural and essential sameness, the things from which it has been naturally brought together; but the same is recognized as one and two: one according to the hypostasis and person, two according to the natures themselves and their natural properties, from which it both received its being one as its portion, and preserved its remaining twofold in nature.

⟦s8⟧ Whence the same, remaining one Christ and Son and Only-begotten, not cut asunder, is seen in both the natures, and he naturally worked the things of each essence according to the essential quality, or natural property, belonging to each. ⟦s9⟧ Which, had he had a nature monadic and unpaired, as he has the hypostasis and the person, he would not have accomplished; and the one and the same would not have worked completely the things of each nature. ⟦s10⟧ For when did Godhead, having no share in a body, naturally work the works of a body? ⟦s11⟧ Or when did a body, bereft of Godhead, operate actions essentially recognized as Godhead's? ⟦s12+13⟧ But Emmanuel, being one, and in the same both the one and the other — that is, God and man — truly did the things of each nature, operating what was done in one respect and in another: as God, the same did the divine things; as man, the same did the human things; wishing to show himself to all, that the same was God and man. ⟦s14+15+16+17+18+19⟧ And for this reason the same both does the divine things and the human, and likewise both says and utters them. ⟦s14+15+16+17+18+19⟧ And it was not one who performed the miracles, and another who accomplished the human things and suffered the sufferings, as Nestorius would have it; but one and the same Christ and Son is he who did the divine things and the human, but in one respect and in another, as the divine Cyril maintained; since in both too he had the authority unconfused, nay more, impartible. ⟦s14+15+16+17+18+19⟧ For inasmuch as the same was eternal God, he did the miracles; inasmuch as the same was recognized as recent man, he did the lowly and human things. ⟦s14+15+16+17+18+19⟧ For as in Christ each nature keeps its own property undiminished, so too each form operates, with the communion of the other, that which it has as its own: the Word working that which is the Word's, with the communion, clearly, of the body; and the body accomplishing what is the body's, [3169] [b: A] the Word, that is, communing with it in the action; and these recognized in one hypostasis, and thrusting away the most loathsome cutting. ⟦s20⟧ For they did not operate what is proper to them dividedly, so that we should suspect a partition of them too.

⟦s20⟧ Let not the frantic Nestorius skip for this, beguiling himself, because each form in the one Christ and Son did, with the communion of the other, that which it had as its own. ⟦s21⟧ For it did not do what it had as its own standing apart from the other, as he would have it; for we do not glorify, as he does, two christs and sons who operate — one, Son and Christ by nature, the wonders, and another, Son and Christ by grace, the poorer things. ⟦s22⟧ And even if we teach as doctrine two forms operating in common, each according to its own natural property, yet we speak of one and the same Son and Christ, who naturally works the lofty things and the poor, according to the natural, essential quality of each of his two natures. ⟦s23⟧ For the natures, remaining immutable and unconfused, and recognized clearly as two, and united without confusion, were not at all deprived of these, and were shown in one hypostasis. ⟦s24⟧ Let not Eutyches and Dioscorus, the champions of a godless confusion that does not exist, leap up in vain; but each nature did what was proper to it with the communion of the other, both fleeing division, and not knowing change, and keeping its difference towards the other, and preserving the communion and composition indissoluble and unbroken.

⟦s25⟧ Hence, being pious, and standing upon the boundaries of orthodoxy, as we say that the one and the same Christ and Son operates both the one and the other, since the same was God and man, and we have no thought of any confusion; so also, when we say that each form operates what is proper to it with the communion of the other, since there are two forms in the one and the same Christ naturally operating what is proper to them, we do not conceive any division at all — as on this side Eutyches, and on that Nestorius would slander us, standing opposite one another, and sharing out between them the impious war against us the pious; counting whom as nothing, we know also, of each nature, the operation of each: the essential, I mean, and natural and corresponding operation, proceeding without division from each essence and nature, according to the natural and essential quality inborn in it, and bringing with it the co-operation, at once impartible and unconfused, of the other essence. ⟦s26⟧ For this makes the difference of the operations too in Christ, just as, indeed, the being of the natures makes that of the natures. ⟦s27+28⟧ For Godhead and manhood are not the same, according to the quality naturally belonging to each, even if they ran together with one another inexpressibly into one hypostasis, and [3172] [b: A] were composed without confusion into one person, and brought to completion for us the same one Christ and Son, through their hypostatic concurrence and composition with one another. ⟦s27+28⟧ For God the Word is God the Word, and not flesh, even if he has assumed flesh rationally ensouled, and has united it by a natural and hypostatic union; and the flesh is flesh rationally ensouled, not the Word, even if it is beheld as the flesh of God the Word. ⟦s29⟧ And for this reason they neither have the same operation, without variation from one another, after the union — the natural and unconfused union, that is, the true and hypostatic — nor do we call their operation one and only — one essential and natural operation, and wholly without variation — lest we should also drive them together into one essence and one nature: the "composite" one, made a plaything by the children of the Acephali, and shamelessly put forward by them in these very words.

⟦s30+31⟧ Just as, then, we confess the natural operation of each essence and nature — from which the union has come about for us in Christ without confusion, and has brought to completion the one Christ and Son, whom we believe wholly God and the same wholly man — each its natural operation, lest we confuse the natures united without confusion, if indeed it is from the operations, and from them alone, that the natures are recognized according to those skilled in such matters, and the difference of the essences is by nature always grasped from the difference of the operations: so also we teach as doctrine that every utterance and operation, whether it be divine and heavenly, or human and earthly, proceeds from one and the same Christ and Son, and from his one composite and single hypostasis, who was the incarnate Word of God; he who also put forth from himself naturally each operation, impartibly and without confusion, according to his natures: according to his divine nature, in which he was consubstantial with the Father too, the divine and inexpressible operation; according to the human, in which the same remained consubstantial with us men too, the human and earthbound operation — the operation dear and suited to each nature; and not allowing any of those who saw him to be scandalized, as though he who naturally worked these things and those were not the same God and man: by the same one Christ and Son operating both the one and the other, stopping up the foul effluence of Nestorius (for we do not, as we have said, venerate two christs and sons, as he does, who operate these things and those); and by showing that what is proper to each nature remains unconfused after the union, and that he likewise puts forth the operation of each of them — the natures being recognized by the natural account, and naturally interpreting their own nature, from which the operation welled up impartibly and naturally, and gushed forth essentially — turning aside the confusion-loving sprouting of Eutyches. ⟦s32⟧ Hence, born [3173] with our birth, he is nursed with milk and grows, [b: A] and passes through the bodily stages of age, until he reached the perfection of human age; and he accepts our hunger and thirst, and endured weariness, as we do, from journeying. ⟦s33⟧ For he also performed the operation of walking, like us, which, being operated humanly, and proceeding according to human essence, was a demonstration of his human nature. ⟦s34⟧ Whence too he passed, as we do, from place to place, since he had truly become man, and had our nature undiminished, and submitted to the circumscription of a body, and wore the shape that fits us. ⟦s35⟧ For the form of the shape too is bodily, that is, of a body, according to which he was moulded when he was conceived in the womb, and which he kept for ever after, and keeps unto the age without end.
