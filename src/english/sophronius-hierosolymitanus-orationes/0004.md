⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ For this reason, when he hungered, he was fed; for this reason, when he thirsted, he was given drink, and as a man he drank. ⟦s2⟧ For this reason he was carried as a child, borne in virginal arms and reclining in a mother's bosom. ⟦s3⟧ For this reason, when he toiled, he sat down, and when he needed sleep, he slept. ⟦s4⟧ Nay, he also felt pain when he was struck, and suffered when he was scourged, and endured the toils of the body, pierced through in hands and feet on the cross; for he gave the human nature, when he also willed it, occasion to operate and to suffer what was its own, lest his renowned incarnation should be judged some phantasm and empty spectacle. ⟦s5⟧ For not involuntarily or by compulsion did he accept these things, even though he admitted them naturally and humanly, and did and performed them by human motions: away with the abominable thought!

⟦s5⟧ For it was God who endured to suffer these things in the flesh, both saving us by his own sufferings and awarding us through them impassibility. ⟦s6⟧ But he suffered when he himself had willed to suffer and to act and to operate humanly, and had decided to benefit those who saw, for whose sake he had also become man in truth; and not when the natural and fleshly motions wished to be moved naturally to operation, even if the godless plotters were eager, with every daring, to fulfil their plots. ⟦s7+8⟧ For he put on a body passible and mortal and corruptible, and subject to our natural and blameless passions; and he allowed it to suffer and to act in a manner corresponding to its own nature, until the resurrection from the dead. ⟦s9⟧ For there he dissolved what in us is passible and mortal and corruptible, and graciously gave us freedom from these things. ⟦s10⟧ So then the lowly and human things he displayed thus, voluntarily and at the same time naturally, remaining God in these too; for he himself was steward to himself of human passions and actions, and not only steward but also president, even though he had become incarnate naturally in a passible nature. ⟦s11⟧ And for this reason his human things were above man: not because his nature was not human, but because he had become man voluntarily, and, having become man, accepted them voluntarily; and not tyrannically or by compulsion, as is sometimes the case with us, and unwillingly, but when and as far as he willed; and he himself consented to give way to those who brought the passions upon him, [3176] and to the sufferings themselves operating according to nature.

⟦s12+13⟧ But the divine and brilliant and supreme things, which plainly overcome our meanness, those which were marvels and prodigies, and the procession of paradoxical works — such as were the seedless conception; John's leaping in the womb; the incorrupt birth; the undefiled virginity, unharmed before the birth and in the birth and after the birth; the heavenly initiation of the shepherds, and the star-moved drawing of the Magi, and with it the bringing of gifts and the veneration; the untaught knowledge of letters («For how does this man know letters,» they say, «having never learned?»), which in part refutes the ill-mannered love of the lovers of ignorance; the filling with wine out of waters; the strengthening of the sick; the recovery of sight of the blind; the straightening of the maimed; the binding firm of the paralytics; the straight-running movement of the lame; the more-than-brilliant cleansing of the lepers; the sudden filling of the hungry; the numbing of the pursuers; the lulling of the winds; the calm settling of the sea; the bodily walking upon the waters; the driving out of the unclean spirits; the sudden movement of the elements; the self-moved opening of the tombs; the rising from the dead on the third day; the never-ending dissolution of corruption; the unceasing overthrow of death; the unhindered going out while the seal of the stone and of the tomb was kept; the unrestrained coming in while the doors were shut; the much-marvelled and bodily going up from earth into the heavens; and all the things like these, which pass beyond the nature of speech and the power of voice, and overcome all human understanding — all these, accomplished above reason and human nature, were admittedly proofs of the divine essence and nature of God the Word, even if they were operated through flesh and body, and were not performed without flesh rationally ensouled. ⟦s14⟧ And not for this shall we guess God the Word to be fleshless, or teach as doctrine that he is outside a body, because he did things higher than bodies. ⟦s15⟧ For truly the Word has been incarnate, and, incarnate without falsehood, has been embodied; and one Son was known, who put forth from himself every operation, divine and human, lowly and exceeding great, earthly and heavenly, fleshly and bodiless, visible and invisible, circumscribed and uncircumscribed, corresponding to the duality of his natures, and through itself proclaiming that duality without silence and announcing it piercingly.

⟦s16⟧ For being one and the same Son, undivided according to hypostasis, and the same being made known as two natures, by the one he worked the God-showing signs, by the other he accepted the lowly things. ⟦s17⟧ And for this reason the God-minded say — those who were crowned for Christ the true God, and received from God the power to speak — weaving for us the most divine understandings: [3177] «Whenever thou hearest contrary sayings about the one Son, divide what is said correspondingly among the natures, assigning whatever is great and divine to the divine nature, reckoning whatever is small and human to the human; for so thou wilt both escape the discord of the sayings, each nature receiving what is by nature its own, and confess the one Son both before all ages and recent, according to the holy Scriptures.» ⟦s18⟧ Nay, and of the one Son they speak thus too: no one could separate any operation from the one sonship, but by reason he could recognize the nature whose own the thing done is. ⟦s18⟧ So it has been taught as doctrine by them most excellently: to confess the one Emmanuel (for so God the Word incarnate is named), and that the same does all things, and not one and another, both the high and the least, without any standing apart. ⟦s19⟧ Through which both the twofoldness of his natures is known without confusion, and the same is not divided throughout into two hypostases and persons; but he is one and the same Son and Christ, uncut, known without division in two natures; and we affirm all things to be his, the one Son's, and believe all his sayings and all his operations to be his: even if some of them are befitting God, and others again befitting man, and others hold a certain middle rank, as having what befits God and what is human in the same. ⟦s20⟧ And we say that of this force is also the common operation, called theandric, not being one, but heterogeneous and different, which the God-speaking Dionysius, captured divinely from the Areopagus by the divine Paul, [b: C] spoke of, as having what befits God and what is human in the same, and by its most graceful and composite appellation fully showing each operation of each essence and nature.

⟦s21⟧ So, glorifying God the Word as before the ages and co-eternal with the Father, we profess that he underwent a temporal birth, by which, having been incarnate, he was born of the Virgin Mary, properly and truly Theotokos. ⟦s22⟧ And for this reason it is fittingly believed by the pious that he was born two births. ⟦s23⟧ And being perfect in Godhead, the same was perfect in manhood; neither divided by the difference of the essences, nor, by the sameness of the hypostasis and of the person, bringing the natures to an essential sameness; but in the natures from which he appeared according to hypostasis, in these he remained without division, [b: D] wisely and truly undergoing all our works and natural and blameless passions, things far from blemish and defilement, and in which no trace of any sin is found; for he did no sin, nor was any guile at all found in his mouth. ⟦s24+25⟧ And having lived among us humanly, as being known as perfect man, even though the same was God without any falling short, and having worked miracles as was fitting — [3180] for he was known as perfect God, and had been mingled with human flesh noetically ensouled — he goes up to the voluntary passion, and is voluntarily betrayed to the Jews, or rather voluntarily betrays himself to them for the salvation of men; and he is bound, and buffeted, and spat upon, and scourged, and mocked, and puts on a purple robe as reigning over all, and is royally handed a reed as a royal sceptre, and is condemned with Pilate judging, and at last is fastened to the gibbet, and is bloodied in hands and feet, nailed to the saving cross, and is lifted up with robbers, and is given vinegar to drink, and tastes gall, and, crying out loudly, gives up his soul to the Father, and is wounded in the side by the spear, and pours forth water and saving blood after death and deadness, and is brought down dead from the cross, and is laid out, and anointed with myrrh, and buried in a three-day burial, and, rising on the third day, comes forth from the tomb, and raises all the dead together with himself, leading them from tomb and corruption to his own unending life through his resurrection from the dead; and, risen from the dead, he appears to the disciples, and confirms the resurrection of his own flesh by eating and drinking and the touch of apostolic hands, and grants them the all-holy Spirit as akin to him and of one stock; and he is borne up into the heavens, or rather goes up as master of the heavens, and sits at the right of his begetter, holding the throne paternal and royal and supreme. ⟦s26⟧ Whence also he will come again to make judgement of the living and the dead, and to render to each according to the deeds which he has done, whether he wrought good and fine deeds or base and blameworthy ones; whom with the Father and the Spirit we believe to reign over all with a reign truly unending, admitting no end and no fulfilment.

⟦s27+28+29+30⟧ But concerning the economy in the flesh — that is to say, the incarnation of God the Word and his likening to us lowly ones — how I speak and think, I have made known briefly. ⟦s27+28+29+30⟧ And concerning the constitution of the visible world which came about from the beginning, and its consummation, which it will receive before long, I confess, God-honoured ones, that the one God fashioned all things, not only the visible but also the invisible — the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, the eternal and beginningless nature — and brought them from things that are not into being, and readily formed what was not before, and wisely put forth their countless differences. ⟦s27+28+29+30⟧ For the Father has made all things through the Only-begotten in the Holy Spirit; and these he also holds together by wise providence, presiding as God over his own works; and, having fixed for all things a beginning in time, he subjected the sensible things to an end in time, while the intelligible and invisible he deemed worthy of a greater worth than these: and they die in no way, nor are they corrupted after the flowing and transient [?] manner of the sensible things, [3181] [b: A] yet they are not immortal by nature, nor have they passed over to an incorruptible essence; but he graciously gave them a grace that keeps them from corruption and death.

⟦s27+28+29+30⟧ Thus the souls of men remain incorrupt; thus angels continue immortal: not having in truth, as we said, an incorruptible nature or a properly immortal essence, but having received by lot from God a grace that furnishes immortality and is for them the procurer of incorruption. ⟦s27+28+29+30⟧ But it is not because the souls of men by God's grace have thrust away the corruption that lurks in all created nature [?] that we shall therefore suspect them to exist before bodies, or conceive them to live, before the bringing forth and framing of the visible world, in some eternal life, and say that they have a heavenly life, living the eternal life fleshless and bodiless in a heaven that once did not exist, as Origen the deranged would have it, and his fellow-initiates and like-minded, Didymus and Evagrius, and the rest of their myth-minding crowd; who not only hold this opinion in error, borne along by Hellenic doctrines and befouling the nobility of Christians, but also senselessly abolish the resurrection of these bodies which we now wear, stammering countless dreadful things worthy of their impious myth-making. ⟦s27+28+29+30⟧ For whom the word spoken by Paul to the Corinthians suffices for rebuke: «That if the dead are not raised, neither has Christ been raised»; and then, to those who thus grow vain in their very reasonings, there will be added: Then your faith is empty. ⟦s27+28+29+30⟧ Or has not the resurrection of this very flesh too become part of our august confession, you there? ⟦s27+28+29+30⟧ For we are required to confess the resurrection of the flesh too when we come to the saving baptism.
