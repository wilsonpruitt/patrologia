⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Thou hast a clear demonstration, thou hast a true declaration. ⟦s2⟧ The Holy Spirit comes down upon thee, the undefiled, to make thee purer, and to furnish thee with fruit-bearing power. ⟦s3⟧ «And» the all-powerful «power of God shall overshadow thee,» that is, the Word and consubstantial Son of God. ⟦s4⟧ For he is, of God himself and the Father, both hand and arm and [3276] [b: A] power, and through him the Father, as through a Son, has made all things, and «in him all things consist» — he who will make the uninterpretable incarnation from thee, and show forth the untilled conception from thee, and work the inconceivable birth from thee.

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 44. ⟦s2⟧ «Wherefore also that which is born holy shall be called Son of God.» ⟦s3⟧ If then he who will be born of thee, the Theotokos Virgin, shall be called Son of God, how will she who bears him not appear a virgin? ⟦s4⟧ For this will be a clear proof and an unshaken mark of his Godhead that rules all things: that thou, being a spotless virgin, bearest the inexpressible birth, and remainest a virgin after the incorrupt delivery. ⟦s4⟧ For if he who is born of thee the virgin is God, as indeed he both is and is proclaimed God, what would he not accomplish if he willed? ⟦s5+6+7+8⟧ For he is God, even if he is also born as man; and he is man, even if also as God he keeps thee a virgin who bearest.

⟦s5+6+7+8⟧ For this reason I myself, Gabriel, have been sent to thee the Virgin, that by my very name I might show that he who is born of thee, the stainless Virgin, is God and man. ⟦s5+6+7+8⟧ For indeed the name Gabriel, brought to interpretation, declares God and man. ⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Since many angels were in heaven, but he did not will to send any one of these; but me, who appear one of them, and am named Gabriel, he sent, that by my word and name I might clearly show thee the one to be born of thee, that he is God and man, and «bears all things by the word of his own power;» and that he is holy, and would sanctify the birth of all men, and that he shall be called Son of the Most High, and would make sons of the Most High by grace those who believe in him. ⟦s2⟧ For the rest, O Virgin, believe my words; for they will surely be fulfilled in deed. ⟦s3⟧ For I speak truth in what I utter to thee, as herald and angel of truth.

⟦s4⟧ And for thy full assurance and a greater confirmation of the fulfilment of my words, I give also an incontestable sign, so that thou mayest remain unshaken in the faith: «For behold, Elisabeth thy kinswoman, she too has conceived a son in her old age; and this is the sixth month with her who is called barren; for [3277] [b: A] no word shall be impossible with God.» ⟦s4⟧ And this will be to thee a most mighty proof that all my words, good tidings to thee, august to all, will receive a true and saving end, for the life and redemption of all men. ⟦s5⟧ For it belongs to the same power with God both that a barren woman should bear, she who does not bear, and that a virgin should bear, she who is unwedded. ⟦s6⟧ Hail thou, then, O thou that art full of grace, virgin and God-filled, for the things in which God, the Master of all, has shown thee grace, and in which thou wouldst beget the Son of God, who shall become joy to all the race of men.

⟦s7⟧ 45. ⟦s7⟧ To whom the Virgin Mother of God, believing, and taking courage that there would surely be a fulfilment of the things spoken to her from the Lord, answers meekly and humbly together, nay more, virginally too; for the all-pure was to bear the Christ «meek and lowly in heart,» and she had to be rich in his lowliness and meekness: «Behold the handmaid of the Lord; be it to me according to thy word.»

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 46. ⟦s2⟧ Gabriel the archangel, then, having learned these things from the Theotokos Virgin, went up into heaven, whence he had also been sent to her. ⟦s3⟧ And she bore in her womb God who sent him, who is glorified above with the Father, and below, and from her, endures fleshly child-bearing. ⟦s4⟧ For at once the Word and God came to her, and through the voice of the angel entered her divine womb and was incarnate, and without change was embodied, taking to himself from her the unploughed flesh, and uniting it to himself in truth, rationally ensouled indeed, but not subsisting before its inexpressible composition with him. ⟦s5⟧ For at once flesh, at once flesh of God the Word; at once flesh ensouled, rational; for it had its hypostasis in him, and not its constitution before him.

⟦s6⟧ Nor was it ever the flesh of any other of the men like us, and then ran together with God the Word, and made its composition with him; but in its being flesh ensouled and rational, it was also allotted to be flesh of God the Word, ensouled and rational, taken from the virginal and stainless blood of the stainless Virgin. ⟦s7⟧ For those who most impiously wish to divide the one Son and Christ into two sons and Christs have no [3280] [b: A] indication or pretext that it ever subsisted by itself, and had its own person, and then thus came together with the Word and God into natural union. ⟦s8⟧ For things that are apart, and are beheld in separation from one another, never receive natural union, or that according to hypostasis. ⟦s9⟧ And where natural union does not go before, nor composition according to hypostasis come about, neither is one and the same hypostasis shown, nor one and the same person announced, undivided together and impartible, and thrusting away every cutting.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1+2⟧ 47. [3281] [b: A] And just as the undivided and impartible incarnation of the Word turns away such madmen, and wholly removes them from the nurslings of piety (these are the Nestoriuses and Pauls and Theodores, and those who are their associates in God-fighting madness), so also those who wish to confuse the natures, and hasten to make one nature out of both natures, it sends away outside the assemblies of the pious. ⟦s3⟧ For neither, according to their madness, did the Word, in being incarnate, become incarnate by changing his nature into flesh; nor did he alter the flesh endowed with mind and soul, which he took to himself from this Virgin, the Theotokos, into his own divine nature, and transform it into a divine essence; but he remained unchangeable, and above every transformation, and free of all confusion, and was incarnate in flesh consubstantial with us, and became for our sakes man in truth. ⟦s4⟧ And the flesh too has remained unalterable, and outside all change and confusion; and having come together with the Word into constitution, and having been united to him according to the impartible hypostasis, having indeed its consubstantiality with us unchangeable, and preserving, so far as nature and essence and existence go, its likeness to us without variation, but undergoing no partition from the Word, or suffering cutting and division; but being styled the Word's own and peculiar flesh, and running together with him into one hypostasis on account of the union with him according to hypostasis, it is shown standing and remaining within the proper bounds of its nature; displaying a natural difference towards him, but knowing no personal difference towards him; but completing with him one and only hypostasis, and this unitary and composite (for not simple and incomposite), but not running together into one composite nature; for this is the beginning of confusion, and is the foundation of changeful alteration.

⟦s5⟧ So then, just as the Word incarnate shakes off those who divide and cut, so also he casts out of the sacred enclosures those who confuse both the natures, and speak of their mixture and alteration: these are the Severuses and Eutycheses, and Dioscoruses, [3284] Apollinariuses and Polemons, and before these the Simons, Valentinuses, Marcions, Basilideses, Maneses and Menanders, and the whole many-headed heresy of the Acephali, now wickedly stirring things up, which has one war, and that common, against piety, but carries on countless wars against itself that never cease. ⟦s6⟧ These, then, the truly holy and all-holy good tidings, and worthy of the inspired ears of us the pious, welcome us sweetly, but drive those foul and mad ones out of this festival of these good tidings, as men who understand and speak of them impiously.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 48. ⟦s2⟧ But I, the least, who have come forward today as herald of these things, to you alone, the children of piety and faith, who receive piously the things said, cry with a great voice, and proclaim and utter expressly: «Good tidings, good tidings, good tidings.» ⟦s3+4⟧ Good tidings of our Saviour, good tidings of God incarnate for our sakes; good tidings of the coming of God to us; good tidings of the becoming man of God, that from us; good tidings of the seedless conception of God; good tidings of the virginal child-bearing of God; good tidings of the unchangeable concourse of God with flesh; good tidings, in which we the outcasts are exalted and deified and become gods by grace: no longer dreading corruption; no longer fearing passions; no longer trembling at death; no longer shrinking from the foul devil; no longer wary of his unseen assaults; no longer afraid of his manifest tyrannies; but all appearing gods, all found godlike, all enrolled in the choir above, all beheld as [3285] [b: A] citizens of the city above, Jerusalem. ⟦s5⟧ For where we have our citizenship, there shall we also receive the inheritances, and dwell in the ineffable mansions.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 49. ⟦s2⟧ Bearing the good tidings of all these things, brethren, we have come to you, most dear, today. ⟦s3⟧ For of all these the beginning and root and foundation and groundwork are the good tidings now brought to you. ⟦s4⟧ For from these, as you have learned, all the abundance of good things wells up for us, gushing forth as from some spring, and watering the whole inhabited world together, and gladdening and nourishing it, and bearing it up to heavenly courts, and rendering it partaker of the most divine mansions there and of blessed gifts. [b: B] And for this reason I often cry piercingly, rejoicing: «Good tidings, good tidings, good tidings;» to which I call and exhort you, and which I have willed you to keep as a feast today. ⟦s5⟧ For hence our salvation proceeds; hence freedom is kindled for us like a beacon; hence our adoption to God is brought to birth; hence our recall is born; hence the Word and God, having come to save us, makes the beginning of salvation. ⟦s6⟧ This he makes the prelude of his compassion towards us; this he showed forth as the beginning of our deification; than which nothing is more heart-pleasing to men; than which nothing is more precious to the earthly; than which nothing is more gracious to mortals.

⟦s7⟧ Whence the second Adam, taking to himself the virgin earth, reshaping himself in the human form, lays down a second beginning for manhood, renewing the oldness of the former, and showing us a second that does not grow old, which has indeed kinship and sameness of stock with that one, but does not bear towards it sameness in manner of life, nor equality in the weight of worth and sacred glory. ⟦s8⟧ That one, through the first Adam, was condemned; this one, through the second Adam, has been freed from blame. ⟦s9⟧ That one, through the first Adam, was orphaned of divine rays; this one, through the second Adam, has been refashioned with heavenly rays. ⟦s10⟧ That one, through the first Adam, the earthy from earth and earthly-minded, bore the yoke of slavery; this one, through the second Adam, the consubstantial Son of God, receives the dignity of adoption; and has been enriched to cry and to shout, «Abba, Father.»

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ 50. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ Are our good tidings, then, brethren, small? ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ Is the proclamation of our good tidings cheap? ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ Is the shining-forth of our good tidings lowly and contemptible? ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ Or does it not surpass all [3288] [b: A] brightness, and exceed all radiance, and obscure all elegance? ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ And for this reason I cry to you, the partakers and like-minded; and crying «Good tidings, good tidings, good tidings,» I am not sated. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ Though my speech wishes still to run, and longs to run its course well, I hold it back. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ For I see the hearers weary, and that though not sated with the hearing of our sacred good tidings. ⟦s1+2+3+4+5+6⟧ For the spirit you have willing, but the flesh weak; since you are beset with satiety of speech, and have not strength to toil further. ⟦s7⟧ And I know that satiety of speech is hostile to the ears, even if the speech be divine and supreme, and most of all to those who in hearing speeches are quickly sated. ⟦s8⟧ Sacredly, then, and loftily let us keep the lofty and sacred feast of our lofty and sacred good tidings; and let us honour it worthily and fittingly; that through it we may most unassailably attain the heavenly goods, of which it has appeared to us root and source and begetter, in Christ Jesus our Lord himself, with whom to the Father and the Holy Spirit be glory, honour, might, majesty, to the ages of ages. ⟦s9⟧ Amen.
