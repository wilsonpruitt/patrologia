⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [3321] ENCOMIUM OF SAINT JOHN THE FORERUNNER

⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 1. ⟦s2+3⟧ Give, O voice of the Word, a voice; give us, O lamp of the light, the radiance; give us, O Forerunner of the Word, the running of the word, that, praising thee as befits thy deeds, we may take our delight today: for we are eager to hymn thee, O Baptist, both by an ancestral debt and more than all that are our own. [3324] But since, more than all, we have taken pride in thy gifts from thy forebears' time, and are not able to do this from our own store, we run again to thee, the lover of giving, and ask thee to become the provider of our praises; lest, honouring thee only with words of our own, we should seem to be insulters of thee through the meanness and dishonour of our words, rather than praisers and thankful, among those who do not know our cheapness in words, but demand that the speech be a match for the greatness or worth of the one honoured, and grant no pardon to the purpose of the speaker, which does not bear the flame of its longing, if it should lay hold by force on what is beyond it. ⟦s4⟧ For that those who would wish to crown thee with praises fall far short of thy worth and are left very far behind, there is no longer anyone who would not proclaim it with his whole mouth, unless he is mad and has gone out of his natural mind. ⟦s5⟧ What then must one like me do, who every hour am watered with thy countless gifts, like some tree that has had its planting by the waters, and have the bounty of thy operative outpouring flowing past me without ceasing? ⟦s6⟧ Is it to keep silent and cry out no thanksgiving at all, because he has not been able to do it worthily; or to cry out as far as he is able and can, and in this way show how irrepressible is the disposition within him, even if the cry is not strong enough, through its own smallness, to make its greatness manifest? ⟦s7⟧ For the one is judged worthy of acceptance, and would be reckoned in the portion of the grateful, as was that one among the ten lepers cleansed by the Saviour, the foreigner and of another race; but the other is joined to the band of the ungrateful, not praising his benefactor according to his power, as these too, who received the cleansing of the disease but did not give glory to Christ who had given them this cleansing.

⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 2. ⟦s2⟧ So then we must not keep silent, but thine encomium must be spoken by us with longing, even if we speak it far behind thy greatness; so that we may not reap the reproach of those nine, but may seize the praiseworthy fellowship of him who was judged grateful. ⟦s3⟧ Cry out then, O Baptist, now too in us, as in the desert before. ⟦s4⟧ For we too have become a desert of word and of voice, and of the radiance that makes these sprout; out of which and through which the crown of praise is plaited for thee. ⟦s4⟧ Cry out now too in us more loudly. ⟦s5⟧ For we shall cry out if thou shouldst cry out, and we shall be silent if thou shouldst be silent, even if we had chosen to cry out with a great voice. ⟦s6⟧ For our cry will be reckoned as nothing, if it does not take its strength from thy more divine cry; and nowhere on earth will our voice be heard, unless we receive its fine sound from thy great voice. ⟦s7⟧ Wherefore more and more we call thee to our alliance, and beg thee to loose our tongue that is enslaved to voicelessness, as thou didst loose it long ago for Zacharias thy father when thou wast born; and we beseech thee to give a voice for the proclaiming of thy praise, as at thy birth thou gavest it to him for the public declaring of thy name. ⟦s8⟧ For if he was fettered in his tongue until thy coming forth loosed it, and the going forth of thy name set it moving, how should we move our tongue to thy praise, [3325] [b: A] unless thou move and turn it, and direct it to thy own course?

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 3. ⟦s2⟧ But my speech, having come to the starting-line of my words, and astounded at the multitude of thy wonders, is held fast by a greater perplexity, whence it should begin the narrative concerning thee. ⟦s3⟧ Should it tell first of the birth which Gabriel announced beforehand as to be a prelude of joy to the world? ⟦s4⟧ Or the pregnancy which the barren old woman hid as a thing beyond nature? ⟦s5⟧ Or the leaping in the womb, the first, which proclaimed to all God in the womb? ⟦s6⟧ Or did it desire to recount the bearing from a barren woman? ⟦s7⟧ But she also accomplished a three days' journey, and remained for a space of three months, that, having beheld thy wonderful birth, she might believe firmly and surely that she, the unwedded one, would be mother of One of the Trinity — was not conceived [?] —? ⟦s8+9+10⟧ Or the suckling, which a breast dry with deep old age paradoxically poured forth for thee from its nipple? ⟦s8+9+10⟧ Or the way of life in the desert, which the world could not contain; the showing from the desert to Israel, last and first, through which he revealed the Christ to them, and preached the kingdom of heaven? ⟦s8+9+10⟧ Or the Jordan, in which thou didst cleanse the people, and through which Christ cleansed thee, taking thee and making thee the minister of his own baptism? ⟦s11⟧ The lawgiving which thou didst ordain with love for man for those who obeyed? ⟦s12⟧ The threat which thou didst weightily set before the disobedient? ⟦s13⟧ The boldness by which thou didst strike rulers and kings with dismay, and which king Herod rightly dreaded, even if through passion for a mere woman he cast off his fear? ⟦s14⟧ The noble beheading, and the taking off of the precious head, by which thou madest thyself the twofold Forerunner of the Saviour, not only going forth and running before him on earth, but also becoming Forerunner in Hades to those who were waiting and remaining there, as to those on earth, proclaiming beforehand his saving remission?

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 4. ⟦s2⟧ But which of thy deeds shall we come to first, or which shall we leave for second? ⟦s3⟧ Each has pre-eminence as its share, and contends with reason to be set in the prelude and to receive in itself the end of the whole present encomiastic discourse, and does not allow the tongue to be carried off to some other narrative, lest it be left by it incomplete and unfilled. ⟦s4+5+6⟧ But it seems good to us if we too should keep the order of time, and what is first in time should now be first in our words also; that it may receive its fitting order, offering a fitting and brief narrative to each, and may escape the confusion of chronological interchange: praising in this Laban the crafty, because he did not endure to betroth in marriage the lesser of his daughters before the elder, and that though she was desired and preferred by him who married her, not for the sake of bodily beauty alone, but of the soul's beauty too? ⟦s4+5+6⟧ So then let our speech be brought back again to the beginning of the speech, and to the conception of the one admired, if [?] the prelude of his passage into being, and let it set his praise in order through all things successively, as he himself who is praised by us may [3328] nod consent to furnish us the speech. ⟦s4+5+6⟧ And perhaps it is not unprofitable to carry the speech higher even than this; for so too it would show him, even before this conception in the womb, as one who would be great and would appear in the expectation of greater good things, in view of our fall in Adam, a fall not unknown. ⟦s4+5+6⟧ For through this we fared wretchedly in every way, and ended in earth and corruption, all of us having yielded once for all to the sentence, «Earth thou art, and unto earth shalt thou depart»: for this was the condemnation of our nature for our disobedience. ⟦s7⟧ And this wretchedness of human life was not the only thing shown, but it had godlessness mingled with it. ⟦s8⟧ For, leaving the Creator and Master of all, we worshipped creatures, our fellow-slaves, and named as gods the works of our own hands. ⟦s9⟧ Wherefore also we were enslaved to the passions of dishonour, since to dishonourable idols and demons we offered the honour of the only honourable God. ⟦s10⟧ This was the sickness of our nature, which, admitting no excess beyond its greatness, needed the greatest healing. ⟦s11⟧ And this was: that the Creator be made like to us his creatures, and God become man like us through the composition with human flesh; and take up in himself the natural sufferings of all of us, those that bring no reproach; and show forth this complete distinction, and so manifestly save the man who willingly had been and was being held fast by these, and lead him back to the first blessedness, which he had joined to him when he fashioned him from the beginning.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 5. ⟦s2⟧ This God promised first to Abraham to do, and granted that in his seed all the tribes of the earth should be blessed, through the incarnation, from him, of his only-begotten Son and Word; this, after Abraham, he swore to David, and covenanted to set from the fruit of his womb the King of glory upon his most royal throne, when the present age should come to its end, and the time determined by him for the written law should receive its fulfilment. ⟦s3⟧ This not Abraham and David alone, who were deemed worthy to receive such promises as their lot, but also many prophets and kings after them desired to see and behold, as in the Gospels Christ himself, the desire of all the saints, declared to the blessed assembly of his own disciples. ⟦s4⟧ The last things of the present age, then, were at hand; and the promise was receiving its proper outcome for each, and the law the fulfilment expected for it through Christ; and all the prophets and righteous in Jerusalem, awaiting the redemption of Israel, and learning by the Spirit that it was at hand, longed to behold its presence, not only with the eyes of the soul but also with those of the body, before they should depart from the present life. ⟦s5⟧ And not only did they long, but they also prayed, and offered petitions to God, and obtained what they asked (for they were worthy). ⟦s6⟧ Such was Simeon, who as a priest received Christ in his arms, and confessed him Master of all [3329] [b: A] creation. ⟦s7⟧ Such was Anna, who decently completed that long widowhood, and night and day attended on God in the temple; who then, standing by in the prophetic Spirit, showed forth Christ present to those then present. ⟦s8⟧ Such was Joseph of Arimathaea, the daring burier of the life of all, and for this reason proclaimed the more a seemly counsellor, because he refused the counsel of the Jews against the Saviour as shameful and unseemly. ⟦s9⟧ «For this man, it says, had not consented to their counsel and their judgement.» ⟦s10+11+12⟧ But this counsel he alone, as splendid and seemly, counselled seemly and splendidly. ⟦s10+11+12⟧ Such too was Zacharias known to be, both elder and priest, who, because of the overripeness of his age and of the death that follows on old age [?], brought to God earnest and frequent petitions, not to be deprived of the sight expected by all, nor, before the sight of it, to behold the death that was looked for and stood at the doors. ⟦s10+11+12⟧ This petition the elder always brought: on the one hand praying that the light of salvation might quickly shine out for all; on the other asking for himself too to see the dawning of this light. ⟦s13⟧ And, lest he be snatched away without a share in this sight at the very time when it was flowering, he made unceasing intercession about this, and especially when he offered to God the worships according to the law, that the change of these to the more spiritual and more perfect might come quickly, and the courses that had the same yoke laid upon them might be redeemed from its burden: in the season of their course, the entry with incense into the Holy of Holies, where once a year the high priest alone used to run in, bringing blood in his hands, and plainly showing in the riddle the one and only offering that was to be of Christ the Saviour, which he, high priest and lamb, offered to himself, to God and the Father, for us men, unto slaughter.
