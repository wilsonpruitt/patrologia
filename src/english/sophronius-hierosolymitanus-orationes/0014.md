⟦p1⟧ ⟦s1⟧ [3287] ON THE HYPAPANTE, OR THE MEETING OF THE LORD *From a Medicean Greek manuscript of the King of France. Edited by the Bollandists, Acta Sanctorum, February, vol. I, p. 274.* ⟦p2⟧ ⟦s1⟧ ⟦p3⟧ ⟦s1+s2+s3+s4+s5+s6⟧

⟦p4⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Chapter 1. ⟦s1⟧ The mysteries of Christ to be celebrated. ⟦s2⟧ The solemnity of this feast.

⟦p5⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 1. ⟦s2⟧ This is another mystery of Christ, this another magnificence of Christ our God. ⟦s3⟧ For his miracles are always great, so that they overcome the approaches and accesses of the orations of all, and exceed the power of all wisdom, not of human wisdom only but also of the angelic, and of those Virtues which do not fall under sight. ⟦s4⟧ For a certain speech and tongue, which cannot be comprehended by us men, has been given apart to the heavenly angels. ⟦s5⟧ Wherefore I too will cry out with a great voice with the great Paul: «Where is the wise? ⟦s6⟧ Where is the scribe? ⟦s7+8⟧ Where is the disputer of this world?» [n: I Cor. i, 20.] Where are the ornaments of the rhetors? ⟦s9⟧ Where the problems of the philosophers? ⟦s10⟧ Where the elegant arts of the grammarians? ⟦s11+12+13+14+15+16⟧ Surely God has made foolish the wisdom of this world, and has shown it superfluous and vain, and useless — and the more for this very reason, that this wisdom too was a gift and a boon of grace, given to the unbelieving nations for [3288] the observation and inquiry of God, and for coming to their senses from vain error. ⟦s11+12+13+14+15+16⟧ But «because in the wisdom of the world the world knew not God, it pleased God by the foolishness of preaching to save them that believe.» [n: I Cor. i, 21.] And these things I rightly cry with Paul, and, wondering still even now, I will exclaim: «O the depth of the riches and of the wisdom and of the knowledge of God! ⟦s11+12+13+14+15+16⟧ How incomprehensible are his judgments, and his ways unsearchable! ⟦s11+12+13+14+15+16⟧ For no one has known the mind of the Lord, nor has anyone been his counsellor.» [n: Rom. xi, 33, 34.] For how should a created thing see the mind of the Maker, or be able to comprehend his genius and understanding? ⟦s17⟧ Or how should the thing formed give counsel to him who formed it, as though he committed himself to the counsel of the thing formed, and stood in need of the imperfect for perfection? ⟦s18⟧ Therefore among the unwise what is preached is foolishness, because the wisdom of men has been made foolish. ⟦s19⟧ Therefore the Academy has fallen asleep, the Stoa has fallen silent, the Peripatos stands empty, the Lyceum [3289] [b: A] sleeps, Athens has been trodden underfoot: so that they should not, forsaking God the Maker of all, make gods of the things created by him. ⟦s20⟧ For these wage war against God and the wisdom of God, which judge that the things which are seen are greater than God, and do not wish to see God their Maker. ⟦s21⟧ Therefore they have been consigned to silence, and cast into the depth of oblivion.

⟦p6⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 2. ⟦s2⟧ Hence rather Nazareth is proclaimed, in which the good tidings of God were recited; hence Bethlehem is foretold, where God is born in the flesh […] where God, working miracles, passed his life. ⟦s3⟧ Hence Golgotha is foreshown, where God takes up the cross; hence the resurrection is sung, in which God rises again from the tomb; hence Sion is proclaimed, and the sides of the north are foreshown, [n: Psal. xlvii, 3.] where Christ, when he had risen from the dead, appears; hence the Mount of Olives is touched with glory, from which God returns to heaven with the human nature. ⟦s4⟧ The former things have been taken away, and others brought in; those made foolish, these shown forth; those reduced to ashes, these made illustrious; those justly despised, because they did not honour God; these fittingly, because they gave God glory as was due, commended in turn to glory. ⟦s5⟧ For those were a stumbling to men, drew them to destruction, bore them down into the pit of hell; but these are salvation to men, and lead them back from death to life, and carry them up from earth to heaven. ⟦s6⟧ Those having been driven into oblivion, these we now worship with a feast; these on this present day we all celebrate together; into these, as is meet, we are initiated. ⟦s6⟧ For they are feasts and mysteries of God, which, as they are fittingly performed according to the condition of men, so they themselves mystically perfect him who performs them.

⟦p7⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 3. ⟦s2+3+4+5+6⟧ But what this mystery now is (for the present celebration is of it), or what this magnificence of Christ is (for this pomp and procession is of it), someone may at once ask us, either held by unbelief, or even affecting ignorance of religion; for to the sons of the Church, and to those initiated by the Holy Spirit, all things that concern Christ are manifest and known, and nothing of the things that are performed is unknown. ⟦s2+3+4+5+6⟧ But I will both cry aloud, and speak out, and proclaim, the things that are proper to this feast. ⟦s2+3+4+5+6⟧ «For I am not ashamed of the Gospel of Christ,» which Paul cried before us; but through it I cover with shame the pride of the wise who are outside. ⟦s2+3+4+5+6⟧ «For the power of God, which cannot be comprehended, is unto salvation to everyone that believes.» [n: Rom. i, 16.] And indeed how could it come about that I should blush to bring forth the ineffable might of the Godhead? ⟦s2+3+4+5+6⟧ Let them not accuse our mysteries in vain, the Aristagorases, the Anaxagorases, the Anaximanders, the Py[3290]thagorases, the Aristotles, the Platos, most wise defenders of a wisdom now corrupted, who do not know what they say, and cannot understand what they assert; whom with good reason both the fisherman's reed has censured with its mark, and the knife of the leather-cutter's craft has mown down, and has shown them to be dead and senseless offscourings of the earth. ⟦s2+3+4+5+6⟧ But these being dismissed from our wise celebration (seeing that they have both driven the wisdom of God out of themselves, and filled themselves with an empty and vain foolishness), let us all, with a theological mouth, sing of the wonders and the things rightly done by God, and celebrate them with pure minds. ⟦s2+3+4+5+6⟧ For this is most of all fitting to the divine feasts of God, and he requires this of those who celebrate his feasts.

⟦p8⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 4. ⟦s2⟧ That eternal Christ was born in the flesh, and after our manner took to himself a mother, who bore him indubitably and ineffably; and her we, who have gathered salvation from her, lately honoured mystically on a festal day. ⟦s3⟧ After which the eighth day duly received the expected circumcision of the flesh; and then, as initiates and clients of God, and as those who have received the divine gifts of God, we again mystically celebrated this. ⟦s4⟧ But now another mystery succeeds those, and gratifies mortals with another gift. ⟦s5⟧ For nothing proceeds from God born that is not wholly referred to the advantage of men: surely from desire of lifting up the humble and abject, he was born for the sake of us men. ⟦s6⟧ But what is this mystery too, which now, as befits God, we celebrate? ⟦s7⟧ The procession of Christ, most admirable; the coming of Christ, most full of felicity; the offering of Christ, most difficult to explain; the passing from place to place of Christ, going forth from most divine Bethlehem, and arriving in this city of Jerusalem.

⟦p9⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 5. ⟦s2⟧ Who can tell forth this migration of God from one place into another? ⟦s3⟧ Wherefore, O men filled with the same wisdom as we, ineffable are the mysteries of Jesus Christ, and of such a kind that the river of speech cannot embrace them. ⟦s4⟧ For how will one, hearing of the procession of the uncircumscribed God, and of the passing of the same from some places into others, not at once laugh at the speaker, as one bringing into the minds of men things incredible and in no way to be received? ⟦s5⟧ Yet the uncircumscribed goes forth (let the gentiles find fault with this proclamation, its folly marked), he goes forth who does not endure natural bounds (since he is God), nay, he who is infinite in nature and in power (for this nature is finite, and of finite power) goes forth after the manner of us men: let him burst, who says that the Son who went forth is one, and — he [3291] raves — another who did not go forth, and so that wretch Nestorius dreams up for us a pair of sons and of Christs. ⟦s6⟧ He rather both persuades himself of it out of himself, impiously, and with unspeakable daring tears the mystery of Christ apart. ⟦s6⟧ For he would have the one born of the Virgin alone go forth, and not the Son born of the Father, that everlasting Word. ⟦s7⟧ But God made a true and manifest procession. ⟦s8⟧ For he had truly been made flesh and body, and had been comprehended by bodily circumscription: let him be exasperated who denies the nature of flesh in the Word, which was made flesh, and dreams that the procession was a mere phantom — the wretched Eutyches. ⟦s9⟧ For if the nature of the Word which was made flesh had remained single, what appeared would have been a phantom, and this mystery of Christ would be a bare shadow, and the Word not truly incarnate. ⟦s10⟧ For how was the Word made flesh without change, if it did not assume the nature of flesh? ⟦s11⟧ But the Word was in very truth made flesh, and in no way underwent conversion into flesh; and it received from the inviolate Virgin the nature of flesh, according to which it was also begotten from her. ⟦s12⟧ The same both admits no dissolution of the natures, and is made known in two natures: not divided on account of their difference, as the mad Nestorius raved; nor confounding the essence of those whose union was made in hypostasis. ⟦s13⟧ So one and the same, and existing in the same, is made known as God and man, displays a single person, constituted, consisting of both natures; yet not confounding the things that were proper to both: for in those from which he was compounded without change, he appears without division, neither confounding his mystery nor dividing his person.

⟦p10⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Chapter 2. ⟦s2⟧ The meeting of Christ. ⟦s3⟧ The lights brought together. ⟦s4⟧ The day of purification.

⟦p11⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 6. ⟦s2⟧ So that heavenly one of Bethlehem goes forth; so he appears visible, who escapes the keenness of the eyes; so he who is without body is shown in the flesh; so he comes circumscribed, who cannot be circumscribed. ⟦s2⟧ But let us all run to meet him, who so piously honour and venerate his mystery; let us all go with an eager mind. ⟦s3⟧ Who will meet him first? ⟦s4⟧ Who will first behold God with his eyes? ⟦s4⟧ Who will first receive God? ⟦s5+6+7⟧ Who will first carry God in his arms? ⟦s5+6+7⟧ Let no one be loath of a hastened course, let no one limp at a quickened step, let no one break off the pious going-out, let no one be slow [?] to the divine meeting; let no one be seen without part in the celebration once tasted, no one appear without share in the mysteries, no one be deprived of the light-bearing gladness; let no one grow more sluggish at the most swift running of the old man Simeon, no one be judged slower of foot than that aged woman Anna; lest in any way the old men, truly of great age, should not only reproach him whom they see failing in his feet [3292] and left behind in the swift course, but should also upbraid him with prophetic words for a slack mind, perhaps an unbelieving one too. ⟦s5+6+7⟧ Let no one, then, be without share in the meeting, no one a stranger to the carrying of the light.

⟦p12⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 7. ⟦s2⟧ We add also the splendour of candles, both showing the divine splendour of him who approaches, from whom all things shine, and, the worst darkness driven away, are illumined by the abundance of eternal light; and, most of all, manifesting the splendour of the soul with which we must meet Christ. ⟦s3⟧ For as the Mother of God and most pure Virgin carried the true light in her arms, and came to those who lay in darkness; so we too, illumined by his illuminations, and holding in our hands the light that appears to all, let us hasten to go to meet him who is truly light. ⟦s4⟧ For indeed, because the light came into the world, and illumined it, wrapped round with darkness, [n: Joan. iii, 19.] and because the Dayspring from on high has visited us, and has shone upon those who sat in darkness; [n: Luc. i, 78.] this is our mystery; for this reason we walk holding lamps, for this reason we run up carrying lights, both so signifying the light that has shone upon us, and hinting at the splendour that will come to us from him. ⟦s5⟧ Wherefore let us run together, let us all meet God; lest, if we are loath to do it, we be accused by him one day either of an ungrateful mind, or of the fearful crime of having despised him; and lest we hear what the Jews too, wrapped in that darkness and not to be illumined, had heard him say before: «Because the light has come into the world, and men loved darkness rather than light: for their works were evil» [n: Joan. iii, 19.] (for malice always darkens the mind, and does not let it see the light); or even that (which the holy Gospel says): «The light shines in darkness, and the darkness comprehended it not.» [n: Joan. i, 5.] So that true light, which enlightens every man coming into this world, [n: Joan. i, 9.] has come. ⟦s6+7+8⟧ All of us, then, O brethren, let us be enlightened; let us all be illumined. ⟦s6+7+8⟧ Let none of us remain uninitiated in this splendour, none persist filled with that night; but let us all go forth shining; illumined, let us all go to meet him together, and receive that bright and everlasting light [?] with the old man Simeon: and, exulting with him in our souls, let us sing a hymn with thanksgiving to the Begetter and Father of lights, who sent the true light, and drove away the gloom, and made us all shining. ⟦s6+7+8⟧ For the salvation of God, which he prepared before the face of all peoples, and manifested for the glory of us, the new Israel, we too have seen through him, and have been at once released from that old and dark crime, as Simeon, when he had seen Christ, from the bonds of the present life. ⟦s9⟧ We too, having embraced by faith Christ, who set out from Bethlehem to us, have become, out of the nations, the people of God (for he is the salvation of God and the Father), and with our eyes we have [3293] beheld God made flesh; and, the presence of God having been seen and admitted into the arms of the mind, we are called the new Israel; and this presence, never to come into forgetfulness of it, we celebrate in yearly feasts.

⟦p13⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 8. ⟦s2⟧ But does Christ, who is present everywhere, approach? ⟦s3⟧ Does the light that fills all things proceed into the midst? ⟦s4⟧ For nothing of his — since his nature is not circumscribed, and can be shut out from no thing, and he wills to fulfil the ancient law — is empty of brightness. ⟦s5⟧ Nay rather, he does approach, and, foreshowing his own wholly supreme birth, he prophesied of himself by the mouth of Moses. ⟦s6⟧ And what is this prophecy? ⟦s7⟧ «Every male that opens the womb,» it says, «shall be called holy to the Lord.» [n: Exod. xiii, 2; Luc. ii, 23.] Which precept indeed, but more a prophetic announcement, he alone fulfilled, when he alone looked forth from the womb of the Virgin, and yet did not deprive her of her virginity, and did not loose her bond as he came forth: which had been plainly foretold of him alone. ⟦s8⟧ For he alone was born of a Virgin alone, and alone was begotten from a womb without experience of seed, and alone, coming forth from the Virgin's womb, kept the Virgin herself: being born without corrupting her grace, coming into the light without lessening her splendour, going forth without loosing her bond: by which things they both show that he who is born is God, and declare that she who bears is properly Theotokos. ⟦s9⟧ Where the wicked Nestorius is seen again, who neither wishes to call him who is born God, nor endures to call her who bore him Theotokos: where the execrable Eutyches too is beheld, who denies to the Lord flesh of the same nature as ours. ⟦s10⟧ For in what way was the Word, which endured to become flesh for our sake, «made flesh», [n: Joan. i, 14.] if the nature of flesh was neither truly assumed by him from us, nor so joined to him as to show clearly that the Word, before without body, took up the same nature as we, and so truly became man with us — if he has neither a body of the same kind as our bodies, nor a soul the same as that of which our natures consist? ⟦s11⟧ But truly «the Word made flesh» wishes to save us: and God, existing in it, truly made man, displays nothing false, nor did he deceive us men; but, having taken up our nature, he graciously gave us his own.

⟦p14⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 9. ⟦s2+3+4⟧ For if this were not so as we believe, how does the Evangelist who writes these things say: «And after the days of their purification were fulfilled»? [n: Luc. ii, 22.] And of what purification, O abominable Eutyches, if the flesh in Christ was not true? if the human nature was not united to the Word? ⟦s2+3+4⟧ For the divine nature, without body, what purification does it need? ⟦s2+3+4⟧ What contamination is beheld in the Godhead without flesh? ⟦s2+3+4⟧ Although, as the condition of men demanded, this was [3294] [b: A] written in humbler wise, most of all on account of thee, and thy imaginary madness, and thy stupor, thunderstruck like that of the Manichaeans. ⟦s2+3+4⟧ For neither did Christ, who is the expiator of all, need any expurgation, especially as that flesh alone immaculate had been taken; nor did she who bore him, the truly inviolate and undefiled Virgin, who bore the expiation of all, need the expiation prescribed through the law. ⟦s5⟧ Although this was ascribed to them according to the law, lest Christ should be thought a dissolver of the law.

⟦p15⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Chapter 3. ⟦s2⟧ The orthodox are admitted to the meeting; the heretics are repelled.

⟦p16⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 10. ⟦s2⟧ But what did she carry in her inviolate arms, or what did Simeon receive in his, but God made flesh and body? ⟦s3⟧ And the prophet both received God with religious affection, and offered him to the Father and God, and fulfilled the law in spirit, saying with grateful lips: «Now thou lettest thy servant depart, O Lord, according to thy word, in peace.» [n: Luc. ii, 31.] Which had been told him by the Spirit: that he would not see the approach of death unless first he saw the Christ of the Lord, and the bond of the law were loosed. [n: ibid. 26.] For that old man bore the person of the law. ⟦s4+5+6⟧ «For,» he says, «mine eyes have seen thy Salvation.» ⟦s4+5+6⟧ For that Mosaic law was not Salvation. ⟦s4+5+6⟧ But the law, old and aged and weak, when Christ was seen, that is, the Salvation of God, was both brought back from weakness to salvation; and, renewed fittingly by newness, was freed from the oldness that was in it: for the light that frees old Israel from all oldness, and leads the nations to him, was beheld with the eyes. ⟦s7⟧ Therefore he cries, and crying declares: «Now thou lettest thy servant depart, O Lord, according to thy word, in peace; for mine eyes have seen thy Salvation, which thou hast prepared before the face of all peoples, a light for the revelation of the gentiles, and the glory of thy people Israel.» [n: Luc. ii, 29.] Being new out of old (both Galilee of the nations is illumined, and the new Israel is shown forth), he beheld Christ, the light.

⟦p17⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 11. ⟦s2⟧ But what did he see with his eyes too, but a body akin to us and visible, and convicting the ignorance of Eutyches, produced from Manichaean seed? ⟦s3⟧ For by its own nature the Godhead escapes sight, and cannot be seen by the eyes of men. ⟦s4⟧ Let Eutyches and Nestorius, then, both go out from this our celebration, enemies and adversaries of the same, striving to violate its gladness: the one dividing Christ the undivided, the other impiously confounding the things that cannot be confounded; for never does our celebration suffer itself to be adorned by such men. ⟦s5⟧ Therefore be purified, therefore shine, therefore honour the mysteries of Christ as friends of Christ; for otherwise he neither admits those who run to meet him, nor endures to see them with his eyes: but from his celebration [3295] he bids them be exiles, and sets them down as profane and without share in the meeting with him. ⟦s6⟧ Let there depart with these the Eunomii and Eudoxii and Asterii, and with them the Manichaeans and the Apollinarii, the Severi and the Dioscori, and all that wretched and truly headless gangrene of the Acephali. ⟦s7⟧ The former, because, on account of the visible flesh of Christ, they tried to diminish and abolish his invisible and perfect Godhead too, and to make it of another nature, and to contend that it is different in essence from the nature which is in the Father who begot, boldly numbering it among created things, and decreeing that Christ God is created, and is to be adored according to both natures, the divine, I say, and the human. ⟦s8⟧ The latter, on account of that Godhead of Christ which is not seen, wish to make a mockery of his flesh too, and to falsify it: who, snatching away the things proper to one nature only, imagine a confusion of the conjoined essences, and gladly accept the wretched juggleries of Apollinarius and Polemon. ⟦s9⟧ With whom let the Ebionites and the Samosatenes, Paul's and Bardesanes', be placed, and be driven from this our feast. ⟦s10⟧ These, because they thought Christ only a bare man and like us, and lacking the things proper to the Godhead, and did not even name him God in any way. ⟦s11⟧ Those, because they affirmed that Christ was not eternal God and equal to the Father in eternity, but, as they supposed, that he became God from Mary only, from the ineffable incarnation itself.

⟦p18⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 12. ⟦s2+3⟧ And if indeed all, as many as either raved impiously after them, or turned out insanely impious after the same, are driven back from the divine meeting of Christ, so that we who think rightly, all keeping this feast day, without having taken any stain of heresy in its many kinds, may go to meet Christ God, and [3296] receive him with the old man Simeon and with the aged woman and prophetess Anna — for both were growing antiquated, the law, namely, and the prophets who followed it, and were awaiting their dismissal. ⟦s4⟧ For up to the showing of Christ, filled with lights, both the law held sway, and the prophets, prophesying, foretold the mysteries wisely performed by God concerning Christ, and the wondrous oracles about Christ. ⟦s5⟧ We, translated from the multitude of the heathen into the new Israel, and called the new people of God, let us confess in turn, and, singing psalms, «Let us sing to the Lord a new song.» [n: Psal. xcvii, 1.] We, beautifully renewed, and lifted on high by the presence of Christ, having cast off all the splendour and oldness, gentile and legal alike, and all that which savours of the flesh and is unworthy of this most bright celebration of Christ, let us sing to the Lord a new song. ⟦s6⟧ For we have been renewed, and made new out of old, and bidden to sing a new song to God and the Father, who by the presence of Christ both renewed us all and showed us to be his new people. ⟦s7⟧ «Let us sing to the Lord a new song: for he has done wonderful things» — and more wonderful indeed through the presence of Christ, through which all things are renewed, the old things lost are recovered, new things are beheld, and things lifted up to God are carried over into their first youth.

⟦p19⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 13. ⟦s2+3⟧ And what these things are, the lyre of the Spirit plainly hands on: «His right hand,» it says, «has saved him, and his holy arm.» [n: Psal. xcvii, 1.] And what, I ask, was this right hand of God and the begetter, and the holy arm, but he alone, the Son, through whom he made all things? ⟦s2+3⟧ Through whom he created the things that before were not, and brought the non-existent to existence: which afterwards, creeping toward destruction and oldness (for each thing, growing old and aging with it, always tends toward destruction from the condition of its own nature), [3297] he saves too with his almighty right hand and his lofty and in every way holy arm. ⟦s2+3⟧ For they confess, enriched with the splendour of divine knowledge, and brilliantly purified both in body and in soul and in intellect, that Christ alone is, and is known to be, he who through the likeness of flesh came to us for the recovery of all created things; through whom we are both manifestly saved, and taken out of our ancient oldness, and redeemed, and made for God a new lump, [n: I Cor. v, 7.] showing ourselves now [?] of the old and sour leaven.

⟦p20⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 14. ⟦s2⟧ For we truly are, and are called, creatures preserved in an unexpected manner by the right hand of God, and renewed by his mighty arm; of whom David, prophesying, tenderly sang as he played: «His right hand has saved him, and his holy arm»: since we needed salvation, and sought retreat and redemption from deadly oldness: to whom, needing salvation at the late coming of Christ, [sic: *serum*] the Psalmist assigns the glorious strengthening of the right hand and arm of the Father for renewal, as is set forth in this divinely inspired song: «The Lord has made known his salvation: in the sight of the nations he has revealed his justice.» [n: ibid. 3] Which Simeon too (that is, the law) receiving, cried out to God, who had graciously granted him the salvation, that is, Christ: «Now thou lettest thy servant depart, O Lord, according to thy word, in peace.» ⟦s2⟧ For Christ is truly signified as peace to things heavenly and things earthly. ⟦s3⟧ «For mine eyes have seen thy Salvation, which thou hast prepared before the face of all peoples, a light for the revelation of the gentiles, and the glory of thy people Israel.» ⟦s3⟧ The prophecy is fulfilled; the promise perfected; that which wandered is illumined; that which was inglorious is endowed with glory; and I (and in me the law) have gone away from oldness, and not only have come out new, but also, having beheld thy Salvation, O Lord, and gazed with aged eyes upon thy glory, have recovered a vigour wholly free of wrinkle, and youthful. ⟦s4⟧ For Christ became a light to the gentiles, and Israel, newly begotten and called new, he endowed with excellent and wondrous glory, imparting to it a glory truly free of old age, and illumining it with a light that never grows old, and does not work wrinkles from the law.

⟦p21⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Chapter 4. ⟦s2⟧ The prophecies of Simeon and Anna explained.

⟦p22⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 15. ⟦s2⟧ And that aged Simeon, having seen the salvation of God, was conversing with him in these grateful words, carrying in his arms that Salvation of God and the Father, by which he sent salvation to all and gave an excellent redemption. ⟦s3⟧ But turning also to the Virgin, who had generated and borne the Salvation of God in the flesh, and to Joseph, who was thought the father of this light, yet was made known to be wholly a stranger to the fleshly birth of the light: «Behold,» he said, «this one is set for the fall and the rising of many in Israel»; [n: ibid. 34.] for the fall [3298] of the sons of bondage, for the rising of the sons of grace; for the fall of those still worshipping the legal oldness, for the rising of those loving the evangelic newness; for the fall of those glorying in the fleshly fatherhood of Abraham, for the rising of those made sons of Abraham through faith; for the fall of those clothed in the prudence of the old Adam, for the rising of those who savour the garment of the new Adam; for the fall of those who savour things dusty and earthly, for the rising of those who desire things lofty and heavenly. ⟦s4⟧ For the first Adam, made from earth, dusty, had an earthly affection. ⟦s5⟧ But this second Adam, who even in flesh is carried in my hands, and himself divinely carries me who carry him, has come to us as a stranger from heaven, and on all who faithfully admit his coming, and spurn the garment of the first Adam but desire the birth of the new, he bestows a heavenly enrolment. ⟦s6⟧ For as many as have received him with faith, to them he grants an immortal adoption of sons with God and the Father, [n: Joan. i, 12.] who are neither begotten of the will of the flesh, nor constituted of the will of man, but are born of God according to grace: for they do not come forth from his essence and nature.

⟦p23⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 16. ⟦s2⟧ For he alone, O inviolate Virgin Mother, was born of God according to nature, and is consubstantial with God the Father: he alone, born of God the begetter, is acknowledged as his only Son; and surely, incarnate of thee the Virgin, he took up a human generation from thee. ⟦s3⟧ And thy own soul — thou who hast both beheld the great mysteries of God and hast borne for us this treasure of grace — a two-edged sword shall pierce, [n: Luc. ii, 35.] and the blade shall run through thy mind struck with stupor, when thou shalt behold him with thine eyes willingly fixed with nails to a cross, and lifted up in the midst between robbers, to slay death itself, by which we were slain; and to give life to us, who were justly dying; and, binding the legions of demons, to loose from the bonds of sins man, bound of old by the demon. ⟦s4⟧ But that piercing sword will not stay, nor at all remain in thee: for never, O Mother of God, wilt thou be given over to forgetfulness of the divine conception from thee, and of the wondrous generation from thee. ⟦s5⟧ Thou wilt be seized indeed with a certain human stupor at the things that will come to pass at the time of the unspeakable Crucifixion: how God, who by nature can neither die nor even suffer, endures to be driven to the cross; how he who is extolled by angels with everlasting glory is himself, in the midst of robbers, treated with insults; how he who gives food to all the hungry, and who gives drink to all the thirsty, drinks gall and vinegar; how that life, which gives life to all who lack it, undergoes slaughter; how he is pierced in the side by a spear, who with a lance that escapes all sense of the eyes pierced death; how he who buries death and corruption, who takes the buried from their tombs, who to those that have received from Adam bodies, and carry them about, the same, [3299] dead because of the stain contracted from the same Adam, first parent, gives back life, raised from death — how he should bear a burial of three days! ⟦s5⟧ For thou wilt be somewhat shaken, seeing all these things: but thou wilt not long appear doubting; for the sword of shocks so great and so terrible will pierce thy soul and mind. ⟦s6⟧ But it will not stay; nor will it occupy a place of abiding or of station, being very swiftly driven off by that seedless conception of thine, and by that ever-virginal childbirth of thine.

⟦p24⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 17. ⟦s2⟧ Than these things nothing at all is either more horrible, or, to those who do not understand, more incredible to see. ⟦s3⟧ Therefore thoughts are revealed out of many hearts, [n: Luc. ii, 35.] which cannot behold thy divine and wondrous mysteries, which not even the powers of the heavens were able to see. ⟦s3⟧ Therefore he falls who does not believe the proclamation: but he rises again, and is illumined to life and immortality, who believes in thy offspring, and proclaims him God with his mouth, and holds thee, who gave birth, to be Theotokos. ⟦s4⟧ Therefore Christ, so born of thee, will truly be set, for those ignorant of his mysteries, for a sign which shall be spoken against; and they will suffer a fall from which they will not be able to be raised, made partakers of unbelief; and the rising from the dead, and the life which is in the heavens, because they did not believe in life eternal, they will never behold. ⟦s5⟧ Having spoken these things he ceases; and, beholding the lawgiver himself now arriving, and standing perfectly by the counsel of his own law, he at the same time halts the dominion of the law. ⟦s6⟧ For Christ came forth, the fulfilment of the law; and against the sentence of Paul there is no one to speak. ⟦s7⟧ Whence to the believer he is indeed unto salvation, but to him who has an unbelieving soul, unto destruction and ruin.

⟦p25⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 18. ⟦s2⟧ Anna too stands by at this work, advancing with aged steps. ⟦s3+4⟧ For the mystery called her too, to fulfil the grace of prophecy, while with a prophetic spirit she testifies that the Lord has made known his salvation, and that in the sight of the nations he has revealed his justice: and that the law is not, which prevailed in shadow and figure, laying on men burdens not to be borne, [n: Matth. xxiii, 4.] now that grace has been revealed to all, which has a sweet yoke, and a burden light and easy to carry. [n: Matth. xi, 30.] And that the law is not, the prophets prophesy of Christ, when he who inspired the prophecies in them, and sent them to prophesy, has himself now arrived. ⟦s5⟧ And this Anna, when she shone with the grace of prophecy, and spoke to those who were waiting for the redemption of Israel, [n: Luc. ii, 38.] engendered in them on every side a divine consolation: that the Redeemer of all had come, that the Saviour of the universe had appeared, who wills to redeem his image, and desires to confer salvation on man created by him. ⟦s6⟧ For this is why Christ's name is Jesus: since Jesus too is interpreted Saviour.

⟦p26⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 19. ⟦s2⟧ And when all these things were both done in very deed and published in words, Mary the Mother of God was astonished, and Joseph, present with her, wondered. ⟦s3⟧ But [3300] after they had fulfilled the things enjoined by the law (for Christ had come to fulfil all things that were of the law, and to put an end to the legal precepts), they return to Nazareth, where she had been greeted by Gabriel the archangel, she who bore the Saviour to the world, being with child of a seedless conception, and remaining a virgin after childbirth, and declaring to all who saw that the Salvation of God had been made known, that the light had shone upon the gentiles, that the justice and redemption of God had appeared to all the ends of the earth, and that divine glory and eternal brightness, knowing no destruction and corruption, had clothed those who waited for him.

⟦p27⟧ ⟦s1⟧ Chapter 5. The mystery of the turtledoves and doves offered. Epilogue.

⟦p28⟧ ⟦s1+2+3⟧ 20. ⟦s1+2+3⟧ But what do the turtledoves mean, or why were young pigeons taken, which Christ, going to Jerusalem to present himself to God his Begetter, brought for sacrifice? ⟦s1+2+3⟧ Or for what reason did he offer one pair of either kind, if she who bore him remained a virgin? ⟦s1+2+3⟧ He offered for himself to the Father these living creatures, which are found pure, and excellent among birds: and, besides that both are clean and not profane, each kind of them together contains a certain proper virtue; and those birds show that there is something excellent and choice, beyond the rest, innate in them, which brings no small profit to those who contemplate spiritually. ⟦s1+2+3⟧ For the precept of the law to be kept does not consist in the letter alone: it contains also something hidden, and more eminent than what appears, and excelling at an incomparable price. ⟦s4⟧ Therefore the letter kills me, if I follow the letter alone; but the Spirit gives life to him who obeys the spirit well; [n: II Cor. iii, 6.] and while the letter in a manner gapes open from its veil, the spirit perfects him who goes down into the depth and lays bare the treasure hidden there, and so has gained those infinite riches. ⟦s5⟧ Turtledoves and doves were pure birds, as has been said; and the turtledoves indeed with the highest temperance, which they so embrace that, if death befalls the mate, the survivor does not endure to come together with another, but passes the rest of its life celibate, and cultivates widowhood rather than suffer conjugal contact with another male. ⟦s6⟧ But the doves are most pure of malice, and free of all harm; whom therefore, for their simplicity and emptiness of malice, Christ calls simple; and who return soon even to their persecutors, as though having suffered no evil: which is a sign of the highest innocence, and, so to say, the token of much simplicity. ⟦s7⟧ And indeed the chaste turtledoves live private and solitary; but the harmless doves are not only tame and dwellers in houses, but also great lovers of men. ⟦s8+9⟧ Of this let that most lovable dove persuade us, returning to Noah into the ark, bringing an olive branch in its mouth, and declaring the mercy of the Lord and the end of the flood, [3301] but mystically, as seemed good to the Father and the Spirit, showing forth the kindly coming of Christ, which in the last times, through a love truly full of mercy, it showed. ⟦s8+9⟧ For then too it was evening, when to Noah, the second begetter of life [?] after Adam the author of the race, that leaf-bearing one flew back prophetically; and foretold the mercy of Christ God to come to us as the age declined.

⟦p29⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 21. ⟦s2⟧ Christ therefore most manifestly wills that those who come to him should above all have the purity of temperance, and savour the splendour of innocence, and be the more illumined by these, and should gladden Christ himself, who made them, and, by that mercy which he had toward men, came down to save the same; that he may both see and rejoice, and gladly receive them as they come together to meet him. ⟦s2⟧ For by no other thing is God so worshipped as by the perfection of temperance and chastity and all innocence. ⟦s3⟧ And it is honourable, sweet and quiet, and free from the heat of business, both to cleave to God, so far as that can be done, and to be entangled more eagerly in no thing, being, namely, withdrawn and torn away from worldly occupations: to whom also that song seems to say: «Be still, and see that I am God,» as though, ever loving, he would show his illumining holiness to those who will be free for him in a solitary life. ⟦s4⟧ With honourableness likewise there shine both gentleness, and communion and mutual love, by which one will be able to act rightly, and the mercy with which one is fittingly affected toward one's neighbour, and the humanity naturally joined with it; in which there comes about a very great likening to God himself.

⟦p30⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 22. ⟦s2⟧ But why did he bring two birds? ⟦s2⟧ For so the law commanded them to bring: because one and the same Christ is beheld twofold in natures, united indeed from two [3302] natures, and made known in two without division and confusion, the divine as well as the human, from which also he is composed. ⟦s3⟧ But we too ourselves, consisting of soul and body, are constituted twofold, and appear both in soul and in body, so that with both we may do what is honourable; and may not fulfil virtue with one part, but limp with the other, and lack the splendour of the virtues. ⟦s4⟧ And this the Lord himself, who is known as the good giver of all virtue, said when he taught: «Be ye perfect, as also your heavenly Father is perfect.» [n: Matth. v, 48.] For whatever is imperfect and unfulfilled is at once judged not acceptable to God either.

⟦p31⟧ ⟦s1⟧ 23. ⟦s2⟧ Knowing this, therefore, let us too, most beloved, so go together to meet Christ God: bringing temperance, carrying chastity, bearing innocence, showing forgetfulness of injuries, separating ourselves from worldly cares, and presenting ourselves pure to God, contributing gentleness of manners and kindness, and having mutual love toward all, and adding to all these a soul that pities all, and abounding in a compassionate mercy that proceeds from the soul; so let us receive Christ as he approaches, so let us behold Christ, so let us clasp Christ in our arms in a certain divine manner, and confess openly with prophetic words, praising his coming to us, and proclaim with our mouth and with great voices that mercy which he exercised toward us; that we may both obtain the kingdom of the heavens, and enjoy eternal goods in Christ himself, our Redeemer and Saviour God, with whom to God the best Father and to the most holy Spirit be glory, honour, and adoration from every side, both now and always and to the ages of ages. ⟦s2⟧ Amen.
