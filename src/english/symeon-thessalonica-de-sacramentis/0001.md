Wherefore, though myriads of celebrants offer each day and at the same time, one is the body [0184] [b: A] and blood, and one the sacrifice, and not many, for the Word of God is living and working, and everywhere, and above the universe.

*Cleric.* Wonderful are the things that have been said, master, and they have put ever so great a pleasure into my soul, and I have come to know more clearly the mystery of the Word of God's becoming man.

CHAPTER 42. Why the Word of God was not united to the better nature of the angels, but to that of men.

*Bishop.* These things are not ours, beloved, but the Fathers'. And thou wouldst find them in many places, examining the discourses of these men. And further consider this too, which affords at once a very great and a true knowledge of the divine mystery. Why the Word of God, having willed [b: B] to be united to his creatures, was not united to the better nature (that of the angels, I mean), which in some way stands as its own and akin toward it by reason of its immateriality, but to this our nature, the corruptible and material? According to reason, because it is most great and divine. For if he had been united to the angelic nature, it alone would have partaken of his communion, and it would not have been saved; for it is saved, but it would only have been exalted, and led up to something greater, having been deemed worthy of the communion of God. And all the other creation would have been profited nothing, but would have remained in corruption, and most of all man, who had fallen away, for whose sake this creation came to be, and through whom it fell under corruption. Consider, then, the wisdom of God, and his counsel, truly ancient, on account of which (I think) he made both the world and the angels [b: C] out of things that are not, and last of all man. For since he is good, and for this reason brought forth the creation, that the creation might partake of his good things, for he himself, being without need and full and before the ages, would not have needed anything from among the things that are, for he himself is the highest good, since the creation of God is intelligible and sensible, and the union of the two is man [d: intelligible and sensible, and the union of the two is man] alone, consisting of an intelligible soul and a sensible body, God takes this man alone, and is united to the utmost, having made the human nature subsist in himself sinlessly from the holy Virgin, by the Holy Spirit. And taking a body with an intellectual and volitive soul, through the intellectual part of the soul he is united to the intellectual nature, that of the angels, and through his [b: D] divine flesh to the creation that is according to sense. And thus the sanctification and the grace pass not into man only, who is intelligible from the soul and sensible from the body, but rightly pass also into the whole **65** creation; and the rational creation, that of the angels and of souls, is united to God, and perceives God, and has communion with him, and is made exceedingly bright, receiving in kindred fashion the divine light from the holy soul of the Word, which is intellectual and rational. For now angels behold that holy soul of the Saviour according to nature, and now receive more vividly the illumination from the invisible, and are filled with greater wisdom from the divine mind of the living Wisdom. And as in some way kindred they draw near to him, and are disposed; and they are filled with wonder; [0185] [b: A] and they are the more humble-minded, having been deemed worthy of such great things; and in like manner also the sanctified souls of the saints. And the sensible creation, together with our bodies, through that holy and deified, or rather, so to say, co-divine flesh, sacrificed for us on the cross and risen again, and having received incorruption, had the first-fruits of immortality, and has them. And, according to Paul, all this creation eagerly awaits incorruption. Which also shall come to pass when the incorruptible and living Word, with his flesh made incorruptible, is revealed together with us at his second and most dread coming; when also the angels will be myriads, cowering and trembling before his beauty, and at the same time rejoicing in the glory which they receive from him. And the elements [b: B] shall be altered, and the righteous shall shine forth as the sun, receiving their shining from the Sun of righteousness who has been united to us, and so shall we ever be with the Lord. Hast thou considered the mystery, O man, so far as there was room for us to speak of it, of the Saviour's becoming man?

*Cleric.* I have considered it and have marvelled, and glory to God who wrought these things on our behalf.

CHAPTER 43. That Christ also received the mysteries into himself.

*Bishop.* It remains necessary to say how the Saviour wrought the mysteries in himself also. And first, baptism, the first of his spiritual gifts: he himself was baptized in the [b: C] Jordan by John, first, not as himself needing it, for how would he who alone is pure? but for our sake, that we might be cleansed, and wash off the filth that comes from the flux and from the ancestral sin, and, having been fashioned from clay in the beginning, which is earth and water, that our body may be in place of the earth, and in holy water and Spirit we may be regenerated together with the soul, and be born a new and sinless man. And second, the chrism, that is, the divine oil and myron, the Saviour received in the baptism, the Spirit descending upon him in the form of a dove. And Isaiah, concerning this, speaks as if from Christ himself, «The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because of which he anointed me.» Concerning this Peter also says, [b: D] That God made him both Lord and Christ, bodily, that is, the Spirit having come upon him.
