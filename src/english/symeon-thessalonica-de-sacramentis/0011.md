And *Is baptized*, he says, and not, *I baptize*, as the Latins, innovating even in this — the bishop bearing witness to the willingness of the one being baptized. For the *I baptize* does not make plain that the one being illumined is baptized willingly. For it is possible, even by some compulsion and against his will, for the one baptizing to baptize him [0229] [b: A] of himself alone. But the «Is baptized,» signifies both the willingness, and that it is by me, the bishop or priest, who am already doing the work, and by the only God in Trinity, whose name too is clearly pronounced over the one being baptized. Thus, then, regenerated, the one baptized comes out wholly new and illumined and a son of God from the sacred font, our spiritual mother, which stands in place of the virginal pure and holy womb. For as Christ came from the Virgin, that he might cleanse the defiled birth, so we too from the pure font; and as there the pure blood and the holy belly of the Virgin in the Holy Spirit wrought the incarnation of the Word, so in us also the pure water of the font and the divine Spirit have accomplished the pure regeneration. Only, just as there, at the baptism of the [b: B] Saviour, there was also a descent of the Spirit, that he might show the whole fullness of the Godhead bodily in him who was made flesh, so in us also there is the chrism of the myron after the baptism, that we too may receive the grace of the Spirit. «For of his fullness, he says, have all we too received, and grace for grace.»

CHAPTER 65. What the chrism, that is, the myron, affords to the one being baptized.

Wherefore he also anoints him straightway with the divine myron, which is not oil alone, but is also compounded of very many other fragrant kinds, presenting in symbols too the manifold power, and the variety and multiformity of the operations of the gifts of the Spirit, [b: C] and the fragrance of his holiness. This, then, is given to us also as a seal and sign of Christ, since Christ too is named for this very reason [lat: the Latin gives *quoniam Christus ideo dixit se … habere*, since Christ therefore said that he had], that he had in himself, bodily also, the whole power of the Spirit from the Father. And Isaiah says this: «The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he hath anointed me.» And we too, having received the grace from him in the myron, are called Christians, and are christs of the Lord; for he does not disdain that we be called after him; because he who is Son by nature calls us sons of God by adoption, and he who is God by nature calls us gods by grace, and he who by nature has the grace in himself calls us christs in grace, as being **84** the Word of God the Father and undivided from the Spirit. Anointing the one now baptized, then, the bishop says thus: «The seal of the gift [b: D] of the Holy Spirit. Amen.» Showing by this that the chrism is a sign of Christ, since the one anointed is also anointed in the figure of a cross, and that it supplies the gift of the Holy Spirit.

CHAPTER 66. That potentially every one baptized and anointed has in himself the divine gifts, revealed in due season, except the priesthood.

For potentially the one baptized and anointed has in himself the divine gifts; and these are revealed to him proportionately in due season [lat: the Latin gives *ea secundum tempus abscondit*, he hides them according to the time], insofar as he cleanses himself, and insofar as he shows himself worthy of their operating in him, except the divine priesthood alone. For this, as a gift of its own and the work of God alone, is bestowed by [0232] [b: A] ordination. And no one will perform any of the sacred things, even if he be a wonder-bearer, without ordination. But to work healings and to prophesy and to be pure in body and in thoughts, and truly to live as a virgin, and to pray unceasingly and undistractedly, and to be warmed and moved toward divine love, and to be deemed worthy of divine illuminations, and of sacred revelations and mysteries and heavenly contemplations, and of such things, and to become almost like the angels — as very many have become, many even without ordination have had the power to attain through the grace from baptism and from the myron, and do attain, and are deemed worthy of such things. And so much concerning this. And before the chrism, after the baptism, the bishop, while the one baptized is putting on a tunic, [b: B] which is blessed by him, offers up the thanksgiving to God, hymning him for the salvation of him who has been regenerated by the water and the Spirit. Then he also entreats that he be deemed worthy of the chrism, and of the gift through it and of the holiness of the divine Spirit. For he is shown forth holy and sealed to Christ, and recognizable to the angels by the divine myron, and not to be captured by the enemies [lat: the Latin gives *jam non nisi aegre inimicis expugnabilis*, not to be taken by the enemies except with difficulty], if only he be watchful. And he asks also that he may attain to the communion of the mysteries, and no longer be a child of the body, but a child of the kingdom of God.

[b: C] CHAPTER 67. Why the tonsure is performed on those being baptized, and the garment of the cowl and of the mantle.

After the chrism — the all-holy myron, I mean — he cuts the hairs of his head in the form of a cross, at once because he has Christ for head, and ought to pray uncovered, as Paul also teaches, and at once also making this tonsure as a kind of seal, since he also cuts him in the form of a cross, so that he may cast off everything superfluous of the thoughts as well. Wherefore indeed the monks too are tonsured. And every believer ought to renounce the superfluous things and those not so much for use. And further, as first-fruits and sacrifice of the human body, the hairs from the one baptized are offered to Christ. For they are as an exhalation of the whole body. Wherefore also the hierarch, taking them, [b: D] does not throw them anywhere at random, but places them in a sacred place. Thus, then, the one baptized, having put on a tunic and been anointed, and been tonsured, and having received on his head a cowl, which indeed is also called a helmet — on the one hand on account of guilelessness and what is still childlike and pure, on the other for the shelter that is from God and the guarding of the senses, for most of the senses are seated in the head — then having worn over it a white tunic too, which is also called a mantle, for a type of the divine light, and of the angelic **85** purity — for the one baptized is a son of light, and has been shown wholly spotless and pure — thus initiated, he stands. If he is of age, he himself bearing a torch for a showing forth and glory of the most divine light; but if an infant, the sponsor bearing the torch for the sake of [0233] [b: A] him, the hymn-singers of the church chant «As many as were baptized into Christ,»; and the bishop, having further completed the rest of the prayers too, that the things of baptism may remain undefiled for the one baptized, and the things of the seal indelible, and that the things of the tonsure may be for the safety of the senses, and having prayed for many other things unto salvation, and having glorified God, joyfully takes the one baptized by the hand again, the sponsor following along. And making a dance, as dancing with the angels and rejoicing with them, he circles the font three times, rejoicing over the spiritual mother, and over him who has been born of her by the divine Spirit.
