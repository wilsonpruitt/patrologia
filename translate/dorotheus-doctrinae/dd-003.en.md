title: Instruction I, On renunciation (part 3)
---
[1633]

13. We also have a belt. Why do we wear it? The belt we wear is a symbol, first, that we are ready for work. For everyone who wants to work first girds himself, and so begins the work, as it says, "Let your loins be girded about."[^1] And again, that just as the belt is made from a dead body, so we too may put our desire to death. For the belt is about our loins, and there are the kidneys, in which the desiring part of the soul is said to be. And this is what the Apostle says: "Mortify your members which are upon the earth, fornication, uncleanness,"[^2] and so on. We also have an analavos. The analavos is laid crosswise over our shoulders; that is, we carry the symbol of the cross on our shoulders, as it says, "Take up thy cross and follow me."[^3] And what is the cross but perfect mortification, which is accomplished in us through faith in Christ? For faith, as it says again in the Gerontikon,[^4] always holds in check what hinders, and makes unhindered for us the work that brings us to such perfect mortification: that is, that a man should put himself to death to all the things of this world; and if he has left parents, that he should struggle against his attachment to them as well; likewise with money or possessions; and every thing that one renounces, that he should want to renounce his very attachment to it too, as we have already said. And this is perfect renunciation. And we receive a cowl, and this is a symbol of humility. For little innocent children wear cowls, but a grown man does not wear a cowl. So we wear it for this reason, that we may be children in malice, as the Apostle says, "Be not children in understanding, but in malice be ye children."[^5] And what is it to be a child in malice? The child that has no malice, if it is dishonoured, is not angry; and if it is honoured, it is not vainglorious; if someone takes its things, it is not grieved. For it is a child in malice: it does not avenge an injury, it does not lay claim to glory. Again, the cowl is a symbol of the grace of God. For as the cowl covers and warms the child's head, so the [1636] grace of God covers our mind, as it says in the Gerontikon: that the cowl is a symbol of the grace of our Saviour God, covering our ruling faculty and sheltering our infancy in Christ, because of the demons who are always trying to strike and wound us. See, we have the belt on our loins, which is the mortification of irrational desire; and the analavos over our shoulders, which is the cross. See also the cowl, which is a sign of guilelessness and of infancy in Christ.

14. So let us live in keeping with the habit, as the Fathers said, so as not to wear a habit that is not our own. But as we have left the great things, so let us leave the small ones too: we have left the world; let us leave its attachments too. For attachments, as I said, even through small things, not worth a word, bind us again to the world, and we do not notice. If then we want to be perfectly rid of them and set free, let us learn to cut off our wills, and so, little by little, with God, we make progress and come to freedom from attachment. For nothing so profits men as cutting off one's own will; truly, by this practice a man advances almost beyond every virtue. And just as a man walking along a road, finding a short cut on it and taking it, by that short cut gains a long stretch of that road,[^6] so it is with one who walks this road of cutting off the will. For by cutting off his own will a man acquires freedom from attachment, and from freedom from attachment he comes, with God, to perfect dispassion. And one can cut off ten wills in a short space. And how? A man walks a short way and sees something, and his thought says to him, "Look over there," and he says to his thought, "Indeed I will not look," and he cuts off his will and does not look. Again he finds some people speaking against others, and his thought says to him, "Thou too say this word," and he cuts off his will and does not say it. Again his thought says to him, "Go and ask the cook what he is cooking," and he does not go, and cuts off his will. He sees something, and his thought says to him, "Ask who brought it," and he cuts off his will and does not ask. And so, by cutting off, he comes into the habit of cutting off, and from the little things he begins to cut off the great ones too with ease; and so at last he begins to have no will at all, but whatever happens contents him, as though it were his own. And though he does not wish to do his own will, he is found always doing it. For one who has nothing of his own, everything that happens is his own. And so he is found, as we said, without attachment, and from freedom from attachment, as I said, he comes to dispassion.

15. See, little by little, to what progress cutting off one's own will brings a man. Such was that blessed Dositheus. From what a life, from what luxury, from what softness: a man who had never even heard a word of God; and yet you heard to what measures holding to obedience and cutting off his own will brought him in a short time; and how God glorified him, and did not let [1637] such virtue of his pass into forgetfulness, but revealed it to the holy elder, and he saw him among all those saints, enjoying their blessedness.[^7] I will tell you another thing likewise, which happened in my own time, that you may learn that obedience, and not having one's own will, rescue a man even from death. Once when I was at abba Seridos', a disciple of a great elder came there from the parts of Ascalon on some errand from his abba. He had an order from the elder to return to his own cell by evening. Meanwhile a very violent storm came on, and rains, and the nearby torrent was in full flood. Then he wanted to go away, because of the elder's word. We begged him to stay, saying that it was impossible for him to come out of [b: B] the river alive. But he would not be persuaded by us to stay. At last we say, "Let us go with him as far as the river; for when he sees it, he will turn back of his own accord." So we went with him, and when we reached the river, he takes off his clothes and binds them on his head, girds on his maphorion, and throws himself into the river, into all that fearful current. We stood there amazed and trembling lest he die; but he kept on swimming, and in a moment is found on the other side, and puts on his clothes, and makes us a prostration from there, and takes his leave,[^8] and goes off running. We stayed marvelling and astonished at the power of the virtue: [b: C] that we could scarcely look on, and that with fear, while he crossed without danger because of his obedience. And that brother whom his abba sent on their business to their agent in the village: how, when he saw himself being dragged by the man's daughter into a shameful union, he had only to say, "O God of my father, deliver me," and at once he was found on the road to Scetis, going to his father.

16. See the power of the virtue! See the efficacy of a word, how great a help there is even in calling on the prayers of one's father: so that he said, "O God, through the prayers of my father, deliver me," and at once was found on the road. And consider the humility and reverence of both. They were in straits, and the elder wanted to send the brother to the man who acted as their agent, and he did not say to him, "Go," but said to him, "Wilt thou go?" Likewise the brother did not say, "I am going," but said to him, "As thou wilt, I do." For he was afraid both of the occasions of sin and of disobeying his father. Then, as they were in still greater straits, the elder says to him, "Get up, go." And he did not say to him, "I hope in my God that he will protect me," but he says, "I hope in the prayers of my father that they will protect me." Likewise the brother, when he was tempted, did not say, "O my God, deliver me," [1640] but, "O God, through the prayers of my father, deliver me." And each of them hoped in the prayers of his father. See how they yoked obedience to humility. For as a chariot is yoked, and one horse cannot get ahead of the other, it is held back; so obedience needs to have humility yoked together with it. And how can anyone be counted worthy of this grace unless, as I said, he forces himself to cut off his own wills, and gives himself up, after God, to his own father, hesitating in nothing, but doing all things, as those men did, with full assurance, as one who is obeying God? [?] Such a one is worthy to find mercy; such a one is worthy to be saved.[^9]

17. A story is told that once holy Basil, passing through his coenobia, says to one of the superiors, "Hast thou anyone among those who are being saved?" The abba says to him, "Through thy prayers, master, we all wish to be saved." Holy Basil says to him again, "Hast thou anyone among those who are being saved?" He understood that saying (for he too was a spiritual man) and says, "Yes." When the brother had come, the saint says to him, "Give me water, that I may wash." He goes and brings him water to wash. And after holy Basil had washed, the saint himself takes the water too and says to the brother, "Take it, and wash thyself too." So he takes the water from the saint without questioning. Then, having tested him in this, he says to him again, "When I go into the sanctuary, come and remind me, so that I may ordain thee." Again he obeys without questioning. And when he saw holy Basil inside the sanctuary, he goes and reminds him, and he ordains him and takes him away with him. For with whom did it befit such a blessed brother to be, if not with that holy and God-bearing man? You have no experience of unquestioning obedience, nor do you know its rest. I once asked an elder, abba John, the disciple of abba Barsanuphius, and said, "Master, since the Scripture says that through many tribulations we must enter into the kingdom of heaven,[^10] and I see that I have not a single tribulation, what ought I to do, lest I lose my soul?", for I had not a single tribulation or care. For if it happened that I had a thought, I would take my tablet and write to the elder (for I used to ask him too by letter, before I served him), and before I had finished writing I felt the benefit and the relief: so great was the freedom from care and the rest. And I, not knowing the power of the virtue, and hearing that through many tribulations we must enter into the kingdom of heaven, was afraid because I had no tribulation. So when I made it known to the elder, he makes this known to me: "Do not be troubled; this is no concern of thine. For everyone who gives himself over to the obedience of the Fathers has this freedom from care and this rest."

## Notes
[^1]: S. Luke 12:35.
[^2]: S. Col 3:5.
[^3]: S. Matt 16:24; Mark 10:21.
[^4]: S. The symbolism of belt, analavos and cowl that Dorotheus credits to the Gerontikon goes back to the prologue of Evagrius' *Praktikos*.
[^5]: S. 1 Cor 14:20.
[^6]: T. κοπενδάριον (twice): Latin *compendiarium*, a short cut, spelled so as to echo κόπτειν, "to cut off," whether as a pun or as a real Byzantine form. Not reported as a misread. Migne's Latin reads *scipione*, "a staff," which misses the sense.
[^7]: S. The *Life of Dositheus*, which precedes the *Instructions* in the tradition.
[^8]: T. λαμβάνει παράθεσιν, "takes his commendation," i.e. his leave. The Latin has *a nobis impetrata venia*. The phrase is uncertain but the OCR gives no sign of a misread.
[^9]: T. Printed Τὶς ἄξιος … τὶς ἄξιος, with grave accents on what cannot be the interrogative here. Migne's Latin has *hic … hic … dignus est*, and the English follows that. Possibly ὁ τοιοῦτος or οὗτος is meant; not settled.
[^10]: S. Acts 14:22.
