[1648] 7. You see the lowliness of the saints, how their hearts are disposed? Nay, even when they were sent by God for the help of men, they would not accept, out of lowliness, fleeing from being glorified. For as a man clothed in pure silk, if a filthy rag is thrown on him, flees, so that his precious garment may not be soiled, so too the [b: C] saints, clothed with the virtues, flee human glory, so that they may not be soiled by it. But those who want glory are like a naked man, who is always wanting to find a little rag or anything at all to cover his shame. So too the man naked of virtues seeks the glory of men. So the saints, when they were sent by God for the help of men, would not accept, out of lowliness. Moses said: I pray thee, appoint another who is able; for I am thin of voice and slow of tongue.[^1] And Jeremiah said: I am too young.[^2] And simply, each of the saints acquired this lowliness from working the commandments, as we said. This humility [b: D] no one can tell in words, what it is or how it comes to be in the soul, unless a man learns it from experience; by word no one can learn it. Once abba Zosimas was speaking about humility, and a certain sophist who happened to be there, hearing what he said and wanting to learn it exactly, said to him, "Tell me, how dost thou hold thyself a sinner? Dost thou not know that thou art holy, dost thou not know that thou hast virtues? Look, thou seest how thou workest the commandments; how, doing such things, dost thou hold that thou art a sinner?" And the elder could not find how to put the answer to him, but said to him, "I do not know how to tell thee, but so I hold." So the sophist pressed against him, wanting to learn how it is. But the [1649] [b: A] elder, not finding how to present the matter to him, began to say with his holy simplicity, "Do not twist me; I for my part hold so." So when I saw the elder at a loss how to answer, I say to him, "Is it not like sophistry and medicine? When a man learns it well and practises it, little by little, from practising it, a kind of habit forms in the physician or the sophist; and he cannot say, nor does he know how to tell, how he came into the habit of the thing; little by little, as I said, imperceptibly, the soul took it on, from practising the art. So it is to be found also with humility: from the working of the commandments a kind of humble habit comes about, and it cannot be told in words." So when abba Zosimas heard this, [b: B] he rejoiced, and at once embraced me, and says to me, "Thou hast found the matter; it is as thou saidst." And the sophist too was satisfied on hearing it, and he too accepted the word.

8. For the elders too have said things that make us understand humility; but the disposition itself, as it comes to be in us, what it is, no one has found how to tell. So, when abba Agathon was about to die, the brethren said to him, "Art thou too afraid, Father?" He said, "I have done what I could to keep the commandments; but I am a man. How do I know whether my work has pleased God? For the judgment of God is one thing, and that of [b: C] men another."[^3] See, he opened our eyes to understand it, and showed us a road to grasp it. But how it comes into the soul, as I have often said, no one has found how to tell, nor been able to grasp it in words, unless[^4] it be the soul that has been deemed worthy to learn it from works. But what it is that brings it, the Fathers have said. For it says in the Gerontikon that a brother asked an elder, saying: What is humility? And the elder said: Humility is a great and divine work; and the road of humility is bodily toils done with knowledge, and thy putting thyself beneath all: this is the road of humility. But humility itself is divine and incomprehensible.[^5] And why does he say that bodily toils bring the soul to humility? In what sense are bodily toils virtues [b: D] of the soul? Putting oneself beneath all, as we said above, is opposed to the first pride. For how can a man hold himself greater than his brother, or be exalted in anything, or blame or despise anyone, if he holds himself beneath all? Likewise praying continually is plainly there because it is opposed to the second pride. For it is evident that the humble man, the devout man, knowing that no good can be accomplished in the soul without the help and shelter of God, does not cease entreating God unceasingly to show mercy to him. And indeed the man who entreats God in everything, if ever he is deemed worthy to accomplish anything,[^6] knows from where he accomplished it, and cannot be exalted, nor ascribe it to his own power, but ascribes all his good deeds to God, and [1652] [b: A] always thanks him, and always beseeches him, trembling lest he fall away from such help and his weakness and powerlessness be laid bare. And so through humility he prays, and through prayer he is humbled; and the more[^7] he does well, the more he is humbled; and the more he is humbled, the more he is helped, and makes progress[^8] through humility.

9. Why then does he say that bodily toils bring humility? What has bodily toil to do with the disposition of the soul? I tell you. When the soul fell away from the commandment into transgression, it was handed over, as saint Gregory says, to love of pleasure and to the self-rule of error;[^9] and it loved bodily things, and in a manner was found to be one thing with the body, and became wholly flesh, as it says: [b: B] My spirit shall not remain in these men, because they are flesh.[^10] And the wretched soul suffers with, and is disposed with, what happens to the body. That is why the elder said that bodily toil too leads to humility. For the soul of a man in health is disposed one way, and of a sick man another, and of a hungry man another, and of a full man another; likewise again, the soul of a man sitting on a horse is disposed one way, and of one sitting on a throne another, and of one sitting on the ground another; of one wearing fine clothes one way, and of one wearing rotten clothes another. Toil then humbles the body; and when the body is humbled, the soul is humbled with it. So he said well that bodily toil too leads [b: C] to humility. Therefore, when Evagrius was warred upon by blasphemy, being a man who had knowledge, and knowing that blasphemy comes from pride, and that when the body is humbled the soul is humbled with it, he spent forty days without going under a roof, so that his body, as the writer says, put out ticks,[^11] like the wild beasts. So the toil was undertaken not for the blasphemy but for humility. The elder said well, then, that bodily toils lead to humility. May the good God grant us humility, for it rescues a man from many evils, and shelters him from great temptations.

**Instruction III.** [b: D] *On conscience.*

1. When God made man, he sowed in him something divine, like a thought, warmer and bright, with the nature of a spark, enlightening the mind and showing it the good from the evil. This is called conscience, which is the natural law. This is the wells which Jacob dug, as the Fathers have said, and which the Philistines filled up.[^12] That is, it was by attending to this conscience [1653] [b: A] that[^13] the patriarchs and all the saints before the written law pleased God. When it was buried and trampled by men through the advance of sin, we needed the written law, we needed the holy prophets, we needed the very coming of our Master Jesus Christ, to uncover it and raise it up, to rekindle that buried spark through the keeping of his holy commandments. It is in our hands now, then, either to bury it again, or to let it shine out and enlighten us, if we obey it. For when conscience tells us to do this thing,[^14] and we despise it, and it speaks again, and we do not do it, but go on trampling it, then we bury it, and [b: B] it can no longer speak to us clearly for the weight lying on it. But like a lamp behind window-panes, so it begins to show us things more dimly, as it were more darkly; and so, little by little, as in water muddied by much earth no one can make out his own face, so we find ourselves not perceiving what our conscience tells us, so that we think we almost do not have it. But there is no one who does not have it; for it is something divine, as we said already, and it never perishes, but always reminds us of what we owe; but we do not perceive it, because, as I said, we despise and trample it. That is why the prophet mourns Ephraim, and says: Ephraim overpowered his adversary, [b: C] and trampled judgment.[^15] He calls conscience the adversary. Hence in the Gospel too it says: Be well-disposed to thine adversary while thou art in the way with him; lest he deliver thee to the judge, and the judge to the officers, and they cast thee into prison. Amen I say to thee, thou shalt not come out thence until thou hast paid the last farthing.[^16] And why does he call conscience adversary? It is called adversary because it always opposes our evil will, and tells us of what we ought to do and do not do; and again, of what we ought not to do and do; and it accuses us. That is why he calls it adversary, and commands, saying, Be well-disposed to thine adversary while thou art in[^17] the way with him. The way, as saint Basil says, is this world.
