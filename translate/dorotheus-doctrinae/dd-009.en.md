title: Instruction V, On not being guided by oneself (end), and Instruction VI, On not judging one's neighbour (part 1)
---
[1680] [b: A] 3. See that brother whom the evil one loved, about whom he said to abba Macarius, "I have one brother who, whenever he sees me, spins like a top."[^1] Such men he loves, in such men he always rejoices: those without a pilot, who do not refer themselves to one who, after God, is able to help them and give them a hand. For did not that demon go [?][^2] to all the brethren, when the saint saw him carrying all those tastes in the little flasks? Did he not offer them to all? But each of them, perceiving his plot, ran and laid open his own thoughts, and found help [b: B] in the time of temptation, and so then the evil one had no strength against them. Only that wretched one did he find guided by himself and having help from no one, and he had him for a plaything, and went off thanking him and cursing the others. Indeed, when he told abba Macarius the matter and the brother's name, and the saint ran to him, he found that this was the cause of his ruin: he found him unwilling to confess, he found him without the habit of disclosing; for this is why the enemy turned him about wherever he wished. For when the saint asked him, "How are things with thee, brother?" he said, "Well, by thy prayers." And when he said again, "Do the thoughts not war [b: C] against thee?" he answered, "So far I am well," and would confess nothing, until the saint, with skill, persuaded him to tell out how it stood with him; and having spoken the word of God to him, he made him safe and went back. So the enemy came again, as was his habit, wanting to throw him down, and was put to shame. For he found him established, he found him no longer made sport of. So then he went off having done nothing; he went off put to shame by him as well. That is why, when the saint asked him again, "How is that brother, thy friend?"[^3] he no longer called him friend but enemy, and cursed him, saying, "He too has been turned aside, and he too will not obey me; he has become wilder than all of them."

[b: D] 4. See why the enemy hates the sound of safety:[^4] because he always wants our ruin. See why he loves those who are guided by themselves: because they work with the devil, plotting against themselves. I know no other fall for a monk than from trusting his own heart. Some say, "A man falls because of this, or because of that." But I, as I said, know no other fall that comes to anyone except from this. Hast thou seen someone fallen? Know that he was guided by himself. Nothing is heavier than to be guided by oneself, nothing more deadly than this. God has sheltered me, and I have always feared this danger. When I was [1681] [b: A] in the coenobium, I referred everything to the elder, abba John; for, as I said, I never brought myself to do anything without his judgement. And sometimes my thought would say to me, "Will not the one who answers tell thee just this? Why wouldst thou trouble him?" And I would say to the thought, "Anathema to thee, and to thy discernment, and to thy understanding, and to thy prudence, and to thy knowledge; for what thou knowest, thou knowest from the demons." So I would go and ask the elder, and it would happen that sometimes he answered me with the very word I had thought of; and then my thought would say to me, "Well then? See, it is just what I said: didst thou not trouble the elder for nothing?" And I would say to the thought, "But now it is good, now it is from the Holy Spirit. For what is thine is evil, it is from the demons, it is from [b: B] a passionate state." And so I never let myself be persuaded by the thought on my own without asking; and believe me, brethren, I was in great rest, in great freedom from care, so that I was even uneasy about it, as I know I have told you at another time. For I used to hear that Through many afflictions we must enter into the kingdom of God,[^5] and I saw myself without any affliction at all, and I was afraid and at a loss, not knowing the cause of such rest, until the elder made it plain to me: "Be not grieved. For everyone who puts himself under the obedience of the Fathers has this rest and freedom from care."

[b: C] 5. You too, brethren, be eager to ask, and not to be guided by yourselves; learn what freedom from care the thing holds, what joy, what rest. But since I said that I was never afflicted, hear also what happened to me then in this regard. While I was still there in the coenobium, there came on me once a great and unspeakable grief, and I was in such toil and straitness that I was nearly ready to give up my very soul. That affliction came from a plot of the demons; and such a temptation, brought on by the envy of the demons, is very heavy but short: heavy, dark, comfortless, having no rest at all, but straitness on every side, choking on every side. But the grace of God comes quickly to the soul, since no one could [b: D] bear it. I was, as I said, in such a temptation and such distress. So one day, as I stand in the court of the monastery, disheartened and calling on God about this, suddenly I look inside into the church and see someone in the habit of a bishop, wearing [?],[^6] going into the sanctuary. Now I never went near a stranger without need or leave; but something seemed to draw me then, and I go in after him, and he stays standing a good while, with [1684] [b: A] his hands stretched out to heaven, and I stood behind him, praying with much fear; for great dread came on me at the sight of him. When he had finished his prayer, he turns and comes toward me, and the nearer he came to me, the more I felt the grief and the dread withdrawing. Then, when he stood before me, he stretches out his hand and touches my breast and strikes my breast with his fingers, saying: Waiting I waited for the Lord, and he heeded me, and heard me, my prayer, and brought me up out of the pit of misery and from the mud of the mire. And he set my feet upon a rock and directed my steps, and put a new song into my mouth, [b: B] a hymn to our God.[^7] And he says all the verses three times, striking my breast, as I said, and so he goes out. And at once there came into my heart light, joy, comfort, sweetness, and I found myself another man. So I went out running after him, wanting to find him, and I did not find him; he had vanished. From that hour, through the mercies of God, I have not known myself troubled again, either by grief or by dread; but the Lord has sheltered me until now, through the prayers of those holy elders.

6. I have told you these things so that you may know how much rest and freedom from care, with all safety besides, there is in a man's not being guided by himself, but casting his affairs on God and on those who are able, after God, to guide him. So you too, brethren, learn to ask; [b: C] learn not to be guided by yourselves; it is a good thing. It is lowliness, it is rest, it is joy. What need is there for anyone to wear himself out in vain? It is not possible for anyone to be saved otherwise than so. But perhaps someone thinks, "If a man has no one to ask, what is he to do?" Truly, if anyone in truth wants the will of God with all his heart, God never leaves him, but surely guides him according to his will. Truly, if anyone directs his heart toward the will of God, God enlightens a little child to tell him his will. But if anyone does not truly want the will of God, even if he goes to a prophet, God, according to his crooked heart, gives it [b: D] to the prophet's heart to answer him, as Scripture says: If the prophet is deceived and speaks, I the Lord have deceived that prophet.[^8] Therefore we ought with all our strength to direct ourselves toward the will of God, and not trust our own heart. Even if a thing is good, and we hear from some saint that it is good, we ought to hold that it is good, but not trust ourselves that we are already doing it well, and as it rightly ought to be done. We ought to do what is in our power, but then again to refer even how we are doing it, and learn whether we did it well; and after that not even so be free from care, but wait as well for the judgement of God, as [1685] [b: A] that holy Agathon said when he was asked, "Art thou too afraid, Father?" He answered, "So far I have done what was in my power; but I do not know whether my work has pleased God. For the judgement of God is one thing, and that of men another."[^9] May God shelter us from the danger of those who are guided by themselves, and count us worthy to hold to the way of our fathers who were well pleasing to his name.

**Instruction VI.** *On not judging one's neighbour.*

1. If we remembered, brethren, the words of the holy elders, if we meditated on them always, we would hardly neglect ourselves. For if, as they said, we did not despise the small things, the things that seem to us of no account, we would not fall [b: B] into the great and heavy ones. I am always telling you that from these trifles, from saying, "What does this matter? What does that matter?" a bad habit forms in the soul, and it begins to despise great things too. Dost thou know what a heavy sin it is to judge one's neighbour? For what is heavier than this? What does God so hate and turn from, as the Fathers too said, that nothing is worse than judging? And yet from these things, he says, that seem trivial, a man comes to so great an evil. For from admitting a small suspicion against one's neighbour, from saying, "What does it matter if I too say this one word? What does it matter if I see what this brother is saying, or what that stranger is doing?" the mind begins to let go of its own sins [b: C] and to chatter about its neighbour's. And then from this comes condemning, speaking ill, despising; and from this a man falls into the very things he condemns. For from not caring about his own evils, nor weeping, as the Fathers said, over his own dead, he cannot set himself right in anything, but is always busy with his neighbour. And nothing so provokes God, and nothing so strips a man bare and brings him to lose all understanding, as speaking ill of, or condemning, or despising one's neighbour.
