title: Instruction VII, On self-accusation (end)
---
[1701] 4. So even if someone says, "How can I not be upset, if I need a thing and do not get it? For look, I need it, and need it of necessity," not even this man has any reason to blame anyone or to be upset with anyone. But if he really needs a thing, as he says, and does not get it, he ought to say, "Christ knows better than I whether I ought to be relieved; and he himself becomes for me instead of this thing, or instead of this food." The sons of Israel ate the manna in the desert forty years.[^1] And the manna was of one kind; but to each it became as he needed: for the one who needed something salty it became salty; [b: B] for the one who needed something sweet it became sweet; and to each, simply, it so became, suited to his constitution. So then, if someone needs an egg and gets nothing but vegetables, let him say to his thought, "If it were good for me, God would surely have sent it to me; besides, he can make these very vegetables like an egg for me"; and let him trust God that it is counted to him as a martyrdom. For if someone is in truth worthy of relief, [?][^2] God moves someone to show him mercy according to his need; but if he is not worthy of relief, or it is not good for him, even if he were to make a new heaven and a new earth, he does not find relief. Indeed, sometimes a man finds relief even beyond [b: C] his need, and sometimes not even his need. And sometimes he provides for someone according to his need [sic: *καὶ ὑπὲρ τὴν χρείαν*], since God, being merciful, supplies each one his need.[^3] And sometimes he provides for someone even beyond the need, showing him the abundance of his love for man and teaching him thanksgiving. But when he does not supply him his need, he works by his word the effect of the thing he needs, and teaches him patience. So in everything we ought to look upward: whether we suffer good or ill from anyone, to look upward and give thanks for what befalls us, always carrying the blame ourselves, and saying, as the Fathers said, that if some good thing ever happens, it is God's providence, and if something bad, it is on account [b: D] of our sins. For in truth, whatever we suffer, we suffer from our sins. For the saints, if they suffer, suffer for the name of God, or so that their virtue may be shown forth for the benefit of many, or so that their reward from God may be multiplied. But we wretches, how can we say this of ourselves? Sinning like this every day and taking the part of our passions, we have left the straight road the Fathers spoke of, to blame oneself, and we walk the crooked road of blaming our neighbour; and each of us is eager in every matter to put the fault on his brother and throw the burden on him. Each one is careless and keeps nothing, and we demand the commandments from our neighbour.

[1704] [b: A] 5. Two brethren once came to me, upset with each other. The elder said of the younger, "I give him an order, and he is upset, and I am upset too, thinking that if he had faith and love toward me he would accept what I say with full assurance." And the younger said, "Forgive, sir; perhaps[^4] he does not speak to me with the fear of God, but as one who wants to command me; and I think that is why my heart has no assurance, as the Fathers say." Put your minds to how the two blamed each other, and neither of them blamed himself. Rather, the two, still upset with each other and making prostrations to each other, remained without assurance. The one said, "He did not make prostration to me from the heart, and that is why I had no assurance; for so the Fathers said." And the [b: B] other said, "Since he was not disposed in love toward me before I made prostration to him, that is why I too had no assurance." Seest thou the mockery, sir? Seest thou the twisting of thought? God knows, I am astonished that we take even the sayings of the Fathers to serve our evil wills and the ruin of our souls. Each of them should have put the blame on himself: the one should have said, "I did not make prostration to my brother from the heart, and that is why God did not give him assurance"; and the other should have said, "I was not disposed in love toward my brother before he made prostration to me, and that is why God did not give him assurance." [b: C] The others too, the ones before these, should have done likewise:[^5] the first ought to have said, "I speak with self-will, and that is why God does not give my brother assurance"; and the other ought to have reasoned, "My brother gives me orders with lowliness and love, but I am unsubmissive and have no fear of God." Neither of them found the way and blamed himself; but each laid the weight on his neighbour.

6. Look, this is why we find no progress, this is why we find no benefit in anything, but we spend all our time against one another, wearing ourselves down. Because each one justifies himself; each one lets himself go, as I said before, keeping nothing, and we demand the commandments from our neighbour. That is [b: D] why we do not grow used even to the good: because if we get a little light, at once we demand it from our neighbour, blaming him and saying, "He ought to have done this," and "Why did he not do it so?" Why do we not rather demand the commandments from ourselves and blame ourselves, as not keeping them? Where is that elder who was asked, "What more hast thou found on this way, Father?" And he answered and said, "To blame oneself in everything." And the one who asked praised it, and says to him, "There is no other way but this." Likewise abba Poemen said with a groan, [1705] [b: A] "All the virtues have come into this house except one virtue, and without it a man stands only with toil." And they asked him which it was, and he says, "That a man should blame himself."[^6] And saint Antony said that this is the great work of a man: to lay his fault upon himself before God, and to expect temptation to his last breath.[^7] And everywhere we find that the Fathers, keeping this and referring everything to God, even the smallest things, were at rest. Such was that holy elder: when he was sick, the brother put into his food, instead of honey, the oil pressed from linseed, which is very harmful; and yet the elder said nothing, but ate in silence the first dish, [b: B] and then the second, taking what he needed, not blaming the brother in his heart by saying that he had slighted him. And not only did he not say this; he did not grieve him by a word either. When the brother learned what he had done and began to be distressed, saying, "I have killed thee, abba, and thou hast laid the sin on me by keeping silent," how gently he answered him, saying, "Do not be distressed, child: had God wanted me to eat honey, thou wouldst have put in honey." And at once he referred the matter to God. What has God to do with it, good elder? The brother made a mistake, and thou sayest, "If God had wanted"? What has that to do with the matter? And he says, "Yes: had God wanted me to eat honey, the brother would have had honey." And this, though the elder was so sick that for so many days he could [b: C] take no food; and yet he was not upset with the brother, but referred the matter to God. And the elder spoke well: had God wanted him to eat honey, he would have turned even the stinking oil into honey. But we, in every matter, go at our neighbour with accusations, blaming him as one who slights us and acts against his conscience; and if we hear a word, at once we twist it, saying, "If he had not wanted to wound me, he would not have said it." Where is that saint who said of Shimei, "Let him curse, for the Lord told him to curse David"?[^8] Did God speak to a murderer, that he should curse the prophet? How then did God [b: D] tell him? But the prophet, having knowledge, and knowing that nothing brings God's mercy upon the soul so much as temptations, especially those brought on and added in a time of affliction and straits, said, "Let him curse David, for the Lord told him." Why? "So that God may see my lowliness and return good to me instead of his curse."[^9] Seest thou how the prophet acted with knowledge? That is why he rounded on those who wanted to avenge themselves on the one who cursed, saying, "What have I to do with you, sons of Zeruiah? Let him curse; the Lord told him." But we cannot bear to say of our brother, "The Lord told him"; but if we hear a word, [1708] [b: A] at once we do what the dog does. Someone throws a stone at it, and it leaves the one who threw it and goes and bites the stone. So we too do: we leave God, who permits the blows to be brought on us for the cleansing of our sins, and go after our neighbour, saying, "Why did he say this to me? And what has he done to me?" And though we could profit greatly from such things, we plot the opposite against ourselves, not knowing that everything happens by God's providence for the good of each one. May God give us understanding, by the prayers of the saints. Amen.
