[1721] 4. See, we have said who it is that lies in his mind, and who it is that lies in word; now we want to say who it is that lies in his very life. He lies in his life when someone who is dissolute pretends to self-control, or is covetous and talks about almsgiving and praises compassion, or is proud and [b: D] admires humility; and he does not admire it because he wishes to praise virtue. For if he spoke with this aim, he would first confess his own weakness with lowliness, saying, "Woe is me, wretch that I am, for I have been made void of every good!" And after confessing his own weakness, then he could admire and praise virtue. Nor again does he extol virtue with the aim of not scandalizing anyone. For he ought to reason this way too: "Yes, I am wretched and full of passions; why should I scandalize another as well? why should I harm another soul too, and bring on myself another's burden as well?" And so, even if he was sinning against himself, he would at least have laid hold of the good. For it belongs to lowliness to call oneself wretched, and to compassion [1724] [b: A] to spare one's neighbour. But such a man does not admire virtue in any of the ways mentioned, as I said; rather, either he takes up the name of virtue to cover his own disgrace, and talks about it as though he too were such a man; or, often, to harm and to entice someone. For no vice, nor any heresy, nor the devil himself can deceive anyone except by putting on the look of virtue, as the Apostle says that the devil himself is transformed into an angel of light: It is no great thing then if his ministers also are transformed as ministers of righteousness.[^1] So too, then, the liar, whether he fears shame so as not to be humbled, or, as we said, wishes to entice and deceive someone, talks about the virtues [b: B] and praises and admires them, as if he had made them his own and had experience of them. This is the one who lies in his very life. This man is not a simple man but double: he is one thing inside and another outside; his life is double and wholly a mockery. See, we have spoken about lying, that it is from the evil one; we have spoken also about truth, that the truth is God. Let us flee lying then, brethren, so that we may be delivered from the portion of the enemy, and let us strive to acquire the truth, so that we may be united to him who said, I am the truth.[^2] May God make us worthy of his truth.

**Instruction X.** *On travelling the way of God with an aim and with watchfulness.*

[b: C] 1. Let us take care of ourselves, brethren; let us be watchful. Who will give us this time back if we lose it? Truly we shall seek these days and not find them. Abba Arsenius used always to say to himself, "Arsenius, for what didst thou come out?"[^3] But we are in such perdition and negligence that we do not know why we came out, nor do we know what it is we wanted; and that is why not only do we make no progress, but we are also afflicted always. This happens to us from not keeping attention in our heart. And truly, if we were willing to strive a little, we would not be afflicted for long or toil. For even if at the beginning someone forces himself, yet by striving he makes progress little by little, and then he does it with rest; for God sees that he forces himself, [b: D] and grants him help. So let us too force ourselves, let us make a good beginning, let us for now at least will the good. For we are not yet in perfection. But the very willing is the beginning of salvation for us: for from willing we come, with God, to striving as well, and from striving one is helped to acquire the virtues. That is why one of the Fathers says: Give blood and receive spirit;[^4] that is, strive, and thou shalt come into the habit of virtue.

[1725] [b: A] 2. When I was reading for secular learning, at the start I toiled very much, and when I came to take up the book I was like someone going to touch a wild beast. So as I kept forcing myself, God helped, and I came into such a habit of the thing that I did not know what I was eating or drinking or how I was sleeping, from the burning ardour of the reading. And I was never drawn away to a meal with any of my friends; I would not even come into conversation with one of them in the time of reading, although I was sociable and loved my companions. So when the sophist dismissed us, I would wash[^5] (for I needed to be moistened every day, since I was dried out by the excess of reading), and go back to where I was staying, not even knowing whether I had anything to eat; for I could not spare the time [b: B] even to give orders about my food. But I had someone faithful, and he would prepare me whatever I wanted. So I took whatever I found he had made, keeping my book beside me on the couch too, and little by little I would bend over it; and when I slept, likewise I had it near me on my stool, and as soon as I snatched a little sleep I would at once leap up to the reading. Again in the evening, when I came back after the lamp-lighting office, I would light the lamp and go on reading until midnight; and I was such that I felt no sweetness of any kind except from the reading. So when I came to the monastery, I said to myself, "If in secular study such a passion and such [b: C] fervour came about, so that from applying oneself to reading one came into the habit of it, how much more in the case of virtue?" And I drew great strength from the thing. So, if someone wishes to acquire virtue, he must not be careless nor distracted. For just as someone who wishes to learn carpentry does not attend to another craft, so it is with those who wish to acquire spiritual work. They must not attend to any other thing, but meditate on it night and day, how they may be able to acquire it. Those who do not come to the thing in this way not only make no progress but are even broken, carried about so aimlessly. For if someone is not watchful and does not strive, he easily falls into the deviations from the virtues.

[b: D] 3. For the virtues are in the middle: that is the royal road, of which that holy elder said, "Travel by the royal road, and count the miles."[^6] The virtues then, as I said, are in the middle between excesses and deficiencies. That is why it is written: Turn not aside to the right hand nor to the left, but travel by the royal road.[^7] And holy Basil says: He is upright in heart whose thought does not lean toward excess or deficiency, but is directed toward the mean of virtue.[^8] For example: evil in itself [1728] [b: A] is nothing. For it is neither some substance, nor does it have any subsistence; God forbid. But the soul, inclining away from virtue, becomes passionate and brings about evil;[^9] and therefore it is also punished by it, finding no natural rest in it. Take wood, for instance. It does not have a worm inside it; but it makes a little rot, and from that rot the worm comes into being, and so the worm itself eats the wood. Likewise copper itself makes the rust, and is itself in turn consumed by the rust. And garments themselves make the moth, and the moth that comes from them destroys them. So too, then, the soul itself makes evil,[^10] which before this was nothing and has, as I said, no subsistence, and the soul itself is punished by the evil. And well did holy [b: B] Gregory say: "For fire, offspring of matter, also consumes the matter, as evil does the wicked."[^11] As one can find in sick bodies too: when someone lives disorderly and does not manage himself in health, there comes either excess or deficiency, and then from this he is out of balance. So that before this there was no sickness anywhere, nor was it anything; and again, once the body is healthy, the sickness is not found at all, nor was it ever anything. Likewise evil too is a sickness of the soul deprived of its own natural health, which is virtue.

4. That is why we said that the virtues are in the middle. For instance, courage is in the middle between cowardice and rashness; humility is in the middle between pride and [b: C] men-pleasing; likewise reverence is in the middle between bashfulness and shamelessness; and so by analogy the other virtues too. If then a man is found who has been made worthy of these virtues, such a one is found honourable before God; and to be sure he always appears eating and drinking and sleeping like the rest of men, but such a one is honourable for the virtues he has. But if someone is not watchful and does not guard himself, he easily turns aside from the road, either to the right or to the left, that is, into excess or deficiency, and brings about the sickness which is evil. See, this is the royal road on which all the saints have travelled; and the miles are the different states which one must measure always, and see where he is, what mile he has reached, in what [b: D] state he is. For example: we are all like people who have the aim of travelling to the holy city. Having set out from one city, some travelled five miles and stopped; others travelled ten; others even as far as half the way; others did not travel at all, but went out of the city and stayed outside the gates in its stench; others, though on the road, sometimes travel two miles and go astray and go back over them, or make two and go back five; others travelled as far as the city itself, but stayed outside and did not go into the city. See, so it is with us too. For there are some of us who left the world and came to the monastery with the aim of acquiring the virtues. And some achieved [1729] [b: A] a little and stopped there; others a little more; others even up to half the matter, and stood still; others achieved nothing at all, but seemed indeed to have come out of the world, yet stayed in the passions of the world and in its stench; others achieve a little good and undo it again. And there are some who undo even more than they achieved.[^12] Others achieved the virtues but had pride and contempt for their neighbours, and stayed outside the city and did not go in; and so these too did not reach their aim; for even though they reached the gate of the city, they stayed outside, so that they too fell from their own aim.

[b: B] ⟨5.⟩[^13] Let each of us then learn where he is: whether he has gone out of his own city but stayed outside the gate in the stench of the city; or advanced a little, or much, or reached the half of the road; whether he makes two miles and undoes the two; whether he makes two and undoes five; whether he travelled as far as the city and entered into Jerusalem; ⟨or⟩ reached the city indeed but could not get inside.[^14] Let each learn his own state, where he is. There are three states in a man: there is the one who acts out the passion, and there is the one who checks it, and there is the one who uproots it. The one who acts out the passion is the one who does it, who champions it. The one who checks the passion is the one who neither acts it out nor cuts it off, but reasons wisely about it and passes it by,[^15] yet has the passion within himself. The one who uproots the [b: C] passion is the one who strives and does the opposite of the passion. These three states have great breadth. For example, tell me: do you want us to take a passion and lay it bare? Do you want us to speak about pride? do you want us to speak about fornication? or would you rather we speak about vainglory? Someone cannot bear to hear a word from his brother. There is one who at times hears a single word and is troubled, and says five words or even ten for the one word, and fights and is troubled; and after he has stopped fighting he goes on thinking against the one who said that word to him, and bearing him rancour, and grieving that he did not say more than he said; and he prepares for himself still harsher words to say [b: D] to him, and keeps saying, "Why did I not say this to him? And I have this to say to him!" and he rages continually. See, that is one state: this is the one that has the evil in habit. God deliver us from such a state; for such a state is altogether liable to punishment, since all sin in act is under Hades. And even if such a one should wish to repent, he does not have the strength alone to overcome the passion, unless he also has help from some saints, as the Fathers too have said. That is why I always tell you: be eager to cut off the passions before you come to be in the habit of them. There is another who hears a word and is troubled, and he too says five words, or even ten for one, and is afflicted that he did not say others three times worse; and he grieves and bears rancour. But he spends [1732] [b: A] a few days at it, and comes round. Another spends a week in these things, and comes round. Another spends one day, and comes round. Another insults, fights, is troubled, troubles others, and at once comes round. See how many differences of states there are; and yet all these are under Hades so long as they act it out.
