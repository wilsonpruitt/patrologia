But see this too: there are times when a soul is found inclined toward a passion, and if it falls only once into the act of that passion, it is in danger of coming straight into a habit. The same thing happens in bodily things too. A man is found to be of a rather melancholic temperament from some earlier neglect, and a single food of that kind can almost at once stir up and inflame the humour against him. So there is need of much watchfulness and diligence and fear, so that one may not fall into a bad habit. Believe me, brethren, that one passion [?]: if a man has ten good works and one evil in habit, that one, coming from bad habit, prevails over the ten good ones.[^1] For it is like the eagle: if it has got wholly out of the snare, [b: C] and only its claw is found caught, by that little thing all its strength is brought down. For even if it is found wholly outside, and only its claw is held, is it not in the snare? Can the fowler not bring it down at whatever hour he likes? So it is with the soul too: if it has only one passion in habit, the enemy brings it down at whatever hour he pleases, for through that passion he has it in his hand. That is why I always tell you: Do not let a passion become a habit in you. Rather let us strive, begging God night and day not to fall into temptation. And if we are overcome, being men, and slip into a fault, let us make haste to rise at once; let us repent of it; let us weep before the goodness of God; let us watch, let us strive. And [b: D] God, seeing our purpose and our lowliness and our contrition, gives us a hand and works his mercy with us. Amen.

**Instruction XII.** *On the fear of the punishment to come, and that he who wishes to be saved must never be free from care about his own salvation.*

1. When my feet were aching and I was ill from them, some of the brethren who had come up to visit me asked me to tell them the cause of the sickness, aiming, as I think, at two things: [1749] [b: A] to comfort me and draw me a little away from the pain, and to give me an occasion to say something profitable to them. But since the pain did not allow me to say to you then what I wished, it is needful now to hear about it; for the telling of an affliction is pleasant after the affliction. At sea too, when a storm rises, everyone is always afflicted; once the storm has passed, they always tell one another with joy, and with delight, what happened. And it is good, brethren, as I always tell you, to refer each thing to God and to say that nothing happens apart from God; but surely God knew that it was good and profitable so, and so he did it, even if the thing has some outward cause. For example: I could have said that because [b: B] I ate with guests and forced myself a little with them to put them at their ease, my stomach grew heavy, and a flux ran down into my foot, and from that I got rheumatism; or other various causes, for they are never lacking to one who wants them. But the more exact and profitable thing is rather this: that God knew it was profitable to my soul so, and so it came about. For there is nothing of what God does that is not good; rather all things are very good.[^2] So no one should lose heart at what befalls him; but, as I said, he should refer all to the providence of God and be at rest. There are some who are so weighed down by the afflictions that befall them that they renounce life itself and would gladly die, so as to be rid of them that way. A man suffers this [b: C] from smallness of soul and great ignorance, not knowing the fearful necessity that comes after the soul's departure from the body.

2. It is a great love for mankind on God's part, brethren, that we are in this world; but we, not knowing the things there, see the things here as heavy. And it is not so. Do you not know what it says in the Gerontikon? That a brother, a great worker, asked an elder, saying, "My soul desires death." And the elder says to him, "That is because thou fleest affliction; and dost thou not know that the affliction to come is far worse than the one here?" And another likewise asked an elder, saying, "Why do I suffer acedia sitting in my cell?" [b: D] And the elder says to him, "Because thou hast not yet seen either the rest that is hoped for or the punishment that is to be. For if thou didst see these exactly, then even if thy cell were full of worms, so that thou wert sunk among them up to the neck, thou wouldst endure it without acedia." But we want to be saved while we sleep, and that is why we grow faint in afflictions, when we ought rather to give thanks to God and call ourselves blessed that we are counted worthy at all to be afflicted a little here, so that there we may find a little rest. Evagrius too used to say that a man who is passionate and prays that his [1752] [b: A] departure may come sooner is like a man who begs the carpenter to break up the sick man's bed quickly. For through this body the soul is drawn away from its passions and is comforted: it eats, drinks, sleeps, meets people, goes about with friends. But when it leaves the body, it is left alone, it and its passions, and then it is punished by them continually, brooding on them, burning with their harassment and torn apart by them, so that it cannot even remember God. For the very remembrance of God comforts the soul, as it says in the psalm too: I remembered God and was glad;[^3] and yet the passions do not allow it even this. Do you want to learn by an example what I am saying? Let one of you go and shut himself into a dark [b: B] cell, and not eat for only three days, and not drink, and not sleep, not meet anyone, not sing psalms, not pray, nor remember God at all; and let him learn what the passions do to him. And if this happens while he is still here, how much more after the soul has gone out of the body, and been handed over to them, and left alone with them?

3. What, then, the wretched soul has to suffer from them at that time, one can understand somehow from the things here, that affliction too. For when someone has a fever, what is it that burns him? What fire, or what wood, makes [b: C] that burning? And if someone is found to have a melancholic, ill-tempered body, does not his very ill temper burn him, and trouble him always, and afflict his life? So too the passionate soul is always punished, the wretch, by its own bad habit, always having the bitter memory and the painful brooding of the passions, which always burn and consume it. And besides this, who can describe, brethren, those fearful places, those punishing bodies that serve the souls in such great pain and are not destroyed, that unspeakable fire, the darkness, those severe avenging powers, the countless other torments, all that the divine Scriptures speak of in various places and in various ways,[^4] all answering to the evil deeds of the souls [b: D] and to their very evil intentions? Just as the saints receive certain places of light and an angelic gladness answering to their good deeds, so sinners too receive dark and gloomy places full of horror and dread, as the saints say. For what is more fearful or more pitiable than those places to which the demons are sent? What more bitter than the punishment to which they are condemned? And yet sinners too are punished with the demons themselves, as it says: Depart into the everlasting fire prepared for the devil and his angels.[^5] But most fearful of all is what the holy John Chrysostom says: that even if no river of fire were flowing, [1753] [b: A] nor fearful angels stood by, but only, as men were called, some were praised and glorified while others were sent away in dishonour, so as not to see the glory of God,[^6] would not the punishment of that shame and that dishonour, and the pain of falling from such good things, be more bitter than any gehenna? For then the very reproof of conscience, and the very memory of what was done, as we said above, is worse than countless unspeakable torments. For the souls remember everything from here, as the Fathers say, words and deeds and intentions, and they cannot forget any of them. And what it says in the psalm, On that day their reasonings shall perish,[^7] it says of the reasonings of this age: [b: B] for instance of building, of lands, of parents, of children, of all buying and selling. These the soul loses as soon as it goes out of the body, and it remembers or cares for none of them. But what it did according to virtue or according to passion, these it remembers, and loses nothing of them. Rather, if one helped someone, or was himself helped by someone, he always remembers both the one helped by him and the one who helped him; likewise if he was harmed by someone, or himself harmed someone, he always remembers both the one who harmed him and the one harmed by him. And, as I said, the soul loses nothing of what it did in this world, but remembers everything after leaving the body; and that more clearly still and more plainly, as being freed from this earthly body.

4. [b: C] Once we were talking with a great elder about this, and the elder himself said that after leaving the body the soul remembers the passion it acted out, and the sin, and the person with whom it committed it. But I said to him, "Perhaps not so; but perhaps it will have the habit that came to be in it from the working of the sin, and that is what it remembers." And we kept on a long while disputing about this question, wanting to learn. And the elder was not persuaded, saying that it remembers the very form of the sin, and the very place, and the very person who sinned with it. And truly, if it is so, we shall have an even worse end for ourselves.[^8] That is why I always tell you, take pains to cultivate good thoughts, so that you may [b: D] find them there. For whatever a man has here goes out with him, and that is what he will have there. Let us take care to be delivered from such a necessity, brethren; let us make haste, and God works his mercy with us. For he is the hope of all the ends of the earth and of those far off on the sea.[^9] Those at the ends of the earth are those in complete wickedness; those far off on the sea are those in utter ignorance; and yet Christ is the hope of such as these. There is need of a little toil; let us toil, that we may find mercy. If a man has a field and neglects it and it runs to waste, is it not filled with thorns and thistles in the measure that he neglects it? And when he comes to clear it, will not his hands bleed the more, the fuller it is, as he tries to pull up that bad weed [1756] [b: A] which he let come up in the time of his neglect? For it is impossible for a man not to reap what he sowed.[^10] And he who wants to clear his field must first of all root out all the weeds well. For if he does not pull up all their roots well, but only cuts them off at the top, they grow again. So one must pull up the very roots, as I said; and after cleaning it well of the weeds and thorns and the like, he must turn the soil well and break up the clods, and so plough it; and then at last he must sow it with good seed. For if, after he has given it so much tending, he leaves it idle, the weeds come up and find the earth soft and good from the tending, and they send their roots down deep, and grow stronger [b: B] and multiply in it.

## Notes
[^1]: T. The sentence breaks off after "one passion" (ἓν πάθος) and starts again with "if a man has ten good works". The Latin reads a full clause here (*vitium omne in habitum conversum gehennae subjectum est*), so something may be lost. Translated as printed.
[^2]: S. Gen 1:31.
[^3]: S. Ps 76:4 (LXX).
[^4]: T. Printed ἄλλην ἄλλως; read ἄλλῃ ἄλλως, "in various places, in various ways" (Latin *pluribus in locis*).
[^5]: S. Matt 25:41.
[^6]: T. Printed ἐπαινοῦντες, "praising", which leaves the clause without a verb and gives the wrong sense; read ἐπῃνοῦντο, "were praised", in parallel with ἐδοξάζοντο (Latin *gloria … donarentur*).
[^7]: S. Ps 145:4 (LXX).
[^8]: T. Printed κάκην ("cowardice"), which does not construe; read κακὴν, "a worse end" (Latin *pejora erunt novissima nostra*).
[^9]: S. Ps 64:6 (LXX). T. Printed παρὰ τῶν; read περάτων, "the ends", as in the psalm and in the next sentence (τὰ πέρατα τῆς γῆς).
[^10]: S. Gal 6:7.
