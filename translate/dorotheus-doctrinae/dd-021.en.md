title: Instruction XIV, On the building and fitting-together of the virtues of the soul (part 1)
---
[1772] **Instruction XIV.** *On the building and fitting-together of the virtues of the soul.*

1. Scripture says of those midwives who kept the male children of the Israelites alive, that because the midwives feared God, they made houses for themselves.[^1] Is it speaking of houses we can see? And what sense is there in such houses being built because of the fear of God? On the contrary, we are taught to leave the [1773] [b: A] houses we have, when the time comes, for the fear of God. So it is not speaking of a visible house, but of the house of the soul, which a man builds for himself by keeping the commandments of God. By this Scripture teaches us that the fear of God prepares the soul to keep the commandments, and through the commandments the house of the soul is built. Let us too take heed to ourselves, brethren; let us too fear God; let us build houses for ourselves, so that we may find shelter for ourselves in the time of winter, in the time of rains and lightnings and thunders; for winter is a great hardship to him who has no house. How the house of the soul is built we can learn exactly from the visible house. For he who wants to build [b: B] this house must make it secure on every side, and raise the building from all four sides, and not take care of one part and despise the others; since that profits nothing, but makes his aim and his expenses and his toil, all of it, in vain. So it is with the soul too. A man must neglect no part of his building, but raise it evenly and fittingly. This is what abba John says: "I want a man to take a little from each virtue, and not do as some do, who take hold of one virtue and stay in it and work at it alone,[^2] and neglect the rest." Perhaps they even have some advantage in that very virtue, [b: C] and because of this they are not weighed down by the passion opposed to it; but then they are robbed by the other passions, and weighed down by them, and it does not trouble them; they think they have something great. These are like a man who builds one wall and raises it as high as he can, and attends to the height of that wall, and thinks he has done something great, and has not seen that one wind, if it comes, throws it down; for it stands alone, without the bond of the other walls. Nor can anyone make himself a shelter from one wall, for it lies bare on all the other sides. That is not how one should do it; rather, he who wants to build his house and make himself a shelter must build it from every side and make it secure all round.

2. [b: D] And I will tell you how. First he must lay the foundation, which is faith; for without faith, as the Apostle says, it is impossible to please God;[^3] and so build on this foundation in due proportion. Obedience has come his way? He must lay one stone of obedience. A brother's provocation happens? He must lay one stone of long-suffering. Self-control has come his way? He must lay one stone of self-control. So from each virtue that comes his way he must lay one stone in the building, and so raise it all round: one stone of compassion, one stone of cutting off the will, one stone of meekness, and the like. And in all these he must take care [1776] [b: A] for patience and for courage; for these are the corners, and through them the building is bound tight and wall is joined to wall, and the walls neither lean nor break away from one another. For without these no one has the strength to perfect any virtue at all. If a man has no courage in his soul, he does not endure either; and if there is no patience, no one can achieve anything whatever. That is why it says: In your patience you shall possess your souls.[^4] Likewise the builder must set each stone in mortar; for if he sets stone upon stone without mortar, the stones crack and the house falls. The mortar is lowliness, since it is of the earth and is under the feet of all. For every virtue done without lowliness is not [b: B] virtue, as it says in the Gerontikon too: As a ship cannot be made without nails, so it is impossible to be saved without humility.[^5] So whatever good a man does, he must do it with lowliness, so that through lowliness what has been done may be kept safe. The house also needs what are called tie-beams, and these are discernment, which strengthens the house and joins stone to stone and binds the building tight, besides giving the house much comeliness. The roof is love, which is the perfection of the virtues, as the roof was of the house. Then after the roof, the parapet that crowns the housetop. What is the parapet of the housetop? In the law too it is written: If you build yourselves a house and make it a housetop, make [b: C] a parapet for the housetop, so that your children do not fall from the housetop.[^6] The crown is lowliness. For it is lowliness that crowns and guards all the virtues. And just as each virtue must be done with lowliness, in the way we said that each stone is set in mortar, so the perfection of virtue also needs lowliness; or again, because the saints, as they advance, come naturally to lowliness; as I always tell you, the nearer a man comes to God, the more he sees himself a sinner.[^7] And what are the children of whom the law spoke, so that they do not fall from the housetop? The children are the thoughts that arise in the soul, which must be guarded by lowliness, so that they do not fall from the very housetop. It is finished: it has the tie-beams, it has [b: D] the roof of the virtues; see, the house, which we said is the perfection;[^8] see too the parapet; and, in a word, the house is completed.

3. Is anything then lacking to it? We have left out one thing more. And what is this? That the builder too should be a craftsman. For if he is not a craftsman, he puts the building askew, and sooner or later the house falls. The craftsman is he who acts with knowledge. For it happens that a man takes toil over a virtue, and from not acting with knowledge he undoes it, or stays at it without ever gathering it together, and does not manage to finish the work; but lays one stone and lifts it off again. And there is another who lays one and lifts off two. What I mean is this: see, thy [1777] [b: A] brother comes and says a word to thee that afflicts thee or wounds thee, and thou keepest silent and makest a prostration: see, thou hast laid one stone. Then thou goest off and sayest to another brother: "So-and-so insulted me; he said this and that to me; and I not only kept silent, but made him a prostration as well." See, thou didst lay one stone and lift off two. Again, someone makes a prostration wanting to be glorified, and it turns out to be lowliness with vainglory. That is laying one stone and lifting it off. But he who makes a prostration with knowledge persuades himself exactly that it was he who was at fault, and assures himself that he himself is the cause. That is making a prostration with knowledge. Another practises silence, but not with knowledge; for such a man holds that he is doing a virtue, and he does nothing. But [b: B] he who keeps silent with knowledge holds that he is unworthy to speak, as the Fathers said; and this is silence with knowledge. Again, someone does not measure himself, and thinks he is doing something great and that he is humbling himself, and does not know that he has nothing, since he does not act with knowledge. But to measure oneself with knowledge is this:[^9] to hold oneself as nothing, and not worthy even to be counted among men, as abba Moses said to himself: "Ash-skin, black one, thou who art no man, why comest thou among men?"[^10] Again, someone serves a sick man, but he serves him to have a reward, and neither is this with knowledge. And then, if anything distressing befalls him, he readily cuts himself off from his good work and does not get as far as finishing it, since he does not act with knowledge. But he who serves with knowledge [b: C] serves to acquire compassion, serves to acquire bowels of mercies.[^11] For he who has this aim, whatever befalls him, whether affliction from outside or the sick man himself slighting him, bears it without agitation, attending to his own aim, and knowing that the sick man does him more good than he does the sick man. Believe me, he who serves a sick man with knowledge is relieved both of passions and of warfare. For I know a brother who was warred on by shameful desire, and through serving a sick man who had dysentery, with knowledge, he was set free from the warfare. And Evagrius tells of a certain great elder that he freed one of the brethren, who was troubled in the nights, from such things by ordering him to serve the sick with fasting. And being asked the reason for doing [b: D] this,[^12] he said that nothing quenches[^13] such passions so well as mercy.[^14] Again, if someone practises asceticism either for vainglory or holding that he is doing a virtue, such a man does not practise asceticism with knowledge; and then from this he begins to despise his brother, holding himself to be something; and he is found not only laying one stone and lifting off two, but in danger of throwing down the whole wall through judging his neighbour. But he who is self-controlled with knowledge does not hold that he is doing a virtue, nor does he want to be praised as an ascetic; but holds that through self-control he acquires temperance, and through it comes to [1780] [b: A] lowliness; as the Fathers say, the way of lowliness is bodily toils with knowledge, and the rest. And in a word, a man must do each virtue in such a way as to acquire it and come into the habit of it. And so he is found, as we said, the good craftsman-builder, able to build his own house in safety.

## Notes
[^1]: S. Exod 1:21.
[^2]: T. Printed *καὶ αὐτὸν ἐργαζόμενοι*: the masculine has no antecedent; read *αὐτήν* (the virtue). Latin *uni semper virtuti insistere*.
[^3]: S. Heb 11:6.
[^4]: S. Luke 21:19.
[^5]: S. The alphabetical *Apophthegmata* give the saying to Amma Syncletica.
[^6]: S. Deut 22:8, very loosely; the children are Dorotheus' own, and he reads them below.
[^7]: T. Printed *ὅτε ἐγγίζει τις… τοσοῦτον*: *ὅτε* ("when") cannot answer *τοσοῦτον*; read *ὅσον* ("the nearer… the more"). Latin *quo quisque magis… eo*.
[^8]: T. Printed *ἰδοὺ ὁ οἶκος ὅνπερ εἴπομεν εἶναι τὴν τελείωσιν*. The relative should belong to the roof, which was called "the perfection of the virtues" above (so the Latin, *hanc dicimus perfectionem esse*), but it is masculine and agrees with *οἶκος*. Translated as printed.
[^9]: T. Migne prints *Τὸ δὲ [μὴ] μετρεῖν*. The bracketed *μή* is set aside: the sense (and the Latin, *metiri se ipsum cognitione*) needs the positive.
[^10]: S. *Apophthegmata*, Moses 3 (spoken to himself when the clergy drove him out to test him).
[^11]: S. Col 3:12.
[^12]: T. Printed *ἐρωτηθεὶς τὸν ποιεῖν τούτου λόγον*: an article seems lost; read *τὸν ⟨τοῦ⟩ ποιεῖν τούτου λόγον*.
[^13]: T. Printed *κατασβέννυται*, a passive with *πάθη* as its object; read active *κατασβέννυσι*.
[^14]: S. Evagrius, *Praktikos* 91.
