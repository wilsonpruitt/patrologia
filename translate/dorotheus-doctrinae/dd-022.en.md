title: Instruction XIV, On the building and fitting-together of the virtues of the soul (part 2)
---
[1780]

4. [b: B] So he who wants, with God, to come to so fair a state must not say, "The virtues are great, and I cannot reach them." For this belongs either to one who does not hope in the help of God, or to one too sluggish to set his hand to anything good. For take whatever virtue you like and let us exercise ourselves in it, and you will see that achieving it too is in our power. See, it says: Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself.[^1] Do not look at how far thou art from the virtue, and begin[^2] to lose heart and say: "How can I love my neighbour as myself? How can I care for his afflictions as for my own, and above all those hidden in his heart, which I neither see nor know, as I do my own?" Do not busy thyself with reckoning these things, and do not think the virtue too heavy [b: C] and hard to achieve; but for now make a beginning, trusting God. Show him thy purpose and thy zeal, and thou wilt see the help he gives thee to achieve it. What I mean is: suppose there are two ladders, the one going up to heaven, and the other going down to Hades, and thou standest on the earth between the two ladders. Do not set thyself to reckon and say: "How can I fly up from the earth and find myself all at once up at the top of the ladder?" For that is not possible, nor does God require it.[^3] But for now guard thyself against going down: do not harm thy neighbour, do not strike him, do not speak ill of him, do not revile him, do not despise him; and so then begin also to do a little good, by tending [b: D] thy brother with a word, by[^4] suffering with him, and if he needs[^5] anything, by giving it to him; and so, climbing one rung, thou reachest,[^6] with God, even the top of the ladder. For by helping thy neighbour little by little, thou comest also to want what profits him as what profits thyself,[^7] and his benefit as thine own. And this is: Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. If we seek, we find; and if we ask God, he enlightens us. For he says in the Gospel: Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you.[^8] Ask, he says, that is, that we [1781] [b: A] entreat him through prayer. And "Seek" is that we search out how this virtue comes: what it is that brings it, what we must do to acquire it. To search like this about each one is: Seek, and ye shall find. And to knock is to carry out the commandments. For everyone who knocks knocks with his hands; and the hands are taken for the practical life. So we need not only to ask, but also to seek with zeal, and to do, so as to be, as the Apostle said, fitted out for every good work.[^9] What is "fitted out"? When a man wants to build a ship, he first prepares everything the ship needs, down to little nails, and a little pitch, and a little tow; likewise, if a woman wants to set up a little loom, [b: B] she prepares down to a little thread. This is called fitting out: having everything the thing needs prepared.

5. So then, that we too may be fitted out for every good work, having all our preparation made, to do the will of God with knowledge, as he wills and as is pleasing to him. What is it that the Apostle says: The will of God, good and well-pleasing and perfect?[^10] All that happens comes about either by God's permission or by his good pleasure, as he says in the prophet: I am the Lord who makes light and creates darkness;[^11] and again: There is no evil in a city which the Lord has not done.[^12] By evil he means all that does harm, that is, the afflictions [b: C] that come to correct us because of our evil: famine, earthquake, drought, diseases, wars. These do not come by God's good pleasure but by his permission, God permitting them to be brought upon us for our profit. These, then, God does not want us to want, nor to run along with him in them. What I mean is this: it is God's will, by permission as we said, that a city be overthrown. Does he then want us, since it is God's will that it be overthrown, to set fire to it ourselves and burn it, or to take axes and pull it down? Again, there is a permission of God that someone be afflicted or fall sick; is it, then, since it is his will that he be afflicted, that he wants us to afflict him, or that we should say, since it is God's will that he be sick, let us not have mercy on him? That God does not want; [b: D] he does not want us to serve that will of his. He wants us to be so good that what he himself does, he does not want us to want. But what does he want? He wants us to want his good will, the will that comes about, as I said, by good pleasure. And this is all that is done by commandment: loving one another, suffering with one another, giving alms, and all such things. There is the good will of God. And what is the well-pleasing? For it is not the case that whoever does good always does it in a well-pleasing way. Let me tell you how. It happens that someone [1784] [b: A] finds a poor orphan girl who is beautiful, and he is pleased by her beauty, and takes her and brings her up, so that he seems to do it for her as a poor orphan, a beautiful one.[^13] See, it is God's will and it is good, but it is not well-pleasing. The well-pleasing is when a man gives alms not for some human thought, but for the good itself, for compassion: this is well-pleasing to God. And the perfect is when a man gives alms not stingily, nor sluggishly, nor carelessly, but with all his strength and all his purpose, giving as though he himself were receiving, doing good as though he himself were the one done good to; and then it becomes perfect. And so a man is found doing the will of God, as the Apostle says, the good and well-pleasing and perfect. [b: B] See, this is acting with knowledge.

6. For one needs to know the very good of almsgiving, its very grace, how great it is: that it can even forgive sins, as the prophet says: A man's ransom is his own wealth.[^14] And again elsewhere he says: Redeem thy sins with alms.[^15] And the Lord himself said: Be merciful, as your heavenly Father is merciful.[^16] He did not say: Fast, as your heavenly Father fasts. Nor did he say: Be poor, as your heavenly Father is poor. But what does he say? Be merciful, as your heavenly Father is merciful. [b: C] For this virtue in a special way imitates God; it bears his stamp. So there is need, as we said, to attend always to this aim, and to act with knowledge. For there is great difference also in the aim of almsgiving. There is one who gives alms so that his field may be blessed, and God blesses his field; another gives alms for his ship, and God saves his ship; another gives for his children, and God saves and keeps his children; another gives to be glorified, and God glorifies him; and God rejects no one. But what each wants he grants him, provided his soul is not harmed by it. But these all have their reward;[^17] for they have laid up nothing for themselves with God, since the aim [b: D] they set before them was not for the benefit of their souls. Thou didst give so that thy field might be blessed: God blessed thy field. Thou didst give for thy children: God kept thy children. Thou didst give to be glorified: God glorified thee. What then does God owe thee? He has paid thee the reward for what thou didst. And there is another who gives alms to be delivered from the punishment to come; see, this man does it for his soul; this man does it according to God; but still it is not as God wants. For he is still in the slave's state. The slave does not do his master's will because he wants to, but for fear of being beaten. Likewise this man does it to be delivered from punishment, and [1785] [b: A] God delivers him from it. Another gives alms to receive a reward. This is higher than the first; but neither is it yet as God wants. For he is not yet in the son's disposition; but as the hireling does the will of the master to receive the master's reward and make a gain, so this man too does it to make a gain and have a reward.

For there are three dispositions through which we ought to do good, as saint Gregory says;[^18] I have told you this at other times too. Either we do good fearing punishment, and we are in the slave's state; or to receive the reward, and we are in the hireling's disposition; or for the good itself, and we are in the son's disposition. For the son does not do [b: B] his father's will from fear, nor as wanting to receive a reward from him, but wanting to serve him, wanting to honour him and give him rest. So we too ought to give alms in this way, for the good itself, suffering with one another as with our own members; tending another in such a way as though we ourselves were tended through him; giving as though we ourselves were receiving. And this is almsgiving with knowledge; so we are found in the son's disposition, as we said. No one can say: I am poor, and have nothing to show mercy with. For even if thou canst not give as those rich men who gave their gifts into the treasury, give two mites, like the poor widow;[^19] and God accepts them from thee above those gifts. Hast thou not even this? But [b: C] thou hast strength, and canst show mercy to the sick through service. Canst thou not even do this?[^20] Thou canst comfort thy brother with a word. Have mercy[^21] on him then by word, and hear him who says: A good word is better than gifts.[^22] Suppose thou canst not help even with a word: thou canst, if thy brother is provoked against thee, show mercy to him and bear with him in the time of his agitation, seeing him abused by the common enemy; and instead of saying one word to him[^23] and agitating him further, keep silent and have mercy on him and on his soul, snatching him away from the enemy. Again, if thy brother sins against thee, thou canst have mercy on him and forgive him his sin, so that thou too mayest receive forgiveness from God. [b: D] For he says: Forgive, and it shall be forgiven you;[^24] and thou art found showing mercy to thy brother's soul, forgiving him what he sinned against thee. For God has given us power, if we will, to forgive one another the trespasses done against us too. And if thou hast no means of showing mercy to the body, thou showest mercy to his soul; and what mercy is great like mercy to the soul? For as the soul is more precious than the body, so too mercy done to the soul is greater than that [1788] [b: A] done to the body. So no one can say: I cannot show mercy. Each one can show mercy according to his power and according to the state of his soul; only let each be zealous to do whatever good he does with knowledge, as we said of each virtue. For we said that he who acts with knowledge is the proven man and the craftsman, who builds his own house in safety. Of him the Gospel too says that the prudent man builds his house upon the rock, and none of the adversaries can shake it.[^25] May God, the lover of mankind, grant us to hear, and to do what we hear, so that these words may not become a judgement on us on the day of judgement; for to him be the glory unto the [b: B] ages. Amen.

[^8]: S. Matt 7:7.

[^21]: T. Printed *Ἐλοῦ*, which does not fit ("choose" / "take"); read *ἐλέει*, "have mercy". Latin *Miserere igitur illius per verbum*, and the whole passage is about *ἐλεεῖν*.
