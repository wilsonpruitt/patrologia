For he who travels without an aim, as the Fathers said, will toil in vain. So everyone who does a thing ought by all means to have an aim, and to know why he is doing it. What then is the [b: B] aim we ought to have when we go off to one another? First, for the sake of love; for it says, "Thou hast seen thy brother; thou hast seen the Lord thy God."[^1] Second, for the sake of hearing the word of God; for in a gathering the word of God is surely stirred up more: what this one often does not know, another asks. Then also, as I said before, for the sake of learning one's own state. I mean something like this. Suppose someone goes off to eat with those who are gathered there: he should consider himself and see, if a fine food is set before him[^2] and pleases him, whether he can be self-controlled and not take of it; or whether he strives not to get the better of his brother and take more than him; or, if anything is set out in portions, whether he strives not to take the bigger portion, and to leave himself the [b: C] smaller. For there are times when a man is not even ashamed to stretch out his hand and set the small one in front of his brother, and take the big one in front of himself. What, after all, can the difference be between the bigger portion and the smaller? How much is there between the two portions, that for such cheap things he is found getting the better of his brother, and sinning? Again, he takes heed whether he can keep himself from many foods, and does not, as might be expected when he finds various foods, give himself up to surfeit; whether he guards himself from boldness; whether he sees his brother preferred, or given more rest than himself, and is not affected; whether he sees another being bold with someone, or talking much, or behaving in some disorderly way, and pays no attention to him, nor [b: D] condemns him, but rather attends to the most reverent, and strives to do as it says of abba Antony:[^3] that when he went off to people, whatever good he saw each of them have, that he took and held fast, from one meekness,[^4] from another lowliness, from another stillness; and he found himself having what was each one's within himself.

3. See, so we ought to do, and for these reasons visit one another; and when we return to our cells, we ought to search ourselves and learn in what we were [1797] [b: A] helped, or in what we were harmed. And as soon as we find that we were sheltered, let us give thanks to God, who sheltered us so that we came through unharmed; and for the things in which we slipped, let us repent, weep, and mourn our own state. For each is harmed or helped[^5] by his own state, since no one harms anyone. But if we are harmed,[^6] it is from our own state, as I said, that we are harmed. For we can, as I always tell you, be both helped and harmed by every thing, if we want. And I tell you an example, so that you may learn that it is so. It happens that someone is standing at night in some place (I do not mean a monk, but anyone at all in the city), and three men pass by him. And the first thinks to himself that he is waiting for someone, so that he may go off and [b: B] they may commit fornication; the second thinks that he is a thief; the third thinks that he has called some friend of his from the house nearby, and is waiting for him to come down so that they may go off together to prayer. See, all three saw the same man, and in the same place, and yet the three did not think the same thought about him; but one supposed this, another that, another something else, each plainly according to his own state. For just as there are melancholic bodies, with bad humours, which turn every food they take into bad humour, even if the food is wholesome (and the cause is not in the food, but the body itself, as I said, is ill-tempered, and so of necessity it works on the foods and alters them according to its own [b: C] temper): so too the soul that has a bad habit is harmed by every thing; even if the thing is profitable to it, it is harmed. Suppose for me that there is a jar of honey, and someone puts a little wormwood into it: does not that little spoil the whole jar, and make all the honey bitter? So we too do: we throw out a little of our bitterness and spoil our neighbour's good, seeing it according to the bad habit that is in us. But those who have a good habit are found to be like someone who has a body with good humours: even if he eats something harmful, he turns it into good humour according to the temper of his body, and that bad food does him no wrong; since, as I said, his body has good humours, and works on the [b: D] nourishment according to its own temper. And just as we said of the first, that according to his ill temper he changes even the good food into bad humour, so this one, according to his well-tempered body, changes even the bad food into good humour. And I tell you an example, so that you may understand.[^7] The pig has a body with very good humours. Its food is carob pods, and date stones, and mire; and yet, since it has a body with good humours, such food as it has it changes into good humour. So we too, if we have a good habit and a good state, can, as I said before, be helped by every thing, even if the thing is not profitable. And well does the Proverb say, "He who looks gently will be shown mercy."[^8] And elsewhere it says, "All things are contrary to a foolish man."[^9]

4. I heard about a certain brother that when he visited [1800] [b: A] one of the brethren, if he saw his cell in disorder and untidy, he would say to himself: "Blessed is this brother: how he has become free of care about many things, or rather about all earthly things, and has so taken his whole mind up on high that he has no leisure even to set his cell in order." Again, if he went off to another and saw his cell set in order, clean, tidy, he would say again to himself: "As the soul of this brother is clean, so his cell too is clean; and the state of his cell matches the state of his soul." And he never said of anyone, "This one is disorderly," or "This one is vain"; but from his own good state he was helped by each.[^10] May the good God give us a good state, [b: B] so that we too may be able to be helped by each, and never to perceive our neighbour's wickedness. And if we do perceive it, or even suspect it, from our own wickedness, let us at once turn our thought around to thinking well. For not knowing our neighbour's wickedness begets, with God, a goodness through which God is pleased; for to him be glory to the ages. Amen.

**Instruction XVII.** *To overseers and disciples in monasteries: how one must oversee brethren, and how one must be subject to those who oversee.*

1. If thou art an overseer of brethren, take care of them [b: C] with sternness of heart and with bowels of mercy, teaching them by deed and word what is to be done, but more by deed; since examples are more effective. If thou art able, shape them also in bodily things; but if thou art weak, by the good state of the soul, and by the fruits of the Spirit numbered by the Apostle:[^11] love, long-suffering, joy, peace, kindness, goodness, faith, meekness, and self-control against all the passions. And at the faults that happen, do not be greatly vexed, but show without agitation the harm too that comes from the fault; and if thou must rebuke, keep regard for the person and for a fitting time. And do not be exacting over the small faults either, as though thou wert thyself strict in justice, [b: D] nor reprove continually; for this is burdensome, and through the custom of reproof it leads to insensibility and contempt. Do not give orders imperiously, but take counsel with the brother with lowliness; for such speech is persuasive, and convinces more, and gives the neighbour rest. And in a time of agitation, when a brother opposes thee, guard thy tongue from saying anything at all in wrath, and do not let thy heart be lifted up against him; but remember that thy brother is both a member in Christ [1801] [b: A] and an image of God, being abused by our common enemy; and have pity on it, lest the devil take it captive by the wound of anger and put it to death in rancour, and a soul perish through our heedlessness, a soul for which Christ died. And remember that thou too art subject to the same judgment of wrath, and from thine own weakness have compassion on thy brother; and give thanks that thou hast found an occasion to forgive, so that thou too mayest be forgiven greater and more things by God. For "Forgive, and it shall be forgiven you."[^12] But dost thou think that thy brother is harmed by thy long-suffering? But the Apostle commands us to overcome evil with good, and not good with evil.[^13]

[b: B] 2.[^14] And the Fathers too say: "If in rebuking another thou art moved to wrath, thou hast fulfilled thine own passion";[^15] and no man of sense pulls down his own house to build his neighbour's. And if the agitation persists, force thy heart and pray, saying thus: "O God, lover of men and lover of souls, who by thine unspeakable goodness made us from nothing to share in thy good things, and when we had departed from thy commandments called us back through the blood of thine only-begotten Son, our Saviour: now too stand by our weakness, and as once thou didst rebuke the sea in its billows, so now rebuke the agitation of our heart; and be not bereaved in one hour of us both, put to death by sin; and do not say to us, 'What profit is there in my blood, when I go [b: C] down to corruption?'[^16] and, 'Amen I say to you,[^17] I know you not,'[^18] because your lamps have gone out for want of oil." And after this prayer, when thy heart has been made gentle, thou canst then, with understanding of heart, according to the apostolic command, reprove and rebuke,[^19] and with compassion heal and set him right as a weak member. For then the brother too accepts the correction with assurance, blaming himself for his harshness; and through peace thou wilt bring peace to his heart. So let nothing separate thee from the holy tradition of Christ: "Learn of me, for I am meek and lowly in heart."[^20] For one must first take care for a peaceful state, so as not to trouble the heart even on just pretexts, or supposedly [b: D] for the sake of a commandment, being persuaded that we practise all the commandments for the sake of love and purity of heart. Directing thy brother thus, thou wilt hear the voice that says: "If thou bring forth [1804] [b: A] the precious from the worthless, thou shalt be as my mouth."[^21]
