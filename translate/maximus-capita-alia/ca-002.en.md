53. Freedom from excess in foods will be found in a quality lower than luxury; and blamelessness in words, in a quantity better than silence.

54. Fire thy reins by abstinence from foods, and examine thy heart by self-control in words,[^3] and thou shalt have the desiring and the incensive power for the service of good things.[^4]

55. The pleasure of the parts below the belly falls away in ascetics when the body has passed its prime; but that of the gullet still remains for him who has not first chastised it duly. One must strive, then, through the cause to wipe away the reproach of the effect, lest, found there a stranger to the virtue of self-control, thou be put to shame.

56. It is necessary for the ascetic to know when and with what foods he must nourish his body as an enemy; and when to comfort it as a friend, and again to console it as a sick man; lest unawares he set the things of the sick before the friend, and the things of the friend before the enemy, and, as having scandalized both, find them in the time of temptation warring against him.

57. When he who is fed holds food in higher esteem than luxury, then the grace of tears, sojourning with him, begins to exhort him to forget every other sweetness too, since it has already been swallowed up in the incomparable sweetness of these.

58. For him who is broadened the tears are contracted; but they gushed out for him who loved the narrow way.[^5]

59. Neither the sinner nor the just man is outside grief: the one because he has not wholly left evil behind, the other because he has not yet laid hold of what is truly good.

60. Of the things within our power according to capacity, the virtues are prayer and silence; and of those not within our power, but for the most part of the body's constitution, they are fasting and vigil. What then is easier for the contestant to grasp, this he must pursue.

61. The house of prayer[^6] is patience; for it is held fast [1416] in it; and its provision is humility, for it is nourished through it.

62. If thou dost not endure words, thou wilt not be honoured with praises; but looking ahead, before the pleasure, to the pain, thou wilt escape its grievousness.

63. Be not bound by the small, and thou wilt not be enslaved to the greater; for the greater evil is not wont to be formed before the small.

64. Looking toward the greater, thou wilt be fearsome to the lesser; but thou wilt be seen easily despised by these, if thou hast given up toward those.[^7]

65. Thou wilt not be able to attain the greater of the virtues if thou hast not laid hold of the summit of those according to thy capacity.

66. In whom mercy and truth prevail, in those is also everything whatever that is pleasing to God. For truth judges no one without mercy, and mercy shows love for man in nothing apart from truth.

67. Having blended self-control with simplicity, thou wilt come into the embrace of the blessedness from there.

68. Thou shalt not cut down all the passions that war against thee, unless first thou leave unploughed the earth from which they were fed.

69. Some hasten to purge only the matter of the body; others, that of the soul too. For some have received strength against sin in activity only; others against the passion too; but against desire, very few indeed.

70. Evil matter: of the body, impassionedness; of the soul, voluptuousness; and of the mind, passionate attachment.[^8] And touch accuses the first, the remaining senses the second, and a contrary disposition the last.

71. The voluptuary is near the impassioned; and the passionately attached, near the voluptuary; but far from both is the passionless.

72. The impassioned is he who has the sinning element more forceful than his reasoning,[^9] even if for the time he does not sin outwardly. And the voluptuary, he who has the activity of sin weaker than his reasoning, even if he suffers inwardly. And the passionately attached, he who clings to the freedom, or rather the slavery, of the middle things; and the passionless would be he who does not know the difference of all these.

73. Impassionedness perishes from the soul through fasting and prayer; voluptuousness through vigil and silence; and passionate attachment through stillness and attention; and passionlessness is constituted from the remembrance of God.

74. From the lips of passionlessness, like honeycombs of honey, [1417] such as are the words of eternal life, drop.[^10] Who then might be deemed worthy to put his own to those lips, and to lodge between her breasts, and to partake of the sweet odour of her garments?[^11] That is, to be delighted by the laws of the virtues, which, it says, are above all the spices of sensible discernment.

75. Of the visible body every soul will be stripped; but of that of sin,[^12] the few of the life here who have enjoyed its sweet things.[^13]

76. All will be seen dead out of the living; but only those will be put to death to sin who have hated it from disposition.[^14]

77. Who would see himself naked of sin before the common death of the body? And who is he that has known himself and his own nature, what it is, before the coming stripping?

*Of the same.*[^15]

A soul wounded with bridal yearning[^16]
Prayer knows how thus to join to the Bridegroom.

78. The rational soul, lying on the border between the sensible and the noetic light, was entrusted through the former to see and do the things of the body, and through the latter those of the spirit.[^17] But since the latter has been dimmed for it, and the former made clear, through the custom from the beginning, unless it comes to be wholly with the noetic in prayer, it cannot gaze wholly on divine things. And it must needs be in the no-man's-land of darkness and light, moving by relation in the former, and by imagination in the latter.

79. Let prayer abide together with the mind, as a ray in the sun; without which the cares of sense, like waterless clouds flying round,[^18] shut the mind out from its own brightness.

80. A passionate mind cannot enter within the narrow gate[^19] of prayer before it leaves behind the care that comes by relation; but it will be pained, busying itself forever about her outer tents.

81. The strength of prayer is voluntary hunger for foods; and of hunger, to see nothing and hear nothing of the things of the world, except where it is of all necessity. He who takes no forethought for these has not made firm the building of fasting, and has caused that of prayer to be broken all round within it.

82. Unless the mind becomes free from excess of all the things of sense, it cannot become upward-borne and know its own dignity.

83. Prayer, strengthened [1420] by tears, puts outside the soul all the thoughts hostile to it; but the distraction of the mind, empowered by the law of jesting, brings them back in; and he who banishes this has banished together with it the all-causing evil of boldness.[^20]

84. Fasting is a symbol of day, because it is manifest; and prayer of night, because it is unmanifest. He then who duly pursues each of these in each will arrive at the city of the watchmen, wherein *pain, grief and sighing have fled away*,[^21] in Christ.

85. Spiritual working is wont to subsist even apart from bodily work. Blessed then is he who has deemed immaterial working better than material work. For through it he has filled up the lack of work, having lived the hidden life of prayer, manifest to God.[^22]

86. The Apostle exhorts us to endure in faith, to rejoice in hope, to persevere in prayer,[^23] so that the good of joy may abide with us. And if this is so, then he who does not endure is not faithful; and he who does not rejoice is not of good hope; for he has cast away prayer, the cause of joy, in not persevering in it.

87. If the mind, having from the beginning kept company with worldly thoughts, acquired so great a friendship among them, how great a familiarity would it not have in continual prayer? For in the things in which, it says, it lingers, in them it is also wont to be broadened.[^24]

88. Just as the mind, long ago divided from its own dwelling,[^25] forgot its brightness, so it must again, coming to be in forgetfulness of the things here, run back up to it through prayer.

89. Like an infant at a mother's breasts grown cold, so will the mind be found toward a prayer that cannot comfort it; but toward one that [1421] is otherwise, as a child that falls asleep from pleasure in her arms.

90. *There*, it says, in the mournful bed, that is, of the virtuous life, prayer too would say to her own lover, like the bride in the Song, *I will give thee my breasts*,[^26] if thou be wholly disposed toward me.

91. He cannot acquire friendship with prayer who has not renounced all matter except food and shelter.[^27] Be outside the rest in prayer, thou who willest to be with the mind alone.

92. The witness of the God-loving man is prayer of a single thought; and of sensible thought, a timely word; and of free sense, let it be a uniform taste. In these three, then, the things of the soul are said to be strong.

93. The nature of him who prays must be well smoothed and softened, as that of children,[^28] so that, like theirs, it may yieldingly receive the unfolding ordered into it from prayer. Therefore be not negligent, thou who lovest to be joined to it.

94. Not all have the same aim in prayer: one has one, and another another. For one prays, if it is possible, that his heart may be always with the prayer, and even toward going up beyond it; another, that he may not be cut off by thoughts in it. But all pray either to be kept in good things or not to be carried away with evils.

95. If from prayer no one comes unhumbled, for he who prays in humility is broken, then he does not pray in humility who is outwardly bold.

96. Looking to the widow who moved her suit before the cruel judge,[^29] thou who prayest shalt not lose heart because of the slowness of the good things of prayer.

97. Prayer will not abide with thee, if thou linger over what is reasoned within and talked of without; but the same will be seen returning to him who for its sake cuts back the most.

98. Unless the words of prayer sink into the flanks of the soul, the tears will not be permitted to flood over the cheeks of the face.

99. For the farmer tears rise up when the seeds have been cast into the earth out of sight;[^30] and for the monk tears gush up when he goes through the words of prayer not without toil.

100. Prayer is the key of the kingdom of heaven.[^31] He who [1424] holds it as he ought sees the good things laid up for its friends. But he who has had no boldness in it looks round only at the things present.

101. The mind cannot in the time of prayer say to God with boldness, *Thou hast burst my bonds; to thee will I sacrifice the sacrifice of praise*,[^32] unless it has broken away, by desire for better things, from cowardice and sloth, from much sleep and gluttony, from which comes stumbling.

102. He who wanders in prayer stands outside the first veil; he who accomplishes it with a single thought comes within; but he alone has peered into the Holy of Holies[^33] who, with the peace of natural thoughts, also considers the things concerning that [essence] which surpasses every mind,[^34] and is deemed worthy of some manifestation of light from there.

103. When the soul has come to be at leisure from the things outside, then a kind of flame, encircling it, renders it wholly red-hot, as iron once for all in the fire; and it is the same soul, but no longer touchable by touches from without, as neither is the red-hot iron.

104. Blessed is he who in this life has been deemed worthy to be seen thus, and has beheld the same statue, of clay by nature, fiery by grace.

105. On beginners the law of prayer presses like some master; but for those who have advanced, it is as yearning that drives on a hungry man as to a costly banquet.

106. For those who pursue practice well, prayer sometimes, like an overshadowing cloud,[^35] keeps the burning thoughts away from them; and sometimes, sprinkling on them drops as of tears, shows the spiritual objects of contemplation.

107. Song will be found the sweetening of a lyre struck by someone from without; but the soul, unless the mystical address in the Spirit comes together with it in prayer, is not wont to show itself readily compunct. For the saying, *We know not what we should pray for as we ought*,[^36] and what follows, leads him who prays to this.

*Of the same.*[^37]

The mind is lit up, to the contemplation of knowledge
Lifted on high, when it has lighted on these words.

108. It is necessary for the man of knowledge[^38] to know when his mind is in the country of concepts, and when in that of thoughts, and when in that of sense; and in this again, whether it is found rather among the things in season or among the things out of season.

109. The mind, not being among concepts, is certainly among thoughts; and being among thoughts, it is not among concepts. But having come into sense, it is with all.
