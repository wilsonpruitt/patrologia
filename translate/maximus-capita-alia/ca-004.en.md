title: Other Chapters, 4
---
[1440] 162. Paradise is the contemplation of intelligible things. Into it the man of knowledge enters within in prayer, as into a house; but the practical man will be seen as a passer-by, desiring to peep within, but not permitted, because the fence rises above his spiritual stature.

163. The bodily passions are like beasts; the psychic, like birds. But the practical man can wall the former off from the rational vineyard;[^1] not, however, the birds as well, unless he comes to be in spiritual contemplation, even though he make the utmost effort to guard what is within.

164. The practical man cannot become superior to moral seemliness unless he too, like the patriarch Abraham,[^2] goes out: as from his house,[^3] from the natural law; as from his kindred, from the life of his own age and lived by relation. For so he too will receive as a seal[^4] the removal of the pleasure of generation,[^5] by which the veil that lies about us from birth does not permit full freedom to be granted to us.

165. Neither does a colt in spring bear to stay inside the manger and eat what is in it, nor can a newly initiated mind endure for long the straitness of prayer; it is rather glad, like the colt, to go out into the breadth of natural contemplation, which is found in psalmody and in reading.

166. Practice has, as *loins girded*, fasting and chastity, its vital powers; and contemplation, as *lamps burning*,[^6] silence and prayer, the virtues of knowledge, setting over them, the one over the former, thought as a tutor; the other over the latter, as a bride's escort, the inward word.

167. An imperfect mind is not permitted to enter the fruitful vineyard of prayer; but only, and hardly, into the bare echoes of the psalms, as a poor man to the gleanings.[^7]

168. As not all who come into converse with a king can dine with him, so neither will all who come into the audience of prayer be seen in the contemplation that belongs to it.

169. A muzzle for anger is timely silence; for irrational desire, measured eating; and for a thought hard to restrain, single-thought prayer.[^8]

170. Whoever wishes to dive both into the lower deep and into the upper, the one for the sensible, the other for the intelligible pearl,[^9] so as to draw it up: [1441] unless this one be stripped of his clothing and that one of sense, both will miss what they seek.

171. The mind that has come to be within its own understanding will be found, in praying, conversing like a bridegroom with the bride in the bridal chamber; but the one not permitted to enter, standing outside, groaning, cries: *Who will lead me into the fortified city? or who will guide me*[^10] until I no longer look *upon vanities and lying frenzies*[^11] in praying?

172. As food without salt is reckoned by the throat,[^12] such too will prayer without compunction be reckoned by the mind that pursues it.[^13]

173. The soul still pursuing prayer is like a woman in labour; but the soul that has attained it, like one who has given birth and become full of joy because of the child.[^14]

174. Of old the Amorite[^15] dwelling in the mountain came out and wounded those who forced their way to pass through;[^16] and now evil forgetfulness pursues those who, before chastity, attempt to climb to the higher prayer of simplicity.

175. The demons are by nature in great enmity to pure prayer. And what strikes them with terror is not a multitude of evils,[^17] as an army strikes the enemies outside; but the concord of the three: of mind with reason, and of reason with sense.

176. Bare prayer will be seen as bread, strengthening beginners; that with some contemplation, as oil, fattening; the formless, as fragrant wine,[^18] from which those who take their fill insatiably are beside themselves.

177. The wild ass is said to laugh at the multitude of the city;[^19] and the unicorn to be able to be bound by no one.[^20] And the mind that has become king of nature and of the principles concerning nature laughs, when praying, at vanity of thoughts; and can be mastered by none of the things under sense.

178. He who contends must draw in his sense to uniform food, and his mind to single-thought prayer; for so, having become without relation to the passions, he will come even to being caught up to the Lord[^21] in praying.

[1444] 179. He who shakes a stick at dogs has made them savage against himself; and he who forces himself to pray purely, the demons.

180. The voluptuous, when they pray, being material, have their thoughts like frogs distracting them; the moderate in passion have contemplations like nightingales delighting them, by the passing from some branches to others, that is, to varied contemplations; but silence attends the passionless, and much quiescence from thoughts and concepts in praying.

181. Of old Miriam, Moses' sister,[^22] seeing the fall of the enemies, took up the timbrel and led the women singing the victory songs;[^23] and now, in praise of the soul that has conquered the passions, love, the better among the virtues,[^24] roused up, taking in hand the cithara with song as a kind of contemplation, laboured for her long ago into an abundance of beauty, does not cease, rejoicing with those about her, to praise God.

182. When, through constancy in prayer, the oracles of the psalms are held fast in the heart of the one who prays, then he too, like good earth, begins of itself[^25] to bring forth: as roses, the contemplation of incorporeals; as lilies, the far-shining clarity of bodies; as violets, the varied, hard-to-contemplate character of the divine judgements.

183. A flame bound in matter becomes light-bearing; a soul loosed from matter is found God-bearing. And the former is of a nature to rise as far as its fuel; the latter, as far as the consummation of divine yearning.

184. The soul that has perfectly denied itself and wholly dedicated itself to prayer, being found above creation, comes down not when it wishes, but when it shall seem just to him who orders all our things by weight and measure.[^26]

185. When acedia is driven out of the soul and wickedness shaken out of the understanding, then the mind, becoming bare in simplicity and in an artless life, and without any veil of shame or screen of ostentation, it too sings a new song to God;[^27] and, striking up a melody in gladness, gives thanks that it keeps in advance the feast of the dedication of the life to come.

186. When the praying soul begins to be acted upon by certain more divine activities, then she too, like the Bride in the Song, sings in answer such things to those of like manner: *My kinsman put forth his hand from the opening, and my belly was stirred for him*.[^28]

187. Both the soldier, returning from war, lays aside [1445] the weight of his arms; and the practical man his thoughts, coming to contemplation: for neither has the one need of arms except in war, nor the other of thoughts.

188. The practical contemplate bodies as they stand in position; the contemplative, as they stand in nature; and only the men of knowledge see the principles of both.

189. In the principles of bodies the incorporeal things are known; and in the incorporeal, the superessential Word,[^29] to whom every earnest soul hastens to depart.[^30]

190. The principles of bodies are like bones covered by the sensible things; which no one may see of those who have not come outside the passionate attachment to sensible things.

191. Both the soldier lays down his arms when he has left the war; and the contemplative his thoughts, departing to the Lord.

192. Both the general who misses the spoils in war falls into despondency; and the practical man in prayer who misses spiritual contemplation.

193. A deer runs to springs of bodily waters, as one bitten by a beast;[^31] and a soul wounded by the sweetest arrow, that of prayer, to the radiances of incorporeals.

194. Neither can the bodily eye see the grain of wheat, nor the practical mind its own nature, unless it be stripped: the former of the husk, the latter of the relation that covers it round.

195. Stars are hidden at the rising of the sun; and thoughts fail when the mind returns to its own kingdom.

196. After the end of practice, the spiritual contemplations, poured round the mind like rays of the sun peeping over the horizon, seem to strike it from outside, though they are its own, and embrace it because of its purity.

197. Such things too a contemplative mind can utter, when it comes down from above, from heaven, bent by the necessities of nature; such as he too who declared:[^32] «What is more wonderful than divine beauty? and what notion more gracious than the magnificence of God? And what longing is so piercing and unbearable as that which comes from God into the soul purified of all vice, and saying from its disposition: *I am wounded with love*[^33]?»

198. It belongs to him to say: *My heart grew hot within me, and in my meditation* [1448] *a fire shall be kindled*,[^34] to him who does not grow weary of following after God through prayer, nor desires to look upon the day of man.[^35]

199. Let the practical soul too say, after the putting off of evils, like the Bride in the Song, against the wicked demons and thoughts that force her again to look upon vanities and lying frenzies:[^36] *I have put off my tunic; how shall I put it on? I have washed my feet; how shall I defile them?*[^37]

200. O the God-loving soul, that dares to say to God: *Tell me, thou good shepherd, where thou pasturest thy sheep, where thou makest thy lambs lie down at noon, that, following them, I may not become as one gone astray among the flocks of companions*.[^38]

201. The practical soul, seeking to grasp the principle of prayer and unable to, cries such things herself, like the Bride in the Song: *On my bed by night I sought him whom I loved; I sought, and found him not; I called him, and he did not hear me. I will rise now*, through more laborious prayer, *and going round in the city, in the broad streets and the market places, I will seek him whom I loved*.[^39] Perhaps he will be found by me who is in this whole and outside the whole. *I shall be satisfied when his glory appears to me*.[^40]

202. When the soul begins to become all tears from the joy of prayer, then she too, speaking freely, cries as to her own bridegroom: *Let my kinsman come down into his garden, and let him eat*,[^41] as of my fruits, the consolation I have laboured for.

203. When the practical soul begins, from the greatness and beauty of creatures, to wonder at the Creator,[^42] and to revel in the pleasure that comes from them, she too, struck with amazement, cries these things: *How beautiful thou art become, bridegroom,*[^43] [1449] *paradise of thy Father. Flower of the field, and its cedars as the cedars of Lebanon. In his shadow I desired and sat down, and the fruit appeared sweet in my throat*.[^44]

204. If he who receives a king into his chamber is found so illustrious and glorious, and [full of] every other joy,[^45] what would the soul feel that, in being cleansed, has received the King of kings according to his promise that does not lie?[^46] And what will she leave undone, casting out what does not seem to make for his rest, and bringing within what is to his pleasing?

205. He who expects to be called by the king on the morrow: in care of what else will he be, than of the meditation of words that will be to his pleasing? Every soul that keeps this will not be seen unprepared for the judgement-seat there.

206. Blessed the soul that, because it expects its Lord to come today, counts as nothing at all the strain of the whole day, nor that of the night, because he is about to appear to it straightway at dawn.[^47]

207. God sees all; and those who contemplate nothing in praying see God. And as many as see God are also heard by him; but as many as are not heard do not see him either. Blessed is he who believes that he is seen by God; for his foot will not be shaken,[^48] for the sake of pleasing him.

208. The good things of the kingdom that is within us,[^49] which the world-loving eye has not seen, and the ambitious ear has not heard, and which have not come up upon a heart empty of the Holy Spirit,[^50] are pledges[^51] of the good things that are to be given to the just in the coming kingdom of Christ; and he who does not delight in these, which are the fruits of the Spirit,[^52] cannot come into the enjoyment of those.

209. The thoughts of the practical are like deer. For as they are sometimes up on the mountains for fear of the hunters, and sometimes down in the valleys for longing for what is in them, so these too can neither always be in spiritual contemplation, because of their poverty, nor always in natural contemplation, because they do not always pursue rest. Those of the contemplatives, however, become despisers of pedestrian objects of contemplation.

210. Drops of dew[^53] make drunk the furrows of the earth
