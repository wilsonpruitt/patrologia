211. Of the Godhead understood in Trinity no one will be seen in contemplation who has not been seen above the material dyad and the neighbouring monad; nor will he become higher than this, unless he has made his own mind singular among the things understood.[^2]

212. It will not be found so hard to cut off the course of a river, so that it is not borne downward, as to rein back the rush of the mind, so that it is not scattered among the things seen, but, when it wills, is gathered for the one who prays toward the things above and kindred;[^3] even though this stands according to nature, and that has been established contrary to nature.

213. Those who are being purified, sending the mind on within[^4] the things seen, are filled with so great an amazement, and are made full of so great a joy, that they are able to contain nothing else of earthly things, not even if all the things that are fought over should flow together to them.

214. The laws of nature, even when only spoken, are quite enough for being greatly wondered at; but when they are considered, they are found to be meadows in fair flower, teeming, as from heavenly nectar, in undefiled flowers with the sweetness of the spiritual banquet.

215. In the dewy flowers of the meadows the bees settle round the queen of the swarm; and in the compunction that has come unceasingly upon the soul, the noetic powers, circling round it as its own, join in longing for the things after its heart.[^5]

216. In the world that is seen, man is seen as another world; and in the world that is understood, reasoning.[^6] For he is the herald of heaven and of the things in the middle to that world; and of mind and sense, and of the things about them, the interpreter is reasoning; without which both worlds would have been struck mute.

217. Not so does a captive, released after a long time, go on his way, as the mind, freed from material relations, goes on its way toward the heavenly things as toward its own, with exulting foot.

218. To him who prays not with attention but scattered, the psalm will be reckoned a barbarian; and he himself to the psalm, as a barbarian;[^7] and both, as raving by demons.[^8]

[1453] 219. Those to whom the world has been crucified, and those who have been crucified to the world, are not the same.[^9] For to the former the nails are fasting and vigil; to the latter, non-possession and being set at nought. And without the second, the labours of the first are unprofitable.

220. He cannot pray purely who is held fast by the passion of love of finery and of love of honour.[^10] For the relations about these, and the thoughts of vanity that have affinity with them, become like ropes twined about him, dragging him down like a tethered sparrow trying to fly up, at the time of prayer.

221. It is impossible that the mind of him who has not acquired self-control and friendly love should become peaceful in prayer. For the one strives to dissolve the enmity against the soul that comes from the body; the other, that toward the neighbour, for God's sake. And thence *the peace that surpasses every mind*,[^11] coming to dwell, has promised to make its abode with him who has thus made peace.[^12]

222. It is necessary for him who strives to enter the kingdom of God that the work of his justice abound in good things:[^13] in almsgiving, through the giving out of his want; and in labours, those for peace, through the deprivation that belongs to endurance in the Lord.[^14]

223. Neither he who is deficient toward virtue through negligence, nor he who is excessive through conceit, will be found within the harbour of passionlessness. For neither of them has come into the enjoyment of the goods that come from justice, since a go-between is found for them, deficiency and excess.[^15]

224. Neither will the earth, unless it gives the farmer back his labour many times over, make him well provided and free of want, supplying only the seed, or even some small increase; nor, for the practical man, can the work accomplished make him just before God, unless his zeal be found greater than his choice.

225. Not all who do not love their neighbour are able to hate; nor are those who do not hate him able to love; and it is one thing to envy his virtue, another not to be found an obstacle to him toward progress. And this will be found the [1456] degree of utmost vice: not only to be stung at his successes, but also to slander his good things, as though they were not such.

226. Other are the bodily passions, and other are the psychic; other those according to nature, and other those contrary to nature. He, then, who repels the one, but takes no forethought for the others, is like a man who sets up a high and close-covered hedge against the beasts, but takes pleasure in the birds that eat the splendid grapes of the rational vineyard.

227. First the soul comes to be in an imagination of the evil; then in desire; then in pleasure or grief; then, thus, in sense; then, after it, in touch, the apparent and the unapparent; the thoughts following along in all, except the first motion; and if this is not admitted, all the evil after it will be found without activity.

228. Those who draw near to passionlessness are shaken by imaginations;[^16] by desires, those moderate in passion; by relations, the voluptuous. And they come to be in sense of the evil who misuse the things of need, but with grief; and in touch, those who keep company with it without grief.

229. Pleasure is seated in all the members of the body; but it does not appear to trouble all alike; but some more in the desiring part of the soul; others in the incensive;[^17] others in its rational part; through gluttony, irascibility, and wickedness, the cause of all the unholy passions.

230. So it is necessary to open the sense-organs, like the gates of the city. But it is necessary not to allow, in the opening for the necessary things, that there come in with them the things that favour the enemies[^18] and are established as causes of battle.

231. The activity of pleasure is desire; of anger, irascibility; of envy, wickedness.[^19] And he does not make peace with the last who does not struggle against the rulers; nor can he enter the harbour of moderation of passion who pursues the commandments by force.

[1457] 232. Those who beat back the assaults do not allow the thoughts to enter like beasts within the rational vineyard and to set about the ruin of its things; but those who pair with them allow them simply to enter, but not to touch any of its things; who, though they converse with pleasure with the passions through the thoughts, yet do not come to consent, are like those who let the solitary beast[^20] come in within the hedge, but did not allow it to come to satiety of the clusters of the vineyard; then they found it stronger than their own power, they who are often found in consent to passions.

233. He has not yet attained simplicity who still needs the care of the hedge that is of self-control. For «the perfect man,» he says, «does not exercise self-control»;[^21] and he is like one who has a vine or a field, not in the midst of many other vineyards and lands, but in a corner somewhere, and for this reason needs much guarding and watchfulness. For of him who has attained simplicity the vine becomes altogether untouched, as of a king, or of some other fearsome man who holds power beside him, who by hearsay alone makes thieves and passers-by shudder at the attempt.

234. Many go up on the cross of hardship; but few accept its nails; many are slaves of elective obedience; but of non-elective shame, only those who have destroyed the love of honour.

235. Many have stripped off all the garments of skin;[^22] but, as the last, that of vainglory, only [1460] those who have loathed its mother, self-complacency.

236. He who receives the praise of men and the ease of the body, but does not admit them, this man, having become naked of the last garment of vainglory, has been deemed worthy from this point on to put on the brightness of the dwelling from heaven, which is sought with many groans.[^23]

237. Activity is one thing, and the effect another.[^24] And the one is indicative of sin completed; the other, only of voluptuousness activated within, but not without: as those who are not moved from their own land, but nonetheless pay as tribute to those who lord it over them the things to their liking.

238. When taste rules in pleasures, it is impossible that all the senses should not follow along too, even if in the colder the parts below the belly seem to be at peace, as with the aged who have [not] received a burning from fire.[^25] But the barren woman who commits adultery will not be judged temperate with respect to bearing. And I would say that everyone is temperate who does not commit fornication, nor is bewitched by sight.

239. In foods and forms and voices the desiring power of the soul is tested, of what sort it is, by the volitive and the otherwise disposed [impulses][^26] in taste and sight and hearing: whether as using them, or as misusing them, or as found midway toward both.

240. In those in whom fear does not lead, the thoughts will be found in confusion, *as sheep not having a shepherd*;[^27] but in good order, and within the fold, by good rule, when it follows or leads.

241. Of faith, fear is the son; and of the commandments, he is the herdsman; and he who has not acquired his mother will not be deemed worthy to be seen a sheep of the pasture that is according to the Lord.

242. Some have only the beginnings of good things; others also their middles; others also their ends; without which each will be found as a light-armed soldier, or an officer, outside the rations. And for this reason the one keeping only his own house, and barely, from those who attempt to abuse it; the other not found in the honour that befits him, among those with whom he comes together.

243. Those who exhort men who are perfect as regards the gullet to come together with pleasures do something like those who bid men reopen wounds already come to health; or scratch the itch for its sweetness; or eat the things that kindle the fever; or [1461] fence in his vineyard, and allow *the mind of the flesh*[^28] to come in with the good notions, *as a wild solitary beast*,[^29] so as to graze on grapes. Whom one must not obey, nor be bent by the untimely flatteries of men and of passions; but strengthen the hedge through self-control, until the beasts, the fleshly passions, cease to roar, and the vain thoughts, like birds, no longer come down to ravage the soul flourishing like a vine[^30] with the contemplations that are in Christ Jesus our Lord. Amen.
