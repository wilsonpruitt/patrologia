---
[320] … this divine teacher said, because for all the things done by him in a manner befitting God he used the intellectual and rational soul united to him according to hypostasis.[^1]

PYRRHUS. In truth, by the very things through which they think to campaign against piety, by these they have undergone refutation without knowing it. But one must work through the other testimony too which they adduce from the Father, so as to leave them no pretext against the truth.

MAXIMUS. What is it? For I do not know it.

PYRRHUS. That in which that wonderful man says: «He was born of a woman, raising up for himself from the first fashioning the form of man, in the showing of flesh, but without fleshly wills and human thoughts, in the image of newness. For the volition was of the Godhead alone.»[^2]

MAXIMUS. Being self-interpreting, it needs no help at all from reasonings.[^3]

PYRRHUS. And how is it doubtful to these men?

MAXIMUS. From much ignorance. For to whom is it not plain, unless the soul's power of sight is wholly maimed, that the Father went through these things not about the natural principle, but about the mode of his existence according to the flesh, wishing to show that the incarnation is a work of the divine and only volition (the Father being well pleased, the Son working it himself, and the Holy Spirit working with him), but not of fleshly movement and human thoughts, that is, of the sequence of marriage? For the God of all, become man, did not innovate the principle of nature; since he would no longer have been man, not having the principle of nature in all things undiminished and unalterable; but the mode, that is, the conception through seed and the birth through corruption. Then the God-minded teachers of the Church in no way denied the natural principles of the things united; but in agreement with the evangelists, and apostles, and prophets, they said that our Lord and God Jesus Christ is, according to both his natures, volitive and operative of our salvation.[^4]

PYRRHUS. And is it possible to show this from the writings of the Old and New?

MAXIMUS. Very much so. For the Fathers too, moved not from themselves but having learned from these, taught us this too with love for man. For it was not they who spoke, but the grace of the Spirit which had wholly interpenetrated them.[^5]

PYRRHUS. Since, purposing to help in imitation of the divine goodness, thou hast given thyself up to these toils of inquiry, teach this too without hesitation.

MAXIMUS. In the holy Gospels it is said about the Lord that On the morrow Jesus willed [321] to go into Galilee.[^6] And it is plain that he willed to go in in respect of that by which he was not there; and he was not there by manhood, for by Godhead he is absent from nothing. Then as man, and not as God, he willed to go in; and he was volitive as man.

And elsewhere again: I will that where I am, they also may be.[^7] If as God Christ is above the where (for as God he is not in place), and it is impossible for created nature to be above the where, then as man he wills that where he is they too may be; and the same was volitive as man.

And elsewhere again: And coming to a place, he said: I thirst. And they gave him wine mixed with gall; and having tasted, he did not will to drink.[^8] According to which part of him is he said to have thirsted? If according to the Godhead, his Godhead will be subject to passion, aspiring after drink contrary to nature. But if according to the manhood, then in the respect in which he thirsted, in that too he did not will to drink what was unsuitable to the nature; and the same was volitive also as man.

And elsewhere he says: And Jesus walked in Galilee; for he did not will to walk in Judaea, because the Jews sought to kill him.[^9] If walking belongs by nature to the flesh, but not to the Godhead united to it according to hypostasis, then as man the same, walking in Galilee, did not will to walk in Judaea; and the same was volitive also as man.

And elsewhere again: And going out from there they passed through Galilee; and he did not will that anyone should know.[^10] It is agreed by all that journeying is by nature of the Lord's manhood, as has been said, but not of his Godhead. And if journeying belonged to him as man, but not as God by nature, then as man too, passing through with the disciples, he did not will that anyone should know; and the same was volitive also as man.

And elsewhere again: And rising from there he went away into the borders of Tyre and Sidon. And entering a house, he willed no one to know; and he could not be hid. If as God Christ was self-subsistent power, but as man weakness (For though he was crucified from weakness, says the divine Apostle, yet he lives from the power of God),[^11] then as man, and not as God, entering a house, he did not will that anyone should know, and he could not be hid; and the same was volitive also as man.

And elsewhere he says: About the fourth watch of the night he comes to them, walking on the sea; and he willed to pass them by.[^12] If anyone takes this to have been said about him as God, by bodily limits (above, I mean, and below; before and behind; right and [324] left) he will be forced to say that the Godhead is encompassed. But if he says that these things were said about him as man, then the same was volitive also as man.

And elsewhere he says: And his disciples came to him, saying to him: Where wilt thou that we go and prepare for thee to eat the Pascha?[^13] If the eating of the Pascha belongs to those under the law, and the Lord became under the law as man, and not as God, then as man, willing, he ate the Pascha; and the same was volitive also as man.

And the divine Apostle too, in the Epistle to the Hebrews, says about him: Become obedient unto death, even the death of the cross.[^14] Willing, then, did he obey, or not willing? But if not willing, it would reasonably be called tyranny and not obedience. But if willing (and he became obedient not as God but as man: «For as God he is neither obedient», according to the Fathers, «nor disobedient»; «For these things belong to what is second and under the hand», says the divine Gregory),[^15] then as man too he was volitive.

And the blessed David too, in the thirty-ninth psalm: Sacrifice and offering, he says, thou didst not will, but a body thou hast prepared for me. Whole burnt offerings and for sin thou didst not seek; then I said: Behold, I come. In the head of the book it is written of me, to do thy will; my God, I wished.[^16] That Christ as man, and not as God, is said to have the Father as his God, just as he has him as Father as God and not as man, I think that not even those on the opposite side doubt. But if as man, and not as God, the Father is his God, then as man too, and not as God, he wished to do the will of the Father and his own; for the will of the Father is his too, since he too is God according to essence. For if this is so, not only as God and consubstantial with the Father was he volitive, but also as man and consubstantial with us.

One must know that the words of the psalm now set down the divine Apostle, in the Epistle to the Hebrews, took as of the Lord.[^17] And the great Moses, at the genesis of man, brings in God saying: Let us make man according to our image and according to likeness.[^18] If then man is the image of the divine nature, and the divine nature is self-determining, then the image too, if indeed it preserves its likeness to the archetype, is self-determining by nature. And if this is so, and the archetype by nature has become image by nature too, then according to both his natures the same was volitive by nature. For it was shown before from the Fathers that the will is the self-determination according to nature.

One must know that self-determination is not said homonymously,[^19] [325] as nature too is not; but it is taken in one way of God, in another of angels, and in another of men. Of God, superessentially; of angels, as the readiness running together with the habit and admitting no interval of time at all; of men, as the habit being conceived in time before the readiness. For if Adam hearkened willing, and looked willing, and ate having willed, then the volition in us is the first to suffer. And if the volition in us is the first to suffer, and according to them the Word, becoming man, did not assume it with the nature, then I have not come to be without sin. And if I have not come to be without sin, then I have not been saved; if indeed the unassumed is unhealed.[^20]

Then, if the self-determining power of the nature is his work and making, and the Word incarnate, according to them, did not assume it with the nature in ineffable union, either, condemning his own fashioning as not good, he sent it away from himself; or, begrudging us its healing, he deprives us of complete salvation and shows himself to be under passion, by not willing or by not being able to save perfectly.

These things, then, concerning the incarnate God the Word being volitive by nature also as man. That the same is volitive by nature also as God, we shall know from here. For our Lord and God himself, the only truth, says about himself in the Gospels thus: Jerusalem, Jerusalem, that killest the prophets and stonest those sent to her, how often I willed to gather thy children, as a hen gathers her young under her wings, and ye willed not! Now your house is left to you desolate.[^21] For it is plain that he did not say this as man, since he had recently become man; but as God, showing the varied modes of his wise providence concerning man, through which, when he willed to gather the nature to himself from the error of outward things, it did not will.

And again he says: As the Father raises the dead and gives life, so the Son too gives life to whom he will.[^22] If the «as» is a comparative adverb, and comparisons are of things consubstantial, then this cannot be said of Christ according to the human. Then the Saviour taught us that, as the Father, being God, [328] gives life to the dead by will, so he too, being consubstantial with the Father and of one will with him, gives life to whom he will. These are the doctrines of the evangelists, and apostles, and prophets. What greater demonstration, then, beyond these, concerning the same being volitive by nature both as he is God and as man?

PYRRHUS. Nothing is clearer than these for the demonstration that the wills are natural. How then did Vigilius, at that time president of the Romans, receive the libellus presented by Menas, who was bishop of the imperial city, containing one will, and this when it had been displayed in the imperial secretum of him who was then reigning over the Romans, and of the senate?[^23]

MAXIMUS. I marvel how, being patriarchs, you dare the lie.[^24] Thy predecessor, writing to Honorius, said that «It was dictated, but was not presented, nor displayed»; but thou, in thy letter to John the pope, now among the saints, saidst that «It was presented and displayed», having been read by Constantine the Quaestor.[^25] Whom then do we believe? Thee, or him before thee? For it is not possible for both to be speaking the truth.

PYRRHUS. And is it so written by my predecessor?

MAXIMUS. So it is written.

PYRRHUS. Let these things be so about Vigilius. What hast thou to say about Honorius, who plainly taught as doctrine to my predecessor one will of our Lord Jesus Christ?

MAXIMUS. Who is the trustworthy interpreter of such a letter: he who composed it in the person of Honorius,[^26] who is still alive and who, with [329] the rest of his good deeds, brightens the whole West with the doctrines of piety too; or those in Constantinople who speak what is from their heart?[^27]

PYRRHUS. He who composed it.

MAXIMUS. The same, then, writing about it to Constantine of holy memory, who became emperor,[^28] again in the person of John the pope, now among the saints, said that «We said one will in the Lord, not of his Godhead and his manhood, but of the manhood alone. For when Sergius wrote that some say two contrary wills in Christ, we wrote in reply that Christ did not have two contrary wills, of flesh, I mean, and of spirit, as we have after the transgression; but one only, which naturally characterizes his manhood.» And a clear demonstration of this is his mentioning both members and flesh, which do not permit those things to be taken of his Godhead also. Then, making an anticipation of an objection, he says: «But if anyone should say: And for what reason, treating of the manhood of Christ, did you make no mention of his Godhead? we say that, first, the answer was made to the question; and then that, as in all things, in this too we followed the custom of Scripture, which sometimes discourses from his Godhead, as when the Apostle says: Christ the power of God, and the wisdom of God; and sometimes from his manhood only, as when the same says: The foolishness of God is wiser than men, and the weakness of God is stronger than men.»[^29]

PYRRHUS. My predecessor, attending to the expression, received this rather simply.

MAXIMUS. I speak the truth: nothing so set me against thy predecessor as his changeableness: that is, his falling at one time and another into different concepts, and his standing firm in no way of thinking. Sometimes, accepting those who name this one will divine, he brought in that the incarnate is God only; sometimes, those who call it deliberative, he brought in that he is a mere man, disposed deliberatively in our way [lying disposed],[^30] and differing in nothing from Pyrrhus and Maximus; sometimes, calling it hypostatic, he brought in, together with the difference of the hypostases, the difference of the wills too in the case of things consubstantial; sometimes, accepting also those who call it authoritative, he brought in a relational union, for authority, and mastery, and such things are plainly motions of inclination, but not of nature; sometimes, taking to himself those who call it elective and of inclination, and setting them up as masters of himself, he brought in the Lord not only as a mere man, but also as changeable and sinful, if indeed inclination is judging of opposites, and inquiring of things unknown, and deliberative of things uncertain; sometimes, those who call it of the economy, the …[^31]
