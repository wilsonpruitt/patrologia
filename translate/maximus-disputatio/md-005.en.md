And what need was there to post papers and to split the holy Church of God?[^2] Nor was he able to perceive what is most common to all. For either, to make you the concession, they contain the doctrines of the synods, as you, not telling the truth, have said, and we have no need of your papers, receiving and embracing these both before and now; or they do not contain those of the synods, and it is far more just to turn away from these and flee them. Since then the posting of these papers is, on either supposition, unjust and lawless, their dissolution too, on either ground, stands just and canonical.

PYRRHUS. Sophronius, who a little before became patriarch of Jerusalem,[^3] made us do this even against our intent, by moving the account about operations at no fitting time.

MAXIMUS. I am at a loss on every side what defence you expect to give, accusing the guiltless so bitterly. For tell me, by the truth itself: when Sergius wrote to Theodore of Pharan,[^4] sending also the libellus which he says is Menas's,[^5] through the mediation of Sergius Macaronas, bishop of Arsinoe,[^6] urging him to say what seemed right to him about the one operation and one will in the libellus, and he wrote back accepting them, where then was Sophronius? Or when at Theodosiopolis he wrote to Paul the One-eyed, who was also of the Severians,[^7] sending to him too the libellus of Menas and the assent of [333] the Pharanite and of himself? Or when he wrote to George surnamed Arsas, who was a Paulianist,[^8] that testimonies be sent to him about their one operation, putting this too into the letter: that in these he is also making the union of the Church with them? This letter the blessed John, the pope of Alexandria,[^9] took away by hand from Arsas; whence, wishing on account of it to make his deposition, he was prevented by the inroad of the Persians which took place in Egypt at that time. Or when he wrote back to Cyrus of Phasis[^10] about one operation and two, having been asked by him, sending to him too the said libellus of Menas? What then? Since Sergius had in many ways set his own disease before the public, and had ravaged the greater part of the Church, and the blessed Sophronius reminded him, with the humble-mindedness befitting his monastic estate,[^11] rolling himself at his footprints,[^12] and in place of every supplication offering him the life-giving sufferings of Christ God, so as not to renew the term of heretics, long ago well quenched by the holy Fathers who had gone through life before us, did he himself become the cause of such a scandal?

PYRRHUS. The account has duly made the overthrow of all that was put forward, and the inquiry about wills lacks nothing at all.

MAXIMUS. Since the inquiry about wills has reached its end, dost thou wish that we make the one about operations too?

PYRRHUS. Being ignorant of the account that the wills are natural, I accepted according to it the one about operations too; and if anything was said by me unwritten or in writing, it looks to this aim. But now, since being naturally constituted to will and being naturally constituted to operate are confessed by implication together, all that was not established about this formerly has been annulled;[^13] and I hold it superfluous to move any further account at all about this.

MAXIMUS. What then? Since God, on account of our intent foreknown to him, has called us to the knowledge of his truth,[^14] must not what was said about this to certain persons, in writing or unwritten, be tested, on account of those who, as is likely, have met with it unguardedly, or who meet with it, being rather easily led?

PYRRHUS. If the testing looks to this, it is necessary. For to take thought for the safety of the simpler is an imitation of divine love for man.

MAXIMUS. If then this is an imitation of divine love for man, let us begin from here the examination of this.

PYRRHUS. Let us begin.

MAXIMUS. In thy writings I found thee laying down as doctrine one operation of Christ, as of a whole. If then there is one operation, as of a whole, and the whole is his hypostasis, therefore this one operation will be hypostatic; and he will be shown to be of another operation, just as also of another hypostasis, than [336] the Father and the Mother; since Christ is neither of them.

PYRRHUS. If on account of the difference of the two natures in Christ you say two operations, and not one on account of the singleness of the person,[^15] two operations will be found of man too, on account of the difference according to essence of his soul and of his body. And if this, there will be three operations of Christ, and not two.

MAXIMUS. What you put forward for the destruction of the natural things, these those who fight against the natures put forward against the natures too. For this alone is your charm: to be carried round with them in everything. Whence we too bring to you, as to men sick with the same things as they, the refutations brought against them by the Fathers: that, if on account of the difference of the natures in Christ you yourselves too say two natures with us, and not one on account of the singleness of the person, two natures of man will thus be found too, on account of the difference according to essence of his soul and of his body; and if this, there will be three natures of Christ, and not two. But if, saying two natures with us on account of the difference of the natures, you do not say three natures in Christ, how will the three operations be gathered against us who say two operations on account of the difference of the natures? For what you say with us to those who put forward those things against the natures, this will suffice for us too, about the operations, against you. But in this way the account has struck you down from equal ground, showing the unreason of the difficulty. And from what goes beyond it we say that the one according to the species of man and the one according to the essence of soul and body are not the same. For the one according to the species of man displays the invariance in all the individuals under the nature; whence we do not say it without qualification either, but adding "of man". But the one according to the essence of soul and body ruins their very being, thrusting it into total non-existence. But if the one according to the species of man and the one according to the essence of soul and body are not the same, then there is no necessity for us, saying the one operation according to species, either to call this hypostatic or to say three operations, because the operation looks to the nature.

PYRRHUS. But with the operations the persons are brought in, according to Nestorius;[^16] and it is necessary that those who say two operations champion his foul doctrines.

MAXIMUS. And yet Nestorius, saying two persons, lays down as doctrine one operation. But if according to you persons are brought in with the operations, the operations too will consequently be gathered with the persons; and you will be forced, following your own canons, either, on account of the [337] one operation of the holy Godhead, to say also one person of it; or, on account of its three hypostases, three operations too; or to say, as Nestorius does, a relational union; for of this union the one operation is the union, as Nestorius himself and those of his fraternity showed in their writings. And in our case too, since the operation according to species is one, but the hypostases many: either, on account of the one operation according to species, to say also one person of all; or, on account of the many persons, many operations too; and in this way the account of the Fathers which says «Things that are of the same essence are also of the same operation»[^17] has fallen through.

Moreover, if according to them a person is brought in with the operation, and they have granted many operations to proceed from one and the same incarnate God Word, they will grant also the persons brought in with the operations that proceeded, if indeed they have decided to keep in step with themselves; and both the persons and the operations of the same one will be found infinite.

And again, if according to their premise a person is brought in with the operation, it is also wholly destroyed together with it when it is destroyed. And if this is true, then by the destruction of the two operations and of the one, both the two persons and the one person are destroyed together; and Christ, so far as lies with them, has passed from being and super-being into not-being; than which what could be more impious?

But if one should wish, out of great abundance, to exercise this in the case of each one of us as well, he will find that we too at one and the same time both reason and walk; or think some things and converse about others with those who happen to be present; which Moses too is recorded to have done,[^18] at the same time both entreating God on behalf of the people and conversing with the people and urging them on to good hopes; and neither by operating dually, congruently with his natures, is each one two; nor, by the one who operates dually being one, does he confuse the movements that belong essentially to his natures.

And in the case of the uttered word likewise,[^19] we see both the concept lying in the word, and the word underlying the concept, and their interpenetration into each other throughout; and neither with their difference is the difference of persons brought in, nor with the utmost union confusion. And what would one say about the red-hot sword too, which no less than the natures, that is, of the fire and of the iron, preserves also their natural operations, I mean the burning and [340] the cutting, and displays them through all at once and in the same? For neither is the burning set loose from the cutting itself after the union, nor the cutting from the burning; and neither on account of the doubleness of the natural operation does it bring in two red-hot swords, nor on account of the singleness of the red-hot sword does it make commixture or confusion of their essential difference.

PYRRHUS. Was not he who operated one?

MAXIMUS. Yes, one.

PYRRHUS. If then he who operates is one, the operation too is one, as of one.

MAXIMUS. This one Christ (for again, since you have turned back to the same things, I shall ask you the same things as above):[^20] is he one in hypostasis, or in nature?

PYRRHUS. In hypostasis; for in nature he is double.

MAXIMUS. Did the same one then operate dually, on account of the doubleness of the nature, or singly, on account of the singleness of the hypostasis? But if the same one, being one, operated dually, then the number of persons is not brought in with the number of operations. But if singly, on account of something singular of the person, the account about this will receive the same absurdities in the same cases. For if the operation is hypostatic, with the multitude of the hypostases the difference of the operations too will be contemplated together.

PYRRHUS. It is not wholly so that, since he operated dually, his operations were two; but since he who operated was one, it was one.

MAXIMUS. Another might say this to thee about the natures too: that not since his nature is twofold would the natures also already be and be called two; but since his hypostasis was one, his nature is and is called one. But, to pass over all that can be said about this: saying one operation, of what sort do you deem it right to call it? Divine, or human, or neither? But if divine, you call Christ God only; if human, not God at all, but man only, a mere man; if neither of these, you lay down as doctrine that Christ is neither God nor man, but without existence.

PYRRHUS. Saying one operation of the Godhead of Christ and of the manhood that is his, we say that it belongs to him not by principle of nature, but by mode of union.

MAXIMUS. If, according to your account, the operation accrued to him from the union, then before the union he was without operation; and, according to you, he suffered tyranny when he created. And again, if operating accrued to him from the union, and the Father and the Holy Spirit were not united according to hypostasis to flesh, then they are not operative. And if not operative, neither are they creators; not to say that they do not exist at all. And again, since the union is a relation and not [341] a thing, then the operation of Christ is a relation and not a thing.

Moreover, you will be forced to call it either created or uncreated; since between created and uncreated there is no middle operation at all. And if you say it is created, it will make known a created nature only; if uncreated, it will characterize an uncreated nature only; for the natural things must wholly be congruent with the natures. And how could it be possible, of a nature created and begun, that the operation be uncreated, both without beginning and infinite and creative and sustaining? Or how, of one uncreated and without beginning, that it be created and begun and limited and held together by another so as not to be dissolved?

PYRRHUS. Dost thou not accept and agree with those either who call the result of the works done by Christ one operation?

MAXIMUS. Of one action one result, of another another, and not one,[^21] as was shown in the case of the red-hot sword. For even if the operation of the fire and that of the sword are united with each other, yet we see the result of the fire to be the burning, and the result of the iron the cutting; even if they are not divided from each other in the burnt cutting, or in the cut burning; just as there is one result of heaven, another of earth, and another of the sun. It is not possible then to call the result one, unless the action too be one and only. If then, looking to the result of the works done by Christ, you have laid down one operation as doctrine, either lay down as doctrine one action too; or, on account of the infinite actions, infinite operations too. But what we are inquiring into is not about the works;[^22] for our account is not about the things outside Christ, but about those in Christ himself; that is, about the natural principle of the essences of Christ, whether it has remained deficient from the union, or undeficient; and if deficient, whether it is possible for there to be existence of a deficient nature; and if undeficient, whether in it, I mean in the constitutive principle of the essence, the operation is contemplated, or it is among the things outside. But that the operation according to nature is not among the things outside is clear from the fact that the nature can be without the works, but without the operation according to nature it is possible for the nature neither to be nor to be known. For by the things in which each thing operates naturally, it is credited as being what it is, unchanged.

Whereas[^23] you laid down as doctrine the one operation, fashioned by you after the manner of a goat-stag,[^24] looking not to the work, but to the natural principle of the united essences; and of this an unfailing proof is the chapters laid down as doctrine by Cyrus,[^25] and by you

## Notes
[^1]: T. The chunk opens mid-turn: Maximus is still speaking, continuing from md-004, and the subject of "he brought in" carries over from there. "He" in "he was without will" is Christ; the Latin's *eos sequens, qui oeconomicam … voluntatem dicerent* belongs to the preceding page.
[^2]: P. The "papers" (χάρται) are the Ekthesis (see note 9, md-001), posted in the narthex of Hagia Sophia.
[^3]: P. Sophronius, monk of Palestine, patriarch of Jerusalem 634–638/9, whose synodical letter first opposed the one operation.
[^4]: P. Sergius (see note 4, md-001); Theodore, bishop of Pharan in Sinai, an early monenergist writer.
[^5]: P. Menas (see note 24, md-004). The libellus to Pope Vigilius ascribed to him, alleging one operation and one will, was later condemned as a forgery at the Sixth Council.
[^6]: P. Sergius Macaronas, bishop of Arsinoe in Egypt, the go-between for Sergius's letter.
[^7]: P. Paul the One-eyed, a Severian (anti-Chalcedonian) leader in Armenia/Theodosiopolis (Erzurum) with whom Heraclius treated for union.
[^8]: P. George Arsas, an Egyptian Paulianist (anti-Chalcedonian sect of Paul of Beth Ukame) leader.
[^9]: P. John the Almsgiver, Chalcedonian patriarch of Alexandria 610–619/20; the Persian invasion of Egypt was in 618–619.
[^10]: P. Cyrus, bishop of Phasis in Lazica, later patriarch of Alexandria (631), author of the 633 Pact of Union.
[^11]: T. σχῆμα: the monastic habit (Latin *ordini suo ac professioni*); rendered "monastic estate" to keep "habit" free for ἕξις.
[^12]: T. Migne prints προκαλινδούμενος [προκυλινδι] (confirmed on the plate): the bracketed, truncated form is the edition's own correction to προκυλινδούμενος; either form means "rolling himself before".
[^13]: T. πάντα τὰ περὶ τούτου πρώην μὴ συσταθέντα: Migne prints the negative (confirmed on the plate), and it construes: "whatever was not established", i.e. whatever was unsoundly put forward. The English follows the print; the Latin (*omnia a me pridem in eam rem asserta atque probata*) has no negative.
[^14]: T. ἐκάλεσε has no expressed object; ἡμῶν ("our intent") suggests Maximus includes himself with Pyrrhus. "Us" supplied.
[^15]: T. τὸ μοναδικόν = singleness (glossary § II), the property of being one individual, as against τὸ διάφορον, the difference.
[^16]: P. Nestorius (see note 21, md-003).
[^17]: S. The patristic axiom, cf. Gregory of Nyssa, *Ad Ablabium*; Cyril of Alexandria, *Thesaurus* 32; quoted again at col. 348.
[^18]: S. Cf. Exod 14:13–15 (Moses speaks to the people while crying to God).
[^19]: T. ὁ κατὰ προφορὰν λόγος = the uttered word (glossary § VIII), the spoken word with its inward concept.
[^20]: T. ὑμᾶς is plural: Maximus addresses Pyrrhus with his party; hence "you".
[^21]: T. ἀποτέλεσμα = result, kept apart from ἐνέργημα (effect of operation) and from πρᾶξις = action. Latin *effectus*.
[^22]: T. Migne prints Πλὴν περὶ τῶν ἔργων ἡμῖν ἐστι τὸ ζητούμενον, with no οὐ (confirmed on the plate); the English follows Πλὴν οὐ περὶ, with the Latin *non est nobis quaestio de operibus*, because the printed affirmative contradicts its own γάρ clause: the works are among "the things outside" (the nature can be without the works, below), and Maximus's account is not about those.
[^23]: T. Migne prints Ὁπόταν οὐδὲ πρὸς ἔργον ἀντιδόντες (both confirmed on the plate). ἀντιδόντες ("having given in return") has no object and does not construe with πρὸς ἔργον; the English follows ἀπιδόντες, "looking to", as a few lines above (πρὸς τὸ ἀποτέλεσμα … ἀπιδόντες) and with the Latin *respicientes*. Ὁπόταν ("whereas, when") construes awkwardly with ἐδογματίσατε; the Latin opens *Quod vero*, as if from ὅτι δέ ("but that …"). The sense is unchanged: Maximus is about to prove from Cyrus's chapters that the one operation was meant of the natures.
[^24]: T. τραγέλαφος, the goat-stag, the philosophers' stock example of a fiction composed of two natures (Aristotle, *De interpretatione* 16a16).
[^25]: P. Cyrus's nine chapters, the Pact of Union with the Theodosians of Alexandria (633); the seventh confessed "one theandric operation".
