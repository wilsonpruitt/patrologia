62. A firm assurance[^2] of the hope of deification for the nature of men is the becoming man of God, which makes man god as much as he himself became man. For he who became man without sin will deify the nature, clearly without its conversion into Godhead, and will raise it up for his own sake as much as he himself, for man's sake, brought himself down.[^3] Teaching this mystically, the great Apostle says that *in the ages to come* the surpassing riches of God's goodness toward us will be shown.[^4]

63. Reason, presiding over anger and desire, makes the virtues; and the mind, applying itself to the principles, gathers the infallible knowledge of the things that have come to be. When, then, reason, after casting off what is opposed, finds what is by nature to be yearned for, and the mind, after passing through the things known, lays hold of the Cause of beings that is beyond essence and knowledge, then the passion of deification by grace supervenes: leading reason away from natural distinction[^5], where there is nothing that is distinguished, and bringing the mind to rest from intellection according to nature, where there is nothing that is known, and making him who is counted worthy of the divine participation god, by the identity that belongs to rest.

64. Toil cleanses the soul that has been rusted with the filth of pleasure, and unnails altogether its relation to material things, the soul having learned instead the loss that friendship toward them brings;[^6] for which cause God permits the devil, by just judgement, to press men down with torments.

65. Pleasure and grief, desire and fear, and what follows on these, were not antecedently created together with the nature of men; since then they would also have contributed to the limit of the nature. I say this having learned from [1205] the great Gregory of Nyssa,[^7] that because of the falling-away from perfection these were brought in, grown onto the more irrational part of the nature;[^8] through which, in place of the blessed and divine image, at once with the transgression, the likeness of the irrational animals became transparent and manifest in man. For it was necessary, the dignity of reason having been veiled, that the nature of men should be justly punished by the marks of irrationality which it had drawn upon itself; God wisely arranging by economy that man should come to an awareness of his rational greatness of mind.

66. The passions too become good in the earnest, whenever, wisely withdrawing them from bodily things, we handle them for the acquisition of heavenly things: for instance, when we make[^9] desire the appetitive motion of the noetic aspiration toward divine things; pleasure, the unharmed gladness of the mind's enchanting activity over the divine gifts of grace; fear, the precautionary care against the punishment to come for offences; and grief, the corrective remorse over a present evil. And, to put it briefly, like the wise among physicians who, with the body of the viper, a destructive beast, take away the injury present or threatened, we use these passions for the destruction of vice present or expected, and for the acquisition and guarding of virtue and knowledge.

67. The law of the first Covenant, through practical philosophy,[^10] cleanses the nature of all defilement. That of the New, through contemplative mystagogy, raises the mind in the way of knowledge from bodily things to the kindred spectacles of intelligible things.

68. Divine Scripture calls *fearing* those who are beginners and are found somewhere at the gateway of the divine court of the virtues; those who have acquired a measured habit of the principles and modes of virtue it is wont to name *progressing*; and those who in it have already come, by way of knowledge, to the summit of the truth that reveals the virtues, it addresses as *perfect*. He, then, who has turned away from his old way of life in the passions, and through fear has given over his whole disposition to the divine commands, falls short of none of the goods befitting beginners, even though he has not yet acquired the habit in the virtues and become a partaker of the wisdom spoken among the perfect.[^11][^12] Yet neither does he who is progressing fall short of any of the goods laid [1208] upon his rank,[^13] even though he has not yet acquired the same knowledge of divine things that surpasses in the perfect. For the perfect, having already been mystically counted worthy of contemplative theology, and having made the mind pure of all material imagination, appear to have taken to their breast the divine love, which bears whole and without lack the imitation of the image of the divine beauty.[^14]

69. Fear is twofold: the one pure, the other not pure. For the fear that arises over offences in expectation of punishment, having sin as the cause of its own coming-to-be, is not pure, nor will it last for ever, vanishing together with sin through repentance. But that which always subsists without grief over offences, this is pure fear,[^15] and it will never pass away; since it is in some manner essentially inborn in God toward creation,[^16] making manifest to all his natural venerableness, that of his pre-eminence above every kingship and power.

70. He who does not fear God as a judge, but reveres him for the surpassing pre-eminence of his infinite power, rightly has no lack, being perfect in love,[^17] loving God with reverence and the fitting veneration; and this is he who has acquired *the fear that endures to the age of the age*,[^18] and *there is no lack* to him at all.[^19]

71. From beings we come to know the cause of beings; and from the difference of beings we are taught the enhypostatic Wisdom of him who is; and from the natural motion of beings we learn the enhypostatic Life of him who is, the life-giving power of beings, the Holy Spirit.

72. The Holy Spirit is absent from none of beings, and above all from those that have in any way partaken of reason. For it holds together the knowledge of each, since, as God and Spirit of God, it passes through all things by power in the way of providence, and stirs up the reason in each according to nature, and through it brings him who perceives to an awareness of what has been done amiss against the ordinance of nature, and him who has his choice pliant to the reception of the right thoughts that come from nature. Hence we find many, even among utterly barbarous and nomadic men, laying claim to nobility of character, and setting aside the bestial laws that had prevailed among them from of old.

73. [1209] The Holy Spirit is in all simply, inasmuch as it holds all things together, provides for them, and stirs up the natural seeds. In a more determinate way it is also in all who are under the Law, inasmuch as it points out the transgression of the commandments and gives light concerning the promise foretold of Christ; and in all who are of Christ, in addition to what has been said, also as making sons by adoption. But as maker of wisdom it is simply in none of those mentioned, save those who understand and who, by their life in God, have made themselves worthy of its deifying indwelling. For everyone who does not do the divine wills, even if he is a believer, has a heart without intelligence,[^20] as a workshop of evil thoughts, and a body indebted to sins, as being held continually in the defilements of the passions.

74. God, who reaches out for the salvation of all men[^21] and hungers for their deification, curses and withers their conceit like the barren fig tree,[^22] so that, preferring being just to seeming to be just, they may strip off the tunic of moral display worn in hypocrisy, and, pursuing without adulteration the virtuous life as the divine word wills, may pass their life in piety, displaying to God the disposition of the soul rather than to men the outward fashioning of their manners.

75. The principle of doing is one thing, and the principle of suffering another; and the principle of doing is the natural power for the activity of the virtues; that of suffering is either the grace of what is above nature or the befalling of what is contrary to nature.[^23] For as we have no natural power for what is beyond being, so neither have we by nature the power for what is not. We suffer deification, then, as being above nature, by grace, but we do not do it; for we do not have by nature a power receptive of deification. And again we suffer vice, as contrary to nature, by inclination, by way of befalling; for we have no natural power for the coming-to-be of vice. While we are here, then, we do the virtues by nature, having the natural power for doing them; but in what is to come we suffer deification, receiving the power to suffer it by the grace of a gift.

76. We do, inasmuch as we have by nature, in activity, the rational power productive of the virtues, and the noetic power receptive, without relation, of all knowledge, [1212] which passes through the whole nature of beings and of things known and puts all the ages behind itself; but we suffer when, having perfectly passed beyond the principles of the things that are from non-being, we come unknowingly to the Cause of beings, and make our own powers cease together with the things that are by nature finite, becoming that which is in no way an achievement of the power according to nature; since nature does not possess a power capable of grasping what is above nature. For nothing originated is by nature productive of deification, since it is not capable of grasping God either. For to the divine grace alone does this properly belong, to bestow deification on beings proportionately, as it makes nature resplendent with the light above nature and sets it above its own limits by the excess of glory.

77. Doing the virtues, we cease after this life; but deification by grace, in return for them, we do not cease suffering. For the passion above nature is unbounded, because it is also efficacious; but that contrary to nature is without existence,[^24] because it is also inert.

78. Types of the divine goods are both the modes according to virtue and the principles of beings; in which God continually becomes man, having the modes of the virtues as body, and as soul the principles of knowledge in the spirit; by which he deifies the worthy, giving an enhypostatic character of virtue and bestowing an enousial existence of unerring knowledge.

79. The faithful and practical mind, like holy Peter, held by Herod,[^25] the law of skin — for Herod is interpreted «of skin», which is the way of thinking of the flesh[^26] — is shut up under two guards and one iron gate,[^27] being warred upon both by the activity of the passions and by the assent of the understanding to the passions; and having passed through these, as through guards, that is, prisons, by the reason of practical philosophy as by an angel, it comes to the iron gate that leads into the city: I mean the relation of the senses to sensible things, firm and harsh and hard to overcome; and the reason of natural contemplation in the spirit, opening this gate so that it opens of itself, henceforth sends the mind on without fear to the kindred intelligible things, free of Herod's madness.

80. [1213] The devil is both enemy of God and avenger: enemy, when, as hating him, he seems somehow to have a destructive love toward us men, through the modes of the voluntary passions persuading our choice, by pleasure, to prefer temporal things to the everlasting goods; by which, stealing the whole aspiration of the soul, he removes us altogether from the divine love, making us voluntary enemies of him who made us; avenger, when, laying bare his hatred toward us, he demands the punishment against us, as of those who have already become subject to him through sin. For nothing is so dear to the devil as a man being punished. And when this has been permitted him, devising successive inflictions of involuntary passions, he falls harshly, like a whirlwind, upon those against whom he has received the authority by God's permission: not wishing to fulfil the divine command, but desiring to nourish his own passion of hatred against us; so that, under the great weight of painful calamities, the soul, sinking down through faintness, may cut off from itself the power of the divine hope, making the infliction of things that befall painfully a cause of godlessness instead of admonition.

81. When those who have received the practical habit and contemplative science pursue these for the glory of men, sketching the manners of the virtues in an outward mode, and only talking words of wisdom and knowledge without the works of justice, and displaying to others their arrogance, as over virtue and knowledge, they are with reason handed over to fitting toils, learning instead, through suffering, the humble-mindedness which, from their vain conceit, they had not known.

82. Each of the demons, according to the aptitude that inwardly underlies it, serves for the infliction of this or that temptation; for one is productive of one vice and another of another, and one is clearly fouler than another, and more apt for this form of vice rather than that.

83. Without the divine permission not even the demons themselves can in any way serve the devil. For God himself knows how, with the fitting providence that loves man and is good, he permits the devil, through his servants, to inflict the different punishments for the things in which we have sinned; and the book about Job[^28] shows this clearly, recording that the devil could not approach Job at all without the divine purpose.[^29]
