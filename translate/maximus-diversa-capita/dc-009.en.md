And by «truth» he signified the end of all knowledges, and of the things themselves that are known; into which, as into the beginning and end of all beings, the natural motions are drawn together by a certain generic principle; since truth, as truth, the beginning and cause of beings, conquers all things according to nature, and draws to itself the motion of the things that have come to be.

32. By the privation of the many that is by way of eminence, truth is of a nature to appear as one and only, covering the knowing powers of all things that are able to understand or to be understood, as being, by a superessential existence, above both the things that understand and the things that are understood; and, by an infinite power circumscribing the extremities of beings at their beginning and their end, it draws together to itself every motion of all things: to some providing a manifest knowledge of the grace of which they were deprived; to others granting, by an ineffable sense, in participation, a manifest recognition of the goodness of which they had the aspiration.

33. The mind, he says, is the instrument of wisdom; reason, of knowledge; the natural full assurance according to both, of the faith constituted according to both; and of the gift of healings, natural love for man.[^1] For every divine gift has in us an apt and connatural receptive instrument, such as a power, or a habit, or a disposition. For example, he who has made his mind pure of every imagination of sensible things receives wisdom; he who has established reason as master of the innate passions, I mean anger and desire, receives knowledge; he who has the unshaken full assurance about divine things according to mind and reason receives the faith that can do all things; and he who has achieved natural love for man after the perfect destruction of self-love receives gifts of healings.

34. Each of us, according to the proportion of the faith in him, possesses the activity of the Spirit made manifest.[^2] So each is the steward of his own grace; and no one who thinks rightly would ever envy another who wins repute in the gifts of grace, since the disposition receptive of the divine goods lies with himself.

35. He says that the cause of the distribution of the divine goods is the measure of each man's faith.[^3] For as we believe, so too we have the increase of eagerness for doing. He then who does shows, in proportion to his action, the measure of his faith, receiving, as he believed, the measure of grace; and he who does not do shows, in proportion to his inaction, the measure of his unbelief, receiving, as he disbelieved, the privation of grace. So the envious man does ill to begrudge those who do rightly, since there lies plainly with himself, not with another, [1276] the selection both of believing and of doing, and of receiving the grace that comes according to the measure of faith.

36. The lover of noble things is urged on willingly, according to providence, by the principles of wisdom toward the grace of deification; but he who has no yearning for them is drawn away from vice unwillingly, according to the just judgement, by the modes of correction: the one, as a lover of God, being deified through providence; the other, through judgement, as a lover of matter, not being permitted to come into condemnation. For God, as good, tends with the principles of wisdom those who are willing, and with the modes of correction heals those who are slow to move toward virtue.

37. Faith that truly is, is truth both holding together and establishing, having no falsehood; and good conscience carries the power of love, as having no transgression of a commandment.

38. *And there shall rest upon him*, he says, seven spirits: *the spirit of wisdom, the spirit of intelligence, the spirit of knowledge, the spirit of science, the spirit of counsel, the spirit of strength, the spirit of the fear of God.*[^4] And the property of these spiritual gifts is: of fear, abstention from evils; of strength, the practice of good things; of counsel, the distinction of opposites; of science, the unadulterated knowing of what is fitting; of knowledge, the comprehension in act of the divine principles in the virtues; of intelligence,[^5] the co-disposition of the whole soul toward what has been known; of wisdom, the indiscernible union with God, by which for the worthy the aspiration becomes enjoyment, making the participant god by participation, and establishing him an interpreter of the divine blessedness, according to the ever-flowing, unostentatious putting-forth and issue of the divine mysteries toward those who need them.

39. The spirit of the fear of God is abstention from evils in act; the spirit of strength, the eager impulse and motion toward the activity and practice of the commandments; the spirit of counsel, the habit of distinction, by which we perform the divine commandments with reason, and divide the worse from the better; the spirit of science, the infallible knowing of the modes of practice according to virtue, by which, in acting, we in no way fall from the right judgement of reason; the spirit of knowledge, the comprehension of the commandments and of the principles in them, according to which the modes of the virtues are constituted; and the spirit of intelligence is the assent, or to speak more properly, the transformation, toward the modes and the principles of the virtues, by which there comes about a blending of the natural [1277] powers with the modes and the principles of the commandments; and the spirit of wisdom is the taking-up and union toward the cause of the more spiritual principles in the commandments, by which, being initiated unknowingly and simply, as far as is lawful for men, into the principles of beings that are in God, we offer to other men in varied ways, as from a welling spring of the heart, the truth that is in all things.

40. From the last things from God, but near to us, we ascend by a way and an order to the first things, far from us but near to God. For from idleness in evils through fear we come to the practice of the virtues through strength; and from the practice of the virtues to the distinction of counsel; and from distinction to the habit of the virtues, that is, science; and from the habit of the virtues to the knowledge of the principles in the virtues themselves; and from this to the habit that transforms toward the known principles of the virtues, I mean intelligence; and from this to the simple and exact contemplation of the truth in all things; setting out from which, we shall render, from the wise contemplation of the essences of beings, both sensible and intelligible, many and varied pious accounts concerning the truth.

41. The good that is first toward us in act, which is fear, the word of Scripture enumerated last, as the beginning of wisdom;[^6] moving from which, we ascend to the end of wisdom, intelligence, after which we become near to God himself, having wisdom alone mediating the union with him. For it is not possible to lay hold of wisdom for one who has not first, through fear and the remaining gifts in between, wholly shaken off from himself both the rheum of ignorance and the dust of vice. For this reason the scriptural order has set wisdom near to God, and fear near to us, that we may learn the definition and law of good order.

42. Ascending, then, through these eyes of faith, that is, illuminations, we are gathered together toward the divine monad of wisdom; gathering the division of the gifts that came about for our sake toward the cause of the gifts, by the particular ascents of the virtues, omitting, by the co-working of God, nothing of what has been said, lest, neglecting little by little, we render our faith blind and eyeless, not having the illuminations of the Spirit through works, and be justly punished for [1280] endless ages, as having blinded in ourselves, as far as lies in us, the divine eyes according to faith.

43. Everyone who has dug out in himself such eyes of faith through idleness in the commandments is wholly condemned, no longer having God looking upon him. For if, he says, the word calls the activities of the Spirit eyes of the Lord,[^7] he who does not open these by the practice of the commandments does not have God looking upon him. For God is not of a nature, as is likely, to oversee those on earth through other eyes, if indeed our illumination according to virtue is a ray of the divine vision.

44. Wisdom is a monad, contemplated without cutting in the different virtues that are from it; and conceived uniformly in their activities; and shown again to be a simple monad by the restorations toward it of the virtues that are from it; when we, for whose sake it makes its procession according to the coming-to-be of each virtue, are gathered upwardly through each virtue toward it.

45. He has a blind faith who does not work the divine precepts according to faith. For if the precepts of God are light,[^8] it is plain that he who does not do the divine precepts is without divine light, and carries about the divine appellation bare, but not true.

46. No one who sins can have the weakness of the flesh as advocate of his sin. For the union with God the Word strengthened the whole nature by the loosing of the curse, making the passionate attachment of the inclination to the passions without excuse for us. For the Godhead of the Word, always present by grace with those who believe in him, withers the law of sin in the flesh.[^9]

47. He who, through faith and love toward God, has conquered the irrational desires, or motions, of the passions contrary to nature, comes to be outside even the law according to nature itself, and is wholly transferred to the land of the intelligibles, and casts out together with himself, from the bondage alien to him, what is of one tribe with him according to nature, with the things that came to it.

48. Knowledge not bridled by divine fear in practice works conceit, persuading the one puffed up by it to put forward as his own what is borrowed, making the contribution of the word for his own praise. But practice, increasing together with divine longing, not taking the knowledge that is beyond what is to be done, makes the practical man humble-minded, drawn in upon himself by the words that are beyond his own power.[^10]

[1281] 49. A heavenly dwelling is the passionless habit according to virtue, and knowledge that has no concept of error warring against it.

50. As the end of the monad set in motion is the myriad, and the beginning of the myriad not set in motion is the monad — for the beginning of every end is plainly the motionlessness according to it, and the end of every beginning is the completion of the motion according to it — so also faith,[^11] being the beginning of virtues according to nature, has as its end the completion of the good through them; and the good according to nature, as the end of virtues, having faith as its beginning, is gathered inwardly toward it. For faith is an inward good, and the good is faith activated. And God is by nature faithful and good: the former as first good, the latter as last object of appetite. And these stand the same as each other in every mode, divided from one another by no principle at all save that according to conception, on account of the motion of the things that begin from him and end in him. So the myriad, bearing the type of the last object of appetite, circumscribes the perfect aspiration of the things moved toward it; and the monad, bearing the symbol of the first good, brings on the perfect base[^12] of the things moved from it.

51. The first passionlessness is the complete abstention from evils in act, contemplated in beginners; the second, the complete rejection, in the understanding, of thoughts concerning assent to evils, coming about in those who pursue virtue with reason; the third, the complete motionlessness of desire concerning the passions, in those who behold intelligibly, through the figures, the principles of the things seen; and the fourth passionlessness is the complete purification even of bare imagination itself, constituted in those who through knowledge and contemplation have made their ruling faculty a pure and transparent mirror of God. He then who has purified himself of the activity of the passions, and been freed from assent to them in the understanding, and taken rest from the motion of desire concerning them, and established his mind undefiled by their bare imagination,[^13] having the four generic passionlessnesses, goes out from matter and material things, and hastens toward the noetic and divine and peaceful lot of the intelligibles.
