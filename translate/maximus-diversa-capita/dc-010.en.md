53. For every practical man, reason and understanding labour after the manner of a male servant and a maidservant, devising and making the modes of practice according to virtue, and having, as it were, all their own power arrayed against the spirits of wickedness opposed to practice; but having fulfilled practical philosophy,[^1] which the sixth number of the years indicated — for it has been said that the number six signifies practical philosophy — they are released free toward the spiritual, plainly, returning to the contemplation of the kindred principles in beings, both reason and understanding.[^2]

54. The foreign male servant and maidservant are anger and desire, whom the contemplative mind, through courage and temperance, yokes continually under the mastery of reason for the service of the virtues, not giving them at all the release to freedom until the law of nature is wholly swallowed up by the law of the spirit, as the death of wretched flesh by infinite life,[^3] and the whole image of the kingdom without beginning is shown purely, having through imitation the whole form of the archetype; having come to be according to which, the contemplative mind makes both anger and desire free: converting the one to the pure pleasure of divine yearning and the undefiled allure, and transferring the other to spiritual fervour and fiery ever-moving and sober madness.[^4]

55. The image of the kingdom without beginning is the mind's unchangeableness concerning true knowledge, and the sense's incorruption concerning virtue; the soul and the body being bound to one another by the divine law of the spirit alone, according to the transformation in the spirit of sense toward the mind, by which they have the activity of reason ever-moved and living and penetrating throughout; in which every unlikeness at all toward the Divine is absent.[^5]

56. They say that pleasure is desire activated, if indeed by its definition it is a present good; and that desire is pleasure not activated, if indeed by its definition it is a future good; and anger a rehearsed motion of madness, and madness anger activated. He then who has subjected these powers to reason will find desire [1285] become pleasure for him, according to the undefiled intertwining in grace of the soul with the Divine; and anger, pure fervour, guarding the pleasure concerning the Divine, and sober madness, carrying the soul's alluring power according to aspiration wholly out of beings. So, as long as the world lives in us, and the soul's voluntary relation to material things, one must not give freedom to these powers, lest, mingled with sensible things as with those of one tribe, they make war on the soul and take it, become a captive of war to the passions, as once the Babylonians took Jerusalem.[^6] For by the age in which the law commanded the foreign servants to serve,[^7] the word signified the soul's relation of inclination toward this world, that is, the present life, showing through the things narrated the things understood.

57. He calls evil begun; for it has as its beginning our motion contrary to nature; and the good not begun; for before every age and time the good is by nature. He calls the good intelligible, which alone one must understand; and evil not intelligible, which alone one must not understand; and he calls the good speakable, for it alone must be spoken. And he calls the good coming-to-be: for being by nature unbegotten, by grace, through love for man, it consents to come to be by us, for the deification[^8] of us who do and speak it, which alone of all must come to be; and evil not made, which alone must not come to be. He calls evil corruptible; for corruption is the nature of evil, having in no way, in anything, existence; and the good incorruptible, as always being, and never ceasing from being, and guarding all things in which it comes to be. This then we seek with the rational power; long for with the desiring power; guard inviolate with the incensive power; with the sensory power, according to science, we distinguish it unmixed from its contraries; with the vocal power, speaking it, we make it manifest to those who do not know; and with the generative power we multiply it; or rather, to speak truly, we are multiplied according to it.

58. The contemplative mind, reigning over the concepts and spectacles in beings and over its own movements, must have a habit barren of vice; that is, a vice that neither conceives at all nor gives birth; upon which it must be borne as it is moved concerning contemplation, lest, while making a spiritual examination of beings, it unawares fall into one of the wicked spirits that are of a nature to corrupt, through some one of sensible things, the pure [1288] examination of the heart.[^9]

59. He who, wounded by some vainglory on account of virtue or knowledge, vainly nourishes the hair of conceit, as Absalom did,[^10] displays his moral way of life artfully contrived and mixed, like a mule, for the deceit of those who behold it; borne aloft upon which, he thinks to overpower the father who begot him through the teaching of the word, wishing, as an arrogant man, to draw tyrannically to himself all the glory of virtue and knowledge that belongs to the father from God. But such a one, having gone out into the breadth of natural contemplation in the spirit, to the rational war on behalf of truth, because of his living sense is held fast by the hair in the thicket of the oak of material spectacles, having that very empty conceit binding him to death, which hangs him between heaven and earth. For the vainglorious man has neither knowledge, like heaven, drawing him up from the conceit that drags him down; nor again earth, I mean the base of practice in humility, drawing him down from the inflation that pulls him up; whom, even dead, the teacher who begot him mourns through love for man, as a lover of God, in imitation of God, not wishing *the death of the sinner, so much as that he turn and live*.[^11]

60. Wisdom is the beginning and end of each man's salvation: as it begins, first making fear, and as it is perfected, later establishing longing; or rather, itself becoming fear at the beginning for our sake by way of economy, that it may stop the lover of vice; and later being found longing, naturally, for its own sake, at the end, that it may fill with intelligible laughter those who have taken its companionship in exchange for all beings.

61. Wisdom is both fear, being a privation that comes about by way of flight for those who have no appetite for it; and it is longing, being found a habit of enjoying activity for those who love it. For it, delivering from passions by the expectation of punishment, both makes fear; and works longing, accustoming the mind by the acquisition of the virtues to look at the things to come.

62. Every confession humbles the soul: the one, teaching it that it has been justified by the grace of God; the other, that it is held liable to charges by the sloth of its own inclination.

63. Twofold is the account of confession: one coming about in thanksgiving for the goods given; the other spoken in reproof and examination of things done badly. For confession is said both of the enumeration, with thanksgiving, of the divine benefactions by those who have been well treated, and of the declaration of things done badly by those who are culpable; and both are productive of humility. For both he who gives thanks for goods [1289] at once, and he who is examined on charges, is humbled: the one judging himself unworthy of the good things given; the other begging to receive remission of his offences.

64. The passion of pride is constituted from two ignorances.[^12] And two ignorances, coming together into union, produce one confused prudence. For he alone is proud who is ignorant both of the divine help and of human weakness. So pride is privation of divine and of human knowledge. For by the negation of the true extremes, there is one, and false, affirmation.

65. Vainglory is a departure from the goal according to God, and a passing over to another goal besides the divine. For he is vainglorious who practises virtue for the sake of his own, but not of God's, glory, and with his own labours purchases the insubstantial praises of men.[^13]

66. The man-pleaser cares only for the outward manners, and indeed for flattering speech, that by the former he may win over to himself the sight, and by the latter the hearing, of those who are pleased by, or even struck with wonder at, only what is seen and heard, and who circumscribe virtue by sense alone.[^14] So we call man-pleasing the display of manners and[^15] words made, as if for virtue, for men's sake.

67. Hypocrisy is a pretence of friendship; or hatred covered with the form of friendship; or enmity activated through goodwill; or envy imitating the character of love; or a life having its comeliness by the fiction, but not the reality, of virtue; or a pretence of justice, preserved by the seeming to be; or deceit having the form of truth: which those practise who imitate the serpent by the crookedness of their manners.[^16]

68. God is the cause of beings, and of the goods in beings. He then who is exalted concerning virtue or knowledge, and does not extend, together with the measure of virtue, or of progress, in grace, the fair judgement of his own weakness, has not escaped the evil of pride. And he who practises the noble for his own glory has preferred himself to God, transfixed by the nail of vainglory. And he who does or speaks virtue *to be seen by men*[^17] has set human acceptance far above the divine, sick with the passion of man-pleasing; and he who has maliciously coloured his manners for deceit with the solemnity of virtue alone, and covers the wicked disposition of his inclination with the apparent form of reverence, buys virtue with the guile of hypocrisy. So such a one has led away his goal toward other things, besides the cause, in each of these.[^18]

69. None of the wicked demons [1292] ever hinders the eagerness of the virtuous man; but rather, having craftily cut off the deficiencies of the virtues, it suggests the intensifications, joining in the eagerness of the contestants; that it may make wholly its own the notion of the one practising asceticism, which, having lost the equal balance of the mean, travels unawares toward another lodging than the one it seems.

70. The demons neither hate temperance nor loathe fasting; not distribution of money, not hospitality, not psalmody, not leisure for reading, not stillness, not the loftiest of learnings, not sleeping on the ground, not vigil, not all the rest through which the life according to God is characterized, so long as the goal and the cause of what is done incline toward them.

71. The other demons the ascetic, perhaps detecting them quickly, easily escapes, and the harm from them; but those that seem to work together with the course of virtue, and as it were to wish to build together with him the temple to the Lord,[^19] what mind even of the very lofty could detect, without the word *that passes through* all things, *active and living, and penetrating to the division of soul and spirit*, that is, discerning which of works or concepts are psychic, that is, natural forms or movements of virtue, and which happen to be spiritual, that is, being above nature and characterizing God, but given to nature by grace; knowing, *of joints and marrow*, the fitting or unfitting convergence, wholly, of the modes according to virtue toward the spiritual principles; and *judging the ponderings and the notions of hearts*,[^20] that is, the hidden relations in the depth to the things said, and the invisible causes of these in the soul; to whom *no creature is hidden*, in us who seem to escape notice; *but all things are naked and laid bare*,[^21] not only what has come to be and been conceived, but already also what will come to be from us and be conceived.

72. He calls *division of soul and spirit* the difference between the virtues from nature, of which we have the principles naturally, and those from the spirit, of which we receive the grace as a free gift. For the word, judging, makes the demarcation of these distinctly.

73. The ponderings and the notions, of which the word is judge, he says are the soul's relations to the divine principles and to the thoughts, and, plainly, the causes of the relations themselves. For pondering moves memory, to which relation is proper; and notion looks toward an end, which characterizes a cause.

74. If God is knowledge according to essence, and mind plainly precedes all knowledge, under which it is of a nature to fall, then God is above it, because [1293] he stands super-infinitely above every mind too, under which knowledge in any way is of a nature to fall.

75. Who, not having the divine word dwelling in the depth of the heart, will be able, having slipped aside from the hidden wiles of the hypocrisy of the demons against us, to stand by himself quite alone, without any mixing, and to build the temple of the Lord, like the great Zorobabel and Jesus and the leaders of the families,[^22] saying expressly with a loud voice to the deceiving spirits of pride and vainglory and man-pleasing and hypocrisy: *It is not for us and you to build the house to the Lord our God; for we alone will build to the Lord of Israel*;[^23] knowing that the mixing of these works the corruption and pulling-down of the whole building, and robs the grace of the comeliness of the divine offerings?

76. No one who has any of the demons mentioned accompanying his virtue can build it to the Lord; for he does not have God as the end of what is done, beholding whom he goes through it, but the passion that he establishes through it.

77.[^24] Those who war by deficiency of virtue are the demons that teach fornication and drunkenness, avarice and envy; and those who war by excess are those that teach conceit and vainglory and pride, and secretly, by means of the things on the right, impale upon the things on the left.[^25]

78. Would that we too might always say to the spirits of wickedness that attack invisibly in the fiction of spiritual friendship, and wish through the good to work out secretly the death of sin, and say: *We will build with you the temple to your Lord*: *It is not for us and you to build the house to the Lord our God; for we alone will build to the Lord of Israel.*[^26] Alone, because, having been freed from the spirits that war by deficiency of virtue, which we have also escaped, we do not wish, lifted up through you by the excesses, to be transfixed again and fall a fall as much harder than the former as for the former there was an easy hope of return, for those pardoned on account of weakness; but for the latter there either is none, or it will be difficult, for those hated on account of pride, and making for themselves something more right than the right. But again not alone, because we have the holy angels as helpers in good things; or rather God himself, manifesting himself to us through the works of justice, and building us a holy temple for himself, and free of every passion.[^27]
