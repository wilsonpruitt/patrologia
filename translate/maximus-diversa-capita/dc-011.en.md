But he who self-willedly puts forward his own weakness as strength against the divine power has passed beyond the limit of virtue; and for this reason, doing wrong, he does not recognize that with him what is superior is coming to stand behind; for he thinks the wrong to be virtue. 80. Since it has been said, *The supplication of a righteous man, made active, avails much*,[^1] I know this to be made active in two modes: in one, when he who prays makes the offering of his supplication to God together with the works according to the commandment, so that the supplication does not lie idle and without hypostasis, falling from the tongue in bare word and an empty echo of the voice, but active and living, ensouled by the works of the commandments.[^2] For the hypostasis of prayer and supplication is manifestly the fulfilment of the commandments through the virtues, by which the righteous man has his supplication strong and able for all things, made active by the commandments. In the other mode, when he who needs the prayer of the righteous man does the works of the prayer, both correcting his former life and making the supplication of the righteous man strong, empowered through his own good conduct.

81. There is no benefit from the supplication of the righteous man when he who needs it takes more pleasure in offences than in virtues. For once the great Samuel too mourned for Saul in his offending; but he could not propitiate God, not having received as the helper of his mourning the fitting correction of the offender. Wherefore God, making his servant cease from the unprofitable mourning, says to him, *How long dost thou mourn for Saul, and I have set him at naught from reigning over Israel?*[^3][^4]

82. And again Jeremiah, most compassionate, praying for the people of the Jews, who had gone mad about the error [1297] of the demons, is not heard, not having, for the power of his prayer, the turning of the godless Jews from their error. Whence God, leading him too away from praying in vain, says, *And do not thou pray for this people, and do not ask that they be shown mercy, and do not make prayer, and do not come to me concerning them any more, for I will not hear.*[^5]

83. It is truly of much foolishness, not to say derangement, for one who in disposition takes pleasure in ruinous things to seek salvation through the prayer of the righteous, and to ask forgiveness for those things in which he glories, stained in act by his intent; whereas he who needs the supplication of the righteous man ought, if indeed he truly hates wicked things, not to let it become idle and unmoved, but to make it active and strong, winged by his own virtues, and reaching him who is able to give forgiveness of the offences.

84. The supplication of the righteous man avails much, made active either by the righteous man who makes it or by him who asks the righteous man that it be made. For made active by the righteous man, it gives him boldness toward him who is able to grant the requests of the righteous; and by him who asks the righteous man for it, it draws him away from his former wickedness, converting his disposition to virtue.

85. Since the Apostle has said, *Wherein you rejoice for a little while now, if need be, that you be grieved in manifold temptations*,[^6] how can one who is grieved in temptations rejoice in that in which he is grieved?[^7]

86. The word of truth knows grief to be twofold: the one formed invisibly according to the soul, the other visibly according to sense; and the one embracing the whole depth of the soul, tormented by the scourge of conscience, the other circumscribing the whole of sense, contracted by the weight of painful things from its natural diffusion; and the one being the end of pleasure according to sense, the other of gladness according to the soul; or rather, the one the conclusion of the passions of inclination according to sense, the other of the passions according to it that are contrary to inclination.

87. Grief is, to speak for myself, a disposition deprived of pleasures; and deprivation of pleasures is an infliction of toils; and toil is plainly a deficiency or retreat of a natural habit; and the deficiency of a natural habit is a passion of the power that underlies the habit according to nature. And the passion of the power that underlies the habit according to nature is the mode according to the misuse of the natural activity; and the misuse of the mode according to activity is the motion of the power towards what is not according to nature and not subsisting.[^8]

[1300] 88. He says that grief according to the soul is the end of pleasure according to sense;[^9] for out of this the grief of the soul is formed; just as, then, the end of pleasure according to the soul is grief according to the flesh; for the gladness of the soul becomes punishment [?] grief of the flesh.[^10]

89. He speaks of grief as twofold: the one about sense, formed by the deprivation of bodily pleasures; the other about the mind, coming to be by the deprivation of the goods of the soul. And he speaks of temptations too as twofold, the voluntary and the involuntary; and the voluntary, he says, are the fathers of bodily pleasure according to sense, and the begetters of grief according to the soul[^11] (for sin alone, once committed, grieves the soul);[^12] and the involuntary, which show themselves in the toils contrary to inclination, are the fathers of pleasure according to the soul and the begetters of bodily grief according to sense.

90. Just as the word knows grief to be twofold, as I said, so it understands the mode of temptations also to be twofold: the one according to inclination, the other contrary to inclination; and the one the creator of voluntary pleasures, the other the bringer of involuntary pains. For the temptation according to inclination plainly forms the voluntary pleasures according to choice; but that contrary to inclination manifestly brings on the involuntary toils contrary to choice; and the one is the cause of grief according to the soul, the other of grief according to sense.

91. The temptation according to inclination forms grief according to the soul, and plainly creates pleasure according to sense; but that contrary to inclination makes to subsist the pleasure of the soul and the grief of the flesh.

92. I think that our Lord and God, teaching his own disciples how they must pray, says, with a view to praying away the form of temptations according to inclination,[^13] *And lead us not into temptation*;[^14] that is, to pray not to be abandoned to take trial of the pleasurable temptations, those of inclination and voluntary; and that the great James, called the brother of the Lord,[^15] teaching those who contend for the truth not to shrink, with a view to the form of the involuntary temptations, says, *Count it all joy, my brothers, when you fall into manifold temptations*,[^16] that is, the involuntary temptations, contrary to inclination and productive of toils. And they show it plainly: there the Lord, adding, *But deliver us from the evil*;[^17] here the great James, *Knowing that the testing of your faith works endurance; and let endurance have its perfect work, that you may be perfect and entire, lacking in nothing.*[^18]

[1301] 93. The Lord teaches us to pray away the voluntary temptations, as productive of pleasure of the flesh and of pain of the soul; but the great James exhorts us to rejoice at the involuntary temptations, as taking away pleasure of the flesh and pain of the soul.

94. He is perfect who fights the voluntary temptations through self-control, and perseveres through endurance in the involuntary temptations; and he is entire who carries through both practice with knowledge and contemplation not without practice.[^19]

95. Since both grief and pleasure are divided into soul and sense, he who acquires the pleasure of the soul and accepts with endurance the grief of sense becomes approved and perfect and entire: approved, through the trial of the contraries according to sense; perfect, as fighting unyieldingly through self-control and endurance against pleasure and grief according to sense; entire, as keeping unharmed, in the steadfastness of identity according to reason, the habits that fight against the dispositions according to sense opposed to one another: I mean practice and contemplation, held together with one another and neither disjoined from the other, but practice showing forth through its manners the knowledge of contemplation, and contemplation armed, no less than with reason, with the virtue of practice.

96. He who has taken trial of grief and pleasure of the flesh may be called approved, as having made trial of the ease and the difficulty of the affairs of the flesh. Perfect is he who has wrestled down the pleasure and the pain of the flesh by the power of reason; and entire, he who has kept the habits according to practice and contemplation unchangeable by the intensity of the divine longing.

97. Of grief according to the soul the mode is twofold: the one formed at one's own offences, the other at those of others. And the cause of such grief is plainly the pleasure according to sense of him who grieves, or of those on whose account he grieves. For by the exact account there is hardly any sin at all among men that does not have, as the beginning of its own coming-to-be, the soul's unreasoning relation to sense for the sake of pleasure. And the cause of pleasure according to the soul is manifestly the grief according to sense of him who takes pleasure and rejoices at his own or at others' virtues. For by the exact account there is hardly any virtue among men that does not have, as the beginning of its own coming-to-be, the soul's reasoned detachment from sense.[^20]

98. Without the soul's passionate relation to sense there is no sin at all among men; and of all grief formed in the soul, pleasure of the flesh goes before.

99. The true coming-to-be of virtue is the soul's voluntary alienation from the flesh;[^21] and he gladdens [1304] the soul spiritually who tames the flesh with voluntary toils.

100. When the soul has acquired, for the sake of virtue, detachment from sense, of necessity sense will be in toils, not having the soul's power that is inventive of pleasant things joined to it according to the relation of inclination; but on the contrary having it manfully slapping back through self-control the uprising of its own natural pleasures; and, towards the infliction of the toils contrary to nature and involuntary, remaining through endurance altogether implacable; and not departing from the worth and glory befitting God according to virtue for the sake of pleasure without hypostasis; and, towards the taking-up of toils, not falling away from the height of the virtues out of sparing the flesh because of the sense in pain. The cause of grief according to sense is the soul's complete occupation with the things according to nature. And pleasure according to sense is manifestly made to subsist by the activity of the soul contrary to nature, which cannot have any other beginning of its constitution than the soul's putting-off of the things according to nature.

FOURTH CENTURY

1. He knows as inventive the noetic power of the soul;[^22] which, when it is separated from the relation according to sense, leaves the flesh bereft of provision for pleasure,[^23] according to the relation in the inclination, not even bearing to console the pain of the flesh, because of its whole leisure, in the relation of the inclination, for divine things.

2. Mind and sense have their natural activity opposed to each other, because of the extreme difference and otherness of the things that underlie them. For the one has as its subjects the intelligible and incorporeal essences, which it is its nature to apprehend incorporeally;[^24] the other the sensible and bodily natures, which it too apprehends naturally.

3. The soul's putting-off of the things according to nature is wont to become the beginning of pleasure according to sense. For when the soul toils about the goods according to nature, there is not the power that discovers the mode of pleasure according to sense.

4. Where reason leads sense in the contemplation of visible things, the flesh is deprived of all pleasure according to nature, not having sense let loose and freed from the bonds of reason for the service of its own pleasures. Whence also, of necessity, when the reason in us prevails, the flesh enslaved to it for virtue is tormented.

[1305] 5. As soon as the mind holds sense to be its own power according to nature, entangled with the surfaces of sensible things, it devises the fleshly pleasures, not being able to pass beyond the nature of visible things, held fast by its passionate relation to sense.

6. If it is not possible for the mind to pass over to the kindred intelligibles without the contemplation of the sensibles set forth in between, and it is altogether impossible for this to come about without sense, which is joined to the mind and kindred by nature to the sensibles, fittingly, if on encountering them[^25] it is held fast by the surfaces of visible things, thinking the sense joined to it to be its natural activity, it has fallen from the intelligibles according to nature, and has seized with both hands, as the saying goes, the bodies contrary to nature; by which being activated contrary to reason, because of the sense that has conquered it, it is established as begetter of grief according to the soul, tormented by frequent scourges of conscience, and becomes the manifest maker of pleasure according to sense, fattened by the conceptions of the modes that procure for the flesh. But if, at the very encounter with the things seen, cutting through the surface that faces sense, it beholds the spiritual principles of beings pure of the shapes upon them, it has wrought the pleasure of the soul, which is held fast by none of the sensibles contemplated, and has formed the grief of sense, deprived by nature of all sensibles.

7. Since pleasure according to sense forms the grief of the soul, that is, toil (for both are the same as each other), and pleasure according to the soul is wont to make the grief of sense, that is, toil, fittingly he who aspires to the life in hope of our God and Saviour Jesus Christ, *through the resurrection of the dead unto an inheritance incorruptible and undefiled and unfading, reserved in the heavens*,[^26] has in the soul exultation, joy unspeakable,[^27] continually rejoicing in the hope of the goods to come; but in the flesh and sense, grief, that is, the toils that come upon it from the manifold temptations,[^28] and the pains at them. For pleasure and toil accompany every virtue: toil of the flesh, deprived of the gentle and smoother sense; pleasure of the soul, delighting in the principles in spirit, pure of everything sensible.

8. The mind must, in the present life (for this is how I understand «now»), grieved according to the flesh because of the many toils of the temptations brought upon it for virtue, always rejoice according to the soul and take pleasure, because of the hope of the everlasting goods, even if it has sense worn down. *For the sufferings of the present time are not worthy against the glory that is to be revealed unto us*, says the divine Apostle.[^29]
