35. On account of the pleasure that came upon the nature contrary to reason, the pain that is according to reason came in after it, through many sufferings, in which and from which is death, working the removal of the pleasure contrary to nature; but not indeed its complete destruction as well, which is what the grace of the divine pleasure according to mind is naturally apt to display.[^1]

36. The devising of voluntary labours, and the bringing-on of involuntary ones, remove pleasure, putting to rest its motion in activity; but they do not destroy the power for its coming-to-be that is laid in the nature like a law. For philosophy according to virtue is naturally apt to work passionlessness of inclination, but not of nature; according to which, namely the [passionlessness] of inclination, [?] grace.[^2]

37. Every labour, as having as the cause of its own coming-to-be the pleasure that precedes it in activity, is clearly a debt, paid naturally, by way of cause, by all who have partaken of the nature. For upon pleasure contrary to nature labour by all means follows naturally in all those whose coming-to-be the law of pleasure has, without cause, gone before. And I call causeless the pleasure from the transgression, as having come into being, clearly, not as the successor of a preceding labour.

38. It was altogether impossible that the nature, having bowed under both the pleasure that is by choice and the pain that is against choice, should be recalled again to the life that was from the beginning, unless the Creator became man, receiving by choice the pain devised for the punishment of the nature's pleasure by choice, a pain not having preceding it the coming-to-be from pleasure; that he might free the nature from the birth that is from condemnation, accepting a birth that has no beginning from pleasure.

39. Since after the transgression all men had pleasure naturally preceding their own coming-to-be, and no one at all was naturally free of the passionate coming-to-be by pleasure, but all, as paying a natural debt, endured the labours and the death [1321] upon them; and the mode of freedom was altogether impossible for those tyrannized by unjust pleasure, and naturally held fast by the just labours and by the most just death upon them: there was need, for the destruction of the most unjust pleasure and of the most just labours on its account, by which man was pitiably torn apart in suffering, having the beginning of his coming-to-be from the corruption according to pleasure, and ending the end of his life in the corruption through death; and for the restoration of the suffering nature,[^3] that a labour and death be devised, unjust and at the same time causeless: causeless, as having in no way a preceding pleasure toward its coming-to-be; unjust, as being the successor of no passionate life at all; that, taken in the middle between unjust pleasure and most just labour and death, a most unjust labour and death might destroy entirely the most unjust beginning from pleasure, and the most just end of the nature through death on its account, and the race of men might become again free of pleasure and pain, the nature recovering its good estate from the beginning, defiled by none of the marks innate in the things that are under coming-to-be and corruption. For this reason the Word of God, being perfect God according to nature, becomes perfect man, constituted of a noetic soul and a body passible according to nature, like us, without sin alone; having in no way at all the pleasure from the disobedience preceding his birth in time from a woman; but having by volition, through love for man, accepted the pain on its account, which is the end of the nature: that, suffering unjustly, he might destroy the beginning of coming-to-be from unjust pleasure that tyrannizes the nature, a beginning not having the death of the Lord as a debt paid on its behalf, as with the other men, but rather cast forth against it; and that he might wipe out the just end of the nature through death, not having as the cause of his being the lawless pleasure on account of which [death] came in and which is justly punished by it.

40. It was needful, truly it was needful, that the Lord, being wise and just and powerful according to nature, as wise should not be ignorant of the mode of the healing; as just, should not make a tyrannical salvation of man, held fast by sin by inclination; and as able to do all things, should not fall short of the fulfilment of the healing.

41. The wisdom of God is shown in his becoming by nature truly man; his justice, in his having assumed, like us, the passible of the nature in coming-to-be; his power, in his having made, [1324] through sufferings and death, eternal life for the nature and unchangeable passionlessness.

42. The principle of wisdom the Lord made manifest in the mode of the healing, becoming man without change and any alteration whatever; and the equality of justice he showed in the greatness of the condescension, submitting by volition to the condemnation of the nature in the passible, and making that very thing a weapon for the destruction of sin and of the death on its account, that is, of pleasure and of the pain on its account, in which lay the power of sin and of death, both the tyranny of sin according to pleasure and the dominion of death on its account according to pain. For in the passible of the nature manifestly lies the power of pleasure and that of pain. For somehow, as the penalty of pain grows more intense according to nature, we strive to console it through pleasure; for wishing to flee the toilsome sense according to pain, we take refuge in pleasure, attempting to console the nature pressed by the torment of pain. But hastening through pleasure to blunt the movements of pain, we ratify the more its handwriting against ourselves,[^4] being unable to have pleasure released from pain and labours.

43. And the strength of his surpassing power he made plain by establishing for the nature an unchangeable coming-to-be from the contraries of what he himself suffered; for through passion giving to the nature passionlessness, and through labours relief, and through death eternal life, he restored it again; renewing by his own deprivations the habits of the nature, and through his own incarnation bestowing on the nature the grace above nature, deification.

44. God became man in truth, and gave the nature another beginning of a second coming-to-be, ending through labour in the pleasure of the life to come. For as Adam the forefather, having transgressed the divine commandment, brought in besides to the nature, beside the first, another beginning of coming-to-be, constituted from pleasure but ending in the death through labour, and devised, according to the counsel of the serpent, a pleasure that was not the successor of a preceding labour but rather passed over into labour, and so had all those born of him in the flesh after his likeness justly brought along with him, on account of the unjust beginning from pleasure, toward the end in death through labour: so too the Lord, becoming man, and creating for the nature another beginning of a second coming-to-be from the Holy Spirit, and accepting the most just death of Adam through labour, a death become in him, clearly, most unjust, as not having as the beginning of his own coming-to-be the most unjust pleasure of the forefather from disobedience, made the destruction of both the extremes, I mean the beginning and the end of human coming-to-be according to Adam, as [1325] not being from God primarily; and he set free from the liability for it all those mystically reborn from him.

45. The Lord, having taken away the pleasure from the law of sin, for the annulment of the birth according to the flesh of those born in him by grace through the Spirit, grants them to receive death, which formerly was for the condemnation of the nature, for the condemnation of sin; they no longer having, of Adam, the pleasure of coming-to-be from Adam,[^5] but only the pain on account of Adam operating in them, not by way of debt for sin but by economy, on account of the natural distress, death against sin. And this [death], whenever it does not have as the mother begetting it the pleasure of which it is naturally the avenger, manifestly becomes the father of eternal life. For as the life of Adam according to pleasure became the mother of death and corruption, so too the death of the Lord on account of Adam, being free of the pleasure from Adam, becomes the begetter of eternal life.

46. After the transgression the nature of men had as the beginning of its own coming-to-be the conception from seed according to pleasure, and the birth according to flux; and as its end, the death through corruption according to pain. But the Lord, not having this beginning of birth according to the flesh, was not liable to capture by the end either, that is, by death.

47. Sin, having at the beginning enticed Adam, persuaded him to come to the transgression of the divine commandment; according to which, having given subsistence to pleasure, and having nailed itself through pleasure in him to the very bottom of the nature, it condemned the whole nature to death, pushing the nature of the things that have come to be, through man, toward passing-away according to death. For this too had been contrived by the sower of sin and father of vice, the wicked devil, who had banished himself from the divine glory through pride, and through envy, both toward us and toward God, had banished Adam from paradise: to make the works of God vanish, and to dissolve the things constituted for coming-to-be.

48. The devil, envying God and us, having by guile persuaded man that he was envied by God, prepared him to transgress the commandment: God, so that his all-hymned power, making man divine, might not become manifest in activity; and man, manifestly, so that he might not become a partaker of the divine glory in form according to virtue. For the most foul one envies not only us the glory with God through virtue, but also God the all-hymned power over us for salvation.

49. As in Adam death was the condemnation of the nature, [1328] the nature having pleasure as the beginning of its own coming-to-be, so death in Christ has become the condemnation of sin, the nature in Christ again recovering a coming-to-be pure of pleasure.

50. If we, who have been deemed worthy by grace to become the house of God through the Spirit, ought to display so great an endurance of sufferings for righteousness, unto the condemnation of sin, and, being good, to bear eagerly like evildoers the outrageous death, *what is the end of those who disobey the Gospel of God?*[^6] That is, what sort will be the end, that is, the judgement, of those who not only have zealously held to the end, living and active, in soul and body, by inclination and by nature, the coming-to-be of Adam according to pleasure that prevailed over the nature, but also did not receive God and Father exhorting through the incarnate Son; nor indeed the Son himself, the mediator who makes embassy on behalf of the Father,[^7] and who for our reconciliation to the Father, by the wish of the Father, gave himself up by volition to the death on our behalf, that he might glorify us through himself, having made us radiant with the beauty of his own Godhead, as much as he himself, on our account, accepted to be dishonoured by our sufferings.

51. Since God will be the place, uncircumscribed and without interval and infinite, of all who are saved, becoming all things to all[^8] according to the proportion of righteousness, or rather according to the measure of the sufferings undergone here with knowledge for righteousness, giving himself to each, as the soul to the members of the body, according to the power underlying each member, shows itself forth active, and through itself holds the members together for being, held together for life: *where will the ungodly and the sinner appear*,[^9] deprived of this grace? For he who is unable to receive the presence of God active according to well-being, where will he appear, having suffered the falling-away from the divine life that is beyond age and time and place?

52. He who does not have God holding together his life for well-being, God who is to become a place for all the worthy, how will he be, not having God himself as place according to the abiding and establishment of well-being in God?[^10] And if the righteous man is saved with much difficulty,[^11] what will be, or what will he suffer, who has made no account of piety and virtue in the present life?

53. God, according to one infinitely powerful wish of goodness, will hold all together, both angels and men, good and wicked; but these all will not partake equally of God, who has pervaded all without relation, but proportionately to themselves.
