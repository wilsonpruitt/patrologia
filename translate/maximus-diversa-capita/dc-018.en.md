title: Various Chapters, 18
---
[1373] 59. Our Lord is truly a light of the nations,[^1] uncovering for them, through true full knowledge, the eyes of the understanding that had closed in the gloom of ignorance; and again having prepared himself for the faithful peoples as a good exemplar of virtue in divine training, becoming for them the outline and sketch of the person that is according to virtue;[^2] looking to whom as the author of our salvation,[^3] we achieve the virtues by imitation, as far as is possible for us, through practice.

60. Everyone who out of envy hates, and noises abroad evilly with slanders, him who is stronger in the contests of the virtues and in the abundance of the word of spiritual knowledge, is Saul choked by an evil spirit,[^4] not bearing the fame of the better man's blessedness in virtue and knowledge; and raving the more for this reason, that he cannot lay hands on his benefactor. And often he even bitterly sends away his dearest Jonathan[^5] himself, I mean the innate thought in the conscience, who reproves the unjust hatred and truth-lovingly recounts the achievements of the one hated.

61. Let us too beseech the intelligible David[^6] to play with the lyre of spiritual contemplation and knowledge upon our mind, seized as in a fit by material things, and to drive away the evil spirit of the material vicissitude toward sense, that we may be able to understand the Law spiritually, and find the mystical word hidden in it, and make it a lasting possession as provision for eternal life.

62. Everyone who yearns for salvation applies himself entirely either to practice or to contemplation. For without virtue and knowledge no one has ever in any way been able to attain salvation; since it belongs to virtue to order the motion of the body, reining it back skilfully, as with a kind of bridle, by right thought from its course toward what is out of place; and to contemplation, to decree that what has been well thought and judged be prudently preferred.

63. Since what thinks is noetic, and what is thought is intelligible, and what is thought is the food and as it were the constitution of what thinks: fittingly God, the bodiless beings being minds, himself being thought, and becoming intelligible to them as far as they reach, illumines them from within, the mind both thinking and being fed.

64. The intelligible is one thing, and the noetic another. For the intelligible is a kind of food, as has been said, of the noetic, [1376] since what is thought, which is intelligible, is greater than what thinks, which is noetic, and is conceived before it. For those things are called noetic which, because they are minds, think the intelligibles that lie above them; for the intelligible is what is thought, which is also the food of the noetic, that is, of what thinks.

65. It must be known that effects have such images of the causes as are possible. For effects are all the things brought forth into creation; causes, those that brought them forth; and there is no likeness of these to one another,[^7] I mean of causes and effects.

66. There is no exact likeness between effects and causes; but the effects have such images of the causes as are possible, while the causes themselves are exempt from the effects and are seated above them according to the principle of their own origin. For the things of the effects pre-exist in the causes superabundantly and essentially.

67. Effects are all the things brought forth into creation, whether in heaven or on earth; causes, those that brought them forth, that is, the three hypostases of the holy Trinity. It is plain, then, that there is no likeness of these to one another, I mean of causes and effects.

68. One must know that our mind has, on the one hand, the power for thinking, through which it sees the intelligibles; on the other, the union that surpasses the nature of the mind, through which it is joined to the things beyond itself. According to this, then, divine things are to be thought, not in our fashion, but with ourselves wholly standing outside our whole selves and becoming wholly God's. For it is better to be God's and not our own. For thus will divine things be given to those who come to be with God.

69. The mind, wishing to think, goes down below itself, descending into intellections. For intellections are lower than what thinks, as being thought and comprehended, and fittingly a scattering and partition of the unity of the mind itself. For the mind is simple and partless; but intellections are very many and scattered, and as it were forms of the mind. Therefore the noetic, that is, the things that think, are less than the intelligibles, that is, the [1377] things thought. He calls «union» of the mind,[^8] as he says more clearly in what follows, that through which it stretches up to the things beyond itself, that is, applies itself to the contemplation concerning God: namely, to stand outside all sensible and intelligible things, and further also its own motion; and then thus to receive the ray of divine knowledge.

70. If the noetic[^9] moves noetically in proportion to itself, it certainly also thinks; if it thinks, it certainly also yearns for what it has thought; if it yearns, it certainly also suffers ecstasy toward it as toward the yearned-for;[^10] if it suffers, it is clearly also urged on; if it is urged on, it certainly intensifies the vehemence of its motion; and if it intensifies its motion vehemently, it does not stop until it comes to be whole in the whole yearned-for, and is encompassed by the whole willingly, receiving[^11] wholly by choice the saving circumscription, that it may be wholly qualified by the whole that circumscribes it, so that what is circumscribed no longer wishes at all to be able to be known, that very thing, wholly, from itself, but from what circumscribes it: as air wholly illumined by light, and iron wholly fired through by the whole fire, or whatever else there is of the kind.

71. Great is the relation of those who think to the things thought, and of those who perceive to the sensible things. And man, being of soul and of a sensitive body, through his natural relation and property toward each of the two sections of creation by way of interchange, both is circumscribed and circumscribes: the one by essence, the other by power. For he is of a nature to be circumscribed by the intelligibles and sensibles, as being soul and body, and to circumscribe them by power, as thinking and perceiving. But God is simply and without bound above all beings, both those that contain and those contained, as being utterly without relation to all.

72. Every pleasure in forbidden things is of a nature to arise entirely from passion, through the mediation of sense, toward some sensible thing. For pleasure is nothing other than a form of sense, shaped in the sensitive faculty by some sensible thing; or a mode of sensitive activity, constituted according to irrational desire. For desire added to sense turns into pleasure, bringing a form upon it; and sense moved by desire produces pleasure, taking on the sensible thing. The saints, then, knowing that the soul, moved contrary to nature toward matter through the mediation of the flesh, puts on the earthly form, themselves moving rather according to nature toward God through the mediation of the soul, resolved to bring the flesh too fittingly into kinship with God, adorning it, as far as possible, through the ascesis of the virtues, with the divine reflections.

73. The saints, magnificently, according to the true and [1380] infallible mode of motion according to nature, crossed the present age of the wrestling-pits: uniting sense, through the mediation of reason, to the mind that has the simple principles of beings; and bringing to God the mind, purely released from motion about all beings, and quiescent even from its own natural activity; by which mode, gathered wholly toward God, they were counted worthy to be blended, whole, with the whole God through the Spirit, bearing, as far as is possible for men, the whole image of the heavenly,[^12] and drawing so much of the divine reflection, if it is lawful to say this, as they themselves, being drawn, were set in God.

74. They say that God and man are paradigms of each other; and that God is humanized for man through love for man as much as man, having been enabled, has deified himself to God through love; and that man is snatched up by God in mind to the knowable as much as man has manifested through the virtues God, who is invisible by nature.

75. Everyone who has *put to death his members that are on earth*,[^13] and has quenched the whole way of thinking of his flesh, and has shaken off entirely the relation to it through which the love owed by us to God alone is partitioned, and has denied all the tokens of the flesh and the world for the sake of divine grace, so as to be able also to say with the blessed Paul the apostle, *Who shall separate us from the love of Christ?*[^14] and what follows: such a one has become *without father, without mother, without genealogy*,[^15] like the great Melchizedek,[^16] having no way to be held fast by flesh and nature, because of the conjunction that has come about with the Spirit.

76. The end of the present life, I think, it is not right to name death, but release from death, separation from corruption, freedom from slavery, cessation of disturbance, destruction of wars, withdrawal of darkness, relief from labours, quiescence of seething, covering of shame, escape from passions, and, to sum up, the limit of all evils; which the saints, achieving through voluntary mortification, presented themselves as strangers and sojourners in life.[^17] For fighting nobly against the world and the body and the uprisings from them, and having choked the deceit that comes from both through the entanglement of the senses with sensible things, they kept for themselves the dignity of the soul unenslaved.

77. A proof, and no small one, that the knowledge of providence has been naturally sown in us, nature itself [1381] gives: whenever, as if pushing us untaught toward God through prayers in sudden distresses, it prepares us to seek salvation from there. For seized suddenly by necessity, without choice, before considering anything, we call upon God; as though providence itself were drawing us to itself even without thoughts, and overcoming the swiftness of the noetic power in us, and showing forth the divine help as stronger than all. And nature would not lead us without choice toward what has not the nature to come to be; and everything that follows naturally upon anything whatever, as is plain to all, has a strong and invincible power for the demonstration of the truth.

78. Since of beings some are good and some bad, and these are either present or to come: the good, when expected, one calls desire, and when present, pleasure; and again the evil, when expected, fear, and when present, grief; so that about good things, whether those that truly are or those that are thought to be, there are, and are contemplated, pleasure and desire; and about bad things, grief and fear. For desire, when it attains, produces pleasure; when it fails, grief.

79. They say that all grief is evil by its own nature. For even if the earnest man grieves at others' evils, as merciful, yet he does so not antecedently by intent, but consequently by distress; but the contemplative remains passionless even in these, having joined himself to God and alienated himself from all things here.

80. All the saints, having genuinely laid hold of the divine and unerring word, crossed this age, planting the footprint of the soul on none of the delights in it. For they spread out the mind, very fittingly, toward the highest principles about God attainable by men, I mean those of goodness and of love; moved by which, they were taught, God gave being to the things that are and graciously granted well-being: if indeed it is lawful to speak of motion in God, who alone is unmoved, and not rather of wish, which moves all things and brings them into being and holds them together, but is itself in no way ever moved.

81. The soul, being a noetic and rational essence, both thinks and reasons: having the mind as its power, intellection as its motion, and the concept as its activity. For this is the limit of the intellection both of what thinks and of what is thought, as being what bounds the relation of the extremes to each other. For the soul, in thinking, [1384] ceases from thinking that which was thought after its intellection of it. For what has once been properly thought no longer calls out the power of the soul toward being thought again; and so at each concept it receives rest from the qualified intellection of what was thought, upon the concept.

82. As ignorance is divisive of those who have gone astray, so the presence of the intelligible light is gathering and unifying of those who are illumined, and perfecting and turning back: turning them back from the many opinions toward what truly is, and gathering the varied sights, or, to speak more properly, imaginings, into one true and pure and uniform knowledge; and filling them with the one and unifying light.

83. The beautiful is the same as the good, because all things aspire to the beautiful and good for every cause, and there is not any of beings that does not participate in the beautiful and good. For to all, the beautiful and good, as truly admirable, is aspired-to and yearned-for and pleasing and to be preferred and beloved. Note how the divine yearning, pre-existing in the good, begot the good yearning in us, through which we aspire to the beautiful and good, according to him who said: *I became a lover of her beauty.*[^18] And: *Yearn for her, and she shall keep thee; set a palisade about her, and she shall exalt thee.*[^19]

84. The theologians call the Divine sometimes yearning, sometimes love, sometimes yearned-for and beloved. Hence, as being yearning and love, it is moved; as yearned-for and beloved, it moves toward itself all things receptive of yearning and love. And to say it again more clearly: it is moved as producing an inward relation of yearning and love in those receptive of them; it moves as by nature attractive of the aspiration of the things moved toward it. And again it moves and is moved, as thirsting to be thirsted for, and yearning to be yearned for, and loving to be loved.

85. The divine yearning is also ecstatic, not allowing the lovers to be their own, but the yearned-for's. And this the higher show by coming to be in providence for the lesser; those of the same rank, in their mutual cohesion; and the lower, in their more divine turning toward the first. Therefore also the great Paul, having come to be possessed by the divine yearning and having partaken of its ecstatic power, says with inspired mouth: *I live, no longer I, but Christ lives in me*;[^20] as a true lover, and as one who has stood outside himself, as he himself says, to God,[^21] and living not his own life but the life of the yearned-for, as exceedingly beloved.[^22]
