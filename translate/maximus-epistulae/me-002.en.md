wholly, through the whole, gods by grace; so much, that we are thought to be another He through all things — apart
from identity with Him in essence.[^3] For this is perfection: bearing in ourselves no mark at all of this age; lest
through that mark there come to be in some respect a deprivation of the divine habit for us, and we be bitterly
pained at the blemish upon our true soundness, and not find the occasions of correction.

When the age of our practices has passed away from us, nothing at all of the present things remains as it now is, in
figure and position. None of the things that are seen holds what is trustworthy and unfalling for those who possess
it. Nothing stands lastingly, remaining on unmoved, for those who have been worsted by it: not glory, not wealth, not
power, not health, not ingloriousness, not poverty, not slavery, not sickness, not beauty, not youth, not eminence,
not ugliness, not old age, not low birth. All things pass by, all wither, all human things vanish like a shadow, and
the whole bulk of human power is more easily blown away than a bubble. For every man, he says, is grass, and all the
glory of man as the flower of grass: the grass has withered, and the flower has fallen.[^4] For truly all human
things have corruption joined, in potency, to their coming-to-be; and often, in some, corruption even preceding their
coming-to-be; so that the middle can be stolen away by the meeting of the extremes with one another at the same
point,[^5] even while it is contemplated through both. And this providence wisely contrives, that the principle of
the things that are altered may become a pedagogy toward assurance of the everlasting. All things change into one
another, and each is turned about by each; and through one another they confirm their own corruption rather than
their coming-to-be.

Let us not, then,[?] bind[?] to the things of the moment the soul that does not perish;[^6] nor weigh down the mind,
dragging it toward earth by care for material things, when it is by nature free of matter. Let us divide naturally
from one another by reason the things that never [377] coincide with one another in essence; and no toil remains for
us to draw away from passionate attachment to perishing things the soul, which is incomposite and can in no way admit
dissolution of nature. Let us flee Sodom — the error of the senses about the sensibles, I mean, and their confusion
— that we may flee the burning: I mean the chastisement that follows these, altogether, through fire. Let us be saved
to the mountain — to the height, that is, of the way of life according to God — lest we be taken along with them, we
who have not yet wholly moved out from the senses, even if we seem, like the blessed Lot, to be idle from vice, in not
working the like things with the Sodomites:[^7] that is, with the passions that come through the senses, the things
that suit passions.

Let us run past, so far as we have power, the things that run by, which can in no way be held by us, having by nature
the passing flux as their very hypostasis,[^8] and leaving in us only the disposition of the soul badly stained upon
them, on account of which every torment is to be held over the condemned. Let us let go of everything readily, or lend
it beforehand to God through those in need[^9] — everything whose deprivation of necessity naturally attends us. Let us
let go by inclination, according to volition, the things that naturally, of necessity, both let go and are let go.
Let us not make the soul, which naturally images its own Maker, a captive, through an irrational relation, to the
things that come to be and cease to be — the soul which never, after coming to be, receives an end of being, and has
been commanded ever to seek God through all things and to hasten toward Him. Let us not will to have what we cannot
hold; nor indeed attach ourselves, as to things everlasting, to the things that altogether pass by. For the figure of
this age passes away, as the Saint said;[^10] and: The heaven and the earth shall pass away, as the God of the Saint
has said.[^11] And let us await[^12] the divine sentence as unfailing, and anticipate by faith the power of the word,
and be assured, by a clear demonstration, of the outcome of what is said. For of that whose parts are subject to
corruptions and alterations, as one of our wise men said,[^13] the whole too evidently is so, even if the time has
not yet come to stand in operation in which the whole will undergo these things; since every man too, from what he
suffers each day, knows naturally that he will die, even if death is not yet present in deed.

Let us become, then, our own, and God's — or rather God's alone, and not at all the flesh's and the world's. Let us be
prepared to call upon our God, according to what is written: Prepare to call upon thy God, Israel[^14] — which is:
let us be cleansed of the evil stains and defilements, casting off from ourselves even the bare imaginations of the
passions, so far as possible. For so truly is everyone prepared who wishes to be well prepared; that we may receive,
through the [380] virtues, as through some divine colours, the exact assimilation to God. For so does he call upon
God fittingly who is not ignorant how God ought to be called upon. For preparation is — to speak for myself —
estrangement from the passions; and invocation is the genuine kinship with God through the virtues. Or again:
preparation is the brightness that comes to be in the worthy through the virtues; and invocation is the reception,
through true knowledge, of deifying grace. For so shall we be able to have our garments white always, according to
what is written[^15] — that is, the modes through the virtues, bright and transparent, and having no token of
darkness. And our soul shall exult in God our Saviour,[^16] rejoicing both at the putting off of the old man, which
is corrupted according to the desires of deceit, and at the putting on of the new, which has been created according to
God in the spirit.[^17] For our God will clothe us with a garment of salvation and a tunic of gladness:[^18] that is,
with the philosophic practice, in character, through the virtues, that has become a habit, and with the unstumbling
contemplation of knowledge in the spirit; that we may also enjoy the primordial goods of the divine and undefiled
bridal chamber to come. In addition to all this, considering exactly how we walk,[^19] and what we counsel concerning
ourselves, knowing that many invisible witnesses stand by the things done and thought by us, not looking at what
appears only, but peering into the souls themselves, and searching out what is hidden in the heart.

For many, in truth, are the choirs of angelic Powers that stand around us on every side, recording in heaven with all
exactness the things done and said by us, and thought, even to the bare reflection, for our conviction on the fearful
day of judgement; when all the creation of visible things, shaken, is driven with an unspeakable onrush toward its own
consummation; and heaven and earth shall pass away with a rushing noise, and the elements between, burning, shall be
dissolved; and there shall be a new heaven and a new earth,[^20] and what follows upon these. When thrones are set,
and the Ancient of days takes His seat, and books are opened,[^21] holding the records of our works and words and
thoughts faithfully and unadulterated; and thousands of thousands of angels minister to Him, and ten thousand times
ten thousand stand by Him; and a shuddering threat of blazing[^22] fire runs through everywhere, seizing all things
round about, and melting mountains like wax. For a river of fire, he says, was drawing before Him.[^23] When Tartarus,
and a gaping and unimaginable pit, and the outer darkness, and the sleepless worm[^24] are shown to be present; and
over these, fear stands through all, in suspense, as they await the outcome; and angels of [381] wrath, flashing forth
chastising fire, looking fire, and breathing fire, stand ready for every vengeance upon every lawlessness. And besides
these, all creation, earthly and heavenly — as much as is among the angels and the powers above angels, and as much as
is among men — stands by with trembling, awaiting the fearful manifestation of the divine counsel; before all of whom
the practices of all of us shall be read, and the stripping bare of hidden things shall come about; so that all shall
know one another's sins as each knows his own, reading without error the book of his own conscience. For I will
convict thee, he says, and set thy sins before thy face.[^25] And again: Their counsels shall encircle them.[^26]

And behold, a man and his work;[^27] and the Judge who cannot be deceived, having standing by Him the fearful and
magnificent power of the heavenly ranks, takes His seat on high, flashing forth all righteousness, and assigning to
each, by a true and just sentence, what accords with his worth: to those who have been set on the right for their good
practices, gently giving the kingdom of the heavens prepared from the foundation of the world; but those who have been
allotted the station on the left for their dearth of good works, thrusting away with wrath into the everlasting fire
prepared for the devil and his angels.[^28] Who will bear that raging fire? Who will not cower at the strange
spectacle of that worm? Who will be able to bear the thickness and the heaviness together of that outer darkness? Who
will endure Tartarus bellowing, and the abyss boiling up with it from the bottom? Who does not tremble, shaken, at the
sharpness and grimness of the gaze, and the gloom of the face, of the angels set over this? Who will not fear what is
more terrible than all evils — the turning away of the face of the God who is by nature gentle and loving to mankind
and compassionate; with whom all creation turns away and feels disgust at those who, through a quarrel-loving
inclination, have pierced themselves through with such evils,[^29] being justly indignant against them, as having
forced the God who is by nature and alone loving to mankind to appear such to them? Who will endure the boundless
shame, according to conscience, that is to be at the manifestation of hidden things? Who will be able to measure the
unceasing weeping, and that bitter and profitless tear, and the gnashing of the teeth, and the shrieks of those
pressed in the torments, or the pain from repentance that lies upon the midst of their heart, and justly smoulders
away the bottom of their soul? Who, the straitness that comes upon them from not hoping for a set term of
transformation, and an end of the punishment that lies upon them, nor to await the expectation of a change back again
to living well —
