title: Letter 1, to George, prefect of Africa, 3
---
[384] who is sufficient to tell it? For last and alone, and fearful and just in truth, is that tribunal; and just
more than it is dreadful, and for this reason perhaps the more fearful, because it is also just; shutting up within
itself all the ages according to the just verdict of God. Who, taking to mind the wailings and the groans brought
forth from the midst of their heart, fastening upon their very marrow, and bitterly gnawing the bowels, will be able
to be free of tears? Who could bear the lament and the mourning without pain, and without the dejection over it, and
with what words of repentance they are uttered — both those of each of the punished to himself, and of all to all?
For then each stands a true acknowledger of his own falls, when things appear naked in themselves, and free of every
covering of deceit.

Just is thy judgement, O God;[^1] for thou didst call us, and we did not obey thee; thou didst hold out words to us,
and we gave no heed, but made thy counsels void; and to thy words we gave no heed. Therefore justly has there come
upon us destruction, and ruin, and tempest, and affliction, and siege; and now we call upon thee, and thou dost not
hearken; we seek from thee, and do not find mercy; for neither did we hearken to thy words, nor did we do mercy to our
neighbour. For we who hated wisdom, and did not choose the fear of the Lord, nor willed to heed God's counsels, reap
such fruits of our own seeds, and are filled with our own folly. For the deceit of life blinded us, and we did not know
the mysteries of God, nor did we hope at all that there is a wage of holiness.[^2] Therefore we were filled with the
paths of lawlessness, and went through trackless deserts — deserts, that is, of divine oversight — but the way of the
Lord we did not know. And now what has pride profited us, and what has wealth with boasting brought us? All those
things have passed by like a shadow, and like a message running past, and like a ship going through the surging
water, of which, when it has passed, there is no trace to find, nor path of its keel[^3] in the waves; or as when a
bird has flown through the air, no token of its journey is found. All those things have passed by, leaving us this
fire as an inheritance, remaining to all the ages.

Just is thy judgement, O God; for we shall never cease from now on saying the same things: we were called, and did
not obey; we were admonished, and gave no heed. About these terrors we were taught every day, and we despised what
was said. The words about these things did not grieve us then even a little; else the things themselves would not now
grieve us so vehemently. A trifle we thought the things that now hold our life fast, when they were often sung over us
then. O the sloth! O the deceit! O the evil [385] counsels! O the wicked dealings! O the wonder of God's just sentence
upon us for these things! How He has repaid us, for each kind of our sins, a fitting torment! For on account of a
pitiable and abominable pleasure of the flesh we have justly been condemned to have this fearful Gehenna. We have been
condemned to dwell in this gloom and this horrible darkness, since we loved all shameful things — sights and sounds
and words; and preferred before all the divine goods together the hippodrome and the theatres and the hunting-shows,
in which hosts of demons danced, and all divine oversight and acknowledgement through angels was absent; where the
dignity of chastity was outraged in many manners, while the evil and the loathsomeness of licentiousness was
conspicuously dignified. Justly have we been deprived now of light, because during our temporary life we did not
behold the creation for the glory of the God who made it; but defiled our eyes and our ears and our tongue — or
rather, through these, we corrupted the soul made according to the image of God, with all the sights and hearings and
words hated by God, and with the envyings against one another. And we have been hurled down into Tartarus, worn away
terribly by frost and by the deep at once; since we preferred pride to the lofty humble-mindedness that procures lofty
and divine things; and thrust away the firmness and intensity of virtue for the overflowing luxury and dissipation of
the flesh. Where now is our pride, and the boastful disposition toward all that came of it, and the flesh's sluggish
and easily dissipated luxury? O the folly! Instead of unrestrained laughter, and the licentious smacking of the
cheeks, this heavy gnashing of the teeth is ours. Not willing to exercise the learning of the divine and saving words,
but preferring immoderate drunkenness to divine praise through speech, we are terribly chastised now, our tongue
burning with a fearful thirst.[^4] And worthy of the hatred toward our brothers — of envy and guile and hypocrisy,
from which and through which comes murder; of reviling and slander, and of remembrance of wrongs and of lying and of
perjury — is the fruit we reap: this sleepless worm, insatiably eating the depth of our soul. Since, perverting the
uprightness of love, we made our heart crooked, and corrupting the impassibility of truth, we twisted it; fashioning
for ourselves, through voluntary hatred, an evil, involuntary worm, and digging, through lying, a more wicked
twistedness, on account of which we were not united to God, who is upright and straight. For He Himself said through
holy David the prophet: A twisted heart has not cleaved to me.[^5] Worthy, then, of what we did, do we receive;[^6] or
rather, we carry off wages worthy of our own disposition by intent:[^7] for the fire of pleasure, the fire of
Gehenna; and for the darkness of ignorance and of [388] temporary deceit, everlasting darkness; and a chastising and
sleepless worm, for the worm that made the heart crooked and twisted through hatred and lying; and for the wanton
smacking and boiling of the cheeks, the smacking and gnashing of teeth; and for the height of vain pride and
dissipation, a fall, and a gaping depth, and the gloom of cowering.[^8]

And, to speak simply, for each of our voluntary evils we justly receive in return the involuntary retribution fitting
to each. O our pitiableness! Why did we wretches not quench the fire of the flesh through fasting and vigil, and the
meditation of the divine words — which are good and easy quenchers of fire — and not be fried now in this fire? Why
did we not accustom our eyes and ears and tongue to see and hear and speak according to nature, that we might not
have now this gloom, and this heaviest silence, but we too might enjoy the light and the word and wisdom of God with
the saints, as having become beholders and hearers and hymn-singers of the divine glory? If through love we had
deadened the voluntary worm of hatred, and through truth made the lie vanish, we would now stand free of this burning
worm, and of the other terrors that now hold our life fast. Just is the judgement of God. We do not find what we did
not seek. The door of the kingdom of the heavens is not opened to us, since we did not knock at the door of the
virtues through practice; we do not receive the enjoyment of the everlasting goods, since we did not ask through
prayer for the grace of knowledge.[^9] For we had no yearning for divine things, but binding the mind to earthly
things, we corrupted all our life along with them. And all those things have passed by, vanishing like smoke; but the
account of the penalty for them remains for ever, not passing away.

These things, then, those men, learning anew the power of divine righteousness from what they suffer, will perhaps
say to one another — to comprehend it in outline. But I, wretch, what shall I become? Leaning on what practices shall
I hope to be delivered from the fearful condemnation, being bereft of all virtue and knowledge? I fear lest, bound
hand and foot, I be cast into a land gloomy and dark, into a land of everlasting darkness, where there is no light,
nor to see the life of mortals,[^10] as having voluntarily bound the practical powers of the soul to the passions, and
hindered the soul's steps from the divine course of the evangelical way of life. Alas for the fearful shame that never
[389] has an end, unless, changing, I become free of my many evils! Alas for the weeping, and the bitter tears, and
the gnashing of the teeth, unless, becoming sober, even late, I shake off the deep sleep of sloth, and put off the
filthy garment of sin! Instead of light, darkness; instead of joy, grief; instead of relief, chastisement and
straitness will surely receive me. And what is the most pitiable of all — or, to say it more truly, the heaviest —
which I am pained only to speak of: how much more to suffer it (be propitious, O Christ, and save us from this pain!)
— the separation from God and from His holy powers; and the kinship with the devil and the wicked demons, remaining
for ever, and having no expected freedom from these terrors. For with those whom in this age, through our wicked
pursuits, we chose by inclination to be with, according to what we willed, with these shall we be condemned, with
reason, of necessity, to be in the age to come, even unwilling. And more chastising and more terrible than every
chastisement is this: to be for ever with those who hate and are hated — even without torments, let alone with them
— and to have been separated from Him who loves and is loved. For God neither is hated by those He judges, judging
justly — being by nature Love, both being it and being called it[^11] — nor does He hate those He judges; for He is by
nature altogether free of passion.

Believing that these things will in truth be, without falsehood, beloved, let us not neglect ourselves; but with all
zeal, with our whole power, while we have time,[^12] let us flee the deceiving world and the world-ruler. For it
passes away, and all the things in it wither.[^13] For there will be — truly there will be — a time when a fearful
trumpet will cry out, sounding a strange voice,[^14] and this whole universe will be dissolved, falling away from the
order now seen in it. And the world that appears will pass away, receiving its own consummation; but the world of
intelligibles, now hidden, will appear, bringing to eyes and ears and understandings mysteries altogether
strange.[^15] And the trumpet, crying out at the divine command, will raise the infinite myriads of human bodies all at
once and together, as from some sleep of death, leading them to examination; and God will render to each the just
recompense, according to worth, for what he did through the body, whether good or bad,[^16] giving to all the things
that are a great and fearful and final enclosing. But may it be granted to all of us to have before our eyes without
interval both the saving fear of Gehenna and the precious longing for the kingdom of the heavens — and it is properly
the same thing to say of both, since God becomes, and is called, all things to all,[^17] according as each has the
quality of vice or of virtue — the fear making in us a complete abstinence from evils and idleness [392] in them, and
the longing urging us abundantly and cheerfully toward the practices of good things.

These things, from me the little and poor, blessed one, let them be tokens of my love for thee, for the guarding and
safety of the good things that are thine — a guarding which I suppose even one who, like thee, is adorned exceedingly
with the virtues in every way, needs. For I have no other way to be able justly to repay thee for thy many good things
toward me than by disposing myself in this manner. And with me, genuinely, all the honourable Fathers who, because of
thee, sojourn in this land[^18] greet thee with one accord, unceasingly beseeching God night and day with tears to
restore to us in health our George, who is truly sweet both to see and to name; that with him we may pass through the
present life unharmed, and enjoy the grace to come of God, the lover of man and the giver of good things; while
there intercede for us, and propitiate God for us, our all-holy, glorious Lady, the Theotokos[^19] and ever-virgin
Mary, with all the saints. Amen.

SCHOLIA[^20]

1. Note: on human rule.

2. What one ought to seek and to will; and what to yield to God.

3. That ruling and being ruled, and being rich and being poor, are neither of nature nor of inclination, but of the
Providence that leads all things.

4. Definition of what is in our power, that is, of self-determination.

5. Definition of what is not in our power, that is, of the things not under self-determination.

6. He calls the extremes coming-to-be and corruption; and the middle between coming-to-be and corruption is what is
generated; in which corruption often anticipates coming-to-be, entering in together with coming-to-be upon what comes
to be, and corrupting its constitution before it has come to be.

7. Those who have the soul nailed to the relation of the senses are not saved, he says, even if they are idle from the
passions that delight the senses, unless they withdraw the soul perfectly from its relation to the senses; just as not
even the blessed Lot, had he not gone out of Sodom, though he did not do the deeds of the Sodomites, would have
escaped the descent of the fire.
