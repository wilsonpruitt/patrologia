[392] LETTER 2

*Of the same, to John the Cubicularius, on love.*[^1]

Having learned already of myself, when present — and, absent, no less, if not even more than by being present — that you, God-guarded ones, hold fast to the holy love, by grace, toward God and toward the neighbour, [393] and take care for it in the fitting manners, and that you undergo all that both is, and is called, proper to the divine love, so that you may have this too that is divine, the uncircumscribed and undefined in virtue: the doing good not only to those present, but the being eager also toward those absent, and far removed by the distance of place; and learning each time of its increase in extent through those who arrive here, and not only so, but also through your honoured syllables, picturing to myself, as through a mirror, the form of divine grace that is conspicuous in you, I rightly rejoice and am glad; and giving thanks for you to God, the giver of good things, I do not cease to cry out with the holy Apostle: «Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed you with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places»,[^2] being persuaded above all, and knowing, that your holy soul hangs indissolubly, in the Spirit, through love, upon my wretched one, having the law of grace for a bond of friendship; according to which, joining me invisibly to yourselves, you make me bright together with you, making my shame from sin vanish by the setting beside it of your own good things.

For nothing, in truth, is more godlike than the divine love, nor more mysterious, nor higher for men toward deification; because it has, gathered together in itself, all the good things that the word of truth recounts in the form of virtue, and is removed, without relation, from all the things comprised in the form of vice, as being the fulfilment of the law and the prophets;[^3] whom the mystery of love succeeds, the mystery that makes us gods from men, and contracts the particular principles of the commandments into their universal principle; by which they are all contained, by good pleasure, in one form, and from which they are issued, in many modes, by way of economy.

For what form of good things does love not possess? Not faith, the first groundwork of the things of piety, which assures him who has it that God and divine things are — and more than the eye, striking upon the surfaces of sensible things, affords to those who see the opinion about them? Not hope, which makes the good that truly subsists subsist in itself, and holds it more than the hand holds the grossest of matter that falls under touch? Does it not give the enjoyment of the things believed and hoped for, having through itself the things to come as present, [396] by disposition? Not humility, the first foundation of the virtues, by which we are able[^4] to become discerners of ourselves, and to cast down the vain swelling of pride? Not meekness, by which we buffet away blame and praise alike, and thrust off the troubling of the diametrically opposed evils — glory, I mean, and ingloriousness? Not gentleness under suffering, by which, even when we suffer, we remain unaltered toward those who do us ill, in no way disposed toward them with enmity? Not mercy, by which we willingly make the calamities of others our own, and do not suffer what is kindred and of one stock with us to be ignored? Not self-mastery and endurance, and long-suffering and kindness, peace and joy, through which we easily lull to rest anger and desire, and their burning heat and fiery inflammation?

And simply, to speak summarily, love is the end of all good things, as leading and bringing to God — the highest of good things, and the cause of every good — those who walk in it, as being faithful, and unfailing, and abiding.[^5] For faith is the foundation of those after it — of hope, I mean, and of love — giving the true a firm hypostasis; and hope is the strength of the extremes — of love, I mean, and of faith — showing forth through itself to both what is to be believed and what is to be yearned for, and teaching them to make their course toward it through itself. And love is the completion of these, embracing the last object of appetite whole with her whole self, and granting them rest from their movement toward it, bringing in through itself, in place of believing that it is and hoping that it will be present, the enjoying of it present.

She alone, properly speaking, shows man to be according to the image of the Creator,[^6] wisely subjecting what is in our power to reason, and not bending reason under it; and persuading the inclination[^7] to go according to nature, in no way at strife with the principle of nature; according to which all of us, as we have one nature, so can have also one inclination and one will with God and with one another, having no separation toward God and one another, whenever we choose to walk in line with the law of grace, through which, by inclination, we renew the law of nature. For it is impossible for those who have not first been joined to God in concord to be able to agree with one another in inclination.

For since in the beginning the devil, who deceived man — deceiving him, by the assault of pleasure, with a guile wickedly contrived through self-love — separated us in inclination from God and from one another, and, perverting the upright and partitioning the nature in this manner, cut it up into many opinions and fancies, and made the method and [397] invention of each evil, by time, a law, using our own powers toward this, and setting in all, as a wicked prop for the permanence of evil, the disagreement in inclination; from which he persuaded man once to turn aside from the movement according to nature, and to move his appetite away from what was permitted toward what was forbidden, and to make three evils subsist for himself, the greatest and most ancient, and, to speak simply, the begetters of all vice — ignorance, I mean, and self-love, and tyranny, hanging upon one another and constituted through one another. For from ignorance about God comes self-love; and from this comes tyranny toward one's kin — and no account will gainsay it — men having made these subsist by the mode of misuse of their own powers, reason and desire and anger; when it was needful, by reason, instead of ignorance, to be moved toward God through knowledge by seeking, most singly; and by desire, pure of the passion of self-love, to be driven by longing toward God alone; and, with anger separated from tyranny, to strive to attain God alone; and to fashion the divine and blessed love that is from these, and for the sake of which these are, which both joins to God and shows forth the lover of God as God.

Since these things befell man evilly, by man's own volition, but by the devil's deceit, God, who both made the nature and wisely heals it when it had grown sick through vice, for the love he has toward us, emptied himself, taking the form of a servant,[^8] uniting it to himself without change according to hypostasis, becoming, wholly, after our fashion, from us, for us, so far man that he was thought by the unbelieving not to be God; and yet being so far God as the ineffable and true account of piety sets him before the faithful; that he might destroy the works of the devil,[^9] and, giving back to nature its powers undefiled, might renew again the power of love — of the conjunction toward himself, and of men toward one another — the adversary of self-love: of the first sin, and the first offspring of the devil, and the mother of the passions after it, as it both is and is known to be; which he who has offered himself worthy of God, having made it vanish through love, has made vanish together with it the whole crowd of vice as well, which has no other foundation or cause of being after it.

For such a man no longer knows pride, the mark of the conceit opposed to God, the composite and monstrous evil. He is ignorant of the glory that falls, and that casts down with itself those puffed up by it. He withers envy, which justly withers first those who have it, making his kin his own through voluntary goodwill; and anger, and bloodthirstiness, and wrath, and guile, and hypocrisy, and dissembling, and rancour, and greed, and all the things by which the one man has been partitioned, he uproots along with it.[^10] For when self-love, as the beginning, as I said, and the mother of evils, has been plucked out, all the things from it and after it are wont to be plucked out together; [400] since, when it is not, no form or trace of vice at all can in any way subsist; and all the forms of virtue are wont to be brought in in their place, completing the power of love — the power that gathers together what has been partitioned, and fashions man again toward one both principle and mode, and equalizes and levels all the inequality and difference in all according to inclination; and leads him forward, as is fitting, into the praiseworthy inequality, each, that is, drawing his neighbour to himself by purpose, and preferring him to himself, as much as he was formerly eager to thrust him away and to excel him; and releasing himself, willingly, from himself for love's sake, by the separation from the principles and idioms understood in him individually according to inclination; and being gathered toward one simplicity and identity, according to which no one is in any way at all set apart from the common, but each to each, and all to all, and to God rather than to one another, they have become one, having the one principle of being, most single both by nature and by inclination, shining forth through themselves; and God, who is understood in it; with whom the principle of the being of the things that are is by nature contemplated together, and toward whom, as cause and Maker, it is by nature led up, being guarded, to be sure, pure and undefiled by us with all attention, and cleansed, by prudent zeal, through the virtues and the labours that attend them, of the passions that are at strife with it.

Which perhaps that great Abraham accomplished, and, having restored himself to the principle of the being of nature, or the principle to himself, and through this having been given back to God, and having received God back — let both be said, since in both the truth is contemplated — he was deemed worthy to see God as man, and to receive him, dwelling together with him, through love for man, by the perfect principle according to nature;[^11] toward whom he was led up, letting go the property of things partitioned and partible, no longer reckoning any other man to be other than himself, but knowing all as the one, and the one as all; looking, that is, not to the principle of inclination, about which there is strife and division as long as it remains in disagreement with nature, but to the most single principle of nature, about which the invariant stands; with which we know that God is altogether manifested together, and through which, as good, he consents to be made known, making his own creatures his own; since creation cannot of itself know God as he is. ## Notes
[^1]: P. John the Cubicularius (κουβικουλάριος, chamberlain of the imperial court), Maximus's most frequent correspondent: Letters 2, 3, 4, 10, 12, 27, 43, 44. Letter 2 addresses him in the plural throughout (ὑμᾶς, θεοφύλακτοι), so *you*; the plural may take in his household. Combefis's note call (d) after the name is not reproduced.
[^2]: S. Eph 1:3, with ὑμᾶς ("you") for the Apostle's ἡμᾶς ("us"), turned to the addressees.
[^3]: S. Rom 13:10 (πλήρωμα νόμου ἡ ἀγάπη), with Matt 22:40 ("the law and the prophets"). Scholion 1, at the letter's end, is keyed here.
[^4]: T. The Greek prints δύνανται ("they are able") and Migne gives [δυνάμεθα] ("we are able") in brackets; the Latin has *possumus*. The English follows the bracket, which agrees with the first persons around it (ῥαπίζομεν, ἀπωθούμεθα).
[^5]: S. 1 Cor 13:8 ("love never fails") and 13:13 (faith, hope, love "abide"); the triad expounded next is the Apostle's.
[^6]: S. Col 3:10 (κατ᾿ εἰκόνα τοῦ κτίσαντος), with Gen 1:26–27.
[^7]: T. γνώμη = *inclination*, here and throughout the letters; γνωμικῶς = *by inclination*. Maximus sets it against φύσις (nature) all through this letter: one nature, and, through love, one inclination.
[^8]: S. Phil 2:7.
[^9]: S. 1 John 3:8.
[^10]: T. The plate's συνακερίζωσε is no word. Combefis's *radicitus evellit* and the sense, against the plucking-out (ἀποτίλλω) of the next sentence, give συνεξερίζωσε ("he uproots along with it"); logged as a doubt.
[^11]: S. Gen 18:1–8, the three men at Mamre, whom Abraham received as guests. συνένδημον = "dwelling together with", "fellow-resident": God made himself Abraham's guest.
