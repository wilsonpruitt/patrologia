title: Letters 3–4
---
[408] LETTER 3

*Of the same, to the same.*

Having received the honoured letter of your Desirableness in the Lord, together with the blessing[^1] sent to the most devout monks of the holy monastery of the holy and glorious martyr George, I gave thanks to God for this zeal of yours for the good: that, by loosing with diligence the bonds of those held fast by poverty, you have freed yourselves from the bonds of sin; and, having set yourselves to nourish bodies, you have nourished your own soul with divine grace, and have given me too occasion to pluck the fruit of true contemplation. For, having come to myself, I knew for what reason God simply legislated that all men must show mercy and be shown mercy by one another: that, wishing to unite us to one another in inclination besides nature, and truly thrusting the whole of humankind toward this, he engraved for us, with love of man, the saving commandments; but the self-love and shrewdness [Perhaps: sense-perception][^2] of men, either thrusting away one another and the law, or sophistically evading them, cut the one nature into many portions; and, introducing the insensibility toward it that now prevails, [409] armed nature itself against itself through the inclination.

For this very reason everyone who, by sober thought and nobility of prudence, has been able to loose this unevenness of nature, has shown mercy to himself before the others, having fashioned his inclination according to nature, and having drawn near to God in inclination because of nature; and having shown in himself what is the mode of the principle of the image, and how God in the beginning, fittingly to himself, established our nature like him, having fashioned it a manifest likeness of his own goodness — itself the same with itself in all things: unwarring, peaceful, free of strife, bound fast by love toward God and toward itself; according to which we hold fast to God with aspiration, and to one another with compassion. And he has shown mercy also to those who need to be shown mercy: not only providing for them, but also teaching how God, being hidden, shines out through those who are worthy; so that they too may be able thereafter, reverencing their neighbour's love of man toward them, readily to lay claim to this grace as God's. But if anyone, being able to do this from his having, overlooks men in need, he will rightly be shown to have broken them off from himself, and himself from God; as having ignored nature through his inclination, or rather having destroyed, through his inclination, the good things that belong to his own nature.

These things, then, [are true of] all who have chosen, by inclination, harshness over love of man, judging kinship and the common stock less honourable than obols, and walling off, by their craving for gold, God's entrances into themselves, themselves suffering at their own hands a pitiable thing, the falling-away from the better things; but those things, of all who wisely strive to unite the rents of nature by the manner of mutual love, and, for the sake of working together with God through love toward the identity in inclination of other men, as far as lies in them, despise all things on earth, as true advocates of God through their works. Doing which, you have been shown, now and before, beloved ones, distributing yourselves eagerly to all in soul and body, in every principle and mode, and by a dexterous scattering of what you have;[^3] and showing in yourselves the inclination to be the same as nature, outdone by nature in one thing only, that it has the seeds of the things that come to be by having received them from there — or rather from God, from whom also the powers of good things have been laid down beforehand in nature.

Wherefore, being with many streams of graces magnificently from God [412] watered, you have abundantly, with all the others, let fall upon the most devout men aforesaid too the mercy [the oil][^4] of love of man; propping up the weakness of nature already tottering from want, and healing, as far as was possible, the hardship worn into them by poverty. Do not cease, then, doing good to the needy, so that by the emptying you may keep the fullness undiminished. And in cheerfulness of soul, confirming faith and hope and love toward God: «Take away the bond, and the stretching-out of the hand, and the word of murmuring»,[^5] as somewhere the divine oracle says; which is about the need to give, and not to deliberate; on which regret always follows — a thing heavier than not doing good at all. For we all know that for the unworthiness of doing or receiving good no one at all has ever yet known exactly whom one ought to blame — whether those able to do good, or those in need of receiving good; but that this belongs to the time to come, in which it will be known of what kind and how many our affairs are, and in what state they may happen to be. So wisely has God been pleased that in the present the abyss of his judgements be covered.[^6] And knowing this, the wise Proverbialist bade us cast our bread even upon the water without reckoning,[^7] as not to be swept away nor lost, but later to meet, with greater increase, those who have made their sharing for the sake of the good in simplicity of heart, from our God and Saviour Christ, who receives into himself all the things done by us to one another, as they may happen to be. For if, as lover of man, he consented to suffer for us, what would he not prove to have for those who imitate his love of man toward us?[^8]

[413] LETTER 4

*Of the same, to the same, on grief according to God.*

I rejoice and am glad, finding in every letter my blessed master grieving the praiseworthy grief; knowing that grief according to God works repentance unto salvation not to be repented of[^9] in those held fast by the divine fear with their whole power; not allowing the soul, turned away from the divine tribunals, to be disposed contrary to nature toward the things that perish; through which, in those who make the course of the present life carelessly, the wicked confusion of the passions, settling in, prepares the wretched mind to be caught in the snares of the devil, and rightly makes it a teacher of all vice; inasmuch as it has yoked its operation according to nature, contrary to nature, under the disorderly impulses of anger and desire. For the soul that has once, through deceit, bound its intellectual power to material things is most resourceful and swift of course about the finding of base things alone. For this reason, night and day, I pray without ceasing — though weighed down by many sins — that this saving grief may be preserved for my blessed master: truly the severe mistress of the passions, but the reverend and glorious mother of the virtues; so that, guarded by it, he may beat off the wicked sting of sin.[^10]

For no one, truly, who has shut it up in the depth of his heart is ever caught by the darts of the devil. No one who cherishes it invisibly in his soul fears the manifold assaults of the demons. No one who has chosen its companionship ever attaches himself to the things that are seen. No one who has it stamping his life is defiled by the filth of the things that perish. No one who has bound it indissolubly to his thoughts lets his senses be borne in disorder toward what they should not. No one whose soul has been qualified by it can at all reckon himself greater than another; but, on the contrary, would believe himself rather both to be, and to be thought, less than everyone. No one who has girded it about his mind slips from the understanding of divine things. No one who has fitted it to himself wholly will be ignorant of the tokens of the pious way of life. This the Holy Spirit fashions, visiting the hearts of the worthy. This alone I know to be the begetter of the joy in the kingdom of heaven. For grief according to God is plainly a divine seed, yielding as ripe fruit the gladness of the everlasting good things.

This the exact recollection, in the understanding, of what one has lived through [416] renews. This the book of the conscience of our offences, diligently read, nurses. This the memory of death, and the most bitter pain that comes upon the soul at it, at the departure, ever expected, preserves. This the fearful and severe inquest of the spirits of wickedness, joined against the soul in the air, ever being formed in the mind, keeps altogether unquenched. For who, beloved, of those like me stained with the filth of sins, does not fear the oversight of the holy angels — by whom, receiving at the divine decree the end of the present life, he is thrust out of the body by force, with wrath, even unwilling? Who, bringing with him a stained conscience, does not dread the most savage and barbarous onset of the wicked demons, each of them in this air, after the departure, cruelly tearing the wretched soul toward himself through the passion that is between them, and shaming it by the conviction of what it has done, and working in it despair of all hope in the good things to come? This grief is savingly nourished by the exact re-imaging of the state of the souls in Hades, after the examination that takes place in the air before the spiritual hosts of wickedness:[^11] how they pass their time in deep darkness and the heaviest silence, having for food the bitterest groans and the tears, and the dejection upon these; awaiting always nothing else, but only the just condemnation upon the divine verdict. This grief is constituted by the hope of the resurrection; by the majesty of Christ's fearful and all-glorious coming; by the most dreadful day of judgement, meditated continually in the understanding; in which heaven and earth, and the whole ornament of the things that are seen, shall pass away with a rushing noise, the elements burning and being dissolved[^12] by the immense fire, which purges beforehand, for the coming of the Pure One, the creation defiled by us; in which thrones are set, and the Ancient of days sits presiding, and the infinite ranks of the holy angels and archangels stand by in liturgy with fear and trembling; and the whole nature of [men] is brought to examination; and books are opened of our works and words and thoughts, having the things written in them with all exactness;[^13] through which there comes about the laying bare of all our deeds before the whole creation; so that all know one another's sins as much as each his own, reading unerringly the book of his own conscience; at which those who stand on the right of the Judge shall receive the promises of the ineffable good things; but those allotted the station on the left, the everlasting fire, and the outer darkness, and the sleepless worm, and the gnashing of teeth, and the unceasing tears,[^14] and the endless shame, at which every man [417] condemned to the torment without end for the ages will be pained more than at all the other forms of punishment together.

These truly fearful things, both deeds and names, the one who has grief according to God in no way forgets; but he will be disposed as one who ever sees the Judge and is seen, and will pass the present life piously and righteously, doing or saying or thinking nothing alien to the divine grace and calling. Of this praised grief the offspring is the good pre-eminent among men, humble-mindedness; by which the pious and God-loving man will in truth reckon himself less than the least of all,[^15] even if he be a king in rank, making natural weakness the measure of his knowledge of himself, under which all who have partaken of the same nature are equally reckoned; in no way carrying his thought away from the principle according to nature because of his rank. Its bulwark is meekness, a habit that thrusts away the movements contrary to nature, of anger and of desire; through which man receives likeness to the angels, among whom there is no raging anger, barking unseemly at its kin, nor yet desire, loosening the intellectual tensions and drawing aspiration down toward matter; but reason alone, having the whole dominion, and leading ardently, in the mind, toward the Word who is above cause and nature. Its fruit is love, according to which, being united to God and to one another, we willingly take thought for one another, as far as is attainable, as much as God, as compassionate, holds fast to all. Its end is the kingdom of heaven, and the enjoyment of the divine good things there. For grief according to God leads to this end those who cherish it and guard it.

And the marks of this grief are: to accustom the eyes to see creation for the glory of the Creator alone, and to look upon the things of one's neighbours without envy and the evil eye; the ears to be opened readily to the reception of God's words alone, and to the supplication of those who ask of us; and to be diligently stopped against all reviling and the loathsomeness of shameful words; the tongue to be sanctified by the praises of others, in no way defiled by insults and revilings against others. And simply, to speak summarily, let us reshape all the members of both body and soul toward what seems good to the Spirit; and may the whole aim of piety be achieved [was achieved][^16] for us.

These things, blessed one, searching ourselves with all exactness, if we find that we have them, let us carefully preserve; but if we possess none of them, let us labour with zeal to acquire them, humbling ourselves, as men, to all men, for God's sake and the common nature's; remaining meek and gentle toward the things that happen contrary to our inclination; [420] generous toward the poor, and hospitable toward strangers; ready, as far as possible, for the help of those who need protection; one in soul and one in inclination with our friends; dexterous in all things toward our acquaintances; approachable to the lowlier; compassionate and loving toward the weak; long-suffering to those who provoke us; forgiving to those who stumble; becoming a consolation to those who grieve. And, to speak simply, let us become all things to all,[^17] both for the fear of God, and for the expected threat of the judgements to come. For without these things enumerated there is for no one any hope of salvation at all.

These things, and more than these, my longing for thee forces me to say. For I have no way to measure it, as it makes me ever picture thee, and converse with thee in soul as present, and forces me to meet thee, absent, through letters. But let us not receive the force of what is said in passing; since, with the rest, we shall surely have such words too, either as advocates or as accusers[^18] — I who speak, and those who hear — on the fearful day of the examination, according as we have been disposed toward them. May it be that all of us, both those who say something of what is needful and those who hear, be shown to be doers of all the divine words, and not hearers only, or speakers,[^19] by the grace of Christ our God and Saviour, who called us to his own glory and kingdom.[^20]

## Notes
[^1]: T. εὐλογία, "blessing", is a gift sent, here the alms John sent to the monks of the monastery of St George (Latin *cum benedictione missa*). ποθεινότης, "Desirableness", is an honorific address ("your Longed-for Self"); Letter 3 is in the plural throughout (ὑμεῖς, ἠγαπημένοι), so *you*.
[^2]: T. σύνεσις [Fort. αἴσθησις]: the bracketed conjecture is printed in Migne's Greek ("perhaps: sense-perception"). Combefis's Latin translates the printed word (*prudentia*).
[^3]: S. Cf. Ps 111:9 LXX (ἐσκόρπισεν, ἔδωκεν τοῖς πένησιν). T. παντὶ καὶ λόγῳ καὶ τρόπῳ is the idiom "by every means", rendered by the glossary's pair.
[^4]: T. τὸν ἔλεον [τὸ ἔλαιον]: the bracket ("the oil") is Migne's, and Combefis's Latin follows it (*humanitatis oleum*); "let fall" (ἐπεστάξατε, "let drip") suits oil. The English gives both.
[^5]: S. Isa 58:9 LXX (ἐὰν ἀφέλῃς ἀπὸ σοῦ σύνδεσμον καὶ χειροτονίαν καὶ ῥῆμα γογγυσμοῦ). T. For the printed Ἀνέχετε ("hold up") the English reads Ἀνέλετε, "take away" (Latin *Auferte*). χειροτονία, literally "stretching-out of the hand": Combefis's note (f) argues that Maximus takes the LXX word as *delectus*, the picking out of some to benefit and the neglect of others, against those who rendered it "ordination".
[^6]: S. Cf. Ps 35:7 LXX ("thy judgements are a great abyss").
[^7]: S. Eccl 11:1. Παροιμιαστής, "the Proverbialist", is Solomon, the author to whom Ecclesiastes was given.
[^8]: T. τί οὐκ ἂν γένοιτο ἔχων is rendered as printed; the construction is loose, and the Latin at the foot of the page is too damaged to check it. Letter 3 ends here. The line printed after it (οὕτως ἐχειροτονήθη) belongs to Combefis's note (f) and is not translated.
[^9]: S. 2 Cor 7:10. Letter 4 is to the same John, now in the singular (δεσπότην, ἠγαπημένε, σε), so *thou*.
[^10]: S. Cf. 1 Cor 15:56.
[^11]: S. Eph 6:12 (τὰ πνευματικὰ τῆς πονηρίας). The examination "in the air" is the soul's passage after death past the demons who claim it, the "inquest" of the paragraph's opening.
[^12]: S. 2 Pet 3:10–12.
[^13]: S. Dan 7:9–10. T. [ἀνθρώπων] is bracketed in Migne's Greek; kept in brackets.
[^14]: S. Mt 25:33, 41; 8:12; Mk 9:48 (Isa 66:24).
[^15]: S. Cf. Eph 3:8 (ἐλαχιστότερος, "less than the least").
[^16]: T. κατορθωθῇ [κατωρθώθη]: Migne prints the indicative in brackets as a variant; the English renders the subjunctive and gives the bracket.
[^17]: S. 1 Cor 9:22.
[^18]: S. Cf. Rom 2:15.
[^19]: S. Jas 1:22. λέκτας, "speakers": Combefis renders *praecones*.
[^20]: S. 1 Thess 2:12.
