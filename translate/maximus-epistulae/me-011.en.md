[469] *On difference; and how one must piously confess two natures in Christ after the union.*

These things having been so wisely declared, according to the sound sense, by the sacred teachers of the Church, it is altogether pious that we too, following them, should confess that the natures that came together into the ineffable union are two, unlike according to essence; and should hold that these have remained unconfused even after being united — the saying that they have remained unconfused not bringing in any division whatever (may it never be!), but signifying that the difference remains unchanged. For difference and division are not the same.[^1] Difference is the principle according to which the subjects differ from one another, and is indicative of the "how they are": that is, that the flesh is by nature and essence what it is, and again that God the Word is by nature and essence what he is. Division is a cut right through, that is, one that cuts the subjects through the whole, and sets them as subsisting privately and part by part and by themselves, and separated from one another. If then such a principle is preserved even after the union — the one that shows that the Word was not changed into flesh, and that the flesh did not depart from the definition that belongs to it according to nature, but confirms that each has remained what it is by nature according to essence, even though a union has come about — how is it not altogether just to confess that the difference of the natures that came together is preserved even after the union? And if this is not in doubt, as having the truth itself as faithful witness, it is manifest to all and plain that the things that came together remain unconfused, neither the Godhead having been changed into flesh, nor the flesh having been altered into the nature of Godhead. What account, then, remains that can demonstrate that it is not reasonable to confess the things that are preserved — if all of us men wish to be led by the fear of God, and everywhere to hold the truth itself as the rule of the divine doctrines, and not our own fallible surmise?

*Otherwise, on the same.*

Besides all these, the Fathers teach that the principles of the things that came together are always preserved, while these very things that came together both concur in one person of the Son and one hypostasis. If so, how is it not needful again, according to the Fathers, through knowing that the principles are preserved, to speak of the difference, and to say that each of the things that came together is preserved after the union in the property that is according to nature; and, through confessing that both concur in one person of the Son and one hypostasis, to know exactly that these are by nature undivided? That the difference of the things that came together, [472] and these very things that came together, are preserved after the union, beyond all change and confusion, the most holy luminary of the Church, Cyril — to whom those who wage war in vain against the holy Church of God pretend to be excessively attached — will suffice as witness: in the second tome against the blasphemies of Nestorius, writing this: « But the mystery that is in Christ having been brought into the midst before us, the principle of the union does not ignore the difference, but sets the division aside; not confusing or mixing the natures, but because the Word of God, having partaken of flesh and blood, is again understood and named one and the same Son »;[^2] and in the Apology to Andrew, who had found fault with the third of the chapters,[^3] speaking thus: « You would be free of all blame in knowing, perhaps, that the flesh is other, according to its own nature, than the Word who sprang from God the Father, and that the Only-begotten, again, is other according to the principle of his own nature. But to know these things is not to partition the natures after the union. » Behold, "after the union" too has been clearly set down by the great Cyril, being both understood and said piously by the faithful according to him. For it shows that the principle of the being of the things united has in no way been harmed by the union, and that the one has not been commixed with the other because of the one-according-to-hypostasis in both, but rather that they are preserved and remain according to what each is by nature to be in essence. For the wise Cyril too, knowing this, says expressly: « The natures are neither confused with one another, by concurring in one hypostasis of the Son, nor divided from one another, by being other and other (in essence, that is), and remaining and being understood so after the union »;[^4] and each with its natural property and difference, according to which the Word is God by nature, and not flesh, even though he made the flesh his own by way of economy; and the flesh is flesh by nature, not God, even though it has become the Word of God's own through the union.

How then is it not necessary, and pious and fitting, according to the teaching of the Fathers, that we who say the difference is preserved after the union should hold that the two natures — of which the difference is, from which he also is — both are and are preserved in the one Christ after the union, knowing too that the things that came together into the indissoluble union are so many, and did not receive any change whatever into one another, or alteration? For there is every necessity, somehow, that every difference be of some things that differ; and where difference can be understood to be, there the things that differ are altogether present. For such things happen to be, in a certain mode, co-introductive of one another, [473] as are causes and effects, being understood as relatives in the same essence. For if the being of other essence[^5] of the things of which he is constituted is productive, as cause, of the difference in Christ, then the difference too is altogether indicative, as effect, of the natural otherness of the things united, as of its own cause. For such things are by nature, as has been said, introduced together with one another: when the one is said, it is altogether necessary to confess the other too; and when one, again, is taken away, it does not follow to say that the other is either. One must therefore also say two, that we may not introduce a bare difference; and use number for this and for this alone, to indicate that the difference of the things that came together has remained, as of things preserved after the union and not divided; besides its being both easier and true that the difference be shown from an utterance that rather signifies the things, than that the things be confirmed to be different from the word alone.

*That number neither divides nor is divided, nor, by its own principle, introduces division at all into the things of which it is said.*

But if some, preferring opinion to what is true, say that number is divisive, or divisible, or introductive of division, and for this reason, fearing a fear where there is none,[^6] refuse to say that two natures, the ones that came together, are preserved in Christ after the union, lest cut and partition be smuggled into the mystery of the becoming man, I do not see what account they have of their understanding on this point. For either they declare themselves wiser in this than the holy Fathers who used such a word and suspected no division — deceived, as though they could intrude upon divine things more spiritually than they;[^7] or, examining the principles of beings by themselves, puffed up with the conceit of their opinion, they have not recognized the truth, which has escaped them. For who, of those trained even a little in the divine teachings of the Fathers, does not know that every number, according to the great and God-bearing Gregory, is indicative of the quantity of the subjects, and not of the division of the things, as these suppose?[^8] For how could it divide — I would gladly ask them — that which does not subsist according to essence? For it belongs to an essence that subsists to be by nature able to do something about something else. And how will that be divided which cannot by its own principle suffer? For this, those skilled in these matters have declared, belongs to an accident, that is, a quality, as proper to the things said to be in a subject. But if number can neither divide nor be divided according to its own principle — for it is not of a nature to act, since that is of essence; nor to suffer, since that is of accident — neither will it be able of necessity to introduce with itself [476] a division of the things: which, as they stand before number in nature and position and in their relation to one another, remain fitted together even after being numbered, having suffered nothing at all novel because of number. For when we say ten men, say, or anything else of the things that subsist by themselves, and signify a divided quantity,[^9] we do not know that they have then received through number their being by themselves and their being marked off from one another by their hypostases; but we have indicated through number that they are this through themselves and not through number, and that they are so many — not by division, but by quantity. And again, when we say a stone is two-coloured or five-coloured, and whatever is of continuous quantity, we do not divide the one stone into two or five stones, nor do we cut the colours in it from one another, but we signify that so many are about it and in it, as in a subject, without confusion; no cut or division having come about, or being able to befall the stone, from the continuous quantity of the colours numbered about it; just as no confusion or commixture befalls the colours either from the one subject, the stone, about which they are. For the stone, having singleness[^10] in its subject, is shown also to possess undividedly the quantity of the colours about it. And likewise the colours of the stone itself, as differing from one another in quality, possessing quantity, have the singleness without confusion by concurring again by composition into the one subject of the stone; and the stone is one and the same, neither divided by the quantity of the colours, nor confused by the singleness of the subject. And having its existence possessed of different principles, according to one it admits number, according to the other it does not accept it. Every number, then, is indicative not of the relation itself of the things — division, I mean, and continuity — but of the quantity of the things of which it is the number,[^11] introducing the principle of how much the quantity is, and not of how it exists. For how could it introduce with itself the relations of the things, which are both before it and can be known without it, when it has no reflection of kinship toward them, being kept apart from them by their quantity in between? For seeing a multitude of men, or of horses, or of oxen, and suchlike, we know that they exist by themselves and that each is particularly divided by hypostases, and that they are this, as I said before, without number; but needing number to indicate their quantity, we in no way make it the cause of the relation, since we have no need of it at all even to know the relation. And again, beholding a stone or a flower or an animal of many colours, and things like these, we in no way take up number to know that they are variously coloured; but to wish to know the quantity of the colours in them, we do not deny that we need it.

[477] *How number is piously taken up to indicate the difference.*

So it is not that, in indicating how much the quantity is, number makes or introduces division; rather, it indicates quantity and introduces difference. For just as every difference is indicative of some quantity admitting number, introducing the principle of the how-of-being — for what is without quantity is altogether without difference too, as simple in essence and in quality — so too every number of a quantity of some things that differ, being indicative according to the principle of how they are, or of how they subsist, is introductive of the difference of the subjects, and not of the relation. And that this is so in the very truth, and that every number is indicative of difference and not of division, the most holy Cyril again bears witness to the account in the letter to Eulogius, writing thus, word for word: « So too in the case of Nestorius: even if he says two natures, signifying the difference of the flesh and of God the Word, yet he no longer confesses the union with us. For we, having united these, confess one Christ, one Son, one Lord; and thereafter, one incarnate nature of the Son. »[^12] As if he were saying: We, examining the all-wise economy of the mystery, when we wish to show that the difference of the things that came together is preserved after the union, say two natures in this respect and only in this, taking up number in contemplation alone,[^13] to indicate the difference; but when we set out precisely the ineffable mode of the union of the mystery, we say one incarnate nature of God the Word. For this, as I think, is what he means to show by « But we, having united them, confess one Christ, one Son, one Lord; and thereafter one incarnate nature of the Word. » That is: we, confessing the union, and articulating its mode exactly and with piety, do not use the word that signifies the difference to indicate the union; but, taking the one fittingly of the difference and the other of the union, we keep unconfused the sense of the things signified. So that it is plain from this that saying two natures was common to him and to Nestorius, so far as knowing the difference goes; but it was no longer common to confess the union too, by saying « one Christ, one Son, one Lord, and one incarnate nature of the Word », which Nestorius would not consent to say. For saying, « So too in the case of Nestorius, even if he says two natures, signifying the difference of the flesh and of God the Word, yet he no longer confesses the union with us », means nothing other than this: that Nestorius confesses the difference with us, by saying two natures; but no longer confesses the union with us, not saying « one Christ, one Son, one Lord, and one incarnate nature of the Word ».
