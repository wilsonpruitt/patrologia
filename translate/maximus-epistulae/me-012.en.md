If then the union always stands, and the things united always remain unconfused, and the difference of the things united, on account of which number was taken up according to the Fathers, is always preserved, how is it not necessary — if indeed the union and the things united and their difference always stand and remain and are preserved — both to say two natures piously, to indicate the difference of the things that came together, and again to confess one incarnate nature of God the Word, to show the union according to hypostasis: neither of the expressions (these, that is) being abolished by the other, as it seems to some who make unwisdom a ground of wisdom, lest confusion or division be brought in? For if we keep silent about the expression that fittingly indicates the difference, we give confusion room to hold its place; and if, again, we do not say the expression that signifies the union, how are we not ignorant of how to put division to flight?[^1] But both are always set down and piously said of the same Christ, though not in the same respect, since the things signified are not the same as one another either. For difference and union are not the same, even if they both are and are said about the same and of the same. For either of these, uttered separately from the other, is both incomplete, so far as indicating the whole mystery goes, not bringing along the other as its advocate, and is shown to carry suspicion: the one of division, because of Nestorius, who denies the union according to hypostasis and does not bear to confess that the uncircumscribed was pleased for our sake to be held by flesh; the other of confusion, because of Apollinarius and Eutyches,[^2] who deny the difference of the things that came together after the union, and are ashamed to bring themselves to confess that the invisible fell under our sense because of the natural property of his holy flesh from us.

So, as has been said, those who wish to be truly pious must both confess one incarnate nature of God the Word, that through this the utmost union may be shown,[^3] and again say two natures, that the difference of the things that came together, and are preserved without confusion after the union, may be indicated; and take up number for this and this alone. For the Fathers did not, it seems, approve that the difference be indicated by any other expression. But if anyone trusts in himself and can devise an expression more fitting for indicating it, [481] let him not grudge us the benefit; and neither shall we grudge him the instruction — not to say the rashness.

*How one must understand the saying set in the letter of Saint Cyril to Succensus; and that the expression «in two» does not conflict with those who say one incarnate nature of the Word, unless it is taken impiously, in the sense Nestorius held.*

But if they should say that the proposition of those who say two natures united undividedly after the union conflicts none the less with those who say one incarnate nature of the Word, as Saint Cyril supposes in the second memorandum to Succensus,[^4] I set this down as plainly the part of men who either do not know or who maliciously twist the words of the Fathers, and who reckon it more profitable to fight against the truth than to make peace with it. For they ought, knowing that we shall render account even for a bare thought to the just Judge of our affairs,[^5] reading the Fathers' writings with understanding and with the fear of God, to hasten not to make the words that teach peace and concord a ground of battle and dissension. For to whom that looks into it in any way at all is it not evident that the blessed Cyril does not appear to forbid such an expression simply, but as put forward according to the ill sense of Nestorius, who says that the union came about relationally — as the blessed Cyril himself is shown to bear witness to himself at the end of the same letter, making his aim plain to the overly contentious, saying thus: « The addition "undividedly" seems somehow, with us, to be significative of right belief. But they do not understand it so. For "the undivided" with them is taken in another mode, according to the empty phrases of Nestorius. For they say that the man in whom he dwelt is undivided from God the Word by equality of honour, by identity of counsel, by authority.[^6] So that they do not put forward the words simply, but with a certain guile and malice. » Behold, this wonderful teacher clearly supposed the mode of "the undivided" to be twofold: the one signifying the union according to hypostasis, which he also embraces, and says is significative of right belief; the other indicative of a conjunction by relation alone, which he also turns away from, and exhorts the faithful to do this very thing, since from the likeness of utterance in the word it can often slip the poison of heretical impiety unnoticed into the more guileless. Whence, with himself, he sets down saying two undividedly as orthodox, since he himself said the union was according to hypostasis; but as [484] put forward by the followers of Nestorius, he makes the word "undivided" suspect, since they held that the union came about according to the relational conjunction alone.

Union according to hypostasis, then, according to the Fathers, is the concourse of things of other essence into one hypostasis, holding the natural property of each of the things composed in it, relative to the other, unadulterated and unaltered and undivided. Relational union is the equal movement of will and the invariance of the identity of inclination of those who subsist by themselves, specifically, in personal monads.[^7] He then who confesses the union according to hypostasis, even if he says two natures in Christ after the union, united undividedly — by the union, that is, according to hypostasis — in no way whatever misses the truth. For he confesses that the things of which Christ is composed have remained unconfused, because of the difference preserved according to the Fathers, being able in no way to know the things that differ from one another by natural principle as outside the one according to hypostasis, and apart, in any mode whatever. But he who champions the union by relation, even if he says "the undivided" ten thousand times, carries the blame: for he says the things signified are united, and have undividedness, by a certain loving co-disposition,[^8] being in no way able to say that God the Word, with his own animate and intelligent flesh from us, is one hypostasis. For that one hypostasis should come to be from two specific hypostases, partitioned by a specific principle from the individuals of the same species, is impossible. So our holy Father Cyril does not simply slander and dismiss saying two natures united undividedly after the union, but the sense signified by such an expression in those who use it badly.

If then it has been shown — even if moderately, yet, to speak for myself, sufficiently, as far as was possible to signify by letter, both from the holy Fathers and from the sequence of common notions — that number can neither make nor suffer nor introduce division of any kind, but simply indicates how many things are, and does not touch their relation, however they may happen to stand: what suspicion of cut or division remains for those who rebel against the holy Church of God and cut themselves off at random from the common body — not doing more than suffering, being pitiably deadened and corrupted by the separation, and preparing for themselves the penalty, so far as concerns the indignity to the body of Christ — so that on this account, at least, they should refuse the right and pious confession of the faith? Unless indeed, under a pretext of reverence, lest they seem to cut the union, embracing [485] confusion, they contrive like knaves not to be caught in impious belief.

*That in Christ the difference is of things, that is, of essences, and not of qualities; and that every difference of necessity introduces with itself the quantity, altogether, of the things it is said to be of; and the quantity, the number that signifies it, whether the quantity is continuous or divided.*

For they ought — if this were truly not so — not to refuse to say what and how many the different things are of which they confess the difference to be: they who put forward the difference not even as of things, but by bare and sole utterance, as though it were predicated of nothing, ironically, to deceive the simpler.[^9] For if they believe that the things from which the union is are preserved after the union, they would altogether confess two things preserved. For so many too are the things that came together into the indissoluble union, having received from the union no change whatever, or confusion, or diminution, or coalescence, or alteration into one another. But as it is, not holding this, as men who have denied that the natures are preserved after the union, they have made themselves liable to refutation, even if they do not wish it, saying that the difference is preserved in quality alone, apart from the things — which is plainly the part of men at play, and of men who, as is likely, count it an insult not to be laughed at, and who clearly empty the Gospel, legislating that qualities can be without underlying essences, and supposing that difference can be recognized without quantity. For the most wise, it seems, did not know that every quality is altogether of an underlying essence, about which it is both contemplated and said, having in no way being by itself; and that every difference, being indicative of the unlikeness and otherness of some things toward others according to the principle of how they are, is by nature such as to introduce with itself the quantity of the things that differ, whether essences, or qualities, or properties. And simply, to speak in sum, of anything else whatever, of all the things they would suppose to differ from one another and say there is a difference, they have recognized the difference, by sense or by intellection, by contemplating the things that differ altogether in quantity. For to affirm that one can show difference without quantity is inadmissible: since things that are not in every way the same as one another will in no way admit one and the same principle of being. For of essence, or nature, say, toward essence, or of quality toward quality, or of property toward property,[^10] or of anything else whatever of the things that are not in every way the same — for things in every way the same as one another do not admit difference either — we can never say one and the same principle of the how-of-being. And that one and the same principle cannot be contemplated in all in the same respect — which is what made the difference — reasonably establishes that those things cannot be one and the same as one another whose principle of being is different and not the same. And in saying that one thing differs from another, or some things from others, we signify many, or at the least two, and not one; even if in another way, and according to another mode and principle, we say they are of one, and can be one: [488] as the qualities and properties about it are of one fire, and the natures from which and in which he is known to be constituted are of one Christ, and the things from which he was composed, and in which he is known to exist, are of one man. And one in hypostasis, in the case both of Christ and of the man as we are — that is, in the whole made of these as of parts, as receiving properly, both, the different principles of being of the parts in their fullness; but not one in nature and essence, since the parts do not receive the principle of being of one another, nor can we know the same principle of being in both. For the principle of Godhead and of manhood is not the same, just as neither is that of soul and body, as is plain to all. Wherefore the principle of truth has never defined Christ to be one in nature, nor simply one, or composite, nature. For the name Christ is not indicative of essence or nature, as of a species predicated of many individuals differing in their hypostases; as the blessed Cyril says in the Scholia: « That the name Christ neither has the force of a definition, nor indeed shows the essence of anything; but of the hypostasis of the Word, understood, that is, in the assumption of flesh having an intellectual soul. »[^11] Nor has it defined man as one nature, the one from soul and body, as though the body were of the same essence as the soul; but as one in species: cut off from the other species by the constitutive difference, and predicated equally of the individuals under it as a species and contained by it. And it has not, on that account, forbidden that soul and body, as soul and body, be two according to the essential principle, and other and other essence according to the principle of being — the things from which, and in which, and which man is.
