[509] LETTER 13

*Of the same, to Peter the illustris: a concise discourse against the doctrines of Severus.*[^1]

My blessed master has truly shown that bodily absence from one another can do nothing toward the diminution of spiritual love, for those who are intellectually fitted to one another through love according to God; not having disdained to visit, through his precious syllables, me, the lowly and sinner, who has nothing worthy of his love. Which[^2] having received, I embraced and greeted them rejoicing, as if he himself were present in the spirit, and gave thanks, as is my custom, to the only God and Saviour of all, Christ, having learned that he had accomplished the watery way in safety. For truly sweet is my blessed master when present, and when absent, no less than when present, he is lovable to all, because of the grace of the poverty of the Holy Spirit[^3] that shines upon him; through which everyone who genuinely lays claim to this, as the virtue of God, becomes a holy temple for a dwelling of God in the Spirit — of him who humbled himself unto the form of a servant, and by work and word showed it in himself to men: whom imitating, my blessed master too has inwardly acquired both meekness and humility. Meekness, that, quenching the passionate movements of anger and desire, he may become well-pleasing to God and men. For meekness is nothing other than [512] a complete immobility of anger and desire toward what is contrary to nature; by which the divine will becomes manifest to those who have it. For the great David, singing psalms, says: He will guide the meek in judgement; he will teach the meek his ways.[^4] And humility, that, cutting out the callosities of pride, he may become both grateful to God and approachable to all men. For humility is each one's recognition, by juxtaposition with God side by side, of the transcendent and productive cause; by which the co-disposition of men toward one another naturally comes to be, and the remission of offences is given to those who owe. For see, says the same great David again, my humility and my toil, and forgive all my sins.[^5]

Being thus, then, in regard to life, my blessed master I pray may be preserved in the firmness of faith in Christ, unshaken as his name declares,[^6] and above every heretical billow, as a genuine child of the holy catholic and apostolic Church of God; founded upon the pious confession, against which the wicked mouths of the heretics, opened like the gates of Hades, have no strength at all. For concerning the bastards, and those bastardly grafted in from the wild olive into the good olive,[^7] I knew, even before you blessed ones wrote,[^8] that, according to the prophetic word, As a dog to its own vomit,[^9] they are bound to return — I mean to the stench of the loathsomeness of their faith; as second Simons, and Demases and Hermogeneses,[^10] having chosen to prefer falsehood to truth; and, as they think, wronging the heralds of piety in no way by their apostasy, but rather being justly harmed themselves by the deprivation of grace; and not even able to have to their heart's content the present age, for whose sake they departed from the truth, since it is by nature unstable and easily altered, and ever runs past those who think they hold it.

For truly loathsome and full of stench, and empty of all grace, is every account[^11] that does not maintain that the things from which Christ is are preserved according to nature in the one Christ after the ineffable union; but as it were denies the whole economy of God concerning man, and cuts off from the Word of God what was assumed from the immaculate and holy all-glorious Virgin and in truth Theotokos: a rational, ensouled body. For truly he cuts it off in fact, even if he seems somehow barely to confess it with the mouth, for the deception of the hearers, and he denies the incarnation altogether, who does not say that after the union the nature of the flesh is, is preserved and is spoken of, united without confusion and without division to God the Word according to hypostasis — on the pretext, forsooth, of reverence, lest he seem to cut the union by the number — clearly denying, after the union, the whole existence itself, to speak properly, of the natures from which [513] Christ is.

*A more natural treatment of number.*

And they suffer this, as it seems, from not knowing that every number, by its own principle, neither divides nor is divided, nor is it at all productive either of division or of union. For it is not of a nature to act or to be acted upon, not being an essence or an accident, about which acting and being acted upon are; but only indicative of the quantity of the subject things, however they may happen to be disposed according to relation, whether united or distinguished. It is, for instance — if I may make bold to say something — a name of quantity, significative without the relation that attaches to it. For neither does number of necessity make the natural distinction of beings, or their quantity; nor does it of itself produce the continuity of things conjoined according to the same species; nor indeed is it universally characteristic of any property of the individuals under the same species that are distinguished from one another by accidents. For to do these things is proper to the divine wisdom and power, which constituted the universe and keeps each thing unconfused by the natural boundaries of the beings throughout the universe; but only, as I said, does number make an indication of the quantity of things simply, and not of their how, as a name fitted to quantity.

*On difference and quantity more exactly, and on their pious confession.*

If, then, according to the Fathers, number is indicative of the quantity of things but not of their relation; and every quantity contemplated in natural otherness wholly admits difference; and those too who fight against the truth say there is a natural difference in Christ after the union: then they must also say, after the union, the quantity of the things that differ — without which it is impossible for difference to be — together with the number that indicates it, without which it is impossible for quantity to be known at all; if they wish to show the account of their own doctrine coherent according to well-ordered sequence. But if they deny the quantity of the natures preserved in Christ after the union, let them not dare to speak even of the difference of the natures from which Christ is after the union; knowing that whatever has difference in any respect whatever cannot be one according to every principle and mode. And what cannot be one according to every principle and mode is clearly, according to some principle and mode, two at the least, or more. Therefore those who say there is difference in Christ after the union cannot say that Christ is one according to every principle and mode after the union. But if Christ is not one according to every principle and mode after the union, clearly, according to some principle and mode, Christ is wholly two after the union. And if Christ is two according to some principle and mode after the union, because of the difference in him of the natures from which and in which he is, as the argument has shown, it is clear [516] that they are impious to no purpose who say the difference of the natures from which Christ is after the union, but plainly destroy by silence, after the union, the natures themselves whose difference it is: for the denial of the things that are and are preserved is truly destruction. For either, by their account, the natures have not been destroyed after the union, and they both are and are preserved, and it is just that they be confessed as preserved after the union; or, if it is not just that they be confessed after the union, it is just, by their account, to say that they neither are nor are preserved in Christ, and they have been destroyed. For what it is not just at all to name, it is much better, as I think, and more just, to say does not exist at all either. Or let them persuade us by argument how it is just that the things from which Christ is, which are and are preserved after the union without any change and alteration whatever, and without conversion into one another according to the essential principle, should not be confessed in Christ; and we readily acquiesce in the privation of the confession of the things preserved and being in Christ.

And just as Christ is not one according to every principle and mode after the union, so again Christ is not two according to every principle and mode after the union. And if Christ is not two according to every principle and mode after the union, then wholly according to some principle and mode — because of the hypostatic identity, that is, the one hypostasis, according to which he cannot have difference — Christ is one. Since, then, the same is one and two according to one principle and mode and another, it is necessary to consider according to what principle, and according to what, the same is one and two.[^12]

*That it is blasphemous to say that Christ is simply one nature.*

If, then, they will say that he is one according to the principle of nature, the whole will clearly, by their account, be one nature simply, without any difference whatever. And if we allow them to say this, Christ will be, by their account, neither God nor indeed man, neither creature at all nor Creator. For a nature spoken of simply admits, once for all, no signification whatever of either of these. But if, fleeing the manifest absurdity, they should say that Christ is one nature, but composite, they have truly not known how the gloom of impiety has thrust them into the last error.

[^1]: P. Peter, *illustris* (ἰλλούστριος, the senatorial rank), addressee of Letters 13 and 14; Combefis's note (*Lectiones*) calls him a man of senatorial order, noble, of proven faith and orthodox, known only from these two letters. The heading calls the piece a Λόγος ἐπιτόμιος, "concise discourse" (Latin *oratio brevis vel liber*).
[^2]: T. Ἄσπερ as printed is read ἅσπερ, "which" (the syllables): a breathing misprint on the plate (OCR table, print); the Latin has *Has ergo cum accepissem*.
[^3]: S. Cf. Matt 5:3 ("poor in spirit"); Eph 2:21–22; Phil 2:7–8.
[^4]: S. Ps 24:9 LXX.
[^5]: S. Ps 24:18 LXX.
[^6]: T. φερωνύμως, "as his name declares": Peter, the rock (Matt 16:18), whose "gates of Hades" follow in the next clause.
[^7]: S. Rom 11:17–24.
[^8]: T. τοὺς εὐλογημένους ὑμᾶς: plural, Peter with those about him, so "you".
[^9]: S. 2 Pet 2:22 (Prov 26:11).
[^10]: P. Simon Magus (Acts 8:9–24); Demas, who forsook Paul "having loved this present age" (2 Tim 4:10); Hermogenes, who turned away (2 Tim 1:15). Δήμαντες is kept as printed (Latin *Dematitesque*).
[^11]: T. λόγος here = account (the doctrine as argued), in the glossary's sense.
[^12]: T. κατὰ ποῖον καὶ ποῖον λόγον: Combefis's note defends the doubled ποῖον against a Venice codex that has it once.
