title: Letter 13, to Peter the illustris, 2
---
[517] …has without choice the coming-together of its parts with one another in the composition.[^1]

Then, too, it possesses its parts contemporaneous with one another and with itself, co-existing according to their coming-to-be into being, neither part pre-existing the other in time.

And further still, it is known to have been made for the completion of the totality that magnificently outlines the universe; none of which is at all contemplated in Christ by those who wish to be pious; lest we make the coming-together of the Word with the flesh involuntary and without choice; and define either God the Word as contemporaneous with the flesh according to the coming-to-be into being, or the flesh as co-eternal with the Word according to his existence without beginning; or else define Christ as having nothing more than the remaining species contemplated according to universal nature, as brought forth as a part, like the remaining species, for the completion of the universe, by the power that constituted the universe. For this is the definition and principle and law of every composite nature, as the power of truth and the course of logical sequence guide those who make their examination of the things that are with understanding to say. And who will any longer be able to be of the portion of the pious, thinking so about God and Word — him who exists before all the ages; or rather, to speak more properly, the maker of the ages, who by volition made his self-emptying toward us men voluntary and self-chosen, and became man for the setting-right and renewal, and not for the completion, of the universe? For by a mode of economy, and not by a law of nature, the Word of God ineffably sojourned among men through flesh. Christ, then, is not a composite nature, according to the innovation of those who empty the Gospel, being altogether unenslaved to the law of composite nature as to the mode of coming into being; but a composite hypostasis, which has no composite nature whatever predicated of it by species: which is indeed paradoxical, to behold a composite hypostasis without the composite nature predicated of it by species.

But if, according to those very men, Christ is a composite nature — the wonderful acropolis of Severus's arguments[^2] — it is altogether either generic or singular.[^3] For to conceive another between these is impossible. And if Christ is a generic nature, he will clearly be predicated of many things, and of things differing in number; and he will be in thought alone, in those in which he has his existence, and not known by himself in some hypostasis of his own, apart from the accidents contemplated in addition in the individuals under him. For such is the definition and principle of every generic nature; and a multitude of Christs has been brought in upon us instead of one, having in no mode any identity whatever with God or with men, through Severus's excessive and unrestrained madness against the truth. But if Christ is a singular nature — to pass over saying that there is in no way at all[^4] a nature circumscribed by one person, even if they put forward, as a last resort, the fabled bird, [520] the phoenix,[^5] as something great and unassailable, an example establishing their own opinion; against which I am even afraid to say anything, lest among the understanding I incur the laughter that folly earns, trying to show the myth to be a myth. For if the phoenix is a bird, it is altogether also an animal. And if an animal, first, it is not wholly singular, inasmuch as it is an animal. Then, if an animal, it is also an ensouled, sentient body. And if the phoenix is an ensouled, sentient body, it is clearly also subject to coming-to-be and corruption. Let us ask the wise beholders of the things that are themselves whether it is possible for any of the ensouled and sentient bodies subject to coming-to-be and corruption to be of a nature singular according to hypostasis — bodies whose succession from one another according to species is the conspicuous character and definition of their being. As the divine Scripture also testifies, furnishing of itself certainty to the account, when it introduces God commanding these things to the great Noah: And God said to Noah, Enter thou and all thy house into the ark, for I have seen thee righteous before me in this generation. And of all the clean cattle bring in to thee seven and seven, male and female; and of the cattle that are not clean, two and two, male and female; and of all the clean birds of heaven, seven and seven, male and female, and of all the birds of heaven that are not clean, two and two, male and female, to keep seed alive upon all the earth.[^6] Among which, if the phoenix, as a winged animal, is numbered, it is not of a nature singular according to hypostasis, by the divine declaration. But, as I said, let us not say these things — for to fight over things not necessary is not dear to us: it is plain to all that, if Christ is a singular nature, he is consubstantial neither with God and Father nor with men. For what is wholly singular according to nature is altogether estranged from natural kinship with anything else whatever. But if, according to those very men, as the account has shown, Christ is wholly estranged according to nature from all the things that are, we shall urge the new dogmatists themselves to say what he is according to nature, and on what account they worship Christ, who, so far as the premises of their arguments go, is blasphemed by them[^7] as being nothing.

[521] If, then, a great and fearful danger of necessity follows, according to the force of the account rendered, for those who say that Christ is one according to nature, that is, one nature, whether called simple or composite, and this either generic, that is, common, or singular, that is, particular,[^8] let us with all vigilance keep our hearts pure of such an opinion,[^9] that we may also be set free from the threat that awaits it. And let us piously confess the "two" in Christ according to the principle of natural otherness, that is, of the difference of the natures from which he is, preserved even after the union, as of parts in a whole; according to which the parts never naturally coincide with one another, so long as each keeps unimpaired its own principle of nature relative to the other; and the "one" according to the principle of hypostatic identity, according to which Christ, as a whole, admits no distinction at all from difference with respect to himself, that is, no partition, so long as he is believed to be one hypostasis both before the flesh and after the incarnation, and is worshipped and glorified by us as one of the holy and royal Trinity. For Christ's hypostasis is singular, as a whole, divided out to the extremes on no account, according to its characteristic property, by which it is marked off from them. By extremes I now mean God and Father, from whom Christ has been begotten divinely before the ages, and the holy, all-glorious Virgin and Mother, from whom the same has been born humanly for our sake — the hypostasis keeping undiminished, by the kinship of its own parts with both the extremes, the identity according to essence; in which parts Christ, admitting the principle of difference, does not depart from his own singular hypostasis. For every totality, and especially one by composition, contemplated from different things, while singularly keeping the identity of its own hypostasis, also has the unconfused difference of its own parts with one another, according to which it preserves unadulterated the principle according to essence of each part relative to the other. And likewise the parts too, in their composition with one another, keeping pure and undiminished the principle according to nature, have the unitary, undistinguished identity of their own totality, according to which they singularly keep wholly undivided the principle according to hypostasis.

If, then, as the account, running with truth, has shown, it is altogether impious in Christ to decree that the "one" lies in the principle according to nature, as, conversely, the "two" in the principle according to hypostasis — for the one is proper to the stench of Apollinarius,[^10] who smears together and confounds the natures into one nature, and makes the economy wholly indiscernible, positing that properly no nature exists after the union. [524] For each of these escapes being what it is, as they pass over and fall into one another. And the other is proper to the Jewish derangement of Nestorius, who partitions the one hypostasis into two hypostases, and denies the whole economy, refusing to say that God the Word was united to flesh according to hypostasis, essentially. We, for our part, let us use our speech[^11] rightly and fittingly, turning the expressions of the perverse to what is straight: the "two" according to the principle of natural otherness, that is, of difference; the "one" according to the principle of the hypostasis; that both the differentness of the natures from which Christ is may be kept in Christ after the union, and the sameness and singleness of the hypostasis of God the Word incarnate may likewise be preserved unmultiplied. For the reciprocal indication and confession of the realities, as the heretics have it, works the obliteration of the subjects; since the truth does not at all endure to allow the proper appellations of the realities to be exchanged for one another. For this reason we confess Christ two natures, as I said, according to the principle of the difference of the natures from which he is, preserved in him even after the union, as being the same God and man after the union.

*That he who calls Christ God and man after the union, having chosen to believe rightly, of necessity confesses together with the names the natures after the union.*

For if Christ is God and man after the union, according to the common confession, clearly, having the realities, he makes the names true after the union. For realities are conceived before their own names; and there could never at any time be a name without the reality, which of itself provides the name with its imposition and receives in return its signification. But if Christ does not have in the union[^12] the realities of which the names are, he will be shown, on their account, to have the appellations bare, as lying with nothing, and empty of realities; and Christ will be, according to them, God and man in name only, and not in reality: than which what could be more godless? But if, refusing to be so mad, they should say that in Christ the appellations are not empty of realities after the union, then, just as they do not refuse to speak of the two appellations of Christ after the union, so let them also speak, after the union, of the two natures of Christ, from which and in which he subsists, of which the appellations also are — if in truth they faithfully confess that the things united are preserved after the union really, according to nature.

*On the expression "In two"; and on the one nature of the Word incarnate, a pious confession according to the Fathers; and a denial of the one composite nature.*

And again we believe the same to be in two natures without division, as in Godhead and manhood. For just as, saying that Christ is from two natures, we understand him to be from Godhead and manhood, as a whole from parts, so too, saying "in two natures" after…

## Notes
[^1]: T. The chunk opens mid-word: ]ρετον is the end of ἀπροαίρετον, "without choice", the first of the three marks of every composite nature that Maximus is listing (the same sentence returns whole at col. 528, πᾶσα φύσις σύνθετος ἀπροαίρετον ἔχει τὴν πρὸς ἄλληλα τῶν οἰκείων μερῶν κατὰ τὴν σύνθεσιν σύνοδον). The other two marks follow: parts contemporaneous, and made for the completion of the universe.
[^2]: P. Severus, patriarch of Antioch 512–518 (d. 538), the chief theologian of the opponents of Chalcedon; "one composite nature" is his formula, and the target of Letters 12–15. "Acropolis" is Maximus's mockery: the citadel of the system.
[^3]: T. γενική / μοναδική = generic / singular (the glossary's μοναδικός, never "monadic"): a nature is either common to many individuals or the nature of one. The dilemma governs the rest of the page.
[^4]: T. τὸ σύνολον, adverbial, "at all"; the Latin agrees (*nullam prorsus naturam*).
[^5]: T. Combefis's note on the passage praises Maximus as almost alone among the Fathers in treating the phoenix openly as a fable, where Clement of Rome (*1 Clem.* 25) and others had used it as a true example.
[^6]: S. Gen 7:1–3 (LXX), quoted nearly verbatim; "to keep seed alive" is the LXX's διαθρέψαι σπέρμα (see the OCR table).
[^7]: T. The plate prints παο᾿ for παρ᾿ (a broken sort, confirmed at the plate check); read παρ᾿ αὐτῶν, "by them".
[^8]: T. ἰδική = particular, the partner of κοινή (common); the Latin doubles it, *privatam ac propriam*.
[^9]: S. Cf. Prov 4:23, πάσῃ φυλακῇ τήρει σὴν καρδίαν.
[^10]: P. Apollinarius of Laodicea (d. c. 390), who taught a Word in place of the human mind, and so a mixture into "one nature"; Nestorius, bishop of Constantinople 428–431, deposed at Ephesus for dividing Christ into two. "Jewish" is Maximus's polemical epithet: a Christ who is a mere man, as the Jews hold.
[^11]: T. χρησώμεθα τῷ λόγῳ: λόγος here is utterance (the Latin *oratione utamur*), set against "the expressions (φωναί) of the perverse".
[^12]: T. κατὰ τὴν ἕνωσιν, "in the union", where the surrounding sentences say μετὰ τὴν ἕνωσιν, "after the union"; the Latin has *post unionem*. Both construe; translated as printed.
