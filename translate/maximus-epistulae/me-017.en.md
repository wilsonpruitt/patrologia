title: Letter 13, to Peter the illustris, 3
---
[525] …the union, we believe him to be in Godhead and manhood, as a whole in parts. Now the parts of Christ are his Godhead and his manhood, from which and in which he subsists. And again we have been taught to believe, to say and to proclaim one nature of God the Word incarnate, in flesh having an intellectual and rational soul, according to the holy Cyril;[^1] understanding, by the saying "incarnate", that the essence of our nature has been brought in. And again, of one and the same Christ both the miracles and the passions; and of the same the two births, the one before the ages, bodilessly, from the Father, and the one in time, bodily, from a Mother and Virgin, accomplished for our sake. Therefore above all, and in truth, we confess her properly Theotokos,[^2] as having given birth to God the Word, begotten of the Father before all the ages, incarnate from her in the last times.

But we altogether refuse to say that Christ is one composite nature: not only for the reasons stated, but because no one at all of the approved Fathers has appeared up to now to have spoken this expression, and as being manifestly the offspring of Apollinarius the heretic and of his disciples; whose error Severus having accepted, he fittingly also decks himself out in their expressions. For Christ is not a natural monad by composition,[^3] as Apollinarius and Severus have it, so long as he keeps preserved, even after the union, the unconfused otherness of the natures from which he is. Nor yet a hypostatic dyad, as Nestorius has it, so long as the things that came together do not subsist by themselves and wholly distinguished from one another; and so long as God the Word incarnate is one even after the incarnation, and assumed the flesh united to him, rationally and intellectually ensouled, which had not pre-subsisted by the proper principle of its nature; but one composite hypostasis, according to the Fathers, according to which the same is both wholly God, and one of the holy and all-praised Trinity, with the manhood, because of the Godhead; and the same wholly man, and one of men, with the Godhead, because of the manhood.

*On the composite hypostasis, a more natural treatment; and an exact demonstration that those who confess Christ one composite hypostasis do not fall into the same absurdities as those who call him one composite nature.*

But if anyone should think that, because every composite hypostasis too possesses its parts contemporaneous with one another, the same absurdity is concluded also for those who say that Christ is one composite hypostasis, such a one, by my account, does not suppose rightly. For not simply every composite hypostasis has its parts contemporaneous with one another in coming-to-be, but that which ranks, clearly, under a composite nature, and has such a species predicated of it; which, having [528] the aggregate of the idioms about it interwoven with the common and universal of the essence or nature predicated of it, is a nature, that is, [?] with idioms.[^4] If, then, the nature under which it ranks, and the species predicated, is composite, it too must altogether be composite, and possess its parts contemporaneous with one another, because of the essence, that is, the nature, predicated of it, which so exists as to be the same as it in species, apart from its distinguishing marks that mark it off. For just as, defining the hypostasis spoken of simply, we say: Hypostasis is an essence with idioms; or, an essence comprehensive of the particulars, of all the idioms in its own individual;[^5] so, defining the hypostasis spoken of with a qualification, and not simply, we say: A composite hypostasis is a composite essence with idioms; or, a composite essence comprehensive of the particulars, of all the idioms in its own individual. For what is contemplated in common in the individuals under the same species characterizes primarily the generic of the essence or nature in the individuals under it. But the composite is common to all the individuals under the composite species; therefore the composite primarily characterizes a composite nature in the individuals under it, and not a hypostasis.

And again, besides, every definition of the individuals that rank under them is from the common and universal; and all the things under the generic naturally have from them the beginning of their own description, so that it may be clear what the thing defined properly and primarily is. But if this is true, as indeed it is, it is plain that he who has called composite a hypostasis referred to a nature has declared, together with the individual of the hypostasis, what inheres generically in the common of the essence, with the idioms that characterize it specifically. So that he who has said that a hypostasis ranking under some nature is composite has said nothing other than a composite nature, that is, essence, with idioms. If, then, as has been shown, every composite nature has without choice the concourse of its own parts with one another in the composition, and possesses its parts contemporaneous with one another, and has come to be for the completion of the totality contemplated in the things that are, which makes up the universe species by species; then clearly every hypostasis too, according to what is natural, has a constitution unto being the same as, and invariant with, its own genus and species. Let them show, then, that Christ's composite hypostasis ranks under a composite nature, and let them conclude that it possesses its parts contemporaneous, and let them justly lay the same blame against us. But so long as they cannot do this, they are setting the props of their own doctrines upon sand.[^6] For he who ignorantly forces Christ under these aforesaid absurdities, because he both is a composite hypostasis and is piously confessed so by us, has wandered far from the road that leads to the truth — or rather, to speak more properly, from the truth itself. For this great and venerable mystery of Christ [529] neither has, as an individual, some universal and generic nature predicated of it as a species; nor indeed is it itself a genus or a species predicated of the individuals naturally under it, so as to be able to be brought under any of the rules stated; inasmuch as it has the concurrence of God the Word with the flesh in the composition not equal and like to that of composite things in their concourse with one another.

*That only the union of the Word with the flesh has come about by assumption,[^7] since he pre-existed and willed to be emptied unto union with flesh.*

For in the case of all things universally that are composite according to nature, it is not the concourse of this with that by assumption that makes the constitution of this species, as of a whole from parts; but the simultaneous coming-to-be of the parts from non-being into being, by their concourse with one another at the same time as their being, makes the composition of the whole. But the mystery of our Lord and God and Saviour Jesus Christ did not come about in this mode; but being God by nature, and by nature Son of God, simple and bodiless, and co-eternal with the Father, and Creator of all the ages, willing, by a wish of love for man, he became man by assumption of flesh; and for our sake, being simple according to nature and bodiless, he became from us, by way of economy, composite according to hypostasis and embodied, as the holy and great Dionysius the Areopagite says, under divine inspiration, in the first chapter of his treatise *On the Divine Names*, going through the matter of the divine incarnation thus: « And man-loving pre-eminently, in that it has wholly shared, in truth, in what is ours, in one of its hypostases, calling back to itself and raising up the human extremity, from which [ineffably] the simple Jesus was composed; and the eternal has taken on extension in time; and he has come to be within our nature who superessentially transcends every order according to nature. »[^8]

If, then, not according to the law and order of the nature of composite things, but by another ordinance, contrary to the nature of composite things — for by assumption the Word of God was inexpressibly composed with the flesh, and not by taking being in a coming-to-be together with the flesh unto composition, for the completion of some whole according to species, as the word of truth piercingly cries out to all — then in vain do the ignorant lawlessly attempt to bring under the ordinances that are under nature the composition that goes beyond every definition and principle of nature. For this reason he who says that the becoming man came about by assumption of flesh both preserves the existence of God the Word before the ages, and piously confesses his voluntary and self-chosen incarnation in time; and keeps unconfused, even after [532] the union, the difference between the Word himself who assumed and the flesh assumed. But he who does not confess that the Word of God became man without change by assumption of flesh will be able in no mode truly to confess any of the things stated. For how, if the Word did not pre-exist without beginning, did he by volition assume flesh different according to essence? — in whose case above all, as I think, the union of what is of another nature is properly said to have come about by assumption; since he alone, and alone in this manner, impassibly and truly assumed what is of another essence, and kept himself unchangeable and unmultiplied in every principle and mode, and what was assumed unalterable: which is impossible to come about in the case of originated nature, in which every composition naturally comes about at the same time as the coming-to-be of the parts, for the completion of some whole according to species, by their simultaneous concurrence with one another, keeping equal, throughout the whole, the proportion of the parts to one another; as is the case with us, and with whatever things have had it as their lot to have a composite nature according to species. For in us the soul has the natural powers of the body proportioned to its own operations, the body being receptive according to nature because of the soul's simultaneous coming-to-be into being together with the flesh. But the Word of God, having in no principle or mode the powers of the nature assumed by him proportioned to his own operations according to nature — for what is above nature is not measured by nature, nor is there anything at all among the things that are receptive of him according to nature — therefore, alone of all, by assumption of flesh rationally and intellectually ensouled, willing, he became man ineffably, as being and pre-being and able for all things, having innovated the natures by the mode above nature, that he might save man.

This faith I have learned, and in it I have been taught, both by our holy and blessed Fathers who have gone before, and by those now living who have been entrusted with the helm of the catholic holy Church of God and steer her rightly to the harbour of the divine will; with which, by their supplications, I shall also depart, leaving the present life, offering to God, in place of every merit, this confession, undefiled and unstained, and higher than every heretical storm. For I cannot boast in a life made splendid with works of righteousness, having become throughout all my life here a self-chosen transgressor of the divine laws.

These things, then, I have written to you, as far as was possible, in summary, my blessed lords, so that you may know, if there is anything useful in what has been written, how you may at least in some measure send away the wolves clothed in the fleece,[^9] who terrify with their meaningless barking the divine and gentle sheep of Christ's holy flock, and may not be swept away by the falsehood that maliciously feigns truth. But the sayings of the Fathers that plead for the Church's faith I have not yet been able to put into the letter, because of [533] the great want of books with me. For you have there my blessed master, father and teacher, the lord abba Sophronius,[^10] truly sober-minded, and a wise advocate of the truth, and an unconquered champion of the divine doctrines, able in deed and word to contend against every heresy, and, with all his other good things, rich in a multitude of divine books, and eagerly enriching those who wish to learn divine things. Resorting to him, then, you will receive, I know well, all the right and unstumbling knowledge of the divine and saving doctrines. Farewell.

## Notes
[^1]: S. Cyril of Alexandria's formula μία φύσις τοῦ Θεοῦ Λόγου σεσαρκωμένη; the gloss that "incarnate" brings in the essence of our nature is Cyril's own defence of it, cf. Cyril, *Ep.* 46 (second to Succensus).
[^2]: T. Θεοτόκος, "she who gave birth to God", kept as Theotokos; the Latin *Dei Genitricem*.
[^3]: T. φυσικὴ μονάς / ὑποστατικὴ δυάς: a "natural monad" (one by nature, the Apollinarian–Severan error) against a "hypostatic dyad" (two by hypostasis, the Nestorian). Lower case: not the Trinity's Monad.
[^4]: T. The Greek reads φύσις ἐστὶν, ἤγουν μετὰ ἰδιωμάτων, "is a nature, that is, with idioms"; the Latin has *natura, id est, substantia cum proprietatibus*, and Maximus's own definitions a few lines on run οὐσία τις μετὰ ἰδιωμάτων. A noun (οὐσία?) may have dropped after ἤγουν; not supplied.
[^5]: S. The patristic definition of hypostasis as essence with idioms, cf. Basil, *Ep.* 214.4 (the common with the proper); the second form ("comprehensive of all the idioms in its own individual") is the handbook definition current in the sixth- and seventh-century florilegia.
[^6]: S. Cf. Matt 7:26.
[^7]: T. πρόσληψις = assumption (προσλαμβάνειν = assume; τὸ προσληφθέν = what was assumed): the Word's taking-to-himself of the flesh, against the simultaneous coming-to-be of the parts in every natural composite.
[^8]: S. Dionysius the Areopagite, *On the Divine Names* 1.4 (PG 3.592A). The subject of the feminine participles is the Thearchy (the Godhead), "it"; [ἀρρήτως], "ineffably", is bracketed as Migne prints it.
[^9]: S. Cf. Matt 7:15.
[^10]: P. Sophronius, the monk who would become patriarch of Jerusalem (634–638), Maximus's master in the African monasteries and the first opponent of the one-operation formula. σώφρονα plays on his name ("sober-minded" / Sophronius).
