title: Letter 15, to Cosmas, deacon of Alexandria, 1
---
[544] LETTER 15

*Of the same, on common and proper, that is, essence and hypostasis, to Cosmas, the most God-beloved deacon of Alexandria.*[^1]

To the most God-beloved lord Cosmas, deacon, Maximus the lowly, greeting.

Since much discussion has been set in motion among us concerning the divine incarnation, and thou, touching upon the very subtle doctrines of piety themselves and taking occasion from them, didst ask my lowliness to say, man of God, what the common and universal is, and what the particular and proper, so that from this the whole account of the union might become clear to us: I shall say nothing at all of my own; but what I was taught by the Fathers, that I state, altering nothing of their teaching on these matters.

[545] The common and universal, then, that is the generic, according to the Fathers, is the essence and the nature; for they say that these are the same as each other. The proper and particular is the hypostasis and the person; for according to them these are the same as each other. And the great Basil shows this, writing as follows to Terentius:[^2] «But if we too must say briefly what seems right to us, we shall say this: that the ratio which the common has to the proper, this the essence has to the hypostasis. For each of us both partakes of being by the common principle of the essence, and by the idioms about him is such a one or such a one.» And again the same, going through such matters with Amphilochius, says this:[^3] «Essence and hypostasis have this difference, which the common has toward what is particular to each. For example, as the living being has toward such a man.» And after other things: «But those who say that essence and hypostasis are the same are compelled to confess only different persons.» And again, teaching a certain canoness,[^4] he wrote, interpreting the sense of the homoousion: «And this word also sets right the evil of Sabellius. For it removes the identity of the hypostasis, and introduces the perfect notion of the persons; for a thing is not itself consubstantial with itself, but one thing with another. So that it stands well and piously, both marking off the property of the hypostases and presenting the invariance of the nature.» And again, in the second to the Neocaesareans, he teaches, saying:[^5] «For one must also know that, as he who does not confess the common of the essence falls into polytheism, so he who does not grant the proper things of the hypostases is carried away into Judaism.» And again, in what he made Eustathius the Armenian sign,[^6] he is seen writing expressly: «It is fitting, then, that they confess plainly that they believe according to the words set forth by our Fathers at Nicaea, and according to the sense soundly conveyed by the words.[^7] For there are some who even in this faith falsify the word of truth, and drag the meaning of the words in it toward their own wish. Seeing that Marcellus too, being impious toward the hypostasis of our Lord Jesus Christ and expounding him as a bare word, dared to pretend that he had taken his starting-points from there, expounding badly the sense of the homoousion. And some of those from the impiety of the Libyan Sabellius, supposing hypostasis and essence to be the same, draw from there the occasions for the construction of their own blasphemy, from its being written in the creed: *But if anyone says that the Son of God is from another essence or hypostasis, him the holy and catholic Church anathematizes.*[^8] For they did not say that essence and hypostasis are the same. For if the words signified one and the same meaning, what need was there of each? But it is clear that, since some denied the "from the [548] essence", and others said "from some other hypostasis", they thus forbade both as alien to the ecclesiastical mind. Since, where they made their own mind plain, they said that the Son is from the essence of the Father, no longer adding "from the hypostasis". So that the former is set down for the destruction of the wicked mind; the latter holds the manifestation of the saving doctrine.»

And in concord with this Gregory too, who is named from theology, is seen saying this in the first theological oration:[^9] «When I say the mean, I mean the truth; to which alone it is well to look, refusing both the bad contraction and the more absurd division; so that neither, the account being contracted into one hypostasis for fear of polytheism, should we be left with bare names, supposing the same one to be Father and Son and Holy Spirit.» And again, after a little: «Since one must both keep the one God and confess the three hypostases, that is three persons, and each with its property. And the one God would be kept, as my account has it, the Son and the Spirit being referred to one cause, not compounded nor blended together; and the three hypostases, no blending or dissolution or confusion being conceived; lest the whole be destroyed by those things by which the One is extolled more than is fitting. And in respect of the one and selfsame — that I may so name it — motion and purpose of the Godhead, and the identity of the essence.»[^10] And again in the Farewell oration:[^11] «We believe in Father and Son and Holy Spirit, consubstantial and of one glory, in whom baptism too has its perfecting[^12] — thou knowest, who hast been initiated — being a denial of godlessness and a confession of Godhead; and so we are made perfect, recognizing the One in the essence and in the indivisibility of the worship, and the Three in the hypostases, that is persons.» And indeed in the oration on the Lights too the same says this:[^13] «When I say God, be ye shone about by one light and by three: three according to the properties, that is hypostases, if anyone likes so to call them, or persons — for we shall not fight about the names, so long as the syllables bear toward the same notion — and one according to the principle of the essence, that is of the Godhead.» So great a concord of the divine doctrines did our God-proclaiming Fathers Gregory and Basil show there to be, saying expressly that the nature is the same as the essence, as common and universal, and the person the same as the hypostasis, as specific and particular; [549] in no way commixing the meaning of the terms through their conversion into one another and confusion. For, being operated upon by one and the same Spirit, they delivered to the peoples the right confession of the faith. And in concord with these thou wilt find all those also who by the grace of the Spirit were entrusted with the helm of the Church, upholding the right account of the pious faith and in no way at all turning aside from this meaning.

*A more natural demonstration, presenting that none of the things that are is the same as another in essence and hypostasis; but those that are the same in essence are other in their hypostases, and those that are the same in hypostasis are certainly other in essence.*

If essence and nature are the same, and person and hypostasis the same, it is clear that things connatural and consubstantial with one another are certainly of other hypostases from one another.[^14] For according to both — I mean the nature and the hypostasis — nothing among the things that are is the same as another. Because things united with one another according to one and the same nature, that is essence — that is, things being of the same essence and nature — could never be united according to one and the same hypostasis, that is person; that is, they will not be able to have one person and one hypostasis; and things united according to one and the same hypostasis, that is person, could never be conjoined according to one and the same essence, that is nature; that is, they could never appear having become of one and the same essence and nature. But things united according to one and the same nature, that is essence — that is, things being of one and the same essence and nature — are distinguished from one another by their hypostases, that is persons, as is the case with angels and men and all the creatures contemplated in species and genus. For angel is distinguished from angel, and man from man, and ox from ox, and dog from dog, according to the hypostasis, but not according to the nature and the essence.

And the argument will dare to say the greater thing: that not even in the very first, beginningless and creative cause of the things that are do we contemplate the nature and the hypostasis as the same as each other; if indeed we know one essence and nature of Godhead, existing in three hypostases differing from one another by their properties, and three hypostases in one and the same essence and nature of the Godhead. For Monad in Trinity, and Trinity in Monad, is what is worshipped by us: Father and Son and Holy Spirit, one God; the Son not being Father, but what the Father is; the Spirit not being Son, but what the Son is. For all that the Father is, save unbegottenness, the Son is (for he is begotten); and all that the Son is, save generation, the Holy Spirit is (for it proceeds):[^15] unbegottenness and generation and [552] procession not cutting the one nature and power of the ineffable Godhead into three unequal or equal essences and natures, but characterizing the persons, that is the hypostases, in which, or which, the one Godhead is, that is essence and nature. And things united according to one and the same hypostasis, that is person — that is, being of one hypostasis and completive of one person — differ by the principle of the essence, that is nature; as is the case with the human soul and body, and with all things that have had their concourse[^16] with one another according to hypostasis. For these are not consubstantial with one another.

Things, then, united according to one and the same essence, that is nature — that is, being of one and the same essence and nature — are certainly consubstantial with one another, and of other hypostases: consubstantial, by the principle of the essential community contemplated in them invariantly in natural identity, according to which one is not more than another that which he is and is called, but all admit the one and same definition and principle of the essence; of other hypostases, by the principle of the personal otherness distinguishing them, according to which one is distinguished from another, not coinciding with one another in the characteristic idioms according to hypostasis, but each, by the aggregate of the idioms about him, bears the most specific principle of his own being according to hypostasis, according to which he does not admit communion with those connatural and consubstantial with him. But things united according to one and the same hypostasis, that is person — that is, being completive of one and the same hypostasis by union — are of one hypostasis with one another, and of other essence: of one hypostasis, by the principle of the personal undivided monad[^17] completed from them by union, according to which the idioms that divide each from the community according to essence proper to it are made, at their concourse with one another simultaneous with being, characteristic of the one hypostasis completed from them; according to which their identity toward one another is contemplated, admitting no difference whatsoever; as is the case with the human soul and body. For the idioms that mark off someone's body from the rest of bodies, and someone's soul from the rest of souls, running together by union, characterize and at the same time mark off from the rest of men the hypostasis completed from them — of Peter, say, or of Paul — but not the soul of Peter or Paul from its own body. For both, soul and body, are the same as each other by the principle of the one hypostasis completed from them by union; because neither of them subsisted by itself, separated from the other, before the composition from them toward the coming-to-be of a species. For at once the coming-to-be, at once the composition, at once also the completion of the species from them by composition. And of other essence, by the principle of the natural otherness toward one another; according to which they in no way admit [553] one another's definitions and principles according to essence; but each renders the principle of its own essence other and other, not coinciding with the principle of the other; according to which, preserving the essential difference of soul and body, we do not confuse the hypostasis completed from them, as though it received annihilation by the conversion and change of one into the other of the parts, that is natures, of which it was composed.

If, then, by the concourse of soul and body simultaneous toward the coming-to-be of a species, the particular man is by nature brought to completion — by the principle of the natural community of his own parts preserving his consubstantiality toward other men; by the principle of their property guarding toward the other men his being of another hypostasis; being united by the community according to nature of his own parts to the other men, toward whom he bears one and the same nature according to species; and being divided by the property of the parts according to hypostasis from the rest of men, toward whom he bears a person other and different according to hypostasis; by which principle he is distinguished from the other men, guarding the unity of his own personal undivided monad wholly without difference; and by which principle he is by nature united to the other men, preserving unconfused the difference of the essential otherness of his own parts — then clear to us from this, and having nothing riddling, has the account become of the incarnation, that is the becoming man, of the one of the holy and consubstantial and worshipful Trinity, God the Word.

*That Christ, united to the extremes by the communities according to essence of his own parts, kept the difference of the parts toward one another preserved; and by the properties of the parts showed the identity according to hypostasis, as of a whole, common to both.*

## Notes
[^1]: P. Cosmas, deacon of the church of Alexandria, known only from this letter; Maximus answers a question Cosmas put to him in conversation on the terms of the Chalcedonian confession. The ΙΕ΄ of the heading agrees with the table (Letter 15). Combefis's Latin title adds *seu persona* after *hypostasi*.
[^2]: S. Basil, Ep. 214.4, to Terentius the count. λόγος here is the "ratio" of a proportion (Latin *rationem*).
[^3]: S. Basil, Ep. 236.6, to Amphilochius; the next quotation («But those who say …») is from the same section.
[^4]: S. Basil, Ep. 52.3, "to the canonesses" (κανονικαῖς). Maximus has the singular, κανονικήν τινα, as does the Latin (*quamdam canonicam*).
[^5]: S. Cf. Basil, Ep. 210.5, to the notables of Neocaesarea (Basil wrote 207 and 210 to that church; this is the second).
[^6]: S. Basil, Ep. 125.1–2, the profession of faith Basil had Eustathius of Sebaste sign in 373; Sebaste lay in Armenia Prima, hence "the Armenian". The quotation runs to «… the saving doctrine.»
[^7]: T. ἐμφερομένην, "conveyed", as printed; Basil's text reads ἐμφαινομένην, "appearing". Combefis's doublet *repraesentant atque ingerunt* covers both; the printed word is kept.
[^8]: S. The anathema appended to the Nicene creed (325): τοὺς δὲ λέγοντας … ἐξ ἑτέρας ὑποστάσεως ἢ οὐσίας … ἀναθεματίζει ἡ καθολικὴ … ἐκκλησία.
[^9]: S. Gregory Nazianzen, Or. 20.6 (*On dogma and the installation of bishops*), which Maximus calls "the first theological"; Combefis's margin wonders at the title. The second quotation is Or. 20.7.
[^10]: T. The last clause («And in respect of the one and selfsame … identity of the essence») stands here, after «more than is fitting», in the Greek as printed. In Gregory's text and in Combefis's Latin (*et si unam atque eamdem divinitatis … motionem et voluntatem, essentiaeque identitatem intelligamus*) it belongs earlier, with «one God … not compounded nor blended together», before «and the three hypostases». Translated in the order printed; logged.
[^11]: S. Gregory Nazianzen, Or. 42.15 (the farewell oration at Constantinople, 381).
[^12]: T. Gregory's text and Combefis's Latin have one more phrase here: «both of names and of realities» (*tam in nominibus quam in rebus*). Plate-checked 2026-11-06: Migne's quotation prints the shorter text, without these words; Gregory's own text has them. Not translated from the Latin. The Latin also ends with *ut nonnullis placet* ("as some like to say") where the Greek has only «that is persons».
[^13]: S. Gregory Nazianzen, Or. 39.11 (*On the Holy Lights*).
[^14]: T. ἑτεροϋπόστατος = "of other hypostasis" (pl. "of other hypostases"), ὁμοϋπόστατος = "of one hypostasis", ἑτεροούσιος = "of other essence", against ὁμοούσιος = consubstantial; ὁμοφυής = connatural. They are the four terms the rest of the letter turns on.
[^15]: S. Cf. Gregory Nazianzen, Or. 41.9 (πάντα ὅσα ὁ Πατήρ, τοῦ Υἱοῦ, πλὴν τῆς ἀγεννησίας …) and Or. 31.9.
[^16]: T. σύνοδος here and through the letter is the "concourse" or coming-together of the parts, not a council. The Latin varies (*coeunt*, *concursus*, *conjunctio*).
[^17]: T. μονάς here is the "monad" (lower case) of the single person, the unit the parts complete. Maximus uses the same word as for the Trinity's Monad above, and that is the point of the comparison.
[^18]: T. Θεοτόκος, "Theotokos", she who gave birth to God, the title confirmed at Ephesus (431); the Latin has *Dei Genitrice*.
