For the community according to essence of each of the parts with the extremes, in the unity of the one hypostasis, preserving unconfused the difference of the nature of each, did not allow the pious to recognize one nature of both from the union; lest there come about a total passing-away of the parts according to essence, or, by composition, the coming-to-be of one nature of the whole from them, having no way to guard the natural kinship of the extremes with the parts, not preserving the difference according to essence of the parts of which it was composed toward one another after the union; but it presented to be seen one hypostasis, composite from the union, maintained in the existence according to nature of the parts of which it was composed. And the property according to hypostasis of each of the parts, as toward the composition of some whole, Christ, being conceived together with the common according to essence, effected a common mark of the parts, that which characterizes the one hypostasis completed from them. For we say that the one hypostasis of Christ, completed from them by the concourse by natural union, that is true and real, has become common to flesh and Godhead in the ineffable union. And I say common, as the one and same most specific hypostasis of the parts, declared from the union; or rather, as being the one and same hypostasis of the Word, now and before: but before, without cause, simple and incomposite; and afterwards, for a cause, by assumption of flesh intellectually ensouled, become without change truly composite; according to which, marked off from the extremes — Father, I mean, and Mother — he is united to himself, having no difference whatsoever; lest the coming-to-be of a difference of the parts according to it become a total passing-away of the identity of the parts according to hypostasis, dissolving the union according to hypostasis into a personal dyad; [557] having no way to show the personal identity of the parts toward one another preserved, being partitioned by the difference according to hypostasis into a dyad of persons.

*That the things of the parts common according to hypostasis marked off the whole, that is Christ, from the extremes; but the things of the parts common according to essence with the extremes joined Christ, as a whole, to the extremes essentially.*

For it was not by the idioms by which the flesh was marked off from the rest of men that it kept its difference from the Word; nor again would it be by the idioms by which it differed from the Word that it was also marked off from us. But by those by which it was marked off from us, by these it preserved its union according to hypostasis with the Word, that is its identity; and by those by which it was united to us naturally, by these, evidently, it had its essential difference from the Word preserved. Just as the Word too, by the idioms by which he was marked off from the common of the Godhead as Son and Word, by these preserved his union according to hypostasis with the flesh, that is his identity; and by those by which as God he kept the natural difference from the flesh, by these, being united according to essence to the Father and the Spirit, he interchanged the difference and the identity toward himself and the extremes: being united according to nature to the extremes by the communities of the parts, and distinguished according to hypostasis from the extremes by the properties of the same, namely the parts; in the identity according to hypostasis of his own parts showing the difference of the extremes toward himself preserved; and in the otherness according to essence of the parts bearing the natural identity of the extremes with the parts. And if he was united to the extremes by the communities of the parts, and marked off from the extremes by the properties of the parts, it is clear that by those by which he was united to the extremes he had the natural difference of the parts toward one another preserved; and by those by which he was distinguished from the extremes he had the identity according to hypostasis of the parts shining through.

So then Christ had, of each of the parts of which he was composed, both the common and the specific: the common, in the principle of the identity according to essence of the extremes with his own parts, according to which he had the difference of the parts toward one another preserved even after the union; and the proper, in the principle of the identity according to hypostasis of the parts, according to which he was marked off from the extremes, preserving unconfused his distinction from them. For the true union according to hypostasis, of each of the parts coming together with one another into the composition of some whole, at their concourse at once toward the coming-to-be of some whole, taking along with the common the idiom that marks off each from the community according to essence, shows it enhypostatic, but not a hypostasis;[^2] because it did not subsist by itself separately, marked off from those of the same genus, or from that which subsists together with it by union toward the coming-to-be of some whole; which is proper to hypostasis. For hypostasis is that which subsists separately by itself; since they say that hypostasis is essence with idioms, differing in number from those of the same genus; and enhypostatic, that which by itself [560] [?][^3]

*That Christ, being perfect God, and the same perfect man, had the common and the proper of the things which he was, by which he made toward himself the union and distinction of the extremes: by the union of the extremes toward himself, assuring that the two natures of which he was composed are preserved in himself; and by the distinction of the extremes toward himself, presenting the singleness of his own hypostasis.*

For if the Word, being God by nature, by assumption of flesh intellectually ensouled became man without change, it is clear that, being one from both, and through one assuring himself in truth to be both, the same had of both the common and the proper, by which he made the union and distinction toward the extremes: by the natural difference toward one another of his own parts being united according to essence to the extremes; and by the identity of the same, namely the parts, according to hypostasis, being distinguished from the extremes. And if the same had difference and identity, it is clear that the same was one and two: one, by the principle of his own undivided [561] monad according to hypostasis; two, by the principle of the otherness according to essence of his own parts toward one another even after the union. For difference is a principle by which the otherness of the things signified toward one another is by nature preserved, and indicative of the how-it-is; and identity is an invariance by which the principle of the thing signified possesses the wholly singular, being recognized by no mode of difference.[^4]

*A concise treatment of difference, by which it is shown that the quantity according to essence of the things united is preserved.*

If, then, the difference according to essence toward one another of the parts of which he was composed is preserved in Christ after the union, there is a clear demonstration for the pious that the quantity according to essence of the things united has remained undiminished after the union, preserving the definition and principle of the essence of each part non-coinciding toward the other in the whole composed from them by composition. And if also the hypostatic identity of the whole toward its own parts — or rather, to speak more clearly, of the parts toward one another according to the composition of a whole from them — is preserved, it is clear that in no way do the parts, according to the one hypostasis completed from them, differ at all toward one another. And thus there is no account able to show either the difference according to essence being confused, or the singleness according to hypostasis being divided.

*A broader and more natural demonstration concerning difference and quantity, and the number that indicates them.*

So then, if we speak of difference in Christ after the union, and with every difference a quantity is by nature of necessity introduced together, of which we recognize number to be indicative, it is fitting that, taking up number for the sole indication of the difference of the things differing after the union, we in no way divide by the number the things signified, but indicate the existence of the things united as preserved; because every number is by nature indicative of quantity only, but not divisive. For that which neither acts nor is acted upon, how could it be master of any relation whatsoever? Since acting belongs to essence alone, as being acted upon belongs to accident, that is quality; from which, and out of which, those trained in such arguments say that the relations proceed. Or rather, as we examine it more exactly, not even of these — essence, I mean, and accident — are the relations the work, but of the cause and power that makes all things, and has placed in each of the things that have come to be a principle constitutive of its being, and presentative of its natural kinship and alienation toward one another. And if the relations properly do not have essence as their efficient cause, much less would they have number; which, insofar as it is not essence, cannot act at all; and insofar as it is not accident, that is quality, is not by nature acted upon. And if number is by nature neither to act nor to be acted upon, then it is a sign indicative of quantity, making no [564] reflection of the relation in the quantity indicated; because it is not a genus able to be predicated of others, but a sign, as I said, reflective of the genus that is borne predicatively of others.

And if number has neither acting nor being acted upon, it is clear that, insofar as it does not act, it is not indicative of any relation whatsoever, that is of union or division — for this, as I said, belongs to another principle — and insofar as it is not by nature acted upon, it is not divided. For in itself every number is undivided, not admitting movement by way of alteration, which is proper to quality; nor contraction and expansion, which is proper to essence; nor indeed increase and decrease, which is proper to quantity. And if it is indicative of quantity, but not of relation — for number is significative of quantity simply so called, but not of quantity called "in a certain way" — it is clear that number is by nature to make neither division nor indeed union, but only the indication of quantity, in whatever way we may conceive this to exist. For acting belongs, as I said, to essence, and being acted upon to accident, that is quality; of which number is neither. For if it were essence, it would also be self-subsistent, not needing another for its constitution; and if it were accident, that is quality, either it would make the difference most properly so called, and, combined with the genus, would assemble the definition of the subject, and would furnish the indication of a species, not of a quantity; or, conjoined with the species, it would introduce the difference commonly so called, and would make the distinction of one from another among the individuals brought under the same species; or it would make the difference properly so called in the aggregate of all the idioms in the individual;[^5] and it would show what is signified through it to be not only other from other, but also of another kind. And if nobody at all takes in number along with the definitions of things, number is neither essence nor quality. And if number is none of these, it is clear that it is by nature neither to act nor to be acted upon; and if number has not acting and being acted upon, it fittingly neither unites at all, nor divides as acting; nor indeed is it divided at all as being acted upon; but, making the indication of the quantity of things alone, it does not touch their relation.

And if number is indicative of quantity alone, but not of any relation whatsoever conceived in respect of it, number is rather a word, so to speak, and a name yoked to a quantity. So then he who speaks of difference in Christ after the union has also wholly conceived, together with the difference, a quantity of the things differing. For what is wholly simple is also not receptive of difference; because it is also in every way the same with itself and singular, and whole, and wholly without relation, as not having the genus in quantity predicated of it; and for this reason it is wholly not numbered.
