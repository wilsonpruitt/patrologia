title: Letters 18–19
---
[584] LETTER 18

*Of the same, in the person of George, the all-praised prefect of Africa, to the nuns who had departed from the catholic Church in Alexandria.*[^1]

I, indeed, thought that you had truly been well established,[^2] and had the footings of the soul immovable from the right and pious confession and hope that accord with the blameless and unstumbling faith of Christ, reverencing the grace of God that called you and united you to the living and sound and blameless [585] body of the holy catholic and apostolic Church; the grace that strengthened you when you were already wasting away with the consumptive sickness of error and ignorance, and made you a serviceable member of the common body of the Church; in which the pious and right and true and saving word of the apostolic faith is preached in its flower, bringing within itself the whole region under heaven, and ever bringing and adding what is lacking to what has already been gathered, and showing that all, from the ends of the earth to its ends, have one soul and one tongue in the spirit, according to the concord and unison of the faith; the faith which the Word of God, the Creator of men, having become man ineffably from men, for men, after the manner of men, truly, by the assumption of flesh having a rational and intellectual soul, and not by a change of the Godhead — without sin, that is — taught us men to confess and to embrace: denying none at all of his natures after the union, that is, after the becoming man; neither the divine, according to which he both is and abides ever God, through himself, and consubstantial with the Father; nor the human, according to which the same truly became man for us, as loving man, and is consubstantial with us. For neither was he changed from what he was and is and will be for ever, God according to nature; nor indeed did he transmute the nature of the ensouled flesh — of what he became, man, by an unsundered union — being one and the same, God and man: not being God and man in name and appellation only, but in fact and in truth both being properly God and man, and truly so named; having the one and singular from the union according to hypostasis, but not according to nature, as the Fathers teach, saying: «For even if the both-together is one, yet not by nature, by the concourse»;[^3] by which concourse, by way of economy, for us, he, as good,[^4] consenting to become composite, keeps unimpaired, even after the incarnation, the simplicity according to nature by which he is of one nature with the Father and abides consubstantial. For Godhead and manhood are not the same. For even if the Word of God has become man, yet it is by the assumption of flesh intellectually [?][^5] united to him according to hypostasis without sundering that he has become man. Therefore the same is properly God and man, as being that and having become this; and being this and that without deficiency: neither confused by the extreme singleness of the hypostasis, according to which the principle of the difference is altogether absent; nor divided by the pure otherness of the natures of which he is constituted, according to which the principle of the difference has in no way been abolished because of the union.

Since, then, the difference of the natures from which Christ is is preserved without confusion even after the union, there is, I think, no account at all able to show that it is just that the quantity of the things that differ naturally and are preserved in Christ by an unsundered union [588] should not be confessed; because every difference brings in with itself, altogether, a quantity of certain things that differ from one another in some respect. For no one, however resourceful he be and most inventive, will ever be able to say that there is difference without quantity, if he thinks rightly and takes even a little account of truth; knowing clearly that every difference is either of the otherness of certain things according to essence, or of the unlikeness of certain things according to quality and property; a difference which the quantity of the subject realities, whether they are in union or apart from it, altogether produces. For in things in which one and the same principle is not contemplated in every way, there is a reflection of quantity, to which difference is proper. If, then, in Christ it follows, by reason, upon the union to speak of the singleness in every way according to hypostasis, as the Fathers teach; and it is consequent and fitting to speak of the quantity with the difference of the natures from which Christ is, preserved in Christ without confusion and without division even after the union: it is plain that, just as he who does not say that Christ, because of the hypostatic union, is one incarnate nature of God the Word — rightly understood according to the interpretation of our most holy Father and teacher Cyril — does not believe that the union has come about; so also he who does not confess the two natures after the union, from which Christ is, cannot say that the difference is preserved. For the difference after the union is of the natural otherness of the things from which Christ is, and not of an unlikeness according to quality alone, apart from the natures subject to the qualities — without which it is among the impossibilities that quality should exist by itself; lest we bring in a difference that is bare, or rather, to speak more properly, false and without existence.

Persuaded that you held this confession of the faith exactly according to the Fathers, I did not shrink from writing, time and again, to all everywhere — to patriarchs and bishops and rulers, and to our most pious and all-gentle emperors themselves — for thanksgiving to God, making manifest to all your genuine conversion to Christ, and your faith. And I never thought, as I said, that you would so quickly be removed from your saving calling.[^6] But since, because of my sins, the evil one, who from the beginning has been dragging the race of men down by deceit into ruin and destruction, has prevailed to persuade you too to prefer the lie to the truth, and, for the sake of an evil habit, to gratify, more than God and the goods laid up for the saints, deceiving men who have been taught only to bait toward death; and has been able to turn you back, like dogs — to speak with the prophet — to your own vomit:[^7] I charge you, if indeed you are so incurably disposed, and have resolved to turn away from the grace of Christ to the end, for the present to give the [589] gifts made to you by me to Theopemptus, my man, sent by me for this alone, with a written order, so that you may have no doubt about this. And again, whatever seems good to our most pious and Christ-loving and all-gentle emperors and to the most holy patriarchs, when they learn all your affairs through me — who, with God, am to be in the presence of their serenity — concerning your apostasy and so great wickedness and ingratitude, I shall judge it well that this be done. For I will not pass over in silence so great an evil. But if, taking to mind the dread tribunal of Christ, before which, in the presence of angels and archangels and of the whole people of the hosts of heaven, and of all human nature, my suit against you on this matter will be brought, you repent and return to God, who calls you again in grace, then I wish you to have the very foundation[^8] already given you by me; and I shall not cease, as long as I am granted, by way of economy, to live this present and temporal life, devising ways toward your more abundant support; and I would not refuse to give myself as a ransom for your souls,[^9] if occasion should of necessity call for this to be done. And I will give it readily, God calling me to this through grace; if only you restore yourselves again to Christ God, and by your return heal well the cut and the separation from his body that has befallen you.

LETTER 19

*Of the same, to Pyrrhus, the most holy presbyter and abbot.*[^10]

Having read thy holy letter, God-honoured Father, I recognized thy mind as wholly imbued by the whole Spirit; and I discerned it to have gone out from natural intelligence by as much as I perceived it to have laid hold of the divine wisdom that is above nature. For I seemed, as it were, to see thy God-wise mind itself present through the letter,[^11] flashing with the pure rays of the Spirit alone, by which he who is counted worthy to be [592] adopted as a son by the Father of all is wont to be transformed toward the more divine; and redeeming me from ignorance of the divine doctrines by as much as I had formerly seemed to hold fast to knowledge of them. Therefore, marvelling at thy sagacity in all things, I cried out and uttered only that word which of old a woman cried to the Lord, struck by the power of his words: Blessed is the womb that bore thee, and the breasts that thou didst suck.[^12] And I call blessed not only her who bore thee, precious Father, as counted worthy to become the mother of such a one, but also thy understanding, which ever conceives, and is pregnant with, and gives birth to the pious word, as having been shown by grace a womb able to contain the Word who is above nature, through the appropriation of thy inclination to the Word himself; and indeed thy firm habits as well,[^13] issued from thy heart, by which, like breasts, thou nursest the Word in practice and in contemplation, as he grows along with the supply of pious concepts and manners, and — to speak paradoxically — makes his own growth the deification of the mind that nurses him. For what else had I to utter to thee, precious Father, beholding thee become the begetter of such words? Through which, as from a mountain — the height of knowledge — thou hast made known the decree[^14] on the divine doctrines, brought and issued like God-engraved tablets through the new Mediator and great Moses of our day, the priest of priests and head of the divine priesthood throughout the whole inhabited world; which the holy and alone-worshipped Trinity dictated as through an instrument, the said high priest, and by which it bound the Churches more firmly to concord, so that they set at nought the innovation concerning the faith that seemed to the many to have taken place in the city of Alexander.[^15]

For the decree that was given preserved entire that conception according to which our God-bearing Fathers handed down to the holy Churches to believe, saying that God the Word is one and the same both before the flesh and with the flesh; the flesh which he himself, for us, united to himself according to hypostasis, intellectually ensouled, assumed from the holy Theotokos[^16] and ever-virgin Mary; himself deigning to become the seed of his own incarnation, that he might also truly become man, and show, by the seedless conception and the incorrupt birth, nature innovated, and suffering no diminution by the innovation. For the mystery was God's self-chosen self-emptying, out of goodness toward men, and not a falling away from Godhead — the voluntary condescension through flesh. For he remained what he was, even becoming what he was not — for he is unchangeable; and he kept what he became, abiding what he was — for he loves man. Through what he operated in a manner befitting God, showing what he became unaltered; and [593] through what he suffered in a manner befitting man, giving assurance that what he was was not changed. For he operated the divine things fleshly, since through flesh not without a share of natural operation; and the human things divinely, since it was by volition, with authority, and not by force of circumstance, that he admitted the experience of human sufferings. For neither the divine things divinely, since he was not God naked; nor the human things fleshly, since he was not mere man. Therefore the miracles were not without passion, and the sufferings were not without miracle; but the former — if I may speak boldly — not impassible, the latter manifestly wondrous; and both paradoxical, because both divine [and human],[^17] as proceeding from one and the same God the Word incarnate, who through both gave real assurance of the truth of the things from which, and which, he was. For what is brought to completion from certain things without confusion, by the natural union that is by concourse, both keeps unchanged the natures from which it is constituted, and preserves undiminished their constitutive powers, for the fulfilment of one work, whether what came to pass was passion or miracle; after the image of thy wonderful example, Father, so apt to the mystery of the divine incarnation: the sword heated in the fire, whose cut we know to be burning, and whose burning we know to be cutting.[^18] For a concourse of fire and iron has come about according to hypostasis, neither having departed from its power according to nature because of the union with the other; nor indeed possessing it at large after the union, and separated from that with which it is compounded and co-subsists.

This, then, is the exactness of the divine doctrines that I found wisely woven into your letters;[^19] upon which, according to the moderate power of thinking that is in me, I fixed my mind, and prayed to be made sufficient to guard it and to hold it fast in my understanding, admitting no erasure from any forgetting. But thou, God-honoured Father, have sympathy with me thy servant, and, as one who loves man, grant the pardon due to one who is unable to undertake what has been commanded. And do not condemn me of disobedience, but rather approve me as one eager to become aware of his own ignorance. For rashness is hateful, and the first offspring of the uttermost ignorance; in which the disease of pride is wont to show itself through, fashioning in those it has seized a habit opposed to God. And accept my excuse in this as pure of every passion; and do not thrust me away when I put forward to thee the same words which, in such matters, Moses earlier and Jeremiah later put forward to God: the one, I pray, Lord, [596] saying, I am not sufficient before yesterday nor before the third day, nor since thou didst begin to speak to me: I am weak of voice and slow of tongue;[^20] the other, O thou who art, Master Lord, saying, I know not how to speak, for I am a youth.[^21] For in truth I am weak of voice and slow of tongue, and bereft of the intelligence for speaking what is good; having neither a mind that reaches to divine things, nor the word of utterance able to serve the greatness of what was asked. For I am still youthful in the passions, and I have the insurrection of the flesh getting the better of the law of the spirit.[^22] And even if we grant, by hypothesis, that I lack nothing of the power for these things, who is so bold and daring as to add ordinances[^23] to what has thus been decreed hierarchically by divine decree? I beg thee rather to make clear to me in thy precious letters, by a definition, what operation is, and in how many ways operation is said, and what, beside it, belongs to the effect of operation; and what difference we assert of these with respect to the work and to the action,[^24] so that I may be able to know the force of what has been written. For I have not yet been able to determine exactly which, and how said or understood, one must take the one operation.[^25] For we do not simply utter meaningless sounds; we signify conceptions by the sounds. For which reason I have found the God-speaking Fathers often conceding words, but conceptions never;[^26] because the mystery of our salvation lies not in syllables, but in concepts and realities. For the one they did, taking thought for peace; the other, establishing souls in the truth. So much on these things.

I beg thy holiness, precious Father, to receive kindly the divine Fathers who bear this modest letter, who have given themselves to this voyage for a need profitable to the common order of monks; and to consent that all be done for them that the exact word of the divine commandment wills for those who aspire to divine things and have the incarnate Word as the one and only pattern of their own life. For they are worthy of [597] your zeal in this, because they draw blamelessly the divine yoke of virtue and of knowledge. I fear, writing these things, lest I carry off a reproach of untimely boldness and tastelessness among those who do not know thee, Father, and have had no experience of thy condescension; and lest there be said justly of me too what one of the wise outside our word said, seeing one of the tasteless puffed up over something: A wise man, by smiling, has maddened a fool to insolence.[^27] In all things, then, holy Father, forgive me thy servant, and by thy all-holy prayers make Christ God propitious to me, whom thou servest continually by purity of mind and exactness of life.

## Notes
[^1]: P/T. Written by Maximus for George, prefect of Africa (Letter 1), to a community of women ascetics in Alexandria who had been received into the catholic communion and had gone back (the Latin: *a catholica fide discesserant*). Their name is lost on the plate (δ[?]); the second read of the leaf gives ἀσκη-[τρίας], "women ascetics", which the Latin renders *moniales*: "nuns". The letter addresses them throughout in the plural (feminine participles): "you".
[^2]: T. The scaffold's ἁπ-|λῶς ("simply") is a reading of a tiny hyphenated line end; the second read of the leaf has κα-|λῶς, and the Latin *probe* agrees: "well".
[^3]: S. Gregory Nazianzen; cf. Or. 37.2: the Christ "both-together" (τὸ συναμφότερον) is one not by nature but by concourse. T. σύνοδος here and below = concourse, not "synod".
[^4]: T. Printed ὁ ἡμῶν ἀγαθός ("our good one"); the second read of the leaf gives ὡς ἀγαθός, and the Latin *ut bonus*: "as good". In the same period the printed μετὰ τοσαύτην σάρκωσιν ("after so great an incarnation") stands against the second read's μετὰ τὴν σάρκωσιν; the Latin has neither word (*et incarnatus*): "after the incarnation".
[^5]: T. σαρκὸς νοερῶς … ἑνωθείσης: elsewhere in these letters νοερῶς qualifies "ensouled" (σαρκὶ νοερῶς ἐψυχωμένῃ, Letter 16; ἐψυχωμένην νοερῶς, Letter 19 below), and the Latin here has *intelligentium more animatae … unitae*. A participle such as ἐψυχωμένης seems to have dropped; not supplied.
[^6]: S. Gal 1:6.
[^7]: S. Prov 26:11 (cf. 2 Pet 2:22).
[^8]: T. κτίσις, elsewhere "creation", is here the house George had founded or endowed for them; the Latin *meas aedes*. Theopemptus, "my man", is George's agent.
[^9]: S. cf. Mark 10:45; 1 Tim 2:6.
[^10]: P. Pyrrhus, then an abbot, from 638 patriarch of Constantinople and with Sergius the voice of the single operation and will; Maximus disputed with him at Carthage in 645. Combefis's long note here sets this letter's praise against Pyrrhus's later course.
[^11]: T. Printed σε ("thee"); the second read of the leaf gives σου, which construes with τὸν θεόσοφον νοῦν, and the Latin has *tuum animum*.
[^12]: S. Luke 11:27.
[^13]: T. As printed (Καὶ μὴν κατὰ τὰς σὰς παγίας ἕξεις …) the clause has no verb; Combefis's margin proposes Καὶ μὴν τὰς σάς, which makes the habits a further object of "I call blessed". Translated so.
[^14]: P. The ψῆφος ("decree") of Sergius, patriarch of Constantinople (633), which forbade speaking of "one" or "two" operations in Christ; Pyrrhus had sent it with his letter. Sergius is the "new Mediator", the "great Moses", the "high priest" of these lines.
[^15]: P. The "innovation" at Alexandria is the Pact of Union of 633 between the patriarch Cyrus and the Severans, with its "one theandric operation".
[^16]: T. Θεοτόκος: Theotokos, "God-bearer", Mary's title confirmed at Ephesus (431).
[^17]: T. The square brackets are printed: an editorial supplement in Migne's Greek. The Latin has *et divina erant et humana*.
[^18]: T. The fire-heated sword is Pyrrhus's own example ("thy wonderful example"), the old image of the union of the natures (cf. Origen, *De principiis* 2.6.6).
[^19]: T. "your letters" here and "your zeal" below: Maximus passes to the plural (τοῖς ὑμετέροις γράμμασι; ὑμετέρας σπουδῆς), perhaps taking in Pyrrhus's community; the English follows the number.
[^20]: S. Exod 4:10.
[^21]: S. Jer 1:6 (LXX, ὁ ὤν).
[^22]: S. cf. Rom 7:23.
[^23]: S. cf. Gal 3:15 (ἐπιδιατάσσεται).
[^24]: T. ἐνέργεια – ἐνέργημα – ἔργον – πρᾶξις: operation, effect of operation, work, action. πρᾶξις, elsewhere "practice", is here a term in the logical series and is rendered "action" (the Latin *actione*).
[^25]: T. Printed τὴν μὲν ἐνέργειαν; read τὴν μίαν ἐνέργειαν, "the one operation", with the Latin *una* and with the lemma of Combefis's note (h) on this line, which observes that Pyrrhus's piece aimed to draw Maximus into assent to one operation in Christ.
[^26]: S. cf. Gregory Nazianzen, Or. 21.35 (Athanasius between the Greek and Latin terms); Combefis's margin cites Gregory's orations.
[^27]: S. A maxim in verse, not located; Combefis notes that it is ascribed to a pagan philosopher.
