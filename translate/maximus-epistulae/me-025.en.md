title: Letters 24–27
---
[608] LETTER 24

*Of the same, to Constantine the Sacellarius.*[^1]

The honoured letter of my God-guarded master came, with peace, bearing himself whole, appearing in his manners. For with the dignity of the words the disposition of the soul too is somehow wont to be reflected, together with the gentleness of the manners. And I rejoiced as though I had received my master himself present, and I embraced him in the spirit, and, enfolding him whole in the room of my heart, I laid him up there; and I glorified Christ our God, who wisely implanted in men the law of love,[^2] according to which those who know how duly to tend the seeds of love can never be absent from one another, even though they stand far apart from one another in body by the distance of place. But since the letter, bringing good tidings of worldly peace,[^3] changed the soul's grief into joy, it is fitting that we, for this peace, should with good reason become in every way grateful to God who granted it, lest we appear ungrateful for the kindness, repaying the benefactor ill. And we shall become in every way grateful by using the peace as is fitting, [609] and by setting aside the friendship with the world and the world-ruler[^4] which has ill come to be in us; and let us dissolve, even if late, the war set up against God through the passions, and, having made indissoluble treaties of peace with him in the abolishing of the body,[^5] cease from our enmity toward him. For it is impossible for us to be made friends with God while we rebel against him through the passions, and consent through vice to pay tribute to the wicked tyrant and murderer of souls, the devil, unless we have first become wholly at war with the wicked one. For so long do we stand as enemies and foes of God — even though we mould about ourselves the name of believers — as we will to be slaves to the passions of dishonour;[^6] and no profit will any longer accrue to us from the peace of the world, while the soul is ill disposed, and rebels against its own Maker, and does not consent to come under his kingship, but is still sold to ten thousand cruel masters, who press it on into vice and contrive that it deceitfully choose the road that leads to destruction rather than the one that saves. But when we sober up from the drunkenness of the passions, and rise up against the wicked and tyrannical kingship of the devil, and shake off from ourselves his most bitter yoke, and see how greatly the friendship with him has corrupted us — then we shall receive the peaceful coming of Christ, the peaceful and meek King, taking place invisibly upon us; and we shall consent to come under his yoke, as he orders and composes us by laws of truth; and we shall accept willingly to contract friendship with him, bringing him willingly tributes and taxes: the fruits of righteousness brought to perfection in us through the virtues according to the commandment. Delighting in these for the sake of our salvation, he who alone is without need will, as one who does not lie, grant us all that he has promised us. And these are: What eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor has it gone up into the heart of man, what God has prepared for those who love him.[^7] For to this end he also made us: that we might become partakers of the divine nature[^8] and sharers in his eternity, and might appear like him according to the deification that is from grace — for whose sake is the whole constitution and continuance of the things that are, and the bringing forth and coming-to-be of the things that are not.

Let us become worthy, then, of the peace, my blessed master, manfully stripping from ourselves the passions within us that rebel against God. Let us waste the body as far as we have power, through which, when it swells with vigour, the devil's entry into the soul naturally comes about. With [612] divine readings let us halt the wandering mind, and lead it back again to God. With the tensions of self-control and vigil let us temperately restrain desire, carried in disorder by appetite toward many things. With prayers and supplications and almsgiving let us wisely lull anger, boiling and troubled. With the fear of God and the threat of what is to come let us stop the sense-organs, moved and operating licentiously. Upon all things, and before all, let us remember death, and the fearful going-out of the soul from the body; and how the principalities and authorities and powers of darkness meet it in this air,[^9] each dragging it apart toward itself and cutting it up, in proportion to the evil kinship that came to be, relationally, toward it through the passion in between.[^10] Let us remember the bitter pain that comes upon the soul in Hades according to conscience, at the memory of the things it did ill through the body. Let us remember the last consummation of the whole world, in which all this is burned up with much and immense fire: the elements fearfully shaken with the melting of the burning; heaven, in its rolling up [and] in fear, fleeing in haste from the face of the fire that cleanses creation beforehand because of the coming of the Pure One;[^11] the sea vanishing; the earth seething, and from its foundation [?],[^12] and yielding up undiminished the infinite myriads of human bodies. Let us remember the awful hour of the defence that will take place before the fearful and awful judgement-seat of Christ, when all the power of the heavens and all the creation of men from the foundation of the world looks upon what belongs to each, down to a bare thought; when the unutterable light will receive some, because of the brightness of their works, and the illumination of the holy and blessed Trinity, shining forth more clearly, for those able through purity of soul to see and receive it; and the outer darkness will receive others, because of the outrageousness of their works, and the sleepless worm, and the unquenchable fire of Gehenna,[^13] and — heaviest of all — the shame in the conscience that has no end. Let us remember all these things, that we may become worthy of the one, and may not be condemned to make trial of the other; and let us become our own and God's — or rather, of God alone and whole, and ourselves whole — bearing nothing earthly in ourselves, that we may draw near to God and become gods, receiving from God our being gods. For thus are the divine gifts honoured, and the presence of the divine peace welcomed with friendliness. And so much on these matters. And may the God of peace, who reconciled us to himself through the cross, and ransomed us with his own blood from the authority of darkness, himself grant his peace that surpasses every [613] mind to my blessed master, and count him worthy of his heavenly kingdom, where is the dwelling of all who rejoice, and the choir of those who keep festival.[^14]

LETTER 25

*Of the same, to Conon, presbyter and abbot.*[^15]

I believe, as I received and was taught, that God is love; and that, as he himself is one, never ceasing from being one, so he makes one those who live according to his love, and grants them one heart and soul, even though they happen to be many;[^16] so that, as having one soul, they may know one another's hearts, and may not toil in ignorance searching about things unseen, each conjecturing his neighbour's disposition toward himself. Therefore, trusting in the grace of Christ that moves in thee, holy Father, that thou art ignorant of nothing of what is in my heart — as perhaps, though it is bold to say, neither am I of what is in thee — I take courage to excuse myself for the time being, at present, from my coming to you, knowing that you believe the excuse to be not of disobedience but of a passion of the body,[^17] forcibly hindering me from the road and not allowing the soul's eagerness to come forward into deed. But, absent in body through the present letter, I who am ever present in the spirit, and never separated from you in soul,[^18] embrace thee, my father according to God and protector, and the guardian of my salvation, and thy holy flock, worthy of the kingdom of Christ; and I beg that supplications be made for me to the Lord, that I may carry away my faith in him living and effective in deed, not deadened by the passions, but having the power of his cross and death, and of his burial and resurrection:[^19] of the cross, by the inaction of sin; of death, by the fullest casting off of vice; of burial, by the putting away of the soul's imagination concerning sensible things; of resurrection, by the wealth of the virtues, and the abundance of the true full knowledge of him, and the raising of the understanding on high from the things that perish — that I may become of one body with him and of one soul. And, to speak simply, according to his promise, like him in all things, apart from identity with him according to nature; lest, having deadened by the works of dishonour the life given me by him through the things said, I justly incur the sentence of those who killed their own life, our Lord Jesus Christ.[^20] For truly fearful, and beyond all condemnation, is it willingly to deaden the life given us by God through the gift of the Holy Spirit, through love for the things that perish. And they know this fear well who have practised preferring the truth to self-love.

[616] LETTER 26

*Of the same, to Thalassius the presbyter, who asked how some of the kings among the nations, on account of a God-sent wrath lying upon the subject multitude, sacrificed children and kinsmen, and the wrath ceased, as is recorded by many of the ancients.*[^21]

To the Greeks too God permitted the receiving of oracles,[^22] through which they devised for themselves modes of foreknowledge of things to come and of deliverance from what grieved them; and he often granted things to turn out for them according to their hopes, preparing the way for all by providence — as each was able to receive it — for the acceptance of the great mystery to come. For if the affection of rulers for those they rule, advancing into deed, snatched the whole subject people out of the necessity lying upon it, it is nothing strange or incredible if he who is by nature the lover of man, and alone Creator and King of men, offered his own Son unto death for all men, by way of economy, through flesh, that he might ransom them from the evils that held them. Of course: when the kings of Israel and Judah had marched against the Moabites in the time of the holy prophet Elisha, and after a great victory were about to sack the very royal city itself, the whole of it, the king of the country, taking his son — according to what was customary, as it seems, among the nations — sacrificed him on the wall before the eyes of the kings;[^23] and in this manner he appeased the kings and persuaded them to turn back from the [617] wrath prepared against him, and saved his subjects.

To the Greeks, then, God, by permitting them to perform what came of their own law, wisely made them his own for the acceptance of the great mystery; to the Israelite people, by giving a law, he prefigured throughout the mystery to come. Hence, after the mystery of our salvation had appeared and come to be in operation, he abolished both these things and those, from Gentiles and from Jews, whatever he had given before, or had permitted to come about, for the prefiguring of what was to come. For from him, and through him, and to him are all things,[^24] whatever has come to be or will come to be, at every time or in every mode: some by good pleasure, some by economy, some by permission. What the ancients record, then, is not a myth: it truly happened, and the remedy for which it was done followed upon it. The detailed account of what has been written let us exercise together, with God, when I am with you. Only, he who has known how even the flesh of a serpent can by art heal the sick will accept with faith the method of divine providence.[^25]

LETTER 27

*Of the same, to John the Cubicularius.*[^26]

Those who, according to the world, have established love toward one another need one another's bodily presence for its preservation, because of forgetfulness, which naturally withers every longing that is set up about bodies alone; and with it the memory of those so disposed toward one another is wont to be quenched as well. And sometimes satiety, supervening, sets apart the very thing that seems able to preserve the worldly relation toward one another, and wipes out the whole disposition that went before. And often the failing of some one of the causes that supply it, or even some small pretext falling in by the way, makes swift the turning into hatred of a love so constituted. But those who according to God have been bound to one another indissolubly by the bond of love, whether they are with one another or absent from one another, tighten its bond the more, having it in themselves because of God, who is love, and who provides to the worthy the power of being able to love — and very justly, as I think. For with things by nature fluid, the disposition of those disposed toward one another by them is wont to flow away as well; but with things stable and holding ever the same, the affection of those disposed toward one another by them is wont to abide with them, altogether unshaken and unbudging, stretching their longing for one another toward the boundless as far as God, the cause of this relation, is by nature able to draw to himself those who hang upon one another in the spirit.

[620] Loving thee, then, in this manner, most beloved, I have thee always inseparable, present with me in soul, stamped deep in the spirit — though the length of time is long, and the distance of the places between is great — never ceasing to see thee intelligibly and to embrace thee. And believing myself loved by thee equally, if not even more, I do not shrink from writing without hesitation about what is needful, knowing that one soul has come to be of us both through the Spirit. And I know that thou dost not think thyself insulted in hearing this, reckoning the condescension of God toward men, fearful and above mind and reason. Receive kindly, then, him who brings this present modest syllable,[^27] and become to him all that he enjoined who made it law for men to share out among themselves one another's misfortunes, and to make the neighbour so much one's own that he is not thought another, according to the disposition exchanged between those who have been changed into one another. For not only to rejoice with those who rejoice, but also to weep with those who weep; and not simply to love, but to love the neighbour as oneself, the divine word enjoins[^28] — not to mention the new commandment, which enjoins laying down even our very lives for one another for love's sake, when it is the time to contend;[^29] which the Lord who gave it fulfilled in deed, not refusing to lay down his own life for us: for us a pattern of perfect well-pleasing, by which the law of self-love is quietly quenched — the law which at the beginning, through pleasure, deceitfully led us away from God and from one another; and, having taught that instead of one God many are to be thought to exist, or none at all, utterly dashed down the power of the reason in us to the flesh, and, cutting the one nature into many portions, made bestial against one another, for pleasure's sake, the power of the anger in us. Become, then, as I said, all things to him who needs thy help, that thou mayest find God becoming such to thee — he who for love for man consents to become all things to all.[^30]
