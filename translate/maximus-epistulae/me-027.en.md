title: Letters 37–43
---
[632] LETTER 37

*Of the same, to the same.*[^1]

Again we have received thy honoured letter, holy Father, with gifts; and again we have enjoyed goods equal to the former. By the gifts we quenched the burning of the passions that blackens our soul — for before them we had eaten the intelligible principles,[^2] not believing that these had been sent by thee at random and in vain and as it chanced, since thou movest in all things with reason and spirit, and makest even the food of the body a lesson of the soul for us; and by the letter, having found humility, the mother of all the virtues, through which both the coming-to-be of good things and their guarding are wrought for those who have it, we wept abundantly, perceiving how far we have been removed from this road. For thy mourning has become a reproof of the evils in which we are held; and it awoke our understanding, which was deeply asleep, and stripped bare our memory, covered with the veil of puffing-up conceit; and it has made us knowers of ourselves, perceiving in due measure the vexation of our own sins. And in some measure it has made us sharers of wisdom, having received discernment of our own offences — if he is true who says: Those who are knowers of themselves are wise.[^3] Do not cease, then, honoured Father, renewing our understanding by word, and asking by prayer deliverance from evils for us; that thou mayest receive as the reward for these, from God who gives it, the perfect cleansing of the soul in conscience.

LETTER 38

*Of the same, to the same.*

We thank thee, honoured Father, that thou hast given thyself to us as a pattern of excellent repentance, persuading us through confession to lay bare the dark treasures of the passions within ourselves; through which the hatred of sin is wont to be confirmed. For we in truth are they who cherish the passions, whose memory after the deed brings disgust: loving, as onions,[^4] the assent to wicked thoughts, by which the soul's power of sight grows duller toward virtue and knowledge, taking on, as it were, scales — the passionate imaginations of visible things. For the onion, according to those who give etymologies, is a closing of the pupil.[^5] And, as garlic, chewing the fulfilment of the passions through operation, by which the mind, being filled with uncleanness, breathes upon those outside, through shameful words and manners, the stench of the evils it has eaten through practice. But teach [633] us, holy Father, how, after casting these off, we shall love the manna — I mean the rational bread of divine knowledge, rained on us from heaven — and keep it incorrupt: neither treasuring it up for the morrow by delay, lest it boil up worms of fleshly passions, rotting through idleness; nor parading it in display, lest it vanish, melting away with vainglory.[^6] With such words nourish us again, honoured Father, thy disciples, and commend us through prayer to God, us who without thy mediation are unable to worship.

LETTER 39

*Of the same, to the same.*

Ungrudgingly thou nourishest, sanctified Father, both our visible and our intelligible man: supplying the body with what makes for its sustenance, and giving the soul what makes for growth in the spirit. For God has made thee rich in both, blessing thy coming in [?].[^7] By coming in I mean the possession of the gifts of grace supplied to thee by God, multiplied through practice; and by going out, the distribution of the word that comes about from thee to others, bearing in those who receive it, as fruit, the operation of what is fitting.

LETTER 40

*Of the same, to the same Thalassius, presbyter and abbot.*[^8]

Having read carefully the wise and honoured letter of your paternal holiness, master, and having examined everything, and read through the whole book of my conscience, I found none of the things it contains in me …;[^9] at once I contracted the tension of my soul, binding tight its slackness, judging it vain to be added to the praises of others' goods, and judging it proper only to a soul that has its propensity toward every alluring deception. For truly it is vain for one who does not have, in reality, the glory of the things said, to try rashly to appropriate praises as though full of virtue. But my master, judging the things of others from his own advantages of virtue, gives his vote — through an abundance of godlike perfection covering the shame — for one wounded by many … of passions,[^10] as is the custom of those who love the Lord. But I, my [636] blessed master, … of all-shining perfection in virtue, the command … most ready, being about perhaps to fall into every reproach and blame, as for my rashness in this, from the …, setting your love higher … to the lord abba Thomas;[^11] readily serving … this matter: holding out, that is, the material — sufficient, as I think, through the winter — so as to be able, with the Lord working with us, to fulfil this command of yours. Fare thou very well for me, beloved and honoured Father, and keep safe by thy prayers to God …[^12] thy household servant and slave — and I will add, lover — delivering him by thine own mediation from every diabolical device.

LETTER 41

*Of the same, to the same.*

One who has been taught by the Spirit to take everything captive to Christ[^13] knows how to reap benefit from all things; as you too, then, do from me, who am utterly estranged from divine grace and stoutly bound by the laws of the flesh. And having nothing [?] and earnestly,[^14] it were needful for me to decline altogether going anywhere at all, lest I do more harm in doing your bidding. And I come when the Lord wills, knowing that you have remained undefiled and unpolluted even while keeping company with me, the profane. For what is holy by habit, as by nature, will be stained by nothing unclean. As for the vestitor's business,[^15] you do well to sympathize, if the matter carries no harm; and especially if you know that he can bear with it.

LETTER 42

*Of the same, to the same.*

The blessed David, that great king of the Lord, after the fearful wars of the foreigners, after the great temptations from those of his own race, after the toilsome flights and the frequent plots in return for his many benefactions, after the uprisings of his kinsmen, after the … and the kingship, when he perceived God … him on every side — then he purposed to build a house to the Lord God ….[^16] And God accepts him, as one who had purposed well; … yet nevertheless a fitting injunction, saying … that the seed from him must bring to completion the good … undertaking, adding the cause: that he had poured out much blood upon the earth.[^17] And he obeys God who counsels him, and embraces the counsel with pleasure, and judges it more honourable than his own [?].[^18] And he himself does not build the temple of God. He sends on [?] to his son abundant …, gives both the material and the pattern; being taught …, as I think, the [637] well-built bonds of salvation.[^19] And, lest I lengthen my discourse going through the contemplation of each of the things said: the peaceful … able … the temple of God … these are, and if anyone …, but able to persuade to serve as bodyguard not by fear, and working together toward the building of the most divine temple, receiving harm; and Hiram king of Tyre, and the hundred and fifty thousand of the foreigners, the stone-cutters and water-carriers.[^20] But stretch forth, and prosper, and reign — even if thou hast reigned without thy Maximus — because of truth and meekness and righteousness, which are these.[^21] And I will add: and watch with all thy might not to be led away in any way with the daughters of the heathen kings, lest they join in dividing the kingdom between a foolish son and an apostate servant.[^22] For much willingly ….[^23] Farewell, honoured Father, and pray for thy servant …, grievously held by many sins.

LETTER 43

*Of the same, to John the Cubicularius.*[^24]

The honoured letter of my God-guarded master came, with peace, bearing himself whole, appearing in his manners. For with the dignity of the words the disposition of the soul too is somehow wont to be reflected, together with the gentleness of the manners. And I rejoiced as though I had received my master himself, and I embraced him in the spirit, and, enfolding him whole in the room of my heart, I laid him up there; and I glorified Christ our God, who implanted in men a light, the law of love,[^25] according to which those who know how duly to tend the seeds of love can never be absent from one another, even though they stand far apart from one another in body by the distance of place.

But since the letter, bringing good tidings of worldly peace, changed the soul's grief into joy, it is fitting that we too, for this peace, should with good reason become in every way grateful to God who granted it, lest we appear ungrateful for the kindness, repaying the benefactor ill. And we shall become in every way grateful by using the peace, that is to say, as is fitting, and by setting aside the friendship with the world and world-ruler which ill exists in us; and, having dissolved, even if late, the war set up against God through the passions,[^26] and having made for ourselves treaties of peace with him in the abolishing of the body, let us cease from our enmity toward him. For it is impossible for us to be made friends with him while we rebel against him through the passions, and consent through vice to pay tribute to the wicked tyrant and murderer of souls, the devil, unless we have first become wholly at war with the wicked one. For so long do we stand as enemies and foes of God, even though we [640] mould about ourselves the name of believers, as we will to be slaves to the passions of dishonour,[^27] and consent willingly to pay grievous tributes to the devil through the works of shame. And no profit will any longer come to us from the peace of the world, while the soul is ill disposed, and rebels against its own Maker, and does not consent to come under his kingship, but is still sold to ten thousand masters, who ever press it on into vice and contrive that it deceitfully choose the road that leads to destruction rather than the one that saves. But when we sober up from the drunkenness of the passions, and rise up against the wickedness and tyrannical kingship of the devil, and shake off from ourselves his most bitter yoke, and see how greatly the friendship with him has corrupted us,[^28] then we shall receive the peaceful coming of Christ, the peaceful and meek King, taking place invisibly in us; and we shall consent to come under his yoke, as he orders and composes us by laws of truth; and we shall accept willingly to contract friendship with him, bringing him willingly tributes, taxes: the fruits of righteousness brought to perfection in us through the virtues according to the commandment. Delighting in these for the sake of our salvation, he who alone is without need will, as one who does not lie, grant us all that he has promised us. And these are what eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor has it gone up into the heart of man, what God has prepared for those who love him. For to this end he also made us: that we might become partakers of the divine nature and sharers in its eternity,[^29] and might appear like him according to the deification that is from grace — for whose sake is the whole constitution and continuance of the things that are, and the bringing forth and coming-to-be of the things that are not.

Let us become worthy, then, of the peace, my blessed master, manfully stripping from ourselves the passions within us that rebel against God. Let us waste the body as far as we have power, through which, when it swells with vigour, the devil's entry into the soul naturally comes about. With divine readings let us halt the wandering mind, and lead it back again to God. With the tensions of self-control and vigil let us temperately restrain desire, carried in disorder by appetite toward many things. With prayers and supplications and almsgiving let us wisely lull anger, boiling and troubled; with the fear of God and the threat of what is to come let us stop the sense-organs, moved and operating licentiously. Upon all things and before all let us remember death and the fearful going-out of the soul from the body; and how the principalities and authorities and powers of darkness meet it in this air, each dragging it apart toward itself and cutting it up, in proportion to the evil kinship that came to be, relationally, toward it through the passion in between. Let us remember the bitter pain that comes upon the soul in Hades according to conscience, at the memory of the things it did ill through the body. Let us remember the [641] last consummation of the whole world, in which all this is burned up with much and immense fire: the elements fearfully shaken with the melting of the burning; and heaven, in its rolling up, in fear, fleeing in haste from the face of the fire that cleanses creation beforehand because of the coming of the Pure One; the sea vanishing; the earth seething, and shaken from its foundation, and yielding up undiminished the infinite myriads of human bodies. Let us remember the awful hour of the defence before the fearful and awful judgement-seat of Christ, when all the power of the heavens and all the creation of men from the foundation of the world looks upon what belongs to each, down to a bare thought; when the unutterable light will receive some, because of the brightness of their works, and the illumination of the holy and blessed Trinity, shining forth more clearly, for those able through purity of soul to see and receive it; and the outer darkness will receive others, because of the outrageousness of their works, and the sleepless worm, and the unquenchable fire of Gehenna, and — heaviest of all — the shame in the conscience that has no end. Let us remember all these things, that we may become worthy of the one and may not be condemned to make trial of the other; and let us become our own and God's — or rather, of God alone and whole, and ourselves whole — and bear nothing earthly in ourselves, that we may draw near to God, having become gods, receiving from God our being gods. For thus[^30] are the divine gifts honoured, and the presence of the divine peace welcomed with friendliness.

And so much on this. And may the God of peace, who reconciled us to himself through the cross, and, when we were enemies, ransomed us with his own blood from the authority of darkness, himself grant his peace that surpasses every mind to my blessed master, and count him worthy of his heavenly kingdom, where is the dwelling of all who rejoice, and the choir of those who keep festival.

[^5]: T. The etymology plays on κρόμμυον (onion) as κόρης μύσις, "closing of the pupil"; the Latin keeps the Greek words.
[^6]: S. Exod 16:4, 19–21; Ps 77:24 LXX.
[^7]: T/S. The Greek has only τὴν εἴσοδόν σου, yet the next sentence explains "going out" (ἔξοδον) as well; the Latin has *benedicens introitum tuum et exitum tuum*, as Ps 120:8 LXX (cf. Deut 28:6). Not supplied.
[^8]: P. Thalassius, presbyter and abbot in Libya, also of Letters 9 and 26. The Latin margin here notes that the letters which follow come from the Florentine and Roman codices.
[^9]: T. The plate prints a damaged stretch (..εσω..ολο..) between ἐμφερομένων and θᾶττον. The Latin runs *nihil in me offendi. Quocirca mentis statim intentionem compressi* ("I found nothing in myself. Therefore I at once compressed …"). From here to the end of Letter 42 Migne prints the Greek with lacunae, where, his apparatus says, the codex is eaten away (*Exesa in codice*); the Latin has gaps at most of the same places. Each [?] marks a printed gap.
[^10]: T. Lacuna between πολλοῖς and παθῶν; the Latin has *multis vitiorum affectuumque … saucium*, "wounded by many [… of] vices and passions".
[^11]: T/P. A run of lacunae (εὐλογημένε … πα[μ]φαοῦς … ἡρῶσ … οιμότατος … παρὰ τῶν … λοσ … νων … τιθε …). The Latin, itself gapped, has "your command … of most shining virtue … I took up most readily; perhaps to be loaded with every reproach and stain, as one who, out of rashness …, preferred your love …, obeying in this part the lord abba Thomas". Thomas: the abbot to whom Maximus addressed the *Ambigua ad Thomam* (so Combefis's note).
[^12]: T. Lacuna after εὐχωλῇσιν (υμ …); the Latin has *tuisque ad Deum precibus*. εὐχωλῇσιν, the Homeric form, is Maximus's own flourish.
[^13]: S. 2 Cor 10:5.
[^14]: T. μηδὲν ἔχοντός εἰμι καὶ σπουδαίως does not construe; something is lost or misread. The Latin has *Cumque nullius frugis ac virtutis compos sim*, "since I possess nothing of fruit or of virtue".
[^15]: T/P. Περὶ δὲ τῆς τοῦ Βεστίτορος: feminine, its noun understood (a matter, or a woman of his household), as is αὐτῆς at the end; Latin *Ad Bestitoris quod attinet*. A vestitor held a court office in the imperial wardrobe; Migne prints the word with a capital, as if a name.
[^16]: T/S. Lacunae: τρ … σιν; … αὔοντος (perhaps a form of "give rest", as 2 Kgdms 7:1); after Θεῷ. The Latin has "after ….. and the kingship, when he perceived that he was propped by deep peace on every side by God's gift". 2 Kgdms 7:1–2; 1 Chr 17:1.
[^17]: T/S. Lacunae after διανοηθέντα, after λέγων, and in τ … ας. The Latin: "Yet it was worth while … that his seed would accomplish the work he had attempted". 1 Chr 22:8; 28:3. διαστολή here = injunction (not the glossary's "expansion").
[^18]: T. μορφῆς ("form") hardly construes; the Latin has *ei, quod sic ipse animo molitus erat*, "to what he himself had so purposed". Perhaps ὁρμῆς ("impulse"); not supplied.
[^19]: T/S. Lacunae after Παραπέμπει, in δαψιλ …, and after διδασκόμενος. The Latin: "Yet he sends rich wealth on to his son, and hands over both the material and the pattern of the house to be built; that he might, I think, be taught the duly ordered laws of salvation". 1 Chr 28:11–19.
[^20]: T/S. The opening of this sentence is lacunose (after εἰρηνικὸν, after ἄνω, after εἴ τις); the Latin too has gaps here. Χηρᾶν = Hiram (3 Kgdms 5:15–18; 2 Chr 2:17–18, the strangers as hewers and burden-bearers); "water-carriers", cf. Josh 9:21–27.
[^21]: S. Ps 44:5 LXX; cf. 1 Cor 4:8 (χωρὶς ἡμῶν ἐβασιλεύσατε).
[^22]: S. 3 Kgdms 11:1–13, 26; 12:1–20 (Rehoboam and Jeroboam).
[^23]: T. Lacuna after ἑκών, and again after δούλῳ (… πουμένῳ); the Latin has the same two gaps (*Multum enim volens….. ac pro servo tuo…..*).
[^24]: P. John the Cubicularius (Letter 27). This letter is Letter 24 again, almost word for word; Combefis records that the Florentine codex addresses Letter 24 too to John. The variants are noted in the OCR table; for the sources see the notes to Letter 24 (Rom 1:26; 1 Cor 2:9; 2 Pet 1:4; Eph 6:12; 2 Pet 3:10–12; Matt 8:12; Mark 9:48; Col 1:13, 20; Phil 4:7; Ps 86:7 LXX).
[^25]: T. φῶς ("a light") where Letter 24 has σοφῶς ("wisely"); the Latin here has *qui lucem (charitatis legem) hominibus indidit*, so Combefis read φῶς. Kept.
[^26]: T. The plate has ἣν before διὰ τῶν παθῶν, which cannot agree with πόλεμον; Letter 24 has καὶ, and the English reads it. In the next phrase σπονδὰς αὐτοῖς is taken as σπονδὰς αὑτοῖς, "treaties for ourselves" (Letter 24: σπονδὰς ἀλύτους, "indissoluble treaties").
[^27]: S. Rom 1:26.
[^28]: T. The plate has ὅσον κατείδομεν ἡμᾶς τῇ πρὸς αὐτὸν φιλίᾳ κατέφθειρε, where κατέφθειρε lacks a subject; the English reads ἡ … φιλία with Letter 24 and the Latin (*quantam … ejus nobis amicitia labem intulerit*).
[^29]: T. τῆς αὐτῆς ἀϊδιότητος, "its" (the divine nature's) eternity; Letter 24 has αὐτοῦ, "his".
[^30]: T. The plate has Οὕπω ("not yet"), and the Latin read it (*Nondum enim divina munera honorantur*); Letter 24 has Οὕτω ("thus"), which the sense requires. The English follows Οὕτω.
