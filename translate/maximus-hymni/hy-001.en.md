[1417] *Hymns of Saint Maximus the Confessor.* [ed: Migne's heading; the hymns are by Maximus Margunius]
(Daniel, Thesaur. hymnolog., III, 97 seqq.)
*I. Hymn of thanksgiving and supplication to God.*

A meadow held me, wretched,[^1] [lat: the Latin has *beatum*, blessed]
Of old, of the best plants,
[1418] When, by the greatest God's
Unspeakable providence
And love of equal measure,
I looked upon this light,
Moulded of earthy dust
And having received a spirit besides,
[1419] Midway between earth and sky
Set as a living creature, alas.
Shining by participation[^2]
In the image of the great God.
But the serpent, the all-wicked,
Creeping up in vehement envy,
With ruinous counsels
And ill-omened sayings
Whispering dreadfully to me,
Deceived me, alas,
And out of the fair delight[^3]
That was there he thrust me,
And from the Father's orchards,
Whose tiller, ah, the Father had set me,
Banishing me utterly.[^4]
And it was a piteous spectacle
To the august choir of the minds.
But since my wound
Would bear no physician,
The Moulder, skilled in every art,
Seeing me with warm compassion,
Strangely came down to me,
In human form, purely,[^5]
Taking from the unwedded
Mary his birth.
And thus he rescues me,
Snatching from the wicked
Dragon's hand,
And snatching from the doom
That was fated for me,
Who from God's command
Had heedlessly fallen away,
For which he gave his own
Body to a bitter doom.
And he crushed its head
In the waters[^6]
Of the streams of Jordan.
And, of my sins,
The dark shamefulness there
Plunging under;
But he once more, his shameless
Tail swaying,
Troubles my mind,
Shaking it with motions
That carry off into the pit,
And trying to trip up
The paths of my journey.
And he goads, impurely,[^7]
The force of unruly horses
That carries to and fro.
Stirring my mind to lust
After reckless shames
And soul-destroying passions.
And forcing to fall in ruin
The charioteer
Who guides the reins.
And he kindles a great blaze
From fire-bearing darts,[^8]
Which, evil-minded, he sets ablaze against me,
Stirring the inward
[1420] Fire in me, which is blended in.[^9]
But do thou grant me
Thy hand, saviour of the people,
Cutting out root and all
The wiles of bitter Beliar;
Thou who of old didst stay a stream
Turned back most swiftly
In a backward-rushing course,
Achieving a strange work [lat: the Latin has *Cladem exterorum*, the destruction of the foreigners].[^10]
Thou didst dry up the sea's
Full-flowing flood,
Bringing across on foot
Thy people Israel,
And thou didst speak, and all things
By thy word were brought into being;
And thou didst check the chariot of flame
Of the swift-running giant,
The greatest light-bringer.[^11]
Thou didst turn, in the sevenfold
Furnace of the three children,
The flame into deep dew.[^12]
Thou hadst mercy on the prodigal;
Thou didst loose the harlot's knot
Of evil-binding loves,
As she shed hot tears.[^13]
And thou didst stay, of her that flowed with blood,[^14]
By a touch of thy hem,
The ever-flowing defilement.
Thou didst raise from the dead
Also the child of Jairus,
And didst set breathing once more.
And thou didst cleanse lepers,
And to the blind didst give sight.
And thou didst thrust out a spirit
From the Sidonian girl.
Another took up his bed,
Having gathered the slackness of his limbs,[^15]
And the bent woman thou straightenest,
And thou drivest out demons.
And of Peter's mother-in-law
By a touch of the hand thou didst still
The fiery heat.
And thou didst more besides,[^16]
As many as escape number.
What wonder, if thou cool
The embers of my flame,
From an ever-flowing bowl
Letting thy dew drip
Upon my coals?
Which mayest thou bring me, Saviour,
Through all my life,
That I may hymn endlessly
Thy goodness,
And the Father's, and thy breath's.[^17]

[^1]: T. τλήμον(α), "wretched", said of the speaker looking back. The Latin has *beatum*, "blessed": a departure of sense, so `[lat: beatum]` stands here (V7). This is the model case; the Latin's reorderings and expansions elsewhere take no mark.
[^2]: T. V1 at work: the Greek's two lines are "Shining, of the great God / by participation in the image"; English wants the genitive last, so *of the great God* goes down one line and nothing leaves the sentence. μεθέξει is a noun, so "by participation" (M1).
[^3]: T. τῆς ἐκεῖθεν … καλῆς τρυφῆς, hyperbaton across the line: "the fair delight that was there". τρυφή is Eden's "delight" (Gen 2:15 LXX), fixed.
[^4]: T. ὀστρακίζων: "banishing"; English *ostracize* has shrunk to a social sense the Greek does not have.
[^5]: T. ἁγνῶς, "purely". The breathing cannot be told at the plate: the mark is the same blob as the rough breathing on ἁμαρτάδεσσιν, so the rough is written. The Latin has *invisibiliter*, "unseen" (as if ἀγνῶς, from ἀγνώς, unknown); it may read the same print the other way, so no `[lat:]` is marked.
[^6]: T. Ps 73:13 LXX, the heads of the dragons crushed upon the water, read of the baptism in the Jordan; ἐμβαπτίσας four lines below is the verb of baptism, "plunging under". κάρηνον, "head", goes up one line and σκοτώδη, "dark", up one line (V1).
[^7]: T. The soul's unruly horses and its charioteer (Plato, *Phaedrus* 246a–254e): the image runs to "the charioteer who guides the reins". ὀργᾶν, "to swell" with desire, is rendered "to lust".
[^8]: T. The fire-bearing darts of the wicked one (Eph 6:16).
[^9]: S. The top line of col. 1420 is badly broken at the plate; ἐμπέφυρται is read from traces (υ against ο not certain). T. From φύρω, "mix, knead": the fire "is blended in", kneaded into the speaker himself.
[^10]: T. The stream stayed and turned back is the Jordan (Josh 3:13–16; Ps 113:3 LXX). ἔργον ξένον, "a strange work" (V10); the Latin takes ξένον of the foreigners and gives *Cladem exterorum complevisti*, "thou didst accomplish the destruction of the foreigners".
[^11]: T. ἐπέσχες, "didst check": the sun stayed for Joshua (Josh 10:12–13), the sun as a giant running his course (Ps 18:6 LXX). The Latin's *regis cursum*, "thou rulest the course", is the looser sense of the verb.
[^12]: T. The furnace heated seven times (Dan 3:19), its midst made "as a wind of dew" (Dan 3:50 LXX).
[^13]: S. The plate prints Θερμὴν (an η, shaped as in Σὴν at the head of this column; no round ο), not the base's Θερμὸν; base corrected (PLATE-CHECKS.tsv). Either form is adverbial with δακρυῤῥοούσης: "shed hot tears" (Lk 7:37–38).
[^14]: S. The plate prints αἱμορούσης with a single ρ and Στῆσας with a circumflex; both kept as printed, the sense unaffected (Mt 9:20–22). The 1601 edition has the same two forms but prints προσάψει ("by touching") where Migne has προσαφῇ ("by a touch"); the sense is the same.
[^15]: T. λύσιν μελῶν, "the slackness of his limbs", the palsy (Mk 2:3–12); ἀθροίσας, "having gathered", the limbs drawn together again. The Latin takes λύσις as release: *Membrorum solutione obtenta*.
[^16]: S. Migne prints ἤρυξας (the plate is clear: ρ), which could only be from ἐρύκω, "ward off", and does not suit a run of miracles. The 1601 edition of Margunius's hymns (p. 33 of the Google copy, seen at the page image) prints **ἔρεξας**, "thou didst" (ῥέζω): *Πλείω δ᾽ ἔρεξας ἄλλα, / ἀριθμὸν ὅσα φεύγει*. That is also what the Latin's *egisti* renders, so Migne's word is a misprint (or Daniel's variant) and the English renders ἔρεξας; the Greek of the file stays as Migne prints it.
[^17]: T. πνοῆς σῆς, "thy breath": the Spirit, with the Father and the Son addressed. πνοή here and ἄημα in Hymn III are both "breath" in English; the Greek words differ. Ἄκραντα, two lines up, is an adverb with the hymning ("endlessly"), not an adjective on χρηστότητα.
