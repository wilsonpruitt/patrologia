---
title: Hymni, 2
---
[1420] *II. Hymn of supplication to Christ, in the form of a confession.*[^1]

Alas for me, for my evils,[^2]
O King without beginning, Saviour,
[1421] And for my mind's ill counsel
And slavish constraint;[^3]
And for the mischievous toil
That I, being vain, took on,
From childhood shutting
Myself in dread fetters
Of a life of evil ways,
Of wanderings and of loves.
I thought myself the best
Builder of fair houses
And of high-roofed towers.
But for the foundations I was laying,
It was not firm rock
That set a support within,
But sand, which most easily[^4]
The winds shake,
And the streams from the springs
Drag off with a roar,
And the rain, falling on it,
Shows soaked through.
And thence the houses fall,
And in ruins the roof
Of the dwellings is piteously seen.
And what then were these
That I, evil-minded, built
To my soul's destruction?
Stony was the heart's
Will, in sins
Ever resting at ease.
It, a strange, whitening
Colour wearing outwardly,
To the unknowing a festering
Way of thinking put on,[^5]
That hid within
An unseemly face,
Hypocrisy thou wouldst call it,
Abhorrent to all.
And I kept house-slaves [lat: the Latin has *Damnosos … hospites*, ruinous guests],[^6]
Who cared for glory
Falsely so called, and for puffing-up,
And which of old hurled the Typhon[^7]
Swiftly from the sky,
The darkened Morning-star,
By God's just judgement.
And to them there were sons
Numberless, and daughters,
Who lent an obedient
Ear to the behests of evil.
Then the rain coming
And the winds blowing
And the streams rushing in
Shook it from the foundations,
An ill-omened jumble,[^8]
And the fall was strange to me,
And for my soul a slipping;
[1422] She gathered so great[^9]
A harm to dwell within her,
And dread anger from above,
And the enmity of mortals.
But now I have resolved to lay
The rock down as foundation,
That the house may be kept safe,
Having those stones
Which are precious,[^10]
With gold and with silver
Spotlessly adorned,
Riches to the godly,
And the best of blessedness.
For what than august temperance[^11]
Has been established better?
Without which to behold
God is not given to mortals.[^12]
Again, than humility,
High-flying, what has been
Better to those who know?
Which true height
Provides to those who possess it.
And than a pure conscience [lat: the Latin has *Consciente agno*, the Lamb being witness],[^13]
Under the eyes of God
Who looks into the heart,
What will ever be better,
Which, like to the angels,
Presents a mortal [lat: the Latin has *Angelicum … Cibum*, angelic food]?[^14]
And a divine winging
The mortal mind undergoes,
If it is pure from above,[^15]
With far-shining breaths,
So as to behold the might,
The greatest, and the root
Self-sprung, and the breath[^16]
Life-bearing in its blowing,
And of glorious mysteries
To tread the depths,
And how much, under providence,
Of good comes down to the world,
And how much in times
That are for ever there will be.
Who could name a thing more blessed?
Such, O Saviour, do I,
The best of buildings,
Thou giving the strength,
Upon the firm rock,
The chief corner-
Stone, which thou thyself art,[^17]
Hasten to raise.
And mayest thou thyself supply me
Thy grace as fellow-worker,
To give these an end
Such as may be best,
And saving for me.
Neither violent rain
Nor springing streams
Nor furious winds
Shall then be able to shake my house
So much as a little,
Built upon the foundation,
Thee, Saviour, unshaken.
And mayest thou give me a hand,
And be a fellow-labourer
With me, and accomplish
The whole, that I may hymn thee,
[1423] Giver of the best things
And their accomplisher,
Which save mortals.
For apart from thee, O mighty one,
Neither work nor counsel,
Thought, nor utterance,
Nor anything of all things,
As many as are of the better lot,
Shall at all attain its end.
Thou didst give me flesh
And, breathing, a spirit too.[^18]
Thou also, raising me fallen,
Didst lead me up skyward,
Lifting me with thy flesh;
Thou raisest me up, dead,
By thy gifts of grace
And boundless tender compassion,
With which thou goest before me, Saviour,[^19]
And deemest me worthy of thy flesh's
Unspeakable communion.
Thou wilt also save me at the last,
If thou find not fruitless
Altogether my journey,
If I bear not a mass
Of lead, weighing heavy
And thrusting me into Gehenna
And unquenchable fire,
[1424] If not impure wood,[^20]
Hay, and kindling for fire.
And freely, in those yonder
Mansions thou wilt set me,[^21]
Living eternally,
A hymn-singer of thy glory,
With the undefiled minds
And all the just together.

[^1]: T. ἐξομολόγησις is both confession (of sins) and, in the Greek psalter, praise; the hymn is the first and ends in the second. The Latin title takes the second: *per modum laudationis*, "by way of praise".
[^2]: T. ὤμοι is οἴμοι in another spelling: "alas", as in Hymn I (V3). The genitives that follow (κακῶν, ἀβουλίας, ἀνάγκης, μόχθου) are the cause of the cry: "alas for".
[^3]: S. The plate prints the Greek question mark (;) after ἀνάγκης, confirmed at the plate, though the sense runs on; kept in the base, and rendered as a pause, not a question.
[^4]: T. The house on the sand (Mt 7:24–27): rain, streams and winds, carried through the hymn to the house built on the rock below.
[^5]: T. ἥ γ', "it": the heart (κραδίης, three lines up). λευκάζον, "whitening", recalls the whited sepulchres (Mt 23:27). ὕπουλον ("festering", said of a wound healed over on top and festering beneath) agrees with φρόνημα, not with ἥγ' (feminine); the Latin takes it as a noun, *Plagam interiorem*. φρόνημα, "way of thinking", as in the Disputatio.
[^6]: T. οἰκέτριβας as printed (confirmed at the plate; the lexica have οἰκότριψ, a slave born and bred in the house): "house-slaves". The Latin, *Damnososque habebam hospites*, reads it as wreckers of the house (οἶκος + τρίβω), which suits the ruined house of this hymn. The 1601 edition of Margunius's hymns prints οἰκότριβας, with ο, so Migne's second vowel is a misprint; "house-slaves" stands.
[^7]: T. τυφῶνα: the whirlwind, and Typhon, the monster hurled from heaven in the Greek myth; here in apposition to the Morning-star (ἑωσφόρος, Isa 14:12 LXX). The relative is the puffing-up (φῦσα) of the line before. The Latin has *exardescens*, "blazing up", for τυφῶνα. The 1601 edition reads differently here: *καὶ ἣ πάλαι ἀντύγων τε / πόλοιο ῥῖψεν αἶψα / ἑωσφόρον σκοτώδη*, "and which of old hurled the darkened Morning-star from the rim of the pole", with no τυφῶνα; the Typhon is Daniel's or Migne's text, kept here as printed.
[^8]: T. τύρευμα, properly a cheese-curd, then a mess or concoction: "jumble". The Latin has *congeries*, "heap".
[^9]: S. The accent on ἡ is indeterminate at the plate (ἡ, ἣ or ἢ). T. Read as the feminine article/demonstrative with the soul (ψυχῇ, the line before) as its antecedent: "she" (the English no longer hangs a relative on "slipping", which is masculine). The Latin has *Ipsa*, "she herself". The 1601 edition prints ἣ (relative, with the accent) and the same antecedent, the soul: so "she" renders "she who".
[^10]: T. The foundation, and the building of gold, silver and precious stones, or of wood and hay for the fire (1 Cor 3:10–15), runs on to the end of the hymn ("impure wood, hay").
[^11]: T. σαοφροσύνη (epic dress for σωφροσύνη, V3), "temperance"; σεμνῆς, "august", goes up one line. The Latin has *Sapientia*, "wisdom".
[^12]: T. Heb 12:14: without holiness no man shall see the Lord.
[^13]: T. συνειδότος ἁγνοῦ, "a pure conscience". The Latin, *Consciente agno*, "the Lamb being witness", reads ἁγνοῦ as if ἀμνοῦ: a departure of sense, so `[lat:]` (V7).
[^14]: S. βροτόν, "a mortal", confirmed at the plate. T. The Latin's *Angelicum quoque / Cibum præstante*, "presenting angelic food", reads βρωτόν: `[lat:]` (V7).
[^15]: T. ἦν as printed (the circumflex is clear at the plate). "Was" does not construe with πάσχει; the line is taken as ἤν, "if" (= ἐάν), with the participle: "if it is pure". The Latin has *Desuper ita fit purus*, "from above it thus becomes pure".
[^16]: T. The three as in Hymn III: the self-sprung root and the life-bearing breath (ἄημα), with the might; the Latin reads them the same way (*Potentiam, originemque / Spontaneam, afflatumque*). πνεῦσιν, "its blowing" (πνεῦσις), kept apart from πνοή, "breath".
[^17]: T. Christ the chief cornerstone (Eph 2:20; 1 Pet 2:6). ἔπλες, epic for "art".
[^18]: T. God breathing into the moulded dust (Gen 2:7 LXX, ἐνεφύσησεν). The Latin has *Animam*, "soul", for πνεῦμα.
[^19]: S. Ἧ printed without iota subscript (confirmed at the plate). T. The relative ᾗ, "with which": the compassion. προφθάνεις, "goest before" (Ps 58:11 LXX).
[^20]: S. ὕλην is read from traces (badly broken at the plate); the Latin's *lignum* agrees. T. ὕλη is both "wood" and "matter"; Paul's list has ξύλα, χόρτον, "wood, hay" (1 Cor 3:12).
[^21]: T. μοναί, the "mansions" of John 14:2. προῖκα, "freely": as a gift; the Latin has *absque merito*, "without merit".
