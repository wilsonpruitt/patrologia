[657] THE MYSTAGOGIA

*Of St. Maximus: Mystagogia, on what the things performed in the holy church at the synaxis have been established as symbols of.*[^1]

PROEM

How the wise man, receiving an occasion, becomes wiser, and the just man, having come to know, will add to his receiving, according to the divine Proverb,[^2] thou thyself, most honoured of all to me, hast clearly shown by experience itself, teaching by deed what the divine word wisely hints at. For having once heard me recount, cursorily and briefly, as far as was possible, the things contemplated, beautifully and mystically, and, as far as could be, in the manner of a teacher, by another, a great elder and truly wise in divine things, concerning the holy church and the holy synaxis performed in it, thou didst demand of me, [660] urging at once, that I make for thee a written account of these things: having wished to have the writing as a remedy for forgetfulness and a help for memory; saying that memory naturally has time as its tamer, which is able imperceptibly, through forgetfulness, to plunder and wholly to efface the impressions and the images of the good things laid up in it; and that for this reason it needs by all means the renewing mode by which the power of the word, ever flourishing, is of a nature to keep memory impassible and undiminished. How much wiser it is than simply hearing, to seek also an indestructible permanence for what has been heard, everyone surely knows who takes even a little care for rational nobility and is not altogether a stranger to intimacy with the word.

And I, for my part, hesitated at the beginning (for the truth shall be told), declining the subject of the discourse; not from not wishing to grant you, beloved,[^3] in every way and according to my power, what you desire; but because I had neither partaken of the grace that leads the worthy to this, nor had any experience of the power and practice of speaking, having been reared in unlettered plainness, and being wholly uninitiated in the technical discourses which have their charm in delivery alone, in which the many chiefly delight, bounding pleasure by hearing, even if they often have nothing of worth in depth; and, to say it more properly and more truly, because I feared lest I should outrage, by the poverty of our discourse, that blessed man's lofty utterance and intellection concerning divine things. Yet nevertheless afterward, yielding to the force of love, which is stronger than all things, I accepted the command willingly, choosing rather to be laughed at by the fault-finders for presumption and want of education because of my ready obedience, than to be thought, through delay, unwilling to share your eagerness in every good thing; casting the care of how to speak upon God, the only wonder-worker, who teaches man knowledge, and makes plain the tongue of stammerers,[^4] and devises a way for those without a way; and who raises the poor from the earth and lifts up the needy from the dunghill:[^5] from the carnal way of thinking, I mean, and the foul-smelling mire of the passions; the poor in spirit,[^6] or the one who is poor in vice and lacking the habit that accords with it; or, the contrary, the one still held by the law of the flesh and by the passions, and for this reason poor and lacking in the grace that accords with virtue and knowledge.

But since by the all-holy and truly God-revealing Dionysius the Areopagite,[^7] in his treatise *On the Ecclesiastical Hierarchy*, the symbols of the [661] rite of the holy synaxis too have been contemplated in a manner worthy of his greatness of mind,[^8] be it known that our discourse does not now go through the same things, nor proceed by the same ways as he. For it is rash and presumptuous and near to madness, for one who can neither contain nor understand him, to attempt to set hand to his things, and to bring forward as his own the mysteries made manifest in an inspired way to him alone through the Spirit; but only such things as were left by him, by the will of God, in love for man, as graspable by others too, for the exposition and exercise of their own habit concerning divine things according to their aspiration; and through which, in proportion to them, the all-radiant beam of the things performed, being contemplated, becomes known, and holds to itself those seized by longing; so that those after him may not be altogether idle through the whole day of the time of the present life, having no word that hires them for that divine vine-dressing, the word that pays out, for the spiritual labour of the spiritual vineyard, the spiritual denarius of the divine and most royal image, which was plundered in the beginning by the evil one through deceit in the transgression of the commandment.[^9]

Nor do I promise to tell in order all the things mystically contemplated by the blessed elder; nor the things told, as they were both understood by him and told. For he, besides being a philosopher and a teacher of all learning, having made himself free, through abundance of virtue and long and most scientific practice and diligence concerning divine things, from the bonds of matter and from the imaginations attached to it, had, as was fitting, his mind illumined by the divine rays, and for this reason able at once to see what the many do not see; and his word the most exact interpreter of what had been understood; and, like a mirror hindered by no stain of passions, able purely both to bear and to speak what others cannot even understand; so that his hearers could see the whole mind riding upon the word, and all the things understood shining forth purely in the whole mind, and could receive them ferried across to them through the mediation of the word: but I tell such things as I carry in memory, and as I am able to understand dimly and to speak more dimly; yet piously, by the grace of God who illumines what has been darkened. For I do not suppose that you, who know how to judge justly, think it necessary that I should understand or speak otherwise than as I can understand and speak, and as the grace from above grants, providing fittingly the power proportionate to me, even if he who handed these things on and taught them is most exalted. Since to seek equal things from those not equal in virtue and knowledge seems to me not far from those who try to show the moon [664] shining equally with the sun, and who force things not in every way the same to be able to agree with one another in all respects: which is impracticable and impossible.

Let God lead the things said and understood, he who alone is mind of those that understand and of the things understood, and word of those that speak and of the things spoken, and life of those that live and of the things given life; and who is and becomes all things to all for the sake of the things that are and become, but for his own sake is in no way, in no manner, at all either being or becoming any of the things which are something among the things that are and become, since he is not at all ranked by nature with any of the things that are; and for this reason he rather admits not-being, because of his being beyond being, as more properly said of him. For it is necessary, if indeed it is truly necessary for us to know the difference of God and creatures, that the positing of the One beyond being be the removal of the things that are, and the positing of the things that are be the removal of the One beyond being;[^10] and that both names be contemplated properly concerning the same, and that neither can be so properly: being, I mean, and not-being. Both properly, since the one posits the being of God as cause of the things that are, and the other, by the pre-eminence of the cause, removes from him all the being of the things that are; and again neither properly, since neither presents the positing of the very what-it-is of what is sought according to essence and nature. For to him with whom nothing at all is naturally yoked by way of cause, whether being or not-being, to him nothing of the things that are and are said, nor of the things that are not and are not said, is fittingly near. For he has an existence simple and unknown and inaccessible to all, and altogether uninterpretable, and beyond all affirmation and negation.[^11] And so much for these things; let us come to the proposed subject of the discourse.

CHAPTER 1

*How and in what manner the holy church is an image and type of God.*

The holy church, then, at the first approach of contemplation, bears a type and image of God, that blessed elder used to say, as having the same activity as he by imitation and type.[^12] For as God, having made all things by his infinite power and brought them into being, holds together and gathers and circumscribes them, and binds them providentially to one another and to himself, both the intelligible and the sensible;[^13] and, encompassing all things about himself as cause and beginning and end, makes the things that stand apart from one another by nature to converge with one another by the one power of relation to himself as beginning; by which all things are led to an [665] identity of movement and existence, incorruptible and unconfused, none of the things that are being at variance with or divided from any other primarily by a difference of nature or of movement; all things having grown together with all, without commixture, according to the one indissoluble relation and guardianship of the sole beginning and cause; a relation which annuls and covers over all the specific relations contemplated in all according to the nature of each of the things that are, not by corrupting them and destroying them and making them not to be, but by conquering and outshining them, as a wholeness does its parts; or as the cause of the wholeness itself, appearing, by which both the wholeness itself and the parts of the wholeness are of a nature to appear and to be, as having their whole cause shining above them; and, as the sun outshining both the nature and the power of the stars, so having their cause covering their existence as things caused. For as the parts are of a nature to be from the wholeness, so too the things caused are of a nature to be, properly, from the cause, and to be known, and to have their own property at rest, when, embraced by their reference to the cause, they are qualified throughout according to the one power, as has been said, of relation to it. For God, who is all in all, he who in infinite measures is above all, will be seen alone by those who are pure in understanding,[^14] when the mind, contemplatively gathering up the principles of the things that are,[^15] comes to its end in God himself, as the cause and beginning and end of the production and coming-to-be of all things, and the ground, without interval, of the circumference of all.

In the same way the holy church of God too will be shown working the same things as God concerning us, as an image to its archetype. For though those who come into it and are reborn by it and recreated by the Spirit are many and nearly infinite in number, men and women and children, divided from one another and differing very greatly in race and form, and in nations and languages, and in ways of life and ages and inclinations and arts, and in manners and customs and pursuits; and again in sciences and dignities, and fortunes and characters and habits; it gives and grants to all equally one divine form and appellation: to be and to be named from Christ; and one relation according to faith, simple and without parts and undivided, which does not allow the many and untold differences that exist about each to be known, not even that they exist, through the universal reference and coming together of all to it; by which no one at all is in any way separated from the common, all having grown together with one another and [668] being joined according to the one simple and undivided grace and power of faith. *For of all*, it says, *the heart and the soul was one*;[^16] so that from different members one body both is and is seen, truly worthy of Christ himself, our true head;[^17] in whom, says the divine Apostle, *there is neither male nor female, neither Jew nor Greek, neither circumcision nor uncircumcision, neither barbarian nor Scythian, neither slave nor free; but he himself is all and in all*:[^18] he who encloses all things in himself by the one simple, infinitely wise power of goodness, as a centre of certain straight lines that proceed from him,[^19] according to one simple and single cause and power; not allowing the beginnings of the things that are to stand apart together with their limits, circumscribing their extensions in a circle, and leading to himself the delimitations of the things that are and have come to be by him; so that the creatures and works of the one God may not be altogether alien and hostile to one another, having nothing about which and whither they might show something friendly and peaceful and identical toward one another; and lest their very being run the risk of falling away into not-being, separated from God.

The holy church, then, is an image of God, as has been said, as working the same union as God concerning the faithful, even though those unified in it through faith happen to be different in their idioms and from different places and manners: the union which God himself is of a nature to work, without confusion, concerning the essences of the things that are, assuaging and making identical what is different about them, as has been shown, by their reference and union to himself as cause and beginning and end.

CHAPTER 2

*On how and in what manner the holy church of God is an image of the world, which subsists of visible and invisible essences.*

At the second approach of contemplation, he used to say that the holy church of God is a type and image of the whole world, which subsists of visible and invisible essences, as admitting the same union and distinction as it.
