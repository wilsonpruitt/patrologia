[56] OPUSCULUM 4 — TO GEORGE, PRESBYTER AND ABBOT, ON THE MYSTERY OF CHRIST

*Of the same, to George, the most holy presbyter and abbot, who asked by letter about the mystery of Christ.*[^1]

Having winged thine own mind with divine aspiration, and [57] having raised up thy reason with zeal, and lifted it above matter and the things about it,[^2] Father honoured by God, thou hast an insatiable reading of the sacred-written Oracles, and, if anyone else has, the untiring power of care for them through diligent attention. For what is above sense knows no satiety; nor indeed does what is above conception know any covering whatever. For the one by nature stands above all flowing and outflowing; the other rises above every form and figure. And the former makes the manifestation of the hidden, as is lawful; and the latter provides the possible comprehension of the indefinite, as much as suffices for the rapture of the understanding, and for the grasping of the Holy Spirit. For up to this, and for this, has been established the condescension, for love of man, of him who is above men through manifestation: by grace making divine those who ascend with him by inclination, and who, by the emptying of the Word,[^3] receive the fullness according to him, in the completion of the working of the commandments. By which thou thyself, led up to the perfection of prudence and courage, temperately pressest down what appears, and justly fattenest what is hidden; that in the former thou mayest display the mortification of Jesus,[^4] wiping out wholly every sin, and in the latter show forth the life, putting forth every virtue in a manner above the world. From which, having accomplished sacred practice and contemplation, thou hast carried off salvation as a sacred end; which, so as to impart it to others also, and to make it known — the thing that imitates God — thou hast chosen to use toward me, who am unworthy, lowly and unknown, honoured syllables: and first to make me wise by word and give me understanding; and further to sanctify me through the grace that is in thee, distributed to all everywhere; for it is uncircumscribed, as having the uncaused Word as its cause.

Then also to lead me sufficiently to the science of the new mystery of the God-fashioning, after the manner of man,[^5] of God the Word, incarnate for us and perfectly become man: by wishing, through a question, not to learn what thou art ignorant of, for thou art in possession of all good things, but to advance to a more perfect initiation one who has not yet received the lesser. And marvelling at thy pious manner, I put forward the account as far as I can: asserting that the human in the Saviour is not [60] other by nature, and that in us other; but the same in essence and invariant, since it was taken from ours, by the ineffable assumption from the virginal undefiled bloods of the all-pure Mother of God;[^6] to which the Word, united after the manner of seed, became flesh, not having departed from being God according to essence; and became perfect man like us, except for sin alone: on account of which we often are at faction, and wrestle against God in volition, as having acquired its nod toward either side;[^7] but he, being by nature free of all sin, as man not bare but God become man, had nothing contrary, but kept our nature undefiled and in every way properly unstained. Whence too he said: «Now the ruler of this world comes, and finds nothing in me»[^8] — of those things, that is, by which we, counterfeiting the nature, display the opposition of the will; on account of which Christ too is said to be unsubjected in respect of what is ours,[^9] until he release us too from it by the power of his becoming man. Confirming the truth of which, he became all things for us, and did them willingly on our behalf, falsifying neither our essence nor any at all of its blameless and natural things; even though he deified it with all those, like red-hot iron, rendering it wholly divine-working, as having interpenetrated it throughout to the utmost through the union, and having become one with it without confusion according to the same and one hypostasis.

Whence the human in him differs from ours not by the principle of nature, but by the newly fitting mode of its coming-to-be: being the same according to the essence, but not the same according to the seedlessness; since it was not bare, but truly of him who became man for us. Just as his willing too is properly natural like ours, but formed divinely above us. For it is plain that seedlessness and seed do not cut nature, but are cut about the same nature, as also unbegottenness and begetting.

For if perchance the Word as man had a difference toward our nature on account of the seedlessness, he would surely have one also toward the essence of the Father, on account of the begetting. For unbegottenness and begetting are not the same. And we too shall have one toward both the old Adam and Eve, who came to be without seed: [61] for the one is a moulding of the moulder, and the other a section of the moulding. But indeed the Son is the same as the Father on account of the Godhead; for he is God and consubstantial; just as we too, toward both Adam and Eve, and toward the very God incarnate for us, are kin on account of the manhood, and consubstantial. For as the unbegotten and begotten is not the essence of God (for who is it that says so?), so neither are seed and seedlessness in any way the nature of manhood.

So the great theologian Gregory affords no reflection of the human in the Saviour being other according to nature, and that in us other, even if it was thought so by some, from his saying:[^10] «We should say that the word is formed as from the man; not him conceived in the Saviour — for his willing is nothing contrary to God, deified wholly — but him who is like us; as the human will does not altogether follow the divine, but for the most part resists and wrestles.» For it is properly his, and is called his, not as being of another, or from another, besides our essence and nature, but as having come to be on account of him and in him, according to the same and one hypostasis; and not by itself apart, or brought forth on account of itself, as we are. Whence too it had as its own seed, making new the introduced mode of the begetting, the Word himself, having received by lot, together with being naturally, also subsisting divinely in him; so that both what is ours might be ratified, and what is above us believed. For in everything one must both preserve the nature assumed by him, God the Word incarnate and perfectly become man for us, with its natural things, without which there is not nature at all, but only an empty phantasy; and keep safe the union: the one preserved in natural otherness, the other again known in hypostatic identity; and so, at once wisely and piously, declare the whole principle of the economy throughout, uncommixed and undivided together.

Proclaiming him orthodoxly, thou shalt have him proclaiming thee in turn, according to his promise,[^11] and bestowing the enjoyment of his own things, as through hope here, so through experience there; of which beseech that I too may partake, sanctified Father, bringing me through thy mediation to him who calls and saves, and who grants, as a wage for the fellow-feeling of those of one kind toward one another, to be glorified with him and beside him; to whom be the glory and the might, with the all-holy Father and his life-giving Spirit, unto the ages of ages. Amen.

[64] OPUSCULUM 5 — AGAINST THOSE WHO SAY ONE OPERATION OF CHRIST «BY PREVAILING»

*Of the same. Against those who say that one must speak of one operation of Christ «by prevailing», because his divine operation, as more effective, prevails over the human: thus one must answer.*[^12]

First, you too confess two operations: one prevailing, the divine, I mean, and one prevailed over, plainly the human; which is among relatives. And relatives altogether introduce together with themselves also the things divided over against them.

Then, that if you speak of the operation «by prevailing», as though the human is abolished through being prevailed over, you introduce a diminution to them. For what prevails is itself too altogether among the things that suffer; for it too is mastered by what is prevailed over. For even if less, still it is mastered by all means; as gold, prevailing over the silver, say, or the bronze mixed with it, is itself too mastered, even if less, plainly according to the quantity admixed.

*Against those who say that «As of the instrument and the mover there is one operation, so too of the Godhead and the manhood there is one operation.»*[^13]

If you say the instrument is natural, the Word will be, according to you, coeval with the flesh, or the flesh coeternal with the Word; for everything that uses a natural instrument has it coeval, as the soul the body; and it is time for us to call the divine nature a creature, or the body uncreated. But if you say the instrument is artificial, you introduce it as soulless, and standing apart by itself and partitioned; and not always moved, but when it seems good to the worker to take it in hand and work; and for the rest you will be shown either of one mind with the mad Nestorius, who divides and sets apart the two natures of Christ God that came together into one hypostasis and one person; or disciples of the impious Apollinarius, who says the body of Christ is soulless and mindless.

*Against those who say one composite operation of Christ.*

The composite operation is altogether of a composite nature. And every composite nature has its coming-to-be simultaneous and involuntary; for it is created, and circumscribed by place and a world of its own; and comprehensive of [65] many individuals. It is time, then, for you who say one composite operation of Christ to say also a composite nature of Christ, of which it is distinguishing: a nature having its parts coeval, and their concourse involuntary and compelled, created and circumscribed by a world of its own and by place; and for the rest also a multitude of Christs, of whom this is predicated.

OPUSCULUM 6 — ON «FATHER, IF IT BE POSSIBLE, LET THIS CUP PASS»

*Of the same. On «Father, if it be possible, let the cup pass from me.»*[^14]

If thou takest «Father, if it be possible, let the cup pass from me», which has a reflection of contraction, from the man, «not him conceived in the Saviour (for his willing is nothing contrary to God, deified wholly), but him who is like us, as the human will does not altogether follow the divine, but for the most part resists and wrestles», as the divine Gregory says:[^15] the next part of the prayer, that is, «Not what I will, but let thy will prevail»,[^16] what does it seem to thee? To be of contraction, or of courage? Of utmost consent,[^17] or of standing apart? But that it is not of resistance, nor of cowardice, but rather of perfect cohesion and consent, no one of those who have a mind will gainsay.

And if it is of perfect cohesion and consent, from whom dost thou receive it? From the man who is like us, or from the man conceived in the Saviour? But if from him who is like us, the teacher's word speaking about him has erred: «As the human will does not altogether follow the divine will, but for the most part resists and wrestles.» For if it follows, it does not resist; and if it resists, it does not follow. For the one is abolished by the other as its opposite, [68] and withdraws. But if thou takest «Not what I will, but let thy will prevail» not of the man who is like us, but of the man conceived in the Saviour, thou hast confessed the utmost consent of the human toward his divine and paternal will; and thou hast presented as two the volitions and operations, which are according to nature, of him who is double in nature, he having in neither any opposition whatever, even though having in all things the natural difference of those from which, and in which, and which the same was by nature.[^18]

But if, shut out by these reasonings, thou art led on to say that the «Not what I will» belongs neither to the man who is like us, nor to the man conceived in the Saviour, but is carried negatively upon the beginningless Godhead of the Only-begotten, excluding his willing anything properly apart from the Father: then thou carriest by necessity also what was willed, which is the refusal of the cup, upon the beginningless Godhead itself. For even if thou sayest that the negation holds the abolition of willing something properly, but not the removal of what was willed — for it is not possible to place the negation on both, both on the Only-begotten's willing something properly apart from the Father, and on what was willed — since altogether the volition of the common will of Father and Son will be the abolition of what was willed by God,[^19] that is, of our salvation. For this is by nature what is willed by him. But if it is not possible to place the negation on both, it is plain that if thou bringest it upon the willing something properly, in order to make the positing of the common will, thou wilt not abolish what was willed, that is, the refusal of the cup; but thou wilt bring it against the common and beginningless Godhead, upon which thou hast also referred the willing negatively. But if this is execrable even to conceive, then plainly here the negation, that is, «Not what I will», removing opposition in every way, presents the cohesion of the human will of the Saviour with his divine and paternal will; inasmuch as the Word, whole, was essentiated in the whole nature, and deified it whole by the essentiation.[^20] Whence, as having become like us for us, he said in a manner befitting man to the God and Father: «Not mine, but let thy will prevail»; inasmuch as he himself, God by nature, had as man also volition: the fulfilment of the paternal will. Therefore according to both the natures from which, and in which, and of which he was the hypostasis, he was known as by nature volitive and operative of our salvation: the one, as being well pleased in it together with the Father and the Spirit; the other, as having become for it obedient to the Father unto death, and the death of the cross;[^21] and as having himself performed through flesh the great mystery of the economy toward us.

SCHOLIA

1. That those who say that the «Not what I will» is carried negatively [69] upon the beginningless Godhead of the Only-begotten, signifying, as they themselves say, that he wills nothing his own apart from the Father, are under necessity to bring also what was willed, which is the refusal of the cup, upon the beginningless Godhead itself; and that, since it is not possible to place the negation on both, both on the Only-begotten's willing something properly apart from the Father, and on what was willed, they must altogether bring it upon the willing something properly alone, not upon what was willed, which is the refusal of the cup, that is, our salvation; than which what could be more irrational? For it follows that God wills that what is by nature willed by him should pass. For whose is «Not what I will», his is altogether «Let the cup pass from me» too.
