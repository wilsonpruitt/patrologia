OPUSCULUM 7 — DOGMATIC TOME TO MARINUS

*Of the same, a dogmatic tome sent to Cyprus, to Marinus the deacon.*[^1]

[69] I was not more struck by the decorum of thy great reverence than I marvelled at the manliness of thy very great zeal, all-holy servant of God, and all-wise initiate and mystagogue of his mysteries. For by the sacred interweaving of the two thou hast acquired perfection in all good things: by the one, having become wholly, in the whole, throughout, God's, through the fulfilment of his divine commandments, thou reckonest thyself, through the utmost excess of poverty, to be wholly among such as I, who with a disposition of soul cleaving to matter wallow in the ichor of the unclean passions; and at the same time thou securest for thyself from this, with reason, what is safe (for nothing is stronger than self-chosen poverty for the fixing and the preservation of good things; for it stands as the unbroken foundation of the divine edifices in the soul); and thou becomest for all others, by whom the good is earnestly pursued, the procurer of salvation, by leading to greatness of virtue, through a lofty lowering, by effective example rather than by didactic address, those who wisely direct both their mind and their life alike towards thee.

And by this — the zeal, I say — having the whole fervour of the Spirit, and having acquired in the earth of the heart the fire which he came to cast upon it,[^2] he who consumes our every wicked habit and movement, the Word, by nature good-loving and a lover of man, thou settest aflame those who out of deceit and folly run mad after matter and mad after drivel, in base deeds and in counterfeit words alike, refuting the unsoundness and emptiness of their [72] ignorance by the putting-forth of the blessed lamp that burns unquenchably in thee according to divine knowledge and virtue; and thou warmest those who are badly chilled by the congealing of this frosty and lightless putting-forth with the ray-bright beams of the divine words, both driving off and dissolving the cloud entirely, and bringing the utmost contraction over to utmost courage and power; and again thou enlightenest from the everlasting mountains[^3] — the immaterial ones, I mean, which have surpassed all knowledge and power of all the senses: the words and doctrines of the Fathers — those who aspire to light; and those who, by the tasting of wisdom, have already been well imaged through hope as plainly that very thing which the longed-for itself is by nature, thou leadest and piously bringest to the total transformation that comes by experience.

For nothing so images the work of thy divine priesthood as the zeal according to God, tempered with reverence and unyielding, and the reason implanted in us that both holds this zeal together and kindles it, preparing us to do, and wisely steering us towards, those things which he who is so by nature and first commands, for our ascent to him and our perfect restoration through holy works and good doctrines, and for flight and turning-away from the things that make us miss this sacred ascent. And what else so violently breaks up and as it were walls off this — I mean the journey to the Word — except the fearful betrayal of him by those who have now sprung up against those who walk the royal road[^4] of the divine and paternal doctrines, and who [73] know no deviation into any of the things borne unhappily to either side, into the pits of confusion and the ravines of division, nor are willing in any way to know one, by the grace of the all-holy Spirit, who wisely guides and leads by the hand towards the perfect, face-to-face knowledge and initiation of the great God and Saviour of all, Christ himself,[^5] those who hasten, as they pray, through sincere and orthodox faith?

For it is betrayal, the alteration of the pious belief that is his and about him, which, by the assumption of the confession and teaching of the heterodox, sells him off, to the destruction of his all-holy flesh, which is from us — or rather, to the overturning of the whole economy; and a betrayal heavier than the one that took place at the first, when the Word was with us in the flesh, by so much as the knowledge of his Godhead and the truth of his manhood have become more perfect and manifest to all, having taken hold of the ends of the inhabited world through the loud voice of the holy Fathers who proclaim it.

For while it is firmly confessed by all everywhere, and believed in orthodox fashion, according to the guidance of those teachers, the God-chosen ones I mean, that the one of the holy and consubstantial Trinity, the only-begotten Son, being perfect God by nature, became perfect man by volition, truly assuming from the holy Mother of God,[^6] the Ever-Virgin, flesh consubstantial with us, ensouled rationally and intellectually, and uniting it to himself according to hypostasis, properly and indissolubly,[^7] and that he is one with it, as also before — yet not incomposite in his hypostasis, though simple in his nature, inasmuch as he remained God and consubstantial with the Father, and again twofold, as having become flesh; so that by the twofoldness of his nature he might be akin according to essence to the extremes, and preserve the natural difference of his own parts towards each other; and by the singleness of his person, having perfect identity in the parts, he possesses, as one and only, the personal difference towards the extremes; and by the undeficient natural and essential invariance towards them (I mean the extremes) he might be perfect in both respects, the same God and man at once — those who impiously teach as doctrine the diminution of the things that belong to him naturally bring him in as imperfect, and as having suffered the falling-away from the things according to nature. For if he does not keep without deficiency the property of each nature, sin alone excepted, according to the divine Fathers — of the natures from which and in which the incarnate Word properly is, even after the union — he is a deficient God, if a God imperfect at all; and again a deficient man, if a man at all who possesses a diminution of the natural things.

So then one must not, under the pretext, forsooth, of the union — which harms nothing, but only binds the realities together into one according to hypostasis — corrupt their existence by the destruction of the natural will [76] and of the essential operation. For if, as some whole from parts, we should perhaps melt down and fuse by composition the two essential wills and the natural operations equal to them in number into one will and one operation, such a thing will be shown to be a fable, and wholly foreign and alien to the communion with the Father and with us; since he does not have a will or an operation composite by nature, nor again do we. For there is no composition of the things in a subject, because no existence at all is considered of them by themselves and outside the underlying essence. For besides the gracelessness of it, it is also utterly contemptible that it should be partitioned and cut in half, upward and downward, by expansion,[^8] because of its natural kindredness with both — and that by men who confess besides the indissoluble union according to hypostasis.

Or if again we keep unharmed the natural will of the divine nature of the incarnate Word, and the operation that belongs to it essentially, but take these away from the human essence that is his and remove them, then too we damage the union surpassing nature, which has nothing to bind into one hypostasis, since the flesh ensouled rationally and intellectually — that is, the essence and nature that is ours — does not at all exist and is not preserved perfectly in the Word. For where, and what sort of, nature is there that has suffered the falling-away from the things according to nature?

If then the Lord was deficient according to the flesh in these natural idioms, or in any of them, he was not flesh and man at all. For either let those who say this show a man who is by nature without these, or a man at all; or, if there is none whatever, it is plain that the Word incarnate has not become man, being deprived by nature of these or of any of them. For how, and by what reason, when the nature has no deficiency of such things? Rather, he is something other, wholly foreign to our essence, and unknown; and either that which he is plainly said to have become was co-essentiated with him from the beginning, and came down with him when he came down from above — and then what is the descent to us, who in no way came together with him through holy flesh not assumed from us and united according to hypostasis? — or the whole is a phantasm, and an outward shape only, cheating the sense, but not the essence of flesh, since it is not the first-fruits of our race, which unifies the lump by grace and releases it from all the divisive things of which the transgression of the old Adam became the cause; on account of which death too was condemned upon the nature.

Why then do they grudge us perfect salvation and confession? Why do they stretch against us their supposedly inescapable syllogisms, and say that the wills follow the operations, and that opposition again follows upon these, from which they bring in also those who will contrary things?

[77] But — to pass over calling their proofs to account — whence and how do they devise such sophistries? I should like only to inquire and learn whether, then, having done such things themselves, and having put the new Ekthesis[^9] into operation, they did this without willing, and who it was that applied force? Then, after the action, did they afterwards acquire the will for what had been done, which before was not at all — from where, and coming from whom? And who compelled them to have the will for what was done, so that what is done should be both done against volition and acquiesced in against wish?

And how again, if the incarnate Word himself neither willed naturally as man nor operated the things according to nature, did he voluntarily admit, willing, hunger and thirst, toil and weariness and sleep and all the rest? For it was not as Word only that he willed or operated these things according to nature, having with the Father and the Spirit the nature that is superessential and beyond-infinite; even if again «with authority, as God, he gave the nature occasion, when he wished, to operate its own things», as says the divine guide of the Nyssenes and great Gregory.[^10] For if he, the same, willed these things as God only, and not as man, either the divine is body by nature, or, changed in essence, he became flesh by the falling-away of his own Godhead; or at all events the flesh that was his was not rationally ensouled, but he was, as to it, altogether soulless and irrational; or, if it was rationally ensouled, he possessed also the natural will. For everything rational by nature is also by nature altogether volitive. And if he had a natural will as man, he willed according to essence just those things which he himself as God, fashioning the nature, placed in it naturally for its constitution. For he did not come to counterfeit the nature which he himself as God and Word had made; but he came to deify throughout the nature which he himself, willing it, by the good pleasure of the Father and the co-operation of the Spirit, united to himself according to the same and one hypostasis, with all the things that belong to it naturally, and without sin.

So then, as God by nature he willed the things divine and paternal according to nature, for he was co-willer with his own [80] Begetter; and again the same, as man by nature, willed the things human according to nature, keeping the economy pure of all phantasm — things in no way falling against the will of the Father. For nothing natural, just as not nature itself at all, would ever fall against the cause of nature; nor even inclination,[^11] and whatever belongs to inclination, when indeed it converges with the principle of nature. For if someone should perhaps say that something of what is according to nature falls against God, the charge belongs to him rather than to the nature, as having naturally placed war in the things that are, for revolt[^12] and battle against him and against one another.

For that nothing natural is opposed to God is plain from these things' having been fashioned by him by way of generation, and from our bearing no accusation for their essential constitution in us; but, quite the contrary, from our plainly undergoing the charges because of their deviation. For by the latter we become, by inclination, men of every vice, after the serpent, the author of evil; but by the former we are, by nature, God's handiwork and a precious creature.

For this reason, then, according to the divine Fathers, we make no diminution whatever of natural wills, nor indeed of operations, just as none of the natures themselves, in the case of the same and one God the Word incarnate; believing in orthodox fashion that the same is in all respects perfect God and man at once, from his both having naturally, and willing and operating, perfectly the divine things and the human, and from his having properly a divine and a human essence and volition and operation at once; lest, by a deficiency in either of the natural idioms, we should unreasonably teach as doctrine the diminution — or rather the perfect non-existence — of each of the natures from which and in which he is.

For that he had a human will according to nature, as also a divine one according to essence, the Word himself plainly shows through the refusal of death, befitting man and by way of economy, which he made for our sake, in which he said: Father, if it be possible, let the cup pass from me;[^13] that he might show the weakness of his own flesh, and that he was not known to those who saw him as flesh in phantasm, cheating the sense, but was truly and properly man, the natural volition bearing witness to this, to which the refusal by way of economy belonged.

And that again it had been deified throughout, converging upon the divine will itself, always moved and shaped from it and according to it, is plain from his having perfectly made the ratification of the paternal will alone, according to which he said as man: Not mine, but thy will be done;[^14] giving himself to us in this too as a type and example, for the setting-aside of our own will for the sake of the perfect fulfilment of the divine, even if we should see death hanging over us on this account. [^8]: T.
