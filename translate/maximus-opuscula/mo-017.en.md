[153] OPUSCULUM 15 — SPIRITUAL AND DOGMATIC TOME AGAINST THE EKTHESIS

*Of the holy Maximus. A spiritual and dogmatic tome, showing that the intrusive Ekthesis of Heraclius the emperor,[^1] which came about by way of innovation at the suggestion of Sergius, president of Constantinople,[^2] is discordant with the sacred Oracles and the Fathers, but concordant with the unholy [156] heretics who tell monstrous tales of confusion and division alike in the mystery of Christ our God. Written from Rome to Stephen, the most holy bishop of Dora,[^3] ranking under the holy and apostolic throne of the holy city of Christ our God.*

The Lord, having likened the present life to night, its movement to a mill, and its rest to a bed, determined that of those who are in each (I mean the mill and the bed) the one is taken and the other left.[^4] And to be taken[^5] is perhaps the one who has been released from the former through contemplation and from the latter through practice, and who has remained, by disposition, neither in the circumscription of the intervals, in which the movement of the things that are seen is seen, nor in the softness of the passions, in which is the rest; but to be left is he who is held fast in these by self-choice, through the soul's love of pleasure and love of the lofty.

But what then? Having learned by trial before, as by hearing today, that I your servant am held fast, how could you, O God-illumined and all-excellent ones, have chosen, in what you have written, to be brought down so sympathetically to condescension toward the poor and needy man — you who have been so far lifted from the earth according to the grace of God, and exalted, as to raise up your life to reason, and your mind to the Lord of the things here, and above the things here; appropriating and making one the image to the archetype; or, to say it more truly, wishing to make the self-chosen condescension of your supreme beauty a demonstration of height, so that by its abundance you might lead us also to the measure of the more-than-infinite God the Word, who by his self-emptying toward us showed uncircumscribably the height of his own glory; to the end that he might refashion us the more longingly, by as much as he himself, for our sake, formed himself in flesh toward what is lowlier: become man by nature without change and confusion, he who is surpassingly the lover of man; leaving nothing lacking for the assumption of what is ours, lest he leave both the grace lacking to himself and the salvation lacking to us, not giving it whole to the nature, as not having taken the nature whole, but diminishing it in that respect in which he had not assumed it.

«For what is unassumed is unhealed,»[^6] says the great Gregory. For how would God the Word, the superessential, incarnate for us from the Holy Spirit and Mary the Ever-Virgin and Mother of God by the assumption of flesh having the intellectual and rational soul, having become new and come to be man by nature, renew the ancient [man], had he not taken him wholly and whole, apart from sin alone, from which comes the growing-old, and through which death was decreed against the nature, having us for its food so far as we tasted of that [sin], bitterly glutted with the tree of the transgression? And how would the same, emptied, raise up the fallen one, had he not united him to himself wholly and whole according to hypostasis from the very first point of conception, apart from the slip alone, [157] from which comes the breaking, and on account of which the penalty?

Thus, then, having assumed the intellectual and rational soul together with the body grown together with it, that is, perfect man, without any blemish, and having united it to himself according to hypostasis, he who is God above nature, having taken also its natural [volition], had in every way a self-determining volition. If, transgressing the commandment, we transgressed by volition,[^7] and not apart from volition, we needed the healing that concerns it, God incarnate himself healing like by the assumption of like; and having assumed it, he would also have had its rational and vital operation, by which, having reasoned out and operated sin — would that we never had! — we stood in need of the salvation that concerns it, he cleansing it of every defilement by the assumption, and making divine our whole nature by the incarnation.

For if God the Word created this [nature] having these things essentially — I mean the volition and the operation — and presented the one as self-determining and the other as operating, it is clear that, having taken the nature, he united it to himself according to hypostasis just as he created it from the beginning, that is, volitive by nature and operative; so that it should not be that, had he assumed only the suffering nature but not also its innate powers, he would not even have assumed the nature, but made a phantasm of the economy. For it is impossible for ours, or for the nature of any other of the things that are, ever to be or to be named a nature apart from its innate powers, by which each is naturally characterized essentially. Hence our nature is not without volition, since then it would not even be a man's; nor indeed without operation, since it is not soulless. But if he was without volition and without operation as regards what is ours, as seemed to some of the heretics of old and of now, then he who is above us did not at all come to be as we are, not confirming what is ours by what is ours, and by the things by which he himself, having created man from the beginning, characterized him naturally, making him self-determining and operative according to nature.

And indeed the composition of the holy Gospels confirms incontrovertibly that he assumed the power of each of these, by those [passages] in which it presents the superessential Word, become man for us, as willing and operating; crying out in one place: *And about the fourth watch of the night he comes to them walking upon the sea, and he willed to pass by them;*[^8] in another: *And arising from there into the borders of Tyre and Sidon, and entering into a house, he willed that no one should know, and he could not be hid;*[^9] and again: *Going out from there they passed through Galilee, and he did not will that anyone should know;*[^10] and: *On the morrow he willed to go forth into Galilee.*[^11] And after these, again: *Jesus walked in Galilee, for he did not will to walk in Judaea, because the Jews sought to kill him;*[^12] and: *Where wilt thou that we prepare for thee to eat the passover?*[^13] and: [160] *They gave him vinegar mingled with gall, and having tasted he did not will to drink.*[^14]

For these — I mean *He willed to pass by them*, and *He willed to go forth*, and *He did not will to walk*, and *to will to eat the passover*, and *not to will to drink the vinegar* — confirm his human volition and operation, as ours is. For example, to will and not to will to pass by, or to eat, or to walk, or to drink, is plainly volition, by which he was known to be volitive by nature. But by tasting and eating, and going forth, and journeying from place to place and walking, he was plainly shown as being operative in operation, in respect of that according to which he later became man by nature for our sake, but not of that according to which he was without beginning, for his own sake, God by nature. For it is not natural to the Word, in that he was God and Son of God according to nature, to will anything bodily and to eat, or to be confined locally by movements from place to place, being everywhere and above the all, infinitely, infinite times over. So too [Scripture],[^15] presenting him as having superessentially the divine and paternal volition and operation, teaches clearly: *As the Father raises the dead and gives life, so also the Son gives life to whom he wills.*[^16] Here, then, too, to will to give life to the dead bears witness to his divine volition; and to give life itself, to his properly all-working operation. And simply, whatever else is such depends from the nature exalted above the things that are, for the manifestation of it, so far as possible.

By these, then, the Word himself, through his own disciples, presents himself, according to both his natures, from which and in which he consists, as being volitive by nature and operative of our salvation; and those after them, their successors and the revealers of the theophany of the Word in the flesh, the God-chosen Fathers of the catholic Church, proclaiming alike each nature of the same, both the divine and the human, and not only that, but also the volition and the operation, say these things with a great voice. For the one named for immortality,[^17] in his discourse on the incarnation and the Trinity:[^18] «And when he says, *Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless not mine, but thy will be done*; and again, *The spirit indeed is eager, but the flesh weak*, he shows here two wills: the human, on account of the weakness of the flesh, refuses the passion, but his divine [will] is eager.» And Gregory, named for Theology, in his second [161] discourse on the Son:[^19] «Let the seventh be said: that the Son has come down from heaven, not that he might do his own will, but that of him who sent him. If, then, these things were not being said of him who came down, we should say that the word is shaped as about the man — not him who is understood in the Saviour, for his will is not even opposed to God, being wholly deified — but him who is as we are; since the human will does not wholly follow the divine, but for the most part resists and wrestles against it.»

And his namesake, who became the guide of the Nyssenes, or rather the teacher of the inhabited world, in his discourse on the Pascha:[^20] «The leper approaches, already wasted away and made useless in body. How does the healing come to him from the Lord? The soul wills, the body touches; through both the passion flees. *For the leprosy*, he says, *departed from him at once*.» And again the same in the same discourse: «Again, those who in many thousands waited on him in the desert he does not will to dismiss fasting; but with his hands he breaks the loaves. Seest thou how through both the Godhead, accompanying, is made public — by the body that operates, and by the impulse of the will that arises in the soul?» And in the second book of those he laboured against Eunomius the impious:[^21] «How did the Lord, reconciling the world to himself, distribute to soul and body the benefaction that came from him to men, willing through the soul, and touching through the body?» And again the same says, in his [books] against Apollinarius:[^22] «Since, then, the human will is one thing and the divine another, he who made our passions his own utters, as from the man, what is suited to the weakness of the nature; but he adds the second utterance, willing that the lofty purpose befitting God be ratified above the human for the salvation of the world. For he who said *Not mine* signified the human by the word; and adding *But thine*, he showed the conjoinedness of his Godhead with the Father, of which there is no difference of will, on account of the communion of the nature.»

And the divine John, who made illustrious the throne of the queen [city], in the discourse entitled *Against those who stayed away from the synaxis, and a demonstration that the Son is consubstantial with the Father*, [164] whose beginning is *Again horse-races, and again our assembly has become smaller*:[^23] «Seest thou how he also proclaimed beforehand his former age? Ask the heretic, then: Does God fear and shrink back? And does he hesitate and grieve? And if he says Yes, stand off from him henceforth, and set him down below with the devil, or rather lower even than him; for not even he will dare to say this. But if he says that none of these things is worthy of God, say: Then neither does God pray. For apart from these, another absurdity also follows, if the words are God's. For the words show not only agony, but two wills, one of the Son and one of the Father, contrary to each other. For to say *Not as I will, but as thou* is [the word] of one who shows this. But this not even those [heretics] ever conceded; but when we always say *I and the Father are one* of the power, they say this was said of the volition, saying that the wish of the Father and the Son is one. If, then, the wish of the Father and the Son is one, how does he say here: *Nevertheless not as I will, but as thou*? For if what is said is said of the Godhead, a contradiction arises, and many absurdities are begotten from it. But if of the flesh, what is said has reason, and there would be no charge. For it is no condemnation that the flesh does not will to die; for this is of nature. For he shows all the things of nature without sin, and with much abundance, so as to stop the mouths of the heretics. When, then, he says *If it be possible, let this cup pass from me*, and *Not as I will, but as thou*, he shows nothing else than that he truly put on flesh that fears death. For to fear death, and to shrink back, and to be in agony, is of that [flesh]. Now, then, he leaves it desolate and stripped of its own operation, so that, having shown its weakness, he might confirm its nature as well; and now he hides it, so that thou mayest learn that he was not a bare man. For just as, if he had shown human things throughout, he would have been thought this, so if he had accomplished the things of the Godhead throughout, the account of the economy would have been disbelieved. For this reason he varies and mixes both the words and the deeds, so that he may give no pretext to the disease and madness either of Paul of Samosata or of Marcion and Manichaeus. For this reason he both foretells what is to be as God, and shrinks back again as man.»

[^1]: P. The Ekthesis ("Exposition") of faith, issued by the emperor Heraclius in 638 and drafted by Sergius: it forbade speaking of one or of two operations and confessed one will in Christ.
[^2]: P. Sergius, patriarch of Constantinople (610–638), the architect of monenergism. T: πρόεδρος, "president", is kept as the Greek title.
[^3]: P. Stephen, bishop of Dora in Palestine, subject to the see of Jerusalem ("the holy city of Christ our God"), sent to Rome by Sophronius. T: the letter, though addressed to Stephen, speaks to plural addressees (θεολαμπεῖς καὶ πανάριστοι, ὑμῶν): rendered *you* throughout.
[^4]: S. Luke 17:34–35; cf. Matt 24:40–41.
[^5]: T. The plate prints αἴροντα with Migne's correction [αἴρεσθαι] beside it; the English follows the correction, which the pairing αἴρεσθαι … ἀφίεσθαι requires (Latin *assumendum*).
[^6]: S. Gregory Nazianzen, Ep. 101 (first to Cledonius).
[^7]: T. Migne prints δίχα θελήσεως, ἀλλ᾿ οὐ θελήσεως δίχα (confirmed on the plate), "apart from volition, and not apart from volition", which contradicts itself; the Latin has *voluntatis nutu non sine voluntate*. The English reads διὰ θελήσεως, "by volition", a conjecture.
[^8]: S. Mark 6:48.

[^12]: S. John 7:1.

[^18]: S. Ps.-Athanasius, *De incarnatione et contra Arianos* 21 (cf.); Matt 26:39, 41.
[^19]: S. Gregory Nazianzen, Or. 30 (the fourth Theological Oration, the second on the Son), 12; John 6:38.
[^20]: S. Gregory of Nyssa, on the Pascha (cf. *In Christi resurrectionem*, the oration *De tridui spatio*); Mark 1:42; the loaves, Matt 15:32–36.
[^21]: S. Gregory of Nyssa, *Contra Eunomium* (cf.); 2 Cor 5:19.
[^22]: S. Gregory of Nyssa, *Antirrheticus adversus Apolinarium* (cf. ch. 32); Matt 26:39. συναφές, "conjoinedness", is kept apart from συνάφεια, conjunction.
