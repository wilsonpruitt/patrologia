title: Opusculum 16, on the two wills, 2
---
[196] nature it is the very first, the innate power of natural idioms and motions. For according to it alone we naturally aspire to being and living, and moving, and understanding, and speaking, and perceiving, and partaking of food, of sleep and of rest, and to not being pained, nor dying; and, in a word, to having perfectly the habits of all that constitutes the nature, and the privations of what injures it.[^1]

And the devout monk seems to me to have done fittingly in this too, in his definition, adding: «and holding together all the idioms that belong essentially to the nature.» For the essential and innate will in us always holds all things together, and aspires to hold them together, and to have privation of none; or rather we do so, both through it and according to it. And I think that whoever takes account in any degree of what is, and not of what seems, will altogether agree that God incarnate has, as man, a natural and human will; and that he wills each of the things enumerated naturally according to it, for confirmation of his flesh animated intellectually and rationally, if indeed he became perfect man, lacking none of what is ours, save only sin; but having all that we have, without omission — the things constitutive according to nature of our essence, as also those of the essence without beginning and divine, through which he made himself known as properly being man and God at once, and as preserving perfectly after the union the natural kinship both toward us and toward the Father.

For to say that only at the time of the saving passion, when he was really figuring what is ours in himself, and as man was refusing death, he was shown to have two wills, seems to me assertive, not demonstrative. For how only at this time, and not before it, and for what cause? For if he had it then, he had it also from the beginning, from when he became man, and did not improvise it later; but if he did not have it from the beginning, neither did he at the time of the passion, but he only feigned the refusal, and besides it the rest through which we have been saved: the tear, the prayer, the grief, the agony, the cross, the death, the burial. For if one of the natural things like ours that are preserved in him is annulled, neither will the others stand. For how would that not, and these would? And what is the account? And what kind of opposition is it to pray, and voluntarily to make a display of natural weakness — of the flesh's, I mean — through contraction, and not to resist at all, but to say, If it be possible,[^2] and, Not what I will, but whatever thou;[^3] and to join to the contraction the strong and eager movement against death? For he was really figuring what is ours in himself through the natural agony for a little while [?],[^4] [197] so that he might free us from this too, and confirm the nature of his own flesh, and make the economy pure of phantasy for all; but at once he presented again the very great impulse against death, and the utmost growing-together and union of his human will with his own and the Father's will, by deciding for this and saying: Not mine, but thine be done:[^5] in this repelling division, in that, in turn, confusion.

But if they say these things because he does not appear at other times to have made a reflection of willing,[^6] except at the time of the passion, let them say this also of his innate intellection, and they will make him mindless. For he does not appear to have said, I have understood. And likewise of smell, and of the rest of the natural things like ours, of which he is not recorded to have made mention. Or also of the soul itself, as not existing earlier in existence, from when he became man, but only when he made mention of it; for he is not declared to have done this long before the passion.[^7] Either then let them say that it was improvised at that moment, because he did not mention it often and from the beginning; or, not saying this, but teaching, with the great Athanasius, «At once flesh, at once flesh of God the Word, animate, rational»,[^8] they will say that the Saviour's natural will as man was not only at the time of the passion, even if he then made mention of it, but co-essentiated with the Saviour from the beginning naturally, as with a man like us; and let them cease at last stirring up and gathering such things out of season. For the present time is one of blood, on account of our sins, and not of doctrines;[^9] and of strong lamentations, for the entreating of God, and not of sophistical rebuttals, to his greater indignation against us.

It occurs to me also to wonder about operation: how they teach that this is one in Christ, by regard and reference, as they themselves say, to the individual. And — to pass over saying that none of the natural things has its reference primarily to the individual, but to its nature and essence; for who could operate anything without the powers that belong naturally to the nature? or whence has he operating or being able, apart from the nature? — this I wish to ask: was it, then, for the destruction of the natural operations that they put this forward, or for what? For if for destruction, how, when, and what kind of operation would there be at all upon their cessation? For as, natures not being, what is from them altogether vanishes as well — I mean the composite hypostasis — so too, the natural operations not being, there will be no other at all. And I do not say this as though another operation were constituted from the natural ones. For there is no composition at all of things in a subject, because it presents not their existence by themselves at all, but what is random.[^10] [200] So that if they say that those which properly are according to nature are not, how will what in no way is exist? For who is there that speaks of a hypostatic operation, and whence is this, and from whom have they received it that they bring it forward?

But if perhaps they declare this, and confess the hypostasis to which it is referred to be composite, and receptive of contraries and opposites: the operation of the same will also, in all likelihood, be mortal and immortal, visible and invisible, circumscribed and uncircumscribed, without beginning and under a beginning. And how otherwise than plainly by habit and privation? And the one set it will undergo as being, the other, as not being, it will bring into a parasitic subsistence.[^11] And how will he too who has such an operation not undergo like things, in the same respect and not in one and another, since neither does it? For it is altogether necessary that the one operating cleave connaturally to his own operation, and it to him. And how is he God who is so by habit, and not God by nature? And how man, who is this by privation, and not properly by essence? And what nature is without operation, or apart from natural operation? For as it is in no way bereft of existence, so neither of natural power. And if it has no share in this, it would have no share in existence either. For the powerless, as altogether inert, is only non-being. For every being whatever has a constitutive difference, the innate movement, taken together with the genus and making the definition of the subject, through which it is properly known that it is and what it is; having invariance toward things of the same species, and in turn difference toward things of other species. And if this is so, how is it possible for the Word become man, having no share of natural power according to the flesh, to be styled perfect man, or man at all? For man is not without natural operation, as neither is any other nature without its own and essential operation. For the lack of this either makes the nature not even to be a nature, or makes all natures the same as one another, and one in place of all, commixed and thoroughly confused by the failing of what constitutes them.

For if they say that the Word is not without operation in what is ours, it is plain that they say he has an innate and human operation. For how else? For it is not possible to operate without natural operation, as neither to exist without essence and nature. But neither is it altogether necessary that the one operating have only one innate operation, if he be twofold in nature; otherwise the one subsisting will altogether have one nature too, even if he be composite in hypostasis. For if that follows because of the relation, this too altogether follows because of the same. For we see this relation borne singularly in both cases: for both the operation is referred back to the one operating, and the nature in turn to the one subsisting. They ought, then, if because of the union and the singleness of the person they say the operation of the one operating is one, for this reason to declare the nature too of the one subsisting one, lest they be caught teaching a relational union, saying it is a union of operations and not also of natures. For it is not possible for them to unite these to a hypostasis, but only to an essence and nature, so far as concerns the [201] natural operations, these being united to it in nothing else but operation.[^12] Otherwise, as the natural difference of these, according to them, makes the operating ones two, so surely, altogether, will the innate otherness of the essences also infer the subsisting ones to be two. Either then let them commix these too into one nature; or let them in no way introduce division into those, which are essences naturally and are numbered. For what does not happen in those would never occur in these either. But the natural difference is shown through their number; and division departs through the utmost union. For it is not possible for a divine or human nature to be known even to be, let alone to differ from another, without essential operation. For the definition of the thing is properly the principle of its essential power; which being destroyed, the subject too is altogether destroyed with it. And for this reason we confess these preserved naturally in the Word incarnate: the one shown in the flesh's onset upon divine things, the other again in the experience of human things, with authority;[^13] so that, together with these, we may confess the natures too, whose essential operations they are.

To whom is it not plain that in the case of one and the same essence and nature one cannot see a difference of existence or of natural operation? For none differs from itself; for this is somehow impossible. But in an individual and person, everywhere and altogether, if it be composite: for we see both the different natures from which it is constituted, and the natural movements by which, operating according to nature, it is known, confirming its own existence; and through the natural operation according to the one and the other it presents in fact that the things from which it is are other and other: through which we find that the assault of him who looks to it — I mean to the individual — and who willingly passes the natures by in order to destroy the natural operations, does not weigh upon us in this more than it works with us. For if the individual is not altogether enclosed in one natural operation, as indeed not in one nature either — for who says so, or is able to show it? — it is plain that it has essential movements equal in number to the natures, that is, the essences, in it; and no one will gainsay it. And I pass over saying that the composite person of Christ is not properly called an individual either. ## Notes
[^1]: T: ἕξις and στέρησις, "habit" and "privation", Aristotle's pair (*Categories* 10): the will aspires to possess what constitutes the nature and to be deprived of what harms it.
[^2]: S: Mt 26:39.
[^3]: S: Mk 14:36 (οὐ τί ἐγὼ θέλω, ἀλλὰ τί σύ).
[^4]: T: the Latin here is fuller, *naturali tantisper mortis formidini ejusque anxio timori cedens*, "yielding for a little to the natural fear of death and its anxious dread"; a participial phrase seems lost between p0116.53 and p0117.01. [?] marks it; logged as `doubt`.
[^5]: S: Lk 22:42.
[^6]: T: ἔμφασις, the glossary's "reflection": here an outward showing of the faculty.
[^7]: S: cf. Mt 26:38; Jn 12:27 ("my soul is troubled").
[^8]: S: the formula ἅμα σάρξ, ἅμα Θεοῦ Λόγου σάρξ, ἅμα σὰρξ ἔμψυχος λογική, cited under Athanasius's name in the dyothelite florilegia; not located in his genuine works.
[^9]: P: probably the Arab conquests of the 640s, which Maximus elsewhere reads as chastisement for sin.
[^10]: T: τὸ εἰκαῖον, "what is random", that is, what is without ground (Latin *debile quidpiam vanumque*).
[^11]: T: παρυποστήσει, from παρυπόστασις, the "parasitic subsistence" of what has no being of its own (cf. Dionysius, *Divine Names* 4.31).
[^12]: T: at the foot of col. 200 (p0118.58) the text carries a rubric, Ὅτι οὐκ ἔστιν ὑποστατικὴ ἐνέργεια, "That there is no hypostatic operation": a marginal summary, not part of the running text, and not rendered here.
[^13]: T: ἐν τῇ σαρκὸς προσβολῇ τῶν θείων: the divine operation shown when the flesh sets about divine works (Latin *qua caro, quae divina essent, aggrediebatur*); the human shown in his undergoing human things "with authority", that is, of his own will.
[^14]: T: Porphyry's ladder: γενικώτατον γένος, "most generic genus"; ὑπάλληλα γένη, "subaltern genera"; εἰδικώτατον εἶδος, "most specific species".
