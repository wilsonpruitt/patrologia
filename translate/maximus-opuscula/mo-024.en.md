[224] OPUSCULUM 19 — SOLUTION OF THE SECOND ABSURDITY

*Of the same holy Maximus, solution of the second absurdity.*[^1]

As for showing that the Fathers speak of natural wills, we shall show it then, when they are able to hearken, or, to put it more properly, are willing to, who have stopped up the hearing power of the soul toward all divine and Fatherly things. For if they are willing to cleanse this, as from some passion, of strife, and clearly, or rather without passionate attachment, to fix the eye of the understanding according to reason on the many-lit and holy radiance of the teaching handed down from the Fathers, they will surely understand that they name natural wills; both from the fact that all expressly say and teach in common: «A natural appetite, and an innate appetite, and the flesh's having naturally the not willing to die; for this is a condemnation of nature. And how, he says, is the will common to both? And where would the difference of nature be shown by the one will? For there is every necessity that the wish concur with the nature, and that the nature not wish to die, but cling to the present life. And just as hungering and thirsting is no charge, so neither is aspiring to the present life. And that the fondness for things present lies in nature.» And: «The flesh which he assumed for us having, with all the things that belong to it naturally, also the deprecating of death.»[^2] These are the doctrines of Athanasius, and Basil, and Gregory, and John, and Theophilus, and Cyril, the ever-memorable, and of the other approved Fathers.

What greater demonstration than these, that they declare natural wills? And what contradiction, and from whom, when there is no ambiguity in these sayings, or any concealment whatever? We then do not on this account innovate expressions, as the adversaries say, but confess Fatherly words; nor do we, understanding them toward our own aim, make coinages of names; for this is a bold thing, and the work [225] of a heretical and mad understanding, an invention; but as they were understood and spoken by the saints themselves, so we piously bring them forward.

But what room, and whence, will they have for not altogether understanding thus, but opining otherwise and contrary to these, if indeed they wish at all to be pious, and not to swerve from the Fatherly teaching? For if they will altogether have hypostatic, that is, personal wills, shrinking from the natural ones,[^3] they will also introduce personal otherness; or if wills contrary to nature, they also make a doctrine of the falling-away of the essences, driving the natures, or rather themselves, into non-existence. For there is nothing of the personal, nor of those contrary to nature, if indeed we shall at all grant that any such thing may be said, since it is not known at all, after the departure of the wills according to essence. For whether divine and human, or deifying and deified, or created and uncreated, or however else they may wish to call them, we are led piously to name the wills in Christ natural, and not otherwise, wishing to signify the natural otherness of those from which he is composed. For if, according to the Fathers, there is every necessity that the wish concur with the nature, and it is not possible to call the will common, plainly, to both the essences, nor is the difference of nature shown by the one will, it is clear that it is altogether necessary to say two natural wills; so that, according to the Fathers, the difference of the essences in Christ may be known without confusion and without division.

What account, then, have they against these things, or what coinage of names would they fashion for themselves, understanding the Fatherly words toward their own aim? And what personal wills would they wish the pious man to choose, falling away from the natural wills? And who will bear the cutting-up of the one into persons? But the wills contrary to nature? And who would bear the pit of vice and of non-existence into which they drive themselves little by little? Only, for this we owe them thanks: that, having let go of fighting the number of the wills, they have turned to the natural account of them; which perhaps we too concede, that they name them as they wish, falling away in this respect from the number, until, learning the absurdity from the incongruous appellation, and to what unprofitable end it would lead them, they too should be persuaded to confess natural wills according to the Fathers.

And who would not marvel also at their shifting about? How, using certain circuits and double courses, they contrive to remove the great mystery of the divine economy toward us? For at one time, bringing the will against the natural operations, through one, as they say, introducing for themselves the one operation, they went after the two operations; at another time bringing ignorance against the wills, as though the will departed together with it, and were reckoned among the things outside; at another time [bringing] the complete [228] negation of all the natural things, through the appropriation,[^4] and, as I said, the whole overthrow of the mystery; all these being taken by them in his case in a mere relation, and not known naturally, according to union, as belonging to God incarnate as man; when they ought not to stand outside the truth, but rather to converge with it through all things and advocate it, according to the assurance, leaving nothing out, of the natures of the same and one Christ our God, and of the things belonging to the natures: a sincere and most firm confession; to whom be glory, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, unto the boundless ages. Amen.

## Notes
[^1]: T: the second of Theodore's two difficulties (Opusc. 18, col. 216–217): that "natural wills" is a phrase the Fathers never used. Maximus answers it here, again to Marinus.
[^2]: S: a chain of patristic sayings without separate lemmas, which Maximus assigns jointly to Athanasius, Basil, Gregory, John (Chrysostom), Theophilus and Cyril of Alexandria; not located individually. «He says» (φησι) is singular in the Greek.
[^3]: T: Migne prints δ... ούμενοι [δυσωπούμενοι] (confirmed on the plate), a lacuna with the editor's supplement; rendered with the supplement ("shrinking from", "abashed at").
[^4]: T: διὰ τῆς οἰκειώσεως, "through the appropriation", the opponents' κατ᾿ οἰκείωσιν of Opusc. 18 (see the note there).
