[268] OPUSCULUM 24 — THAT ONE CANNOT SAY ONE WILL IN CHRIST

*Of the blessed Maximus. That it is impossible to say one will in the case of Christ.*

The name «Christ» is indicative not of a nature, but of a composite hypostasis. That is, the whole Christ is both Lord and God and almighty, having in himself also the flesh which, for us and for our salvation, he wore without division and without confusion: flesh passible and not almighty, created, visible, circumscribed, the flesh not almighty by nature, but having an almighty will in Christ. For Christ is not mortal and immortal by hypostasis; nor again powerless and almighty, visible and invisible, created and uncreated; but the one by nature, the other by hypostasis. And, to speak simply, not by contrariness of inclination, but in property of nature. For Christ is one, as I said before, having each naturally.

For the words «Not as I will, but as thou»[^1] show nothing other than that he truly put on flesh, fearing death. For to fear death, and to shrink back, and to be in agony, are proper to it. Now, then, he leaves it destitute and stripped of its own operation, so that, having shown its weakness, he may confirm its nature as well. But now he does not hide the operation, so that thou mayest learn that he was not a mere man. For just as, if he had shown the human things throughout, this is what he would have been thought; so, if he had always accomplished the things of the Godhead, the account of the economy would have been disbelieved. For this reason he varies and mixes both words and deeds, so that he may give a pretext neither to the disease and madness of Paul of Samosata nor to that of Marcion and Manichaeus. For this reason also he foretells what is to be as God, and shrinks back again as man.[^2]

Thou who refusest to say or to confess two wills in the one and only Christ: this one will which thou sayest of him, is it without beginning, and co-without-beginning and co-eternal with the Father and the Holy Spirit, or wholly divine in quantity, simple and incomposite, as being of the divine essence? Or does it seem to thee something different because of the becoming man? Show of what sort, and tell us what is the name of this will. For the name of the will before the becoming man, that is, of the divine will, I myself indicate. For just as the divine nature, the three-hypostatic, exists without beginning, uncreated, incomprehensible, simple, and incomposite in wholeness, so too does its will. Behold, then, I have said the will before the becoming man; and all will speak in agreement with me, as having spoken the truth; and thou thyself, whether thou wilt or wilt not. Tell us, then, the will after the becoming man: what name has it? Search the whole Old and New Testament. Tell thou the name of this one will devised in Christ. [269] Search, and do not shrink.

But then, since the divine will is called divine, and the human will is called human, wouldst thou say that Christ has a theandric will?[^3] I do not think so; since the Father and the Holy Spirit do not have a theandric will. But then wouldst thou dare to say «composite»? Likewise this again is novel to the Godhead. But then wouldst thou say «natural»? Thou too wilt confuse, after Severus;[^4] for it is impossible for two natures or natural wills to become one nature or one natural will without confusion. But «hypostatic»? Then again thou wilt alienate the Son from the Father, and thou wilt appear to introduce three volitions not concurring with one another, as the hypostases also do not.

If thou sayest the one volition and operation as of one, thou wilt be compelled to say the volition and operation of the Father as of one, and that of the Spirit as of one, whether thou wilt or wilt not, and the account will be found falling into polytheism. If thou sayest also «relational», thou introducest the personal division of Nestorius. And if thou sayest also «contrary to nature», thou corruptest the existence of the one willing; for what is contrary to nature is a corruption to the nature, as the Fathers defined. But then wilt thou again tear the monad to pieces, and set us moving to pebbles, to two-thirds and thirds[^5] of the one will held by thee, so that we may find out how far it is divine and how far human? Likewise also how far uncreated, and how far created, and, in short, whatever it is dear to thee to say. Tell us the name of the will which is championed by thee in Christ. And if the one will which thou sayest in Christ is, according to thee, nameless, rank it with the nameless psalms,[^6] so that there at least it may find room and place. Since, most learned one, we cannot confess anything nameless. And, as is likely, until now it has not been made manifest to any of the earth-born.

Engrave, then, by way of definition, from the fathers' teachings, and give us in outline the declaration of the name. «For what we count, these we also confess,» according to the great and God-revealing Basil.[^7] I say that the life-giving flesh of the Son of God had, in God the Word himself, all the things inherent in its own nature, the two natures contributing into one person and one hypostasis of God the Word. For one Son, as one of the holy Trinity, we worship together with the Father and the Holy Spirit, as before the ages, now and unto all the ages, and after the ages of ages. Amen.

OPUSCULUM 25 — TEN CHAPTERS ON THE TWO WILLS, WRITTEN TO THE ORTHODOX

*Of the same. Chapters on the two wills of our Lord and God and Saviour Jesus Christ. Written to the orthodox.*[^8]

1. If, just as the Word is of one hypostasis with [272] the flesh, so also of one will, according to some, O blessed ones, it is necessary that he differ both from us and from the Father, as in hypostasis so also in will. And how will the identity of nature toward the Father be shown by the difference of the wills?

2. In what the Word has oneness with the flesh, in these he is separated from the Father; and in what he has oneness with the Father, in these he is distinguished from the flesh. But if he has one will with the flesh, according to some, O blessed ones, therefore he has been separated from the Father in will.

3. If in what the Word is united to the Father and the Spirit, in these he is judged to differ from the flesh; and he is united, according to the common opinion of Christians, in will to the Father and the Spirit; therefore by the will of the flesh he differs from the Father and the Spirit,[^9] as the Fathers themselves defined for themselves and for us, and as we, O blessed ones, are eager to keep.

4. This one will, it is necessary for them, O blessed ones, to say is either natural or hypostatic; since the constitution of beings is considered in essence and hypostasis. For what else beside these would there be to say, [for those to whom it was not permitted]? But if they say it is natural, they will also say one nature of the Word and the flesh, even unwilling. And if hypostatic, they will be compelled, because of the holy three hypostases, to say three wills also, if indeed they have resolved to be consistent with themselves.

5. If the will of the nature of the Word is creative of all things that have come to be; and this, according to them, O blessed ones, is also the will of the flesh; then the flesh of the Word too will manifestly be creator of the things that have come to be. And thus the common opinion of Christians, that it is created, is destroyed. For how is that a creature which creates from things that are not, and has the distinctive idiom of the divine nature?

6. If, because of the one will of Father and Son and Holy Spirit, their one nature too was shown by the God-possessed Fathers; and there is, according to them, O blessed ones, one will of the Word and of the flesh; then thus there will be one nature also of the Word and of the flesh. But if, in the case of the flesh and the Word, the one will does not infer the one nature, neither in the case of Father and Son and Holy Spirit will the one will infer the one nature; and our faith is empty, empty the preaching of the Fathers.

7. This one will is either elective or natural. But if natural, there will also be one nature, as has already been shown, of the flesh and the [273] Word; and if elective, Christ will be separated from the Father and the Spirit, as in hypostasis, so also in inclination.

8. If, according to the great Cyril,[^10] the things which are of the same essence and nature are also of the same will, how can the things which are not of the same nature be of the same will?

9. If, according to John the golden mouth,[^11] the flesh has not willing to die as natural; and the Word united it to himself without change according to hypostasis with all the idioms belonging to it naturally; how do they, denying what belongs to it naturally, not deny together with the natural idioms the nature also?

10. If they say that a person too is introduced together with the will, and on this account, fearing a fear where there was none,[^12] they do not endure to say two wills in Christ, lest two persons also be necessarily introduced along with them, they will be forced, if indeed they wish to show the account of their own doctrine coherent, either, because of the one will of the Godhead, to say one person too, since according to them a person follows the will by necessity; or, because of the three persons, to say three wills too, and to introduce the coalescence of Sabellius and the division of Arius.

But since, O blessed ones, homonymy often becomes a cause of error, the hearer passing over to another signification than the one the word indicated, I beseech your understanding in Christ, for the sake of a well-distinguished and unconfused declaration of the things signified, to make clear to you[^13] before all things whether there is will at all, and of what significations, that is, things, such a name is predicated; and whether some of the things called by it are composite, others simple; and which are the composite. And whether, then, the composite have their parts contemporaneous or not contemporaneous; and which are prior in origin to which, and which posterior to which. And what is the demonstrative and definitive account of each part, so that we may, understanding it, accept the one will. For the account among the Fathers is not about sounds, but about things. Whence we have found the Fathers conceding sounds, but concepts not at all. And besides these, what is choice, and what inclination; what is a will of inclination, and what an elective will? For all these must concur toward the presentation of the wills. And he who does not know these with precision, and holds forth about wills, is blind, short-sighted,[^14] knowing neither what he says nor about what he makes assertion.[^15]

[276] OPUSCULUM 26 — FROM THE QUESTIONS PUT TO HIM BY THEODORE THE MONK

*Of the same. From the questions put to him by Theodore the monk.*[^16]

Nature is, according to the philosophers, a principle of movement and quiescence; but according to the Fathers, a species predicated of many things differing in number, in answer to «of what sort is it?»[^17] Essence is, according to the philosophers, a self-subsistent thing not needing another for its constitution; but according to the Fathers, the true being, and that natural,[^18] of many things differing in hypostases. An individual is, according to the philosophers, a gathering of idioms whose sum cannot be considered in another; but according to the Fathers, such as Peter or Paul, or any other of those who are by themselves, marked off from other men by his own personal idioms. And a hypostasis is, according to the philosophers, essence with idioms; but according to the Fathers, each man individually, marked off personally from the other men.

*Of the holy Irenaeus, bishop of Lyons, disciple of the holy John the apostle and evangelist, from the discourses on faith to Demetrius, deacon of Vienne,[^19] whose beginning is: «Seeking God, hear David saying.»*

«The volition and operation of God is the making and providing cause of every time and place and age, and of every nature. Volition of the intellectual soul is the principle in our power, as being its self-determining power. Volition is appetitive mind, and appetite of understanding inclining toward what is willed.»[^20]

*Of Clement the Stromatist, from the discourse On Providence.*[^21]

«Volition is a natural power, appetitive of what is according to nature. Volition is a natural appetite, appropriate to the nature of the rational. Natural volition is the self-determining movement of a sovereign mind; or mind moved by self-choice about something. Self-determination is mind moved according to nature; or a sovereign intellectual movement of the soul.»

[277] *Of the holy Alexander, pope of Alexandria, from the letter against the Arians to Aeglon, bishop of Cynopolis.*[^22]

«Natural volition is the self-chosen power of every intellectual being, as having nothing involuntary in respect of essence.»

*Of the holy Eustathius, patriarch of Antioch, from the discourse On the Soul.*[^23]

«Volition is a natural movement of the reason within us. Volition is rational and vital appetite.»

*Of the holy Athanasius, pope of Alexandria, from the greater discourse On Faith.*[^24]

«Volition is a self-determining movement of an intellectual soul. The first idiom of the intellectual soul, and indicative of its essence, is volition. The mind of the Lord is not yet the Lord, but either volition, or wish, or operation.»

*Of the holy Gregory, bishop of Nyssa, from the discourse Against Apollinarius.*[^25]

«Volition is a certain mind, and a disposition toward something. Self-determination is a sovereign movement of the will. Volition of the intellectual soul is its choosing with authority among the things of inclination, through elective volition. Volition of every rational nature is its life according to nature. The privation of life so understood is the destruction of essence.»

*Of the holy Diadochus, bishop of Photice, from the sixth chapter of the Ascetica.*[^26]

«Self-determination is the volition of a rational soul coming about without hindrance toward whatever it wishes.»

*Of the holy Anastasius, patriarch of Antioch.*[^27]

«Will is a word turning in the heart, able to be made manifest through operation alone. Will is a wish of the soul unable to be checked [except] from within.»

*Of the holy Nemesius, bishop of Emesa.*[^28]

«Volition is a deliberative appetite of things in our power and able to come about through us, and having an uncertain end.»

*Of the same: In what respect man is rational, and what is the inward word, and what the uttered.*[^29]

«The inward word is the motion of the soul which comes about in the rational power itself, without any utterance; whence often, being silent, we go through a whole discourse within ourselves, and converse in dreams. And in respect of this above all we are all rational; but not as in respect of the uttered word. For those deaf from birth, and those who have lost their voice through some affliction or also disease, are no less rational. But the uttered word has its operation in the voice and in the languages.»

[280] *Of the holy Maximus, from his syllogisms on wills: what is a natural will, and what a will of inclination?*[^30]

«Natural will is the impulse and aspiration, holding simply and in the same way, of the things constitutive according to nature. But will of inclination is the qualified and differing movement and appetite of the things that rouse toward pleasure.»

*Of the same, from the letter to Marinus the presbyter: What is volition, and what choice?*[^31]

«They say that will is natural, that is, volition.» Look back likewise in the same letter, concerning counsel and inclination and prudence and opinion and authority.[^32]
