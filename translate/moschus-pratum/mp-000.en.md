title: The Spiritual Meadow, dedication and chapters 1–10
---
[2852] DEDICATION

John Eviratus[^1]

The sight of meadows in spring, beloved, I find full of much delight, which the manifold budding of the flowers affords to those who behold it. It holds back those who pass by, and feasts them in many ways: in one part it gladdens the eyes, in another it now sweetens the sense of smell. For one part of this meadow, as it may be, glows with the redness of roses; another spot may belong to the lilies, well able to draw the beholder from the roses to themselves; from elsewhere flashes the colour of violets, a likeness of royal purple. And altogether the varied and diverse grace of the numberless flowers, of fragrance and of delight, springs up on every side.

Such, then, consider the present work to be, holy and faithful child Sophronius. For thou wilt find in it the virtues of holy men who have shone out in our times, and who, according to that saying of the Psalm, are planted by the outgoings of the waters;[^2] all of them, by the grace of Christ our God, alike dear to God, but one adorned at one time, another at another, with beauty and comeliness in different virtues. From these I have plucked the fair flowers, and taking a woven crown of an undefiled meadow I offer it to thee, most faithful child, and through thee to all. That is why we have called this present work the Meadow, for the delight and the fragrance in it, and the profit to those who read it. For virtue of life and a grave manner of living are not only the study of divine things, and right thinking, and lingering the more lovingly over the things that are here;[^3] but also setting down in writing the ways of life of others. That is why I have pressed on to the present compilation, assuring your love, child, that I have made a large and true collection, drawing up, as with the wisest bee, the soul-profiting achievements of the Fathers. From here, then, I shall begin to tell these things thus.

CHAPTER 1

There was an elder living in the monastery of abba Eustorgius, whom our [?] archbishop of Jerusalem, who is among the saints, wanted to make superior of the monastery.[^4] But he would not accept, saying, "I want to go to Mount Sinai, for prayer." The archbishop kept pressing him to become superior first, and then go away. But the elder would not bear it, and he let him go, on his promise to take up the headship after his return. And so, having embraced the archbishop, he went out, making his journey to Mount Sinai, and took his own disciple. And when they had crossed the river Jordan, about a mile on, [2853] [b: A] the elder began to shiver and to run a fever. As he could not walk, they found a small cave, and went into it so that the elder could rest. As he went on in the fever, and could scarcely even move (for he spent three days in that same cave), the same elder sees in his sleep someone saying to him, "Tell me, elder, where dost thou want to go?" He says to the one who appeared to him, "To Mount Sinai." He says to him, "Don't, I beg thee, don't go." When he did not persuade the elder, he withdrew from him. And the fever pressed on the elder more heavily. Again, the next night, the same one stands by him, in the same form, saying, "Why dost thou want to wear thyself out, good elder? Listen to me, and go nowhere." The elder said to him, "And who art thou?" The one who appeared answered him, "I am John the Baptist; and that is why I tell thee, go nowhere. For this small cave is greater than Mount Sinai. Many times our Lord Jesus Christ came into it, visiting me. So give me thy word that thou wilt dwell here, and I give thee health." The elder gladly accepted, and gave his word that he would stay in the cave. And at once he was made well, and he stayed in it to the end of his life. He made that cave a church, and gathered a brotherhood too. It is the place called Sapsas. On its left lies the torrent Chorath, to which Elijah the Tishbite was sent in the time of the drought, the one opposite the Jordan.[^5]

CHAPTER 2

[b: C] Another elder was in that same place of Sapsas, and he came to such virtue that he received the lions that came into his cave, and set food before them in his lap. So full of divine grace was the man of God.

CHAPTER 3

We visited the laura of our father among the saints Sabas, and abba Athanasius. The elder told us: "When we were at the coenobium of Penthucla, there was one Conon by name, a Cilician, priest for the baptism.[^6] And as the elder was a great man, they appointed him to perform the baptisms, and he himself anointed and baptized those who came for it. So whenever he anointed a woman he was troubled, and for this reason he wanted to withdraw from the coenobium. So whenever he had the thought of withdrawing, holy John would stand by him, saying, 'Endure, and I will relieve thee of the warfare.' Then one day a Persian girl came to be baptized. She was so fair, so very lovely, that the priest could not bring himself to anoint her with the holy oil. And when she had waited two days, archbishop Peter heard of it, and was astonished at what had happened, and wanted to appoint a deaconess for the purpose.[^7] But he did not do it, because the custom did not allow it. The priest Conon took [2856] his sheepskin and withdrew, saying, 'I will not stay in this place.' So as he went out onto the hills, behold, holy John the Baptist meets him, and in a gentle voice says to him, 'Go back to thy monastery, and I will relieve thee of the warfare.' Abba Conon says to him in anger, 'Believe me, I will not go back; for many times thou hast promised me, and done nothing.' So holy John took hold of him and sat him down on one of the hills, and uncovering his clothes he sealed him three times with the sign of the cross below the navel, and said to him, 'Believe me, priest Conon, I wanted thee to have a reward from the warfare; but since thou dost not want it, I have relieved thee of the warfare, and thou hast no reward for this matter.' And the priest Conon went back to the coenobium of the baptism, and the next day he anointed and baptized the Persian girl, without noticing at all that she was a woman by nature. And he went on another twelve years anointing and baptizing, neither stirred in his body nor any more noticing a woman according to nature. And so he was perfected."

CHAPTER 4

Abba Leontius, of the coenobium of our holy father Theodosius, told us: "After the new laurites were driven out of the New Laura, I went and settled in that same laura. One Sunday I went down, I went to the church to communicate; and going in I saw an angel standing on the right side of the altar. And seized [with fear][^8] I withdrew to my cell. And a voice came to me, saying, 'Since this altar was consecrated, I have been entrusted to remain at it.'"

CHAPTER 5

Abba Polychronius told us: "At the laura of the Towers by the Jordan I used to see one of the brethren there neglecting himself, and never completing the rule of holy Sunday. Then, after some time, I see that same man who had been so neglectful applying himself with all diligence and with great eagerness. So I say to him, 'Thou art doing well now, brother, taking care of thine own soul.' He says to me, 'Lord abba, I am about to die now.' And after three days he went to his rest."

CHAPTER 6[^9]

The same abba Polychronius, priest of the New Laura, told me: "Once when I was living in the laura of the Towers, a brother died, and the steward says to me, 'Do me a kindness, brother: come, so that we may carry [2857] the brother's belongings to the steward's store.' So as we began to move the belongings, I see the steward weeping. [b: A] I say to him, 'Truly, lord abba, why art thou weeping so?' He says to me, 'Because today I carry the brother's belongings, and after two days others will carry mine.' And so it happened. For on the second day the same steward went to his rest, as he had said."[^10]

Abba Polychronius the priest told us that he had heard from abba Constantine, superior of Holy Mary the Theotokos, the New Church: "One of the brethren went to his rest in the hospital of Jericho, and we took him and carried him [b: B] to the Towers, to bury him there. And from the time we came down from the hospital until we reached the Towers, a star did not cease to travel along above the dead man, and to shine, until we laid him in the earth."

CHAPTER 7

An elder lived in the laura of the Towers. Now the priests of that laura and the rest of the brethren, since their superior had been perfected, wanted to make him superior, as he was a great man and well-pleasing. But the elder kept begging them, saying, "Allow me, Fathers, to weep for my sins. For I am not such as to take care [b: C] of souls; this is the work of the great Fathers, abba Antony and those with him, and the rest." So every day the brethren would not let him be, entreating him; and he would not be persuaded. When he saw himself pressed by them for a long while, he says to them all, "Let me pray for three days; and whatever God wills, that I have to do." It was a Friday. And on the Sunday, at dawn, he went to his rest.

CHAPTER 8

There was an elder in the laura of the Towers named Myrogenes, and [he came] [?] to such hardness of life that he became dropsical.[^11] And he kept saying to the elders who visited him to care for him, "Pray for me, Fathers, that my inner man may not become dropsical. For myself, I pray God that I may stay a long time in this sickness."

Eutychius [?], archbishop of Jerusalem, hearing about abba Myrogenes, wanted to send him what he needed, and he never accepted anything at all from him, but said, "Pray for me, Father, that I may be delivered from eternal punishment."[^12]

[2860] CHAPTER 9

In that same laura of the Towers an elder lived. He had no possessions at all, and he had besides the gift of almsgiving. So one day a poor man came to his tower asking for alms. The elder had nothing but one loaf, and he brought it out and gave it to the poor man. The poor man says to him, "I don't want bread, but a garment." And the elder, wanting to satisfy him, took him by the hand and brought him into his tower. And the poor man, finding nothing at all except what he was wearing, was struck with compunction at the elder's virtue, and untied his own sack, and emptied out into the middle of his cell all that he had, saying, "Take these, good elder; I will find my keep elsewhere."

CHAPTER 10

[b: B] There was an anchorite in the caves of the holy Jordan, named Barnabas. One day as he was going down to drink from the Jordan, something stuck in his foot, and he went on with the splinter in it, not allowing a doctor to see it. And his foot rotted, and he was compelled to take a tower for himself at the Towers. And his foot went on rotting for a time. And he used to say to all who visited him that as much as a man suffers outwardly, so much does the inner man flourish.

After abba Barnabas the anchorite had gone up from his cave to the Towers, and had spent some time there, another anchorite goes off to his cave. And going in he sees an angel of God standing at the altar which the elder had made in that very cave, and consecrated. And the anchorite says to the angel, "What art thou doing here?" He says to him, "I am an angel of the Lord, and since it was consecrated, it has been entrusted to me by God."

## Notes
[^1]: P. The heading names the author John Eviratus, Migne's form for Moschus's byname; the addressee is his disciple Sophronius, later patriarch of Jerusalem.
[^2]: S. Ps 1:3.
[^3]: T. *περὶ τὰ τῇδε μᾶλλον ἐμφιλοχωρεῖν*, rendered as printed; the sense of *τὰ τῇδε* ("the things here") is unclear, and the Latin paraphrases the whole clause (*fidesque recta*).
[^4]: T. The doubled article (*ὁ ἐν ἁγίοις ἡμῶν ὁ ἀρχιεπίσκοπος*) wants a name between, and the Latin has *sanctus Elias*; see the OCR table. The Latin also names the elder *Joannes*, which the Greek here does not.
[^5]: S. 1 Kgs (3 Kgdms) 17:3. P. Sapsas and the torrent Chorath (Cherith) lie east of the Jordan, opposite Jericho.
[^6]: T. The Latin reads *Alexandrinus genere*; the Greek has *Κίλιξ*, Cilician.
[^7]: P. Peter, patriarch of Jerusalem 524–552.
[^8]: T. *ληφθεὶς* stands alone; "with fear" is supplied from the sense (Latin *magno pavore correptus*).
[^9]: T. No head is printed before this story or before chapters 8 and 10 in our Greek. Numbers 6, 8 and 10 are set by the sequence (V and VII, IX and the Latin CAPUT X); the star story is taken as part of chapter 6, as the Latin links it with *enim*.
[^10]: T. The Latin has *tertia die*; the Greek says the second day.
[^11]: T. The clause has no verb; probably *ἤλασεν*, as in chapter 2 (*εἰς τοσαύτην ἤλασεν ἀρετήν*), has dropped out.
[^12]: T. The Latin names the archbishop *Eustochius*, who was patriarch of Jerusalem 552–563; the Greek as given reads *Εὐτύχιος*. Rendered as printed, marked for the plate.
