title: The Spiritual Meadow, chapters 26–32
---
[2872] CHAPTER 26

[b: B] An elder lived in the laura of Calamon, which is at the holy Jordan, named Cyriacus; and the elder was great in the sight of God.[^1] A brother from abroad visited him, from the country of Dara, named Theophanes, to ask the elder about the thought of fornication.[^2] And the elder began to anoint him with words about chastity and purity. So the brother, greatly profited, says to the elder, "Lord abba, in my own country I communicate with the Nestorians, and for this reason I cannot stay; otherwise I would have stayed with thee." When the elder heard the name of Nestorius, he was grieved at the brother's perdition, and he admonished him, and begged him to depart from this harmful heresy and to come to the holy catholic and apostolic Church. And he said to him, "There is no other salvation but to think rightly and to believe that the holy Virgin Mary is in truth the Theotokos." The brother says to the elder, "Truly, lord abba, all the heresies say the same: 'Unless thou communicate with us, thou art not saved.' So what I am to do, I do not know, wretch that I am. Pray to the Lord, then, that he may assure me by a deed which is the true faith." The elder received the brother's word with joy, and he says to him, "Stay in my cell, and I have hope in God that his goodness will reveal the truth to thee."[^3] And leaving the brother in his cave, he went out towards the Dead Sea, praying for the brother. And about the ninth hour on the second day, the brother sees someone standing over him, fearful to look on, and saying to him, "Truly, [2873] [b: A] come and see the truth." And taking him, he carries him off to a dark and stinking place, with fire, and shows him in that very fire Nestorius and Theodore, Eutyches and Apollinarius, Evagrius and Didymus, Dioscorus and Severus, Arius and Origen, and some others. And the one who appeared says to him, "This place is prepared for the heretics, and for those who blaspheme the holy Theotokos, and for those who follow their doctrines. So if the place pleases thee, abide in thy doctrine; but if thou dost not want to make trial of this punishment, come to the holy catholic Church, in which the elder also teaches. For I tell thee, if a man does all the virtues and does not glorify God rightly, he comes to this place." And at this word the brother came to himself. And when the elder came back, he told him everything that had happened, as he had seen it. And he went and communicated with the holy catholic and apostolic Church. And he stayed with the elder at Calamon; and having spent a good number of years with him, he fell asleep in peace.[^4]

CHAPTER 27

Ten miles from the city of Aegeae in [b: B] Cilicia there is an estate named Mardardus [?].[^5] In it there is a church of holy John the Baptist, and in it an elder, a priest, lived. The elder was very great and virtuous. So one day the people who lived on that estate come to the bishop of Aegeae against him, saying, "Take this elder away from us, for he is a burden to us. Sunday comes, and he holds the synaxis at the ninth hour, and does not keep the established order of the holy synaxis." And the bishop took the elder aside and says to him, "Good elder, why dost thou do so? Or dost thou not know the ordinance of the holy Church?" The elder said to him, "In truth, my great lord, it is so, and thou sayest well. But what to do, I do not know. For after the night office of holy Sunday, [b: C] I sit near the holy altar; and until I see the Holy Spirit overshadowing the holy altar, I do not begin the synaxis. But when I behold the descent of the Holy Spirit, then I perform the liturgy." And the bishop, wondering at the elder's virtue, and having assured the people of the estate, sent them away in peace, glorifying God.

To this elder abba Julian the Stylite sent a greeting, and sent him also a cloth tied up, with three coals of fire inside it. And the elder, receiving the greeting and the coals unquenched, sent back to abba Julian in the same cloth, after putting water in it and tying it up. They were about twenty miles apart from each other.

[2876] CHAPTER 28

Abba Cyril [?], the disciple of the aforesaid abba Julian the Stylite, told this:[^6] "I and my father and my brother came from our country to abba Julian, hearing what was said of him. For I had," he says, "an incurable affliction, which none of the doctors had been able to cure. So when I came, the elder prayed and cured me at once. So the three of us stayed with him, and renounced the world. And the elder put my father in charge of the grain. So one day my father comes and says to abba Julian, 'We have no grain.' The elder answered, 'Go, brother, gather up what thou findest and grind it, and as for tomorrow, God will take care for us.' At the word he was troubled (for he knew that he had left nothing in the granary), and he withdrew to his cell. As the need was pressing, the elder sends word to him: 'Come here.' So when he had barely come, the elder says to him, 'Brother Conon, go, and what thou findest, make it ready for the brethren.' And he, half in anger, took the keys of the storeroom and went off, meaning to bring the dust. So he undid the lock and tried to open the doors, and could not; for the granary was quite full of grain. And seeing it, he made a prostration to the elder, glorifying God."

CHAPTER 29

About thirty miles from Aegeae, the city of Cilicia, there are two stylites, about six miles apart from each other. Of these the one communicated with the holy catholic and apostolic Church; the other, who had also spent the longer time on the pillar, near an estate called Cassidora, was of the heresy of Severus. And the heretic condemned the orthodox one in various ways, [2877] [b: A] setting upon him and wanting to draw him over to his own heresy. And sending him many words, he seemed to be getting the better of him. But the orthodox one, as though inspired from God, sent word to him to send a portion of his communion. And he, with joy, as though he had already led him astray, sends it to him at once, not hesitating at all. And the orthodox one received the portion sent to him by the heretic, that is, by the Severian; he had a cauldron boiled before him, threw in the portion he had received, and it dissolved in the boiling of the cauldron. And taking also the holy communion of the Orthodox Church, he threw it into the same cauldron, and at once the boiling cauldron cooled, and the holy communion remained whole and unwetted. And he keeps it still. He showed it to us when we visited him.

CHAPTER 30

[b: B] Tadae is a trading-town of Cyprus.[^7] In it there is a monastery near the one called Philoxenus's. So visiting there, we found in it a monk, a Melitenian by race, Isidore by name. We saw him weeping without ceasing, with lamentation. And when he was entreated by all to give up his mourning a little, he would not be persuaded, but said to all, "I am a great sinner, such as there has not been from Adam until today." And when we said, "Truly, lord abba, in truth no one is without sin but God alone," he answered, saying, "Believe me, brethren, I have not found among men a sin such as I have not done, neither written nor unwritten. But if you think that I am accusing myself idly, hear my sin, so that you too may pray for me.

"When I was in the world," he says, "I had a wife. And the two of us were of the doctrine of Severus. So coming home one day, I did not find my wife, but I hear that she has gone off to the neighbour's to communicate; and that woman communicated in the holy catholic Church. And I ran at once to stop her. So going into the neighbour's house, I found my wife, having just now received the holy portion, and communicating. So I seized her by the throat and made her vomit up the holy portion. And taking it, I hurled it down; and it fell into the mud. And at once I saw lightning in that very place take up the holy communion. And after two days, I see an Ethiopian man, clothed in rags, and saying to me, 'I and thou together have been condemned [2880] to one punishment.' And I say to him, 'Who art thou?' And the Ethiopian who appeared answered me, saying, 'I am he who struck on the cheek the Maker of all things, our Lord Jesus Christ, in the time of the Passion.'[^8] So for this reason," says the monk, "I cannot give up weeping."

CHAPTER 31

Two of the elders were going from Aegeae to Tarsus in Cilicia; and coming to an inn, by the dispensation of God, so that they might rest (for it was hot), they found there three young men going to Aegeae, who had a harlot with them. So the elders sat apart. And [b: B] one elder took the holy Gospel out of his wallet and was reading. And the harlot who was with the young men, when she saw the elder reading, left the young men and came and sat down near the elder. But the elder shook her off and says to her, "Thou seemest very shameless, wretched woman: wast thou not ashamed to come and sit near us?" She answered, saying, "Don't, Father, don't abhor me. For even if I am full of every sin, yet the Master of all, our Lord and God, did not thrust away the harlot who came to him."[^9] The elder answered her, "But that harlot did not stay a harlot any longer." She says to him, "I hope in the Son of the living God that from this day I too do not stay in this sin." And leaving the young men and all her things, she followed the elders. And they put her in a monastery near Aegeae, the one called Nakkiba's. I too have seen her, an old woman, living with great good sense; and from her I heard these things.[^10]

CHAPTER 32

There was a mime in Tarsus of Cilicia, named Babylas. He had two lady-friends (the name of the one was Cometo, and the name of the other Nicosa), and he lived prodigally, doing everything that was worthy of the demons working with him. So one day he went into the church; and by the dispensation of God the Gospel was being read, in which was the passage that says, "Repent: for the kingdom of heaven is at hand."[^11] And pierced at this, he began with tears to bewail himself for the things he had done. And at once he went out of the church, and calling his two lady-friends he says to them, "You know how I lived with you in prodigality, and that I [2881] [b: A] never preferred one above the other. And now behold, you have everything I got for you; take also all that is mine, and divide it. For from today I am going away; I renounce the world, and I become a monk." But they, as from one mouth, answered him, saying with tears, "We shared with thee in sin and in the perdition of our souls; and now, when thou wilt do this work pleasing to God, dost thou leave us and do it alone? Truly thou dost not leave us so: we share with thee in the good too." And the mime straightway shut himself up in one of the walls of the city's tower [?];[^12] and they sold their things and gave to the poor, and taking the ascetic habit themselves too, they made a cell for themselves near the tower and shut themselves in. ## Notes
[^1]: P. Calamon, a laura by the Jordan near Jericho. T. The name is printed *Κυικός*; read *Κυριακός* with the Latin *Cyriacus*.
[^2]: P. Dara, the fortress-city on the Persian frontier in Mesopotamia (Latin *Dora*).
[^3]: T. The Latin turns this into a command (*spem in Deo habeto*, "have hope in God"), as if from *ἔχε*; the Greek as given has *ἔχω*, "I have", and that is rendered.
[^4]: T. The Latin reads four years (*quatuor … annis*) for the Greek *ἔτη ἱκανά*, "a good number of years".
[^5]: P. Aegeae (Ayas), a port of Cilicia. T. The Latin spells the estate *Mardandos*; rendered as printed, marked for the plate.
[^6]: T. The Latin names the narrator *Cyriacus*; the Greek as given reads *Κύριλλος*. Rendered as printed, marked for the plate. The father is named Conon below.
[^7]: T. The Latin reads *Dade*. P. Melitene, in Armenia Minor.
[^8]: S. John 18:22 (cf. Matt 26:67).
[^9]: S. Luke 7:37–50.
[^10]: T. The Latin adds *Erat autem illi nomen Maria*, "her name was Mary"; the Greek has no name.
[^11]: S. Matt 4:17.
[^12]: T. *εἰς ἕνα τῶν τειχῶν τοῦ πύργου* does not agree (masculine *ἕνα* with neuter *τειχῶν*) and reads oddly; the sense is probably "one of the towers of the city wall", as the Latin has it (*in una turrium civitatis*). Rendered as printed, marked for the plate.
