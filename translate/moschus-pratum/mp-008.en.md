title: The Spiritual Meadow, chapters 72–76
---
[2924] CHAPTER 72

Abba Palladius told us, saying: "An old man, a layman, was arrested for committing a murder. And being punished under the law of Alexandria, he said that another man had been with him as his partner in the murder. The one the old man named was a young lad of about twenty. So both of them were punished severely. And the old man kept saying, 'Thou wast with me when I did the murder.' But the young man said he had nothing to do with the murder, nor had he been with him. So when they had been much punished, they received sentence that both be hanged on the fork. So they went off to the Fifth;[^1] for there [2925] [b: A] they are accustomed to punish such men. And there is, about one stade off, a ruined temple of Kronos. So when they came to the place, the people and the soldiers wanted to hang the young man first on the gibbet. So he made a prostration to the soldiers and said, 'For the Lord's sake do me a kindness, and hang me facing east, only so that I may look that way as I hang.' The soldiers said to him, 'Why?' He said to them, 'Truly, my lords, I have had, wretch that I am, only seven months since I received holy baptism and became a Christian.' When the soldiers heard it they wept over the young man. But the old man, enraged, said loudly, 'By Serapis, make me face Kronos.' Then, when the soldiers heard the old man's blasphemy, they let the young man be and hanged the old man first. And as they were hanging the old man, behold, a horseman sent from the Augustal[^2] said to the soldiers, 'Do not kill the young man, but bring him.' So that there was joy among all the soldiers standing by. And they led him off and brought him into the praetorium; and the Augustal released him. And the young man, saved beyond hope, went away and became a monk." We have written this down for the profit of many.

CHAPTER 73

[b: C] Abba Palladius told us this too, saying:[^3] "There was a soldier of Alexandria named John, who had this way of life. Every day, from dawn until the ninth hour, he sat at his monastery, near the steps of Saint Peter's, alone, wearing sackcloth and weaving baskets [?],[^4] keeping silent and saying nothing at all to anyone. He sat in a house of prayer doing his handiwork, saying only this, with a chant: 'Lord, cleanse me from my secret faults,[^5] so that I may not be put to shame when I pray.' Having said this verse he would keep silent for a long hour; and again after the hour he would take up the same verse. He said it seven times [?] through the whole day, saying nothing else at all.[^6] And at the ninth hour he took off the sackcloth, and [?] put on the soldier's dress,[^7] that is, his clothes, and so went off to his own standards. I too stayed with him eight years, and was greatly profited by his silence and his settled ways."

CHAPTER 74

Admonishing us, the elder said this to us too: "Believe me, children, nothing else has made the schisms and the heresies in the holy Church but this: that we do not greatly love God and one another."

[2928] CHAPTER 75

[b: A] The same abba Palladius told us on another occasion, when we visited him, something like this: "There was a Christ-loving man in Alexandria, very devout, merciful, and one who received the monks as guests. He had a wife too, she also very humble, who fasted every day; and he had a little daughter, about six years old. So one day this Christ-loving man went abroad to Constantinople, for he was a merchant. So, leaving his wife and the child, and one slave, in the house, he went off to sail. As he was leaving for the ship, his wife said to him, 'To whom dost thou leave us, my lord?' Her husband said to her, 'To our Lady the Theotokos.' So one day, as the woman sat working, with the child beside her, the slave, worked on by the devil, wanted to murder the woman and the child, take whatever they had, and flee. And taking a knife from the kitchen, he went towards the dining room where his mistress was. So when the lad reached the doorway of the dining room, he was seized with blindness, and could go neither into the dining room nor back to the kitchen. When he had spent about an hour so, struggling and forcing himself to get in, he began to call his mistress, saying, 'Come over here.' She wondered that he stood in the middle of the doorway and did not come to her, but called out; and she said to him, 'Thou come here, rather,' not knowing that he was held by blindness. The lad began to adjure her to come near him. But she swore not to go to him. He said to her, 'Then at least send me the child.' She would not do that either, saying, 'If thou wilt, come thyself.' Then the lad, since he could do nothing, struck himself with the knife and cut his own belly open. His mistress, seeing what he had done, cried out; and the neighbours came in; and at once the men of the praetorium came too, and they found the lad alive, and learned everything from him; and they glorified God, who had shown wonders and saved the mother and her child."

CHAPTER 76

The same abba Palladius told us this too, saying: "I heard a shipmaster tell something like this: 'One time, as I was sailing with passengers, men and women, we came out onto the open sea; and while everyone else was sailing well, some for Constantinople, some for Alexandria, the rest elsewhere, the wind being favourable for them, we alone could not sail, and we stayed about fifteen days without moving from the place where we were. So we were in great despondency and perplexity as to what this could be. And I, as shipmaster, having the care of the ship and of everyone in it, began to beseech God about this. And one day a voice came to me, invisibly, saying, [2929] [b: A] "Put Mary over the side, and thou wilt sail well." And I wondered what this could be, and who Mary is. As I was at a loss about it, again the voice came to me: "I have told thee: put Mary over the side, and you will be saved." Then I thought of something of this sort: I cry out all at once, "Mary!" For I did not know who Mary was. And she, lying on her mattress, answered, saying, "What dost thou bid, my lord?" Then I say to her, "Do me a kindness, come over here." She got up and came. So when she came, I took her aside and say to her, "Seest thou, sister Mary, what sins I have, that because of me you are all going to perish?" She groaned deeply and said, "Truly, my lord shipmaster, I am the sinner." I say to her, "Woman, what sins hast thou?" And she: "Woe is me, for there is no sin I have not done, and because of my sins you are all about to perish."' Then the woman told me, he said, something like this: '"Truly, my lord shipmaster, I, wretch that I am, had a husband, and two children by him, one about nine years old and the other about five; and after that my husband died, and I remained a widow. A soldier lived near me, and I wanted him to take me to wife; and I sent some people to him. But the soldier said, 'I do not take a woman who has children by another man.' Then, when I heard that he would not take me because of the children, and loving him as I did, I slaughtered my two children, wretch that I am, and let him know: 'See, now I have no one.' When the soldier heard what I had done about the children, he said, 'As the Lord lives who dwells in heaven, I will not take her.' So, fearing that this would become known and I would die, I fled."' Having heard this from the woman, I did not even so want to throw her into the sea; but I contrived something, he said, and I say to her: '"Look, I am getting into the skiff; and if the ship moves, know, woman, that it is my sins that are at work in the ship."' Then, he said, I call the boatman, saying, '"Bring the skiff in."' So when I had got down into the skiff, nothing more: neither the ship nor the skiff moved. Then I climbed back into the ship and said to the woman, '"Thou too go down into the skiff."' She went down; and as soon as she went down, at once the skiff turned about five times and went straight to the bottom. And the ship sailed so well that in three and a half days we finished a voyage that would have taken us fifteen days.'"

## Notes
[^1]: P. The Fifth, the fifth milestone outside Alexandria. The Latin reads *quinto extra urbem milliario*.
[^2]: P. The Augustal, the prefect of Egypt, at Alexandria.
[^3]: T. Chapter numbers. The Latin gives LXXIII here and the Greek head is lost. The numbers 74–76 come from the sequence, with no head surviving except the garble at p0077.50. "Νουθετῶν δὲ ἡμᾶς" is taken as its own short chapter (74). If it belongs to 73, the next two chapters are 74–75.
[^4]: T. μαλάκια is not a basket word I know; the English follows the Latin *sportellas*.
[^5]: S. Ps. 18:13 (LXX).
[^6]: T. ἕβδομον is "seventh"; the Latin *septies* suggests ἑπτάκις or ἑβδομάκις, which the English follows.
[^7]: T. ὃ ἐφόρει τὸ στρατιωτικὸν σχῆμα does not construe as printed. The Latin *exuebat cilicium, induebatque militarem habitum* wants καὶ ἐφόρει, and the English follows the Latin sense.
