title: The Spiritual Meadow, chapters 77–79
---
[2929] CHAPTER 77

One day the lord Sophronius and I went off to the house of Stephen the sophist, to study.[^1] It was midday. He lived by the church of the holy Theotokos which the blessed pope Eulogius built, the one called Dorothea's.[^2] So when we knocked at the philosopher's house, a girl peeped out and said, "He is asleep; but wait [?] a little."[^3] Then I say to the lord Sophronius, "Let us go to the Tetrapylon, and wait there." Now the place of the Tetrapylon is held in great reverence by the Alexandrians:

[2932] [b: A] for they say that Alexander, the founder of the city, took the remains of Jeremiah the prophet from Egypt and laid them there.[^4] So when we went to the place, we found no one but three blind men, for it was midday. So we went near the blind men quietly and in silence, and sat down with our books. And the blind men were talking to one another, and one says to the other, "Tell me truly, how didst thou become blind?" And he answered, saying, "When I was young I was a sailor; and we were sailing from Africa, and out at sea I got a disease of the eyes, and having no way to be treated, I got white films on my eyes, and was blinded." He says to the other one too, "And thou, how didst thou become blind?" That one also answered, saying, "I was a glassblower by trade, and from the fire both my eyes got a flux, and I was blinded." The two of them say to the other as well, "And thou, how didst thou become blind?" And he answered, "Truly, I will tell you. When I was young I hated work, hated it badly; and I became a wastrel besides. So having nothing to eat from then on, I took to stealing. So one day, after I had done many evil things, I was standing in the market-place, and I see a dead man being carried out to burial, finely dressed. So I follow behind the funeral, to see where they were going to bury him. And they went behind Saint John's, and put him in a tomb, and went away. And when I saw they had gone, I went into the tomb, and stripped him of whatever he was wearing, leaving him one linen cloth only. So as I was about to come out of the tomb, with a great deal taken, my evil habit says to me, 'Take his linen cloth too, for it is a good one.' So I went back, wretch that I am; and as I was stripping the linen cloth off him, to leave him naked, the dead man sits up in front of me, and stretching out his two hands at me, with his fingers he raked my face and tore out my two eyes. Then I, miserable man, left everything, and came out of the grave in great distress and danger. There: I too have told you how I became blind."

When we had heard this, the lord Sophronius nods to me, and we withdrew from them. And he says to me, "Truly, lord abba, let us not study today; for we have been greatly profited." So, being profited, we have written it down, so that you too, hearing it, may be profited. For truly, no one who does evil escapes the notice of God. And as for this tale, we heard it with our own ears from the man to whom it happened.

CHAPTER 78

Abba John, superior of the monastery of the Giants, told us another such story when we came to him at Theoupolis:[^5] "A little while ago a young man came to me, saying to me, 'For God's sake take me in, for I want to repent.' And he said this with many tears. And I, when I saw him so broken, and in great despondency, say to him, 'Tell me, how hast thou come to such compunction?' And he says

[2933] to me, 'Truly, lord abba, [b: A] I am a great sinner.' And I say to him again, 'Believe me, child, just as sins are many and various, so too the cures are many. But if thou wouldst be healed, tell me thy deeds in truth, so that I may apply the penances that fit them.[^6] For the fornicator is treated one way, and the murderer another, and the sorcerer another; and the help for the covetous man is different again.' And he groaned deeply and beat his breast, and was choked with tears and groans; and from the great turmoil in his heart he could not even let out his voice freely. And when I saw him fallen thus into helplessness and unspeakable grief, and not able at all to tell out his own misfortune, I say to him, 'Child, listen to me, [b: B] and force thy thought a little, and tell the thing. And Christ our God can give thee his help. For he who for his unspeakable love of man and his measureless compassions suffered all things for our salvation, and ate with publicans, and did not turn away the harlot, and received the thief, and was called the friend of sinners, and at the last accepted the cross as well: he will receive thee too, repenting and turning, with his own hands, rejoicing. For he does not desire the death of the sinner so much as that he should turn and live.'[^7]

"Then, forcing himself, and holding back his tears a little, [b: C] he says to me, 'I, lord abba, full of every sin, unworthy of heaven and of earth: two days ago I hear that the daughter of one of the leading men of this city, a virgin, had died, and been buried with many garments in a tomb outside the city. When I heard it, being in the habit of this lawless practice, I go off by night to the tomb, and I began to strip her. And I stripped her of everything she was wearing, not sparing even her inner shift, but robbing her of that too, and making her naked as she was born. Then, as I was about to come out of the grave, she sits up in front of me, and stretching out her left hand she took hold of my right hand, and says to me: "So, man, was it right for thee to strip me naked like this? Didst thou not fear God? Dost thou not dread the judgement of the retribution to come? Oughtest thou not to have pitied me, if only as a dead woman? Hadst thou no reverence even for our common nature? A Christian, and didst thou judge it fit that I should stand before Christ naked like this, with no reverence even for my nature? Did not that very nature bear thee? Hast thou not outraged thine own mother along with me? What defence, wretchedest of men, hast thou to give for me before the dread judgement seat of Christ? For while I lived no stranger saw my face, and thou, after death and burial, hast stripped me, and gazed on

[2936] [b: A] my naked body. Alas for mankind,[^8] to what misery it has come! With what heart, or with what hands, dost thou come forward and partake of the all-holy Body and Blood of our Lord Jesus Christ?"

"'When I heard this, and saw it, I was seized with dread and terror, and trembling I could hardly say to her, "Let me go, and I will not do it again." And she says to me, "Truly, as thou wouldst, thou camest; as thou wouldst, thou shalt not go out of here. No: the grave shall be common to us both. And do not think that thou wilt die at once; but for many days in torment thou shalt give up thy soul, an evil man by an evil death." And I begged her with tears to let me go, swearing many oaths by God almighty that I would not do this lawless and wicked deed again. Then, after I had pleaded with her much, and shed many tears, she answered and says to me: "If thou wilt live, and be delivered from this plight, give me thy word that if I let thee go, thou wilt not only leave off these foul and profane deeds, but wilt go at once, this very moment, and renounce the world, and become a monk, and repent of what thou hast done, and serve [?] Christ."[^9] And I swore to her, saying, "Not only will I do whatever thou tellest me, but from this very day I will not set foot in my house: from here I go to a monastery." Then the girl says to me, "Dress me as thou foundest me." And when I had laid her out, she fell back again and died. So at once I, wretch and sinner, came out of the tomb and came here.'

"When I had heard this from the young man, I anointed him with words on repentance and self-control, and after a time I tonsured him; and clothing him in the monastic habit, I shut him in a cave on the mountain inside the city, giving great thanks to God and striving for his own soul."

CHAPTER 79

When we came to Seleucia by Antioch, we met abba Theodore, bishop of that same city of Seleucia; and he told us: "In the time of the blessed Dionysius, my predecessor as bishop of this city, something of this kind happened. There was a merchant here, very devout and rich; but he was a heretic, of the doctrines of Severus. And he had a steward who was in communion with the holy catholic and apostolic Church. This steward, by the custom of the country, received Communion on Holy Thursday. And putting it in a cloth,[^10] he laid it away in his own cupboard. Now it happened after holy Easter that the steward was sent to Constantinople on business, and through forgetfulness he left the holy Communion in his cupboard. And he had given the key of the cupboard to his master. So one day the master opened the cupboard and found the cloth with the holy Communion in it. And he was distressed at this, and at a loss what to do with it. For he could not bear to partake

[2937] of it, [b: A] because it was of the catholic Church and he was of the heresy of Severus. And for the time being he left it in the cupboard, reckoning that the steward would come back and partake of it. But when the holy day of Great Thursday came round, and the steward had not come back, the master wanted to burn it, so that it should not stay on into a second year; and opening the cupboard he sees that all the holy portions had sprouted ears. And seized with great fear and trembling at the new and wonderful sight, he took the holy portions, and with all his household, crying 'Lord, have mercy,' he ran to the holy church, to Dionysius the bishop, now among the saints. And this great and fearful wonder, beyond all speech and all mind and all thought, was seen not by two, [?] three,[^11] nor only by a few that could be counted, but by the whole church: citizens and country people, natives and strangers, those who travel by land and those who sail the sea, men and women, old men and children, young and old, masters and servants, rich and poor, rulers and ruled, wise and simple, those in the clergy, those in virginity, those in the ascetic life, those in widowhood, those in honourable marriage, those in authority, those in power. And some cried out 'Lord, have mercy,' and others sang praises in other ways. And all gave thanks to God for his unspeakable and wonderful works of wonder. And many, believing because of this wonder, came over to the holy catholic and apostolic Church."

## Notes
[^1]: T. πράξωμεν, "do our lessons": the Latin has *doctrinae gratia*. P. Stephen the sophist (also "the philosopher" below) is Stephen of Alexandria, the Alexandrian philosopher.
[^2]: T. The Latin adds that the church stood to the east, by the great Tetrapylon. The Greek lines here do not have it.
[^3]: T. ἐξικακήσατε does not construe (no such form). Rendered by the sense of the Latin, *oportet exspectare pusillum*. See the OCR table.
[^4]: P. This follows the tradition that Jeremiah died in Egypt and that Alexander moved his remains to Alexandria.
[^5]: P. Theoupolis, "City of God", the official name of Antioch after 528.
[^6]: T. ἐπιτίμια: penances a spiritual father lays on a penitent.
[^7]: S. Ezek 18:23; 33:11. Earlier in the sentence: Matt 9:10–11 (publicans); Luke 7:37–50 (the harlot); Luke 23:40–43 (the thief); Matt 11:19 (friend of sinners).
[^8]: T. This reads ἀβάλε, an interjection ("alas"); the Latin has *Vae*. The band letter A stands at the same spot.
[^9]: T. δουλευθεὶς, a passive participle, does not fit the run of the future verbs. The Latin has *serviens Christo*. Rendered by the sense.
[^10]: T. μουζίκιον, a cloth (Latin *linteo mundissimo*).
[^11]: T. The Greek as given reads οὐχ οἱ δύο, οἱ τρεῖς; the Latin has *non unus, aut duo, aut tres*. Something may be lost.
