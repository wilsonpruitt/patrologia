title: The Spiritual Meadow, chapters 164–173
---
[3032] CHAPTER 164

A brother visited abba Victor the solitary at the Laura of Elusa, and said to him: "What shall I do, Father? For the passion of faintheartedness lords it over me."[^1] The elder answered: "It is a sickness of the soul. For just as people with sore eyes, the more they suffer, think they see more light, while the healthy see little; so too the faint of soul are quickly set alight by a little faintheartedness, and think the faintheartedness great; but those who are healthy in soul rejoice the more in temptations."

CHAPTER 165

The Christ-loving man told us about a robber named Cyriacus, that he used to rob around Emmaus, which is Nicopolis; and he became so savage and inhuman that they called him the Wolf. He had with him others robbing too, not only Christians but also Jews and Samaritans. One day some people came from an estate in the district of Nicopolis to the holy city in Great Week, to have their children illumined.[^2] When they had had them illumined and were going back to their own estate to keep the holy day of the Resurrection, the robbers met them, the chief robber not being there. The men took to flight; but the Hebrews and the Samaritans seized the women, threw down the newly illumined, and had them. As the men were fleeing, the chief robber met them and asked them, "Why are you fleeing?" They told him what had happened to them. So he took them and came to his companions, and found the children thrown on the ground. And learning who had done it, he beheaded those who had done the abomination; and he made the men take up the children, since they could not bear to touch the women because of the defilement upon them; and the chief robber brought them safe as far as their estate. A little while after, the chief robber was caught, and he spent ten years in prison, and none of the governors put him to death. Afterwards he was even released. And he used always to say: "Because of those children I escaped a bitter death. For I used to see them in my sleep, saying to me, 'Fear not; we are pleading for thee.'" Abba John, the priest of the coenobium of the Eunuchs, and I met him, and he told us the same things, and we glorified God.

CHAPTER 166

[Abba Sabbatius used to say: "When I was living in the monastery of abba Firminus, a robber came to][^3] [3033] [b: A] abba Zosimus the Cilician, and besought the elder, saying, 'Do me a kindness, for God's sake, since I am guilty of many murders: make me a monk, so that from now on I may rest from my evil deeds.' The elder admonished him and made him a monk, giving him also the holy habit. A few days later the elder said to him, 'Believe me, child, thou canst not stay here; for if the governor hears of it, he will arrest thee; and likewise thy adversaries [?][^4] will come and kill thee. But listen to me, and I will take thee off to a coenobium far from here.' And he took him to the coenobium of abba Dorotheus, near Gaza and Maiuma. And he spent nine years there, and learned the Psalter and the whole order of the monastic life; and he came up again to Firminus's, to the elder, and said to him, 'Lord abba, [b: B] have mercy on me, and give me my clothes, my worldly ones, and take the monastic ones.' The elder was grieved and said to him, 'Why, child?' He answered, saying, 'Behold, as thou knowest, Father, I have nine years in the coenobium; and as far as in me lay I fasted, and kept self-control, and lived in subjection with all stillness and fear of God; and I know that His goodness has forgiven me my many evils. But every time I see a boy saying to me, "Why didst thou kill me?" I see him in my sleep, and in church, and in the refectory, saying these things to me, and he does not give me one hour's peace.[^5] So for this reason, Father, I want to go, so that I may die for the boy. For I killed the child for nothing.' So he took the clothes and put them on, and went out of the laura, and went off to Diospolis; and the next day he was arrested and beheaded."

CHAPTER 167

Abba Agathonicus, superior of the coenobium of the Castellium of our holy father Sabas, used to say: "One day I went down to the Ruba to visit abba Poemen the Grazer.[^6] And when I found him, I told him my thoughts. When evening came, he left me in a cave. It was winter, and that night there was a great frost, and I was bitterly cold. So in the morning the elder came and said to me, 'What is it, child?' I said to him, 'Forgive me, Father, I had a hard night from the cold.' And he said to me, 'Truly, child, I was not cold.' I was amazed to hear it, for he was naked. And I said to him, 'Do me a kindness: how wast thou not cold?' He said, 'A lion came and slept beside me, and it warmed me. But I tell thee, brother, I am to be eaten by wild beasts.' I said to him, 'Why?' He said to me, 'Because when I was in our country' (both of them were Galatians), 'I was a shepherd of sheep, and I overlooked a stranger passing by, and my dogs ate him; and though I could have saved him, I did not save him, but let him be, and the dogs killed him. And I know that I too must die so.'" And three years later the elder was eaten by wild beasts, according to his word.

[3036] CHAPTER 168

Abba Alexander the elder, abba Vincent's elder, used to say to the brethren: "Our fathers pursued the deserts and afflictions; but we pursue the cities and ease."

The elder said again: "In the days of our fathers these virtues flourished, poverty and lowliness; but today love of money and pride hold sway."

He said again: "Our fathers never once washed their faces; but we even open the public baths."

The elder said again: "Woe is me, children, we have wiped out an angelic way of life." His disciple, abba Vincent, said to him: "We are truly weak, Father." The elder said: "What sayest thou, Vincent? 'We are weak'? Believe me, child, in body we are like Olympic athletes; it is our soul that is weak."

He said again: "We can eat and drink much and dress well; but we cannot keep self-control and be humble."

The elder said again: "Woe to thee, Alexander, woe to thee: how shalt thou be put to shame, when others are crowned!"

CHAPTER 169

There was an elder in Scetis, blind in his eyes, in the laura of abba Sisoes; and his cell was about two-thirds of a mile from the well. And he never allowed anyone else to fill water for him; but he made a rope, and tied one end of it to the well and the other to his cell, and it lay on the ground. So when he went to fill water for himself, he walked along on top of the rope. The elder did this so that he would come out at the well itself. When the sand from the wind covered the rope, he would take hold of the rope with his hand and shake it out, and lay it again on the ground, and walk along on top of it. So a brother begged the elder to let him bring him water. And the elder answered: "Truly, brother, I have been filling water for myself like this for twenty-two years, and dost thou want to take my toil from me?"

CHAPTER 170

There is a monastery some distance from Jerusalem [?].[^7] Two of the fathers went to Mount Sinai [3037] [b: A] to pray. And when they came back to the monastery they told us, saying: "After we had worshipped at the holy mountain, it befell us on the way back to lose our way in the desert; and we were carried along in the desert as on the open sea for many days. One day we saw a little cave far off, and we made for it. Drawing near the cave, we saw by it a very small spring, and around the spring a few herbs, and the tracks of a man; and we said to ourselves, 'Truly a servant of God is here.' So we went in and saw no one; only we heard someone groaning. After we had searched the place a long while, we found something like a manger, and someone lying in it; and drawing near the servant of God, we begged him to speak to us. When he did not answer us, we took hold of him. And his body was still warm, but his soul had departed to the Lord. Then we knew that as we were entering the cave he had gone to his rest. So we took his body from the place where it lay, and we dug a grave in the cave itself; and one of us took off the pallium he had on, and we wrapped the elder's body in it, and in it we laid him out for burial. And we found that it was a woman. And we glorified God. And having said the office over her, we buried her."[^8]

CHAPTER 171

In Alexandria there were two wonderful and virtuous men, abba Theodore the philosopher and Zoilus the reader; and we had much acquaintance with both of them: with the one on account of studies, with the other because we had a common homeland and way of life. Abba Theodore the philosopher owned nothing but a tunic-cloak[^9] and a very few books, and he slept on a bench in whatever church he happened on at the hour. Later he renounced the world at the coenobium of Samala, and there he finished his life. Zoilus the reader too had the same poverty. For neither did he own anything but a tunic-cloak, and that a very old one, and a few books; and he gave his time to fine copying. And he too, perfected in the Lord, was buried at Lithazomenon,[^10] in the monastery of abba Palladius.

About this abba Theodore the philosopher and Zoilus the reader, some of the fathers visited master Cosmas the scholasticus[^11] and asked him, saying: "Which of the two held out more in the labours of ascesis?" He said to them: "Common to both were the food, the bedding, the clothing, and the abstaining from everything superfluous, [3040] and the lowliness, and the poverty, and the self-sufficiency. But abba Theodore the philosopher, barefoot, with a great weakness of the eyes, learned the Old and the New by heart; yet he had as his consolation the company of the brethren and the visits of friends, and much distraction, when he was at work and when he was expounding. Of Zoilus the reader, though, not only his life as a stranger is worthy of praise, but also his solitude, and his measureless toil, and the guard on his mouth, and that he was friendless, owned nothing of his own, kept no company [?],[^12] in total idleness from all worldly business; and he gave himself over to no consolation, and would not accept even the barest service from anyone: he cooked for himself, he washed for himself, not even having the refreshment of reading; ready besides to serve others, having no care for cold or heat or bodily sickness; ever clean of laughter and gloom and accidie and dissipation; and besides the roughness of his garment, he was eaten away by a multitude of lice, unremittingly [?].[^13] But this one too, beyond the first, had no small recreation from going out, having full freedom to go out all night and all day; yet the measurelessness of his toil cut short even this freedom, and amid much company he showed himself set apart [?][^14] from men of the world. So each will receive his own reward according to his own toil, and according to the measures of the resurrection, and of purity, the spiritual and that of the mind, and of fear of God, and of love, and of liturgy, and of compunction, and of undistracted psalmody and prayer, and of earnest faith, and of that pleasing of God which is hidden from men and unseen."

CHAPTER 172

About this master Cosmas the scholasticus many told us many things, others other things, and very many very many things. But what we were eyewitnesses of, and followed closely, these we write, for the profit of those who read. For the man was humble, merciful, self-controlled, a virgin, still, without anger, a lover of his companions, hospitable, a lover of the poor. So this wonderful man brought us great profit, not only by being seen and by teaching, but also because he had more books than anyone in Alexandria, and gladly lent them to those who wished. He also owned nothing; in his whole house there was nothing else to be seen but books, and a bed, and a table. Anyone might go in, and ask about the things that profit, and read. Every day I used to go in to him, and, in all truth, I never once went in without finding him either reading or writing against the Jews; for he had great zeal to turn the Hebrews to the truth. That is why he often sent me [3041] [b: A] to certain Hebrews, so that I might dispute with them from Scripture, because he himself did not readily go out of his house.

I visited this master Cosmas the scholasticus one day, and since I had great freedom with him, I said to him: "Do me a kindness: how long hast thou been keeping stillness?" When he kept silent and answered nothing, I said to him again: "For the Lord's sake, tell me." He held back a little and said to me: "I have thirty-three years." When I heard it, I glorified God.

Another time I visited him again and questioned him, saying: "Do me the kindness in full, knowing that I ask thee for my soul's profit: tell me, in so long a time of stillness and self-control, what hast thou achieved?" He groaned deeply from the depth of his heart and said to me: "What can a layman achieve, and above all sitting in his own house?" But I besought him again, saying: "For the Lord's sake, tell me, and profit me." Then, hard pressed by me, he said to me: "Forgive me: these three things I know that I have achieved: not to laugh, not to swear, not to lie."

CHAPTER 173

There was an anchorite in the region of the holy Jordan named Theodore, a eunuch. He was obliged by some need to go down to Constantinople, and so he went aboard a ship. And as the ship lingered at sea, the water ran out, and the sailors and passengers were in great despondency and helplessness. The anchorite rose and stretched out his hands to heaven, to God who saves our souls from death;[^15] and he made a prayer, and sealed the sea with the sign of the cross, and said to the sailors, "Blessed is the Lord: draw as much water as you need." And they filled all the vessels with the sweet water from the sea, and all glorified God.

## Notes
[^1]: T. ὀλιγωρία (thrice in the chapter, beside οἱ ὀλιγόψυχοι) is rendered "faintheartedness"; the Latin has *pusillanimitas*. Real reading, not corrected.
[^2]: T. φωτίζω / νεοφώτιστος, "illumine / newly illumined": baptism, received at Easter.
[^3]: T. The Greek of our witness begins at *τὸν ἀββᾶν Ζώσιμον*; the chapter head and opening clause (bottom of col. 3032 or top of 3033) are absent from the lines given. The bracketed words translate the Latin (*Dicebat abbas Sabbatius: Cum sederem in monasterio abbatis Firmini, venit latro quidam ad*), not Greek. Plate check needed.
[^4]: T. *ἀντιφαντιασταί* is no word I can place; the Latin has *adversarii*, and the sense is surely "adversaries / enemies".
[^5]: T. The Latin adds *et cum ad communionem pergo*, "and when I go to communion", which the Greek does not have.
[^6]: P. βοσκός, "grazer": one of the monks who lived on wild plants. The Ruba is the desert by the Dead Sea; the Castellium is Sabas's foundation on the hill of Hyrcania.
[^7]: T. The Greek reads *Μοναστήριόν ἐστιν ἀπέχον ἀπὸ Ἱεροσολύμων.,* and goes straight to *δύο τῶν Πατέρων*; a line seems lost. The Latin: "a monastery about twenty miles from Jerusalem, called Sampson's; from this monastery two fathers…". The chapter head is also missing from the Greek.
[^8]: T. κανών here is the burial office.
[^9]: T. στιχαροφελόνιον, a tunic and cloak in one (Latin *penula*).
[^10]: P. Lithazomenon, a place near Alexandria; the name means "being stoned".
[^11]: T. κυρός "master", the polite title for a layman; σχολαστικός "scholasticus", a trained advocate.
[^12]: T. *ἀσυναλάλητος* is not attested to my knowledge; perhaps ἀσυνάλλακτος, "having dealings with no one". The Latin has *sine … contubernio atque commercio*.
[^13]: T. *ἀταπείνωτος* ("unhumbled") does not fit; the Latin has *indesinenter*, which is what the English follows.
[^14]: T. *πολλῇ τῇ συναναστροφῇ* sits oddly with the sense; the Latin reads *raro processu saecularium familiaritatem excludebat*, "by rarely going out he shut out familiarity with laymen". Translated as printed.
[^15]: S. Echoes Ps 32(33):19.
