title: The Spiritual Meadow, chapters 186–189
---
[3061] CHAPTER 186

We visited the coenobium of the Cave of abba Sabas, and abba Eustathius the superior.[^1] There was a customs officer named Moschus.[^2] And he told us, when we visited at Tyre: "When I was customs officer, late one evening I went off to bathe, and on the road I find a woman standing in the dark. And I went up and made advances to her, and she agreed to follow behind me. And I, out of devilish joy, did not even bathe, but hurried off to supper. And I pressed her hard, and she would not bring herself to taste anything. At last we got up to go to bed, and as I came to embrace her, she cried out with a loud voice, with tears,

[3064] [b: A] "'Woe is me, wretched woman!' And I, trembling, asked her the cause. And she, weeping the more, says, 'My husband is a merchant, and he was shipwrecked and lost his own goods and other people's. And he is in prison for the other people's; and not having what to do, so that I might at least get him bread, I resolved to sell my body, out of great want, so that we might find at least bread. They have even seized everything.' And I say, 'And what is the debt?' [?][^3] And she says, 'About five pounds.' And I took the gold and gave it to her, saying, 'See, for fear of the judgement of God I have not touched thee. Give it, and get him out, and pray for me, both of you.'[^4]

"Then after some time a slander is laid against me before the emperor, that I had squandered the customs' money; and the emperor sends and plunders my house, and drags me off in my shirt to Constantinople, and puts me in prison, and I spend a long time there in my old shirt. One day [?][^5] I heard that the emperor meant to kill me. And so I gave up hope of my life; and as I wept and lamented, I fell asleep. And I see, as it were, the lady who had then had her husband in prison, and she says, 'What is the matter with thee, lord Moschus? Why art thou held here?' And when I had said, 'I have been slandered, and I think the emperor will kill me,' she says to me, 'Wilt thou have me speak to the emperor about thee, and he releases thee?' And I say to her, 'And does the emperor know thee?' And she says, 'Yes.' And I woke, and was at a loss what this was. And again, a second and a third time, she was saying the same: 'Fear not; for tomorrow he releases thee.'

"And about daybreak they take me to the palace, at the emperor's order. And when I went in, and he saw me in my filthy garment, he says to me, 'I take pity on thee for now; mend thy ways from here on.' And I saw that woman standing at the emperor's right hand and saying to me, 'Take courage, and fear not.' The emperor orders that I take back my property; and giving me many good things besides, with much honour he restored me to my place, making me his deputy as well.

"That same night the same woman appears and says to me, 'Dost thou know who I am? She to whom thou didst show mercy, and thou didst not touch my body, for God's sake. See, I too have delivered thee from danger. Dost thou see the loving-kindness of God? That is: Thou didst it for my sake, and I have magnified my mercy upon thee.'"[^6]

CHAPTER 187

As we were going from holy Gethsemane to the holy Mount of Olives, there is a monastery called that of abba Abramius, which the great Abramius founded, he who was of the New Church of the all-praised Theotokos and Ever-Virgin Mary, having become its superior after Eudoxius.[^7] In this monastery the superior was abba John [3065] [b: A] of Cyzicus. So we asked him one day, saying, "How does one acquire virtues?" And the elder answered: "If someone wants to acquire a virtue, unless he first hates the vice diametrically opposed to it, he cannot acquire it. So if thou wouldst have mourning, hate laughter. Wouldst thou have lowliness? Hate pride. Wouldst thou be self-controlled? Hate gluttony. Wouldst thou be chaste? Hate lust. Wouldst thou be without possessions? Hate matter. Wouldst thou be merciful? Hate the love of money. He who wants to live in the desert hates the cities; he who wants to practise stillness hates familiarity; he who wants to be a stranger hates making a show of himself;[^8] he who wants to become master of his anger hates living among laymen; and he who wants to be free of rancour hates abuse; [b: B] he who wants to be undistracted will stay alone; he who wants to master his tongue, let him stop his ears so as not to hear much; he who wants always to have the fear of God will hate bodily rest and will love affliction and straitness; and so he will be able to serve God sincerely."

CHAPTER 188

Abba Theodore, the superior of the Old Laura,[^9] told us: "There were two brothers in Constantinople, silversmiths, Syrians. And the elder brother says to the younger, 'Come, let us go down to Syria and take possession of our family house.' The younger says to him, 'And why both of us, so that we leave the silversmith's shop? Either thou go down and I will stay here, or I go down and thou stay.' So it is settled by agreement that the younger should go down. So when the younger had gone down, after a little time the one who had stayed in Constantinople sees in his sleep: he saw an elder of priestly bearing, saying to him, 'Dost thou know that thy brother has committed fornication with the tavern-keeper's wife?' And he woke and was grieved, saying in himself, 'I am the cause; for why did I let him go alone?' And again after some time he sees the same one saying to him, 'Dost thou know that thy brother has fallen with the tavern-keeper's wife?' Again, having seen him, he was grieved. Again, a third time, after a while, he sees the same one saying to him, 'Dost thou not know that thy brother has lost the free woman, and has rolled away to the tavern-keeper's?'[^10] And he writes to him from Constantinople to Syria: 'At once, this very moment, leave everything and come to Byzantium.' And he received the letter, left everything, and went up to his brother. Then, when he saw him, he takes him to the Great Church,[^11] and began with grief to reproach him, saying, 'Well done, that thou hast committed fornication with the tavern-keeper's wife?' And when he heard this he began to swear by God the Almighty, 'I do not know what thou art saying: I have neither committed fornication, nor have I known any woman in sin, except my lawful wife.' So when the elder brother heard this, he says to him, 'Then hast thou done something else, worse?' And he affirmed this:[^12] 'I know of nothing out of the way that I have done, except that I found monks in our village of the doctrine of Severus, and, not knowing whether it is wrong, I communicated with them. Anything else whatever, [3068] [b: A] I do not know that I have done.' Then the elder brother understood that this was his fornication: that he had left the holy catholic Church and fallen into the heresy of Severus the Acephalite, who is the tavern-keeper, and had been put to shame, and had defiled the nobility of the right faith."

CHAPTER 189

We visited Ascalon, at the guest-house of the Fathers; and abba Eusebius the priest told us: "A merchant of our city sailed and lost his own goods and other people's, but he himself alone was saved. So when he came here and was seized by those who had lent to him, he was cast into prison, and everything in his house was plundered too. And nothing was left him except what he himself had on, and his wife. So she, out of great distress and straitness, took it as her charge [?] to feed her husband, even if only with bread.[^13] So as she was sitting one day and eating with her husband in the prison, a great personage came in to give a charity to those cast in there; and seeing the lady sitting with her husband, he was smitten with her (for she was very beautiful indeed), and sends word to her by the jailer. And she went gladly, expecting to receive some charity. And taking her aside he says to her, 'What is the matter with thee? Why art thou here?' And she told him everything. And he says to her, 'If I pay thy debt, wilt thou sleep with me this night?' And she, who was truly most beautiful and chaste, says to him, 'I have heard, my master, the Apostle saying that the wife has not authority over her own body, but the husband.[^14] Allow me, master: I will ask my husband, and what he bids, I will do.' And she came and told her husband everything. And he, full of good sense and of love for his own wife, was not carried away by the thought of being freed from the gaol; but groaning deeply, he says with tears to his wife, 'Go and send the man away, sister; and we hope in God that he will not forsake us to the end.' And she rose and dismissed the man, saying, 'I told my husband, and he would not.'

"And at that very time there was a highwayman cast into the prison further in, and he was watching everything as the husband and wife talked together; and he groaned within himself, saying, 'See in what a plight these are, and they have not betrayed their freedom for money, to take it and be freed. But they have counted chastity above all wealth, and have despised all the things of this life. And what shall I do, wretch that I am, who never once took it into my mind that there is a God at all, and for this reason am the cause of many murders too?' And calling them to himself through the window where he was shut in, he says to them, 'I was a robber, and I did many evil things and many murders, and I know that whatever hour the governor comes and I come out to trial, I am to die as a murderer. So, seeing your chastity, [3069] [b: A] I was pierced with compunction for you. But go off to such-and-such a place of the city wall, and dig, and take the money you will find there. And keep it, so that you may pay your debts, and much blessing besides. And pray for me, that I too may find mercy.'

"And after a few days the governor came to the city and ordered the robber to be brought out and beheaded. And a day after, the wife says to her husband, 'Dost thou bid me, my lord? I will go off to where the robber told us, and see whether he spoke the truth.' And he said, 'As it seems good to thee, do it.' And she took a small hoe, in the evening, and came to the place and dug, and found a pot covered over, holding the gold. And using it prudently, she paid it out little by little, as though borrowing from this one and that, until she had paid them all; [b: B] and after that she got her husband out too."

And the one who told us said: "See: just as these kept the commandment of God, so has our Lord and God magnified his mercy upon them."

## Notes
[^1]: P. The coenobium of the Cave (τὸ Σπήλαιον), a foundation of St Sabas near the Great Laura. The Greek has the dative, "to abba Eustathius"; the Latin turns him into *Eustachium*.
[^2]: P. "Customs officer" renders κομερκιάριος (Latin *commerciarius*), the imperial official over trade and customs at a port.
[^3]: T. The Greek prints τί ἄλλο ἐστὶ χρέος, literally "what else is a debt?"; the Latin has *Quantum est debitum?*. The sense "what is the debt?" is secure, but the wording is not.
[^4]: T. "Get him out" renders vernacular εὔγαλε. The Greek does not name the husband; the Latin supplies *redime virum tuum*. "Pray" (εὔξασθε) is plural.
[^5]: T. Printed Ἡμερούσιον; the Latin has *die quadam*.
[^6]: S. The wording echoes Lk 1:58 (ἐμεγάλυνεν Κύριος τὸ ἔλεος αὐτοῦ); "for my sake" (δι᾿ ἐμέ) recalls Mt 25:40. The Latin expands the sentence.
[^7]: P. The New Church (Nea) of the Theotokos in Jerusalem, built by Justinian; Abramius had been its superior after Eudoxius.
[^8]: T. φανητία, "showing oneself, display". The Latin has *apparentiam* and keys a note (41).
[^9]: P. The Old Laura, Chariton's laura of Souka (Latin *antiqui monasterii*).
[^10]: T. "The free woman" (ἐλευθέραν, against printed ἐλευθερίαν; see OCR table) follows the Latin *liberam mulierem*. The allegory is the Church as the free woman, against the tavern-keeper's wife, which is the heresy.
[^11]: P. The Great Church: Hagia Sophia.
[^12]: T. Printed διέβαλεν, "slandered", which does not fit; read as διεβεβαίου, after the Latin *asseverabat* (see OCR table).
[^13]: T. Printed ὡς ἐντολὴν ἐλάμβανεν, "she took it as a commandment"; the Latin has a negative, *non poterat virum vel pane nutrire*, "she could not feed her husband even with bread". Translated as printed.
[^14]: S. 1 Cor 7:4, loosely given.
