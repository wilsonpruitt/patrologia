title: The Spiritual Meadow, chapters 195–196
---
[3077] CHAPTER 195

While we were in Alexandria, Leontius of Apamea, a Christ-loving and devout man, came from the Pentapolis; for he had made his dwelling there, in Cyrene,[^1] for a good many years. He came in the time of [?] the pope of Alexandria, who is among the saints, [?] of that same city of Cyrene;[^2] and when we had met together, he told us:

"In the time of Theophilus, the blessed pope of Alexandria, Synesius the philosopher became bishop in Cyrene.[^3] When he came to Cyrene he found there a philosopher named Evagrius, his companion from the schools and his true friend, who was given over madly to the worship of idols. The bishop Synesius wished to bring him over; and not only did he wish it and press for it, but he took great pains and care over it, because of the [?] he had had for him from the beginning.[^4] But the other would not bear it, and would not accept his teaching at all. Yet the bishop, out of his great friendship for him, neither lost heart nor ceased, day after day, to teach him and exhort him and admonish him, so that he might believe in Christ and receive the knowledge of him.

So it came about that one day the philosopher even said this to him: 'Truly, my lord bishop, among all the rest this too displeases me, what you Christians say: that there is to be an end of this world, and that after the end all men who have ever been from the beginning rise again in this body, and having this flesh incorruptible and imperishable they will live for ages and receive their rewards; and that he who has pity on the poor lends to God,[^5] and he who scatters money among the poor and needy lays up treasure in heaven,[^6] and receives it a hundredfold from Christ, with eternal life, in the regeneration.[^7] All this, when it is said, seems to me deceit and mockery and a fable.' But Synesius the bishop maintained that all the things of the Christians are true and have nothing false in them or foreign to the truth, and he tried by many proofs to show that these things are so.

And indeed after a long time, having brought him to become a Christian, he baptizes him, and his children, and all his household. Then some time after his baptism, he gives the bishop three [?] centenaria of gold[^8] for the poor's account, saying: 'Take the three centenaria and give them to the poor, and make me a bond that Christ will repay me these in the age to come.' And he, having received the gold, readily made him the bond as he wished.

Having lived some years after holy baptism, he fell sick with the sickness unto death; and when he was about [3080] to die, he says to his children: 'When you bury me, put this paper into my hands, and so bury me with it.' And when he had died they did as he had commanded them, and burying him they laid him in the ground with the note of hand. And on the third day after the burial he appears to the bishop Synesius at night as he slept, saying: 'Come to the tomb where I lie, and take thy note of hand. For I have received the debt and been paid in full, and I have no claim against thee; and for thy assurance I have signed a note of hand in it.' Now the bishop did not know that his note of hand had been buried with the philosopher.

And in the morning he sent for his sons and said to them: 'What did you lay with the philosopher in the tomb?' They supposed he was speaking to them of money, and said to him: 'Nothing, master, except his grave-clothes.' 'What then,' he says, 'did you not bury some paper with him?' Then they remembered (for they had not known that he was speaking of the paper), and they say to him: 'Yes, master, when he was dying he gave us a paper, saying: "When you bury me, give me this paper to hold in my hands," without anyone knowing anything of it at all.' Then the bishop told them the dream he had seen that night; and taking them, with the clerics and the landowners and some from the city, he went off to the philosopher's tomb. Opening it, they find the philosopher lying there, holding the bishop's note of hand in his own hands; and taking the note of hand out of his hands they opened it, and found it newly written in the philosopher's hand, thus: 'I, Evagrius the philosopher, to thee, the most holy lord bishop Synesius, greeting. I have received what is written in this note of thine, and have been paid in full, and I have no claim against thee on account of the gold that I gave to thee, and through thee to Christ our God and Saviour.' Those who saw it were astounded, and for many hours, crying 'Lord, have mercy,' they glorified God, who works wonders and always gives such assurance to his servants."

And this same lord Leontius also affirmed that the note of hand bearing the philosopher's signature is preserved to this day and lies in the treasury of the church of Cyrene: whoever is appointed treasurer there receives it too along with the sacred vessels, and guards it carefully, and it is handed on with them safe and unharmed.

CHAPTER 196

George, who had been prefect of the land of Africa,[^9] the Christ-loving, the lover of monks and of the poor, who rejoices in all the good things that God loves, told us:

"In my country (it was in the parts of Apamea, of the province of Second Syria),[^10] there is a village some forty miles from the city, called Gonagon. In the hills of that same [3081] [b: A] estate, about one mile off, children were pasturing flocks. And as happens, and is bound to happen with children, they wanted to play, after the way of children. And as they played they say to one another: 'Come, let us hold a synaxis and offer the offering.'[^11] And this pleased them all, and they put forward one of themselves to stand as priest, and two others to stand as deacons. And they come to a flat rock; for they were playing there.[^12] And upon the rock, as upon an altar, they set out the loaves, and wine in an earthenware jug. And they stand by, the one as priest, the others as deacons, on this side and on that. And the one was saying the prayers of the oblation, and the others were fanning with their kerchiefs. Now the said priest turned out to know the anaphora by heart; for the custom had grown up in the Church that the children should stand before the holy sanctuary at the holy synaxes, and should partake of the holy mysteries first after the clergy. And since in some places the priests are wont to say it out loud, the children were found to have learned the prayer of the holy anaphora by heart from its being said aloud so constantly.

So when they had done everything according to the church's custom, before they broke the loaves, fire came down from heaven and devoured all that had been offered, and burned up the whole rock, so that no memorial at all was left, neither of the rock nor of what had been offered on it. And the children, seeing this happen all at once, fell to the ground for fear and remained half dead, unable to utter a sound or to rise from the ground. And when they did not come into the estate at the hour at which they were accustomed to come in, but lay stricken on the ground, their parents went out from the estate to see why they had not come back as usual. And searching, they found them lying there, neither knowing any of those present nor able to speak to those who spoke to them. So the parents, seeing them half dead, each lifted his own child and carried him to the estate; and seeing their children in such a trance they were amazed, unable to learn the cause of the trance. And though they asked them many times through the whole day, they got no answer from them, and could learn nothing at all of what had befallen them, until that whole day and the night had passed. For then the children came to themselves little by little and told them everything, how it was done and had come about. And the parents took them, and the landowners of that same estate, and went out; and they showed them the place where this strange wonder happened, pointing out some traces of the fire that had come down.

These, having heard what had happened and being persuaded by the thing itself, ran into the city and reported everything in order to the bishop of the city. He, marvelling at the magnitude of what was told, [3084] [b: A] went out to the place with all the clergy; and having seen the children, and heard from them what had happened, and looked on the signs of the heavenly fire, he put the children into a monastery and made the place a holy monastery; over the place of the fire he built the church, and set up the holy altar."

And this same lord George told us that he had seen one of those very children in that monastery where such a wonder happened. And this divine and angelic wonder George the Christ-loving related.

## Notes
[^1]: T. Κυρηνίᾳ as printed (the district of Cyrene); the Latin has *Cyrene*. Not corrected.
[^2]: T. p0153.11–12 breaks off at πά- / α: the Latin reads *diebus Eulogii sancti Alexandrini patriarchae, ejusdem Cyrenensis urbis futurus episcopus*, so our Greek has lost at least the pope's name (Εὐλογίου) and Leontius's office ("who was to be bishop") — probably a dropped line or part-line. P. Eulogius, patriarch of Alexandria 580–607/8.
[^3]: P. Synesius of Cyrene, bishop (of Ptolemais) c. 410, under Theophilus of Alexandria (385–412).
[^4]: T. διὰ ἣν: a noun is missing before the relative; the Latin has *pro charitate quam*, so probably τὴν ἀγάπην (or φιλίαν) ἥν.
[^5]: S. Prov 19:17.
[^6]: S. Ps 111(112):9; Matt 6:20.
[^7]: S. Matt 19:28–29.
[^8]: T. p0153.51 prints δηνάρια τρία; the next line (τὰ τρία κεντηνάρια) and the Latin (*tria centenaria*) give κεντηνάρια. A centenarium is a hundred pounds of gold.
[^9]: P/T. ἡ τῶν Ἄφρων χώρα = Africa (the African prefecture). The Latin calls him *Gregorius*; the Greek has Γεώργιος all three times, so it is kept.
[^10]: P. Apamea, metropolis of Syria Secunda. T. The Latin adds *ex oppido quod dicitur Thorax*, absent from our Greek.
[^11]: T. προσφορά, here the eucharistic offering (bread and wine); προσκομιδή below is rendered "oblation", ἀναφορά "anaphora".
[^12]: T. καὶ γὰρ ἔπαιζον "for they were playing [there]" as printed; the Latin paraphrases *erant enim in planitie*.
