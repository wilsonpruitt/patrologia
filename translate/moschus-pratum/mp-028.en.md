title: The Spiritual Meadow, chapters 197–201
---
[3084] CHAPTER 197

Rufinus, who wrote the Ecclesiastical History, recorded something like this done in olden times by children at play.[^1] For he speaks of holy Athanasius — the great champion and herald of the truth, who presided over the great city of the Alexandrians and shepherded all soberly and as was pleasing to God — telling of his boyhood, and how his advance to the presidency came to him from God and took its beginning by divine foreknowledge; [?] to take up the man's early history, and to recall what manner of life he led from a boy, and what upbringing, as we have received it; and for us too to bring it before the hearer.[^2]

Alexander, who is among the saints, pope of Alexandria, who came after Achillas according to the prophecy of holy Peter, archbishop and martyr, the one who condemned the impious Arius[^3] — one day, as he was looking out and watching the sea, he sees some playing by the shore, as is the custom of children, and imitating a bishop and all that is customarily done in the church. And watching a long while, he saw that some of the hidden things of the mysteries were being done by them. So he was troubled, and at once called the clerics and showed them what they were doing, and ordered them to go and seize all the children and bring them to him. And when they came he asked what their game was and what they had been doing. And they were frightened, being children, and tell him the whole thing duly: that they had even baptized some catechumens through Athanasius, whom those children had set up for themselves as a bishop. Then Alexander inquired of them carefully whom they had baptized; and having learned, and found that they had performed all those things according to the custom of our religion, he took counsel with his own clerics and ruled that those who had been granted the holy washing ought not to be baptized a second time. And Athanasius and the others who had been enrolled as clerics he charged their parents to bring up in the admonition and discipline of the Lord,[^4] and Athanasius above all, whom not long after he consecrated to God. Enriched with divine gifts, and advanced by the high priest of that time to a higher rank, he became eminent. Athanasius, then, and the others whom in that game he had seemed to have as [3085] [b: A] priests or deacons, he called their parents and, under God's witness, handed them over to the Church, so that they should be brought up there.

A little time having passed, when Athanasius had been taught thoroughly by the shorthand-writer and sufficiently by the grammarian, at once he is handed over by his parents to the priest as a faithful deposit of God, and like another Samuel he was brought up in the temple of God.[^5] And when Alexander in his old age went to the bishops, he was chosen to follow him and carry the vestment of the priesthood, which in the Hebrew tongue was called Ephod.

So great were the contests of this Athanasius in the Church against the heretics that the saying might seem to have been written of him: "I will show him how many things he must suffer for my name."[^6] For against his persecution the whole world was banded together, and the kings of the earth were stirred, and kingdoms and armies came together against him; but he kept the oracle of God which says: "If then camps stand against me, my heart shall not fear; if a council rise up against me, in this will I hope."[^7] But since such and so many deeds of his are told that the greatness of the things allows me to pass over nothing, while the multitude of the deeds compels me to keep silent on much, my mind labours in uncertainty, unable to choose what I must keep and what I must leave out. And so let us recall a few of those that belong to the matter; the rest report will tell. Yet report will certainly tell less than the truth; for it can neither add anything to the truth, nor will it find anything to add.

CHAPTER 198

Holy Athanasius, the pope of Alexandria, was once asked whether one can be baptized while not believing according to the faith and preaching of the Christians; and if someone is baptized for some other pretexts, pretending to believe, what then will become of him, or how God receives him. And he answered, saying: "You have heard [?] from the ancients[^8] how, to the blessed Peter, the martyr, when there was a great dying, and many were simply running to baptism for fear of death, someone appeared in the form of an angel, saying: 'How long will you send up here purses that are sealed, but altogether empty, with nothing inside?' So, as far as one may guess from the angel's voice, they have the seal of baptism, since they were baptized only [3088] [b: A] thinking they would gain some benefit if they received baptism."[^9]

CHAPTER 199

One of the fathers told that a certain elder, being pure and holy, used to see angels standing at his right and at his left when he was making the oblation. Now he had received the oblation from heretics, and as one unskilled in the divine doctrines, when he offered he said it in simplicity and guilelessness, not knowing that he was in error.

By a dispensation a brother came to visit him who had experience of the divine doctrines. And it happened that the elder made the offering in his presence; and the brother (for he was a deacon) said: "These things that thou didst say at the oblation, Father, are not of the Orthodox faith but of false belief." But the elder, since he saw angels when he offered, paid no heed to what was said, but despised it. The deacon persisted, saying: "Thou art in error, good elder; for the Church does not accept these things." So when the elder saw himself thus accused and reproved by the deacon, he saw the angels as usual, and asked them: "Since the deacon says this to me, what is it that he says [?]?"[^10] And they said to him: "Bear with him; he speaks rightly to thee." The elder said to them: "And why did you not tell me?" And they said to him: "Because God so disposed it, that man should be corrected through men." And from then on he corrected himself, giving thanks to God and to the brother.

CHAPTER 200

One of the fathers told us that a certain gifted young man went off and was apprenticed to a goldsmith, and learned the craft thoroughly. Then one of the patricians commissions him to make a jewelled cross, so that he might offer it to the church; and since the young man was very gifted, the master gives this work over to him. So the young man reckons: "This man offers so much money to Christ; why should I not also mix my wages into the cross, so that Christ may reckon me like the two mites of that widow?"[^11] So he reckons how much pay he is to receive, and borrows it, and puts it into the cross-work. Then the patrician comes and weighs the cross before the stones were set, and finds it over the weight he had given. He began to threaten, saying he had committed a fraud by debasing the gold. Then the young man says to him: "He who alone knows the secrets of the heart knows that I have done no such thing. But seeing how much money thou didst offer to Christ, I reckoned to put in my wages, so that I too might have a share with thee, and Christ might accept me like the two mites of that widow." And he, astonished at this, said: "Indeed, child? Didst thou reckon so?" He says to him: [3089] "Yes." He said to him: "Since thou hast reckoned this, and hast [?] made thy whole purpose common with Christ,[^12] wishing to have a share with me, behold, from this day I will make thee my son and heir." And he took him with him, and made him his heir.

CHAPTER 201

One of the fathers told: "Once I went up to Constantinople on some business. And as I was sitting in the church, there comes in one of the great ones of the world, very Christ-loving, and seeing me he sat down; and he began to ask about the salvation of the soul. So when I told him that to those who manage earthly things well, heavenly things are given, he said: 'Thou hast said well, Father: blessed is he who has his hope toward God and gives himself up wholly to God.' And he said: 'I am the child of one of the very illustrious of the world. My father was very merciful, and gave much to the poor. So one day he calls me and shows me all his money and says to me: "Child, which wouldst thou rather? Shall I leave thee the money, or Christ as thy guardian?"[^13] And I, edified by what he was doing, said to him: "Christ rather; for all these things are here today and pass away tomorrow."[^14] And from the time he heard this from me, he gave unsparingly, so that when he died he left little. And I, being poor, went about humbly, having my hope in God, to whom he had left me.

'Now there was again a certain rich man, one of the great, [b: C] who had a Christ-loving wife who feared God; and he had an only daughter. So the wife says to her husband: "This is the only daughter we have, and the Lord has given us so many good things; what then does she need? If we seek to give her to someone of our own rank who is not good in his ways, he will always afflict her; let us rather seek for her a humble man who fears the Lord, so that he may love her and cherish her according to God." He says to her: "Thou hast counselled well; go then to the church and pray earnestly, and sit there, and whoever comes in first, he is the one sent by the Lord." So she did so, and after she had prayed and sat down, I was found to be coming in at once. So she immediately sends a servant and calls me, and began to ask me: "Where art thou from?" And I said to her: "I am of this city, and the son of such a one." And she said: "Of that merciful man?" And she says to me: "Hast thou a wife?" I said to her: "No." And I told her what my father had said to me and what I had said to him. And glorifying the Lord she said: "Behold, the good guardian whom thou didst choose has sent thee a wife and money, that thou mayest use both in the fear of God." And I pray to walk my father's road to the end.'"[^15]

## Notes
[^1]: S/P. Rufinus of Aquileia, *Hist. eccl.* 10.15 (book 1 of his continuation; the Latin cites *l. 1 Hist. c. 14*).
[^2]: T. The infinitives ἀναλαβεῖν … μνησθῆναι … εἰσενεγκεῖν have no governing verb in our Greek; the Latin has *Consequens autem mihi videtur esse*, so a phrase such as "it seems fitting" is probably lost before τῆς ἀρχαιολογίας. "Upbringing" reads ἀγωγῆς for ἀναγωγῆς (table).
[^3]: P. Alexander of Alexandria (312–328), successor of Achillas (311–312); Peter I of Alexandria, martyred 311.
[^4]: S. Eph 6:4.
[^5]: S. 1 Sam 2:11–3:21.
[^6]: S. Acts 9:16.
[^7]: S. Ps 26(27):3, translated as printed (συμβουλή, "council", where the LXX has πόλεμος).
[^8]: T. Ἠκούσατε "you have heard" as printed; the Latin has *Audivi* ("I heard"), which would be Ἤκουσα. Not corrected.
[^9]: T. The sentence has μέν without δέ and no main verb of its own; the Latin supplies *Erunt itaque … signum habentes baptismi*. θνῆσις ("a great dying") as printed; Latin *mortalitas*.
[^10]: T. τί ἐστιν ὃ λέγεις printed: λέγεις "thou sayest" does not fit an address to the angels (plural) about the deacon; rendered as ὃ λέγει. Latin *quomodo se habet veritas?*
[^11]: S. Mark 12:42; Luke 21:2.
[^12]: T. ἐκαίνωσας ("renewed") printed; the Latin has *obtulisti*, and the following "wishing to have a share" suggests ἐκοίνωσας ("made common, shared"). Rendered on that reading.
[^13]: T. κουράτωρ (Latin *curator*), the legal guardian of a minor's estate; rendered "guardian".
[^14]: T. σήμερον αὔριον, the idiom "here today, gone tomorrow"; the Latin expands (*Christus autem manet in aeternum*).
[^15]: T. Before the last sentence the Latin adds *Deditque mihi filiam suam et pecunias* ("and she gave me her daughter and the money"); our Greek lacks it. Whether the plate has it is for the image check.
