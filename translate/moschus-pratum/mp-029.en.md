title: The Spiritual Meadow, chapters 202–207
---
[3092] CHAPTER 202

One of the Fathers told us that a certain layman had a son who was devout, a virgin, and self-controlled in all things; indeed from a little boy he had drunk no wine. And he had it in mind to withdraw. But his father wanted to put him into business, and he would not bear it. He had other brothers too, but he was the eldest. And since his aim did not suit his father's aim, his father was always reproaching him, rebuking him for his self-control and saying, "Why art thou not like thy brother, and why dost thou not give thyself to business?" But he held out in silence. And everyone loved him for his devoutness and his chastity.

When his father was about to die, some of his kin, and others who loved him, thinking that the father hated him, since he was always rebuking him, gathered together and said, "The father may deprive the servant of God of his inheritance. Let us plead for him" (for he was rich). And they go up to him and say to him, "We have something to ask of thee." He says to them, "What do you want to ask of me?" They say to him, "About master Abibus" (for that was his name), "that thou wilt not pass him over." He says to them, "Is it for him you are pleading with me?" They say to him, "Yes." He says to them, "Call him here to me." They thought that he wanted to rebuke him, as his custom was. And when he came in, he says to him, "Come near me." And as he came near, the father threw himself at his feet, weeping and saying, "Forgive me, my child, and pray God not to reckon it against me if I afflicted thee in anything; for thou wast seeking Christ, and I was moved by the world." He calls his other sons too, and says to them, "This is your master and father. Whatever he says: if he says, 'You have something,' you have it; if he says, 'You have nothing,' you have nothing." And they all wondered. And as soon as their father had died, he gives his brothers their share, and takes his own share and gives it all to the poor, keeping nothing back for himself. He built a small cell to withdraw to, and after finishing the cell he falls sick. So his end comes upon him. And his brother sat by him. And he says to him, "Go and give thy household some comfort, for it is a holy day." For it was the feast of the holy apostles. His brother says to him, "How shall I leave thee and go away?" He says to him, "Go away, and when the hour comes, I will call thee." So when the hour came, he rose and went to the window and knocks, and his brother looks, and he beckons to him: "Come." And as soon as his brother came, he gave up his spirit to the Lord. And they all wondered and glorified God, saying, "He has found an end worthy of the love with which he loved Christ."

[3093] CHAPTER 203

[b: A] One of the Fathers told us that once a certain stone-worker, of the kind they call a cabidarius,[^1] having precious stones and pearls, went aboard a ship with his servants, wishing to go abroad and trade. It happened, by a dispensation, that he grew fond of a certain boy of the ship, who waited on him; and the boy too was refreshed by him, eating of what he ate. One day the boy hears the sailors whispering and settling it among themselves to throw the cabidarius into the sea, because of the stones he had. Then, when the boy came in to the free man with great gloom to wait on him as usual, he says to him, "Why art thou gloomy today, lad?" He kept it to himself, saying nothing. So he asked him again, saying, "Tell me truly, what is the matter with thee?" Then he bursts out weeping and says to him, "The sailors have plotted so and so about thee." He says to him, "For certain?" He answered him, "Yes, that is how they have settled it about thee among themselves." Then he calls his servants and says to them, "Whatever I tell you, do it without hesitating and without any second thought." Then he spreads out a linen cloth and begins to say to them, "Bring my caskets."[^2] And they brought them. And opening them, he began to spread out the stones, and when they had all been laid out, he began to speak thus: "Is this life? For these I run into danger, I fight the sea, and in a little while I die, taking nothing with me out of this world?" He says to his servants, "Empty it all into the sea." So at once, at his word, they flung them into the sea. And the sailors were astounded, [b: C] and their plot came to nothing.

CHAPTER 204

Someone used to say that a man was bitten by a snake and went into a certain city to be treated. A devout woman who feared the Lord took him in and nursed him. When he had a little relief from his pains, the devil began to sow thoughts in him about her, and he began to want to touch her hand. But she said to him, "Not so, father; keep Christ before thee [?].[^3] Remember the grief and the repentance with which thou wilt repent as thou sittest in thy cell; [3096] [b: A] remember the groans and the tears that thou wilt shed." When he had heard these things from her, and others like them, the warfare fled from him, and in his shame he wanted to flee, not able to look her in the face. But she again, in the bowels of Christ, said to him, "Do not be ashamed at all; thou still needest treatment. It was not of thy pure soul to think those things; it was a suggestion of the envious devil." And so, having nursed him without offence, she sent him away with provisions for the road.

CHAPTER 205

Someone told us that a brother living in a coenobium used to be sent on the coenobium's errands; and there was a devout layman in a certain village who received him in faith whenever he came to the village. The layman had one daughter, newly widowed, who had lived with her husband a year or two. So as the brother went in and out among them, he was warred against over her; but she, being sensible, perceived it, and kept herself from coming into his presence. One day her father went into the city nearby on some business, leaving her alone in the house.

The brother came as usual and finds her alone in the house, and says to her, "Where is thy father?" She says to him, "He has gone into the city." So he began to be troubled by the warfare, wanting to fall upon her. She says to him, with good sense, "Do not be troubled at all; my father is not coming till late. We are here, the two of us. But I know that you monks do nothing without prayer. So get up, pray to God, and whatever he puts into thy heart, let us do it." But he would not bear it, but was troubled by the warfare. She says to him, "Hast thou really ever known a woman?" He says to her, "No; and that is why I want to see what it is." She says to him, "So that is why thou art troubled: thou dost not know the foulness of wretched women." And wanting to lessen his passion, she says, "I am in my monthly courses, and no one can come near me or bear the smell of me, for the stench," calling her own body wretched. When he had heard these things from her, and others like them, he was disgusted,[^4] and coming to himself he wept.

So when she saw that he had come to himself, she says, "See, if I had listened to thee and been persuaded by thee, we should already be done with it, having committed the sin. With what face, then, couldst thou have looked upon my father after that, or gone back to thy monastery and heard the choir of those holy men singing psalms? So I beg thee, be sober, and do not for a little pleasure throw away all the labours [3097] [b: A] thou hast, and be robbed of eternal good things." Hearing these words from her, the brother, the one who suffered what this story tells, told it to the man who related it, giving thanks to God, who through her good sense and chastity had not let him fall away entirely.[^5]

CHAPTER 206

One of the Fathers told us that a lady of senatorial rank came to venerate the holy places; and going down to Caesarea, she preferred to stay there and keep stillness. So she begged the bishop, saying, "Give me a virgin, to form me and teach me the fear of God." So the bishop chose a humble one and gives her to her. After a time the bishop met her and says, "How is the virgin who was given thee?" She said, "She is good, but she does not profit my soul much, since she lets me do my own will, being humble. What I need is someone to rebuke me and not allow me to do my own will." So again the bishop took another, a harsh one, and gives her to her, and she would say to her, "Rich fool!" rebuking her with other such things. After this the bishop asked her again, "How is the virgin?" And she says to him, "Truly this one profits my soul." And so she shone out in great meekness.

CHAPTER 207

Abba Theonas and abba Theodore [b: C] told us that in Alexandria, in the time of Paul the patriarch,[^6] a girl was left behind by her parents, who had great wealth. And she was still unbaptized. One day, as she was going about [?] in the garden which her parents had left her[^7] (for there are gardens in the middle of the city, in the houses of the great men), while she was in the garden, then, she sees a certain man getting himself ready to hang himself. So she runs and says to him, "What art thou doing, good man?" He says to her, "Truly [?], let me be, woman, for I am in great affliction."[^8] The girl says to him, "Tell me the truth, and perhaps I can help thee." He says to her, "I owe a great deal, and I am sorely oppressed by my creditors; and I have chosen to die quickly rather than live so painful a life." The girl says to him, "I beg thee, see, take all I have and pay it back; only do not destroy thyself." And he took it and paid off the debt. But she was in straits after that, and having no one to care for her, bereft as she was of parents, she was forced to it, and began to play the harlot.

And some of those who knew her, and in what standing her parents had been, said, "Who knows the judgements of God, how a soul is allowed to fall away for some cause?"

Yet after some time she fell sick, and she came to herself. And pierced with compunction she said to her neighbours, "For the Lord's sake, have mercy on my soul and [3100] speak to the pope,[^9] that he make me a Christian." And they all despised her, saying, "Will he really receive this one, a harlot as she is?" And she was in great affliction because of it. While she was in this state, and losing heart, an angel of the Lord stood by her in the form of the man to whom she had done the mercy. And he says to her, "What is the matter with thee?" She said, "I long to become a Christian, and no one will speak for me." He says to her, "Dost thou long for it in truth?" She said, "Yes, I beg thee." He says to her, "Do not lose heart at all. I am bringing some people, and they will take thee to the church." So he brings two others, angels too, and they carry her off to the church. And again they change their form into certain distinguished persons of the staff of the Augustal;[^10] and they call the clerics appointed over the baptisms. And the clerics say to them, "Does your charity stand surety for her?" They say to them, "Yes." So the clerics, having done what the order of service requires for those about to be baptized, baptized her in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. And they put on her the garments of the newly baptized as well; and wearing white she went in, carried by them; and having set her down, they vanished.

So her neighbours, seeing her wearing white, said to her, "Who baptized thee?" And she told them, saying, "Some people came and took me to the church, and spoke to the clerics, and they baptized me." And they said to her, "Who are these people?" So since she could not tell them, they went away and reported it to the pope. So the pope calls those over the baptistery. And he says to them, "Did you baptize this woman?" So they confessed that they had, at the request of So-and-so and So-and-so of the Augustal's staff. So the bishop sends for those who were named, and asks them whether they had stood surety for her. And they said, "We neither know nor are acquainted with those who did this." And then the bishop knew that the thing was of God. And calling her to him, he said, "Tell me, daughter, what good hast thou done?" She says to him, "Being a harlot and poor, what good had I to do?" He says to her, "Dost thou know of no good thing at all that thou hast done?" She says to him, "No; except that I saw a man wanting to hang himself, choked by his creditor, and I gave him all my substance and set him free." And having said this she went to her rest in the Lord, set free herself from her offences, willing and unwilling. Then the bishop glorified the Lord and said, "Righteous art thou, O Lord, and upright are thy judgements."[^11]

## Notes
[^1]: P. *Cabidarius* (Greek καβιδάριος): a jeweller or gem-cutter, as the Greek's own gloss λιθουργός says.
[^2]: T. ζικία is a rare word; the Latin has *capsulas*, and "caskets" follows it. The scaffold's trailing "(" is read as Migne's note-call with its number lost (see OCR table).
[^3]: T. The Greek reads Χριστὸν ἔχης, which does not construe as printed; the Latin has *Christum time*. The sense "keep Christ (before thee)" assumes ἔχε; marked doubt.
[^4]: T. Reading σιχανθείς ("being disgusted", from vernacular σιχαίνομαι) for the scaffold's non-word σιανθεὶς; it fits her talk of stench. The Latin's *compunctus* paraphrases.
[^5]: T. The Greek sentence is loose (ἀκούσας with no main verb of its own) but complete as printed. The Latin adds that he went back to his monastery and did penance for his sin; the Greek has no such clause.
[^6]: P. Paul of Tabennesi, patriarch of Alexandria 537–540.
[^7]: T. διακρίνουσα ("discerning") does not fit; the Latin has *ingressa*. Some verb of going in or going about is wanted; marked doubt.
[^8]: T. Τὸ ὄντως σοι does not construe; rendered by the sense, "truly". Marked doubt.
[^9]: P. "Pope" here is the patriarch of Alexandria; later in the same story the text calls him "the bishop".
[^10]: P. The Augustal was the prefect of Egypt (*praefectus Augustalis*), whose staff (τάξις) were officials of standing in Alexandria.
[^11]: S. Ps 118 (119):137.
