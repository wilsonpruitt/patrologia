title: Panegyric on the Emperor Anastasius, 1–7
---
[2793] PANEGYRIC ON THE EMPEROR ANASTASIUS

1. Already, most mighty emperor, every city, proud in [b: A] thee and glorying in thy trophies, and having learned by experience what happiness truly is, contriving by every means not to be seen lagging behind thy gifts, [2796] [b: A] is at a loss for a requital to contend with thy deeds;[^1] and hence admires its benefactor the more, since by the good he does he outstrips the requitals of those he has done good to. For this is an adornment that befits an emperor: to conquer, together with his enemies, his subjects too — the one by arms, the other by the abundance of the goods at his command, and both by his virtues. But our city, having received through his image the benefactor himself,[^2] like some passionate lover rises up to the sight and rouses every age: father stands beside child, old man comes with young, and points, and together they leap for joy at the sight. Now perhaps I shall seem, alone among the rest, to be venturing upon so vast a mass of deeds, and to have such boldness that I have stood up in the middle of the theatre and dare to say something, and will not stop though someone threaten. But it is not so: the whole city is moved to a just requital for the good it receives; and judging the time not enough for each man to speak in turn, all by common vote content themselves with the orator's voice; for he who is put forward to speak on the city's behalf gives utterance, with one tongue, to the minds of all. And from this it comes about, against all reason, that I am at once confident and afraid. That the matter, drawing in nothing low, lifts the speech to a height and sets the speaker in abundance — this is a great and splendid piece of good fortune for me; but I fear lest, struck dumb by the sheer excess and multitude of what is there, and not knowing what use to make of what lies before me, I turn the wealth into speechlessness, and stand there at a loss. As in a pleasant meadow that offers a varied sight, all is beautiful to look upon; but the viewer, since all alike delight him, not knowing what to prefer and pluck, stands holding off from all alike. Nevertheless, though this is how things stand, words, which dare everything, now too grow bold just the same, and leap about in disorder, and come forward as if revelling in delight, trusting to fortune rather than judgment, and reckoning nothing of what is to come. So we shall dare something even beyond our power, holding out our eagerness as plea enough for pardon.

2. For to thee fortune, as though divining the future well, furnished everything that befits the rule of the Romans; and first, as was right, she bestows a native city that surpasses the happiness of the others. For Epidamnus the historians sing,[^3] and report cries out along with them: a city thrust out from the mainland [2797] [b: A] into the sea, as though inhabiting each of the two elements, and having the advantage in all that land and sea know how to bear; for there one may both live on an island, in part, and be mainlanders, and keep clear of what is troublesome in both, enjoying either one purely.[^4] For it lies out ahead as though holding out a hand to those coming from the open sea, and meeting them kindly; and a man sailing out over the Ionian leans out from the ship and turns toward it, and feasts his gaze with the pleasure of what he sees. Epidamnus, earth-born, came forth from here and bestows his name upon the city; and later moves Pythian Apollo too to love of it, and he adorned it with a colony of Greeks, as a seer, surely, who knew the future well: that the common benefactor of all land and sea must appear from here. So then, if thou hast marvelled to hear that the Athenians are earth-born, thou mayest take pride along with them; if thou admirest the Phaeacians' kindness to strangers, the city is from them.[^5] It has, besides, the stock of the Corinthians and the ambition of Sparta, and Heracles for an ancestor, and through him Zeus.

3. Having received these and the like as the first gifts from the Higher Power, thou didst not shame, by Zeus, a lineage so splendid and sung through all the world; rather thou didst eclipse those who went before; and they who conquered others by their virtues learn in thy case defeat instead, and are conquered with a most sweet victory, and one in which fathers would pray to be conquered. For, sprung from such forebears, and reared as befitted one who has so conquered in all things, and increasing thy virtues with the advance of time, and thinking it right that a fair foundation be laid beneath the best life, thou didst reckon that foundation to be the possession of piety; this thou didst add to thy longing,[^6] pursued it in practice, and pressed on to the summit of the matter, and very swiftly arrived. If this is boasting and the vanity of words, I have wandered far from the truth. But the witnesses of these things are not some few or worthless men, whom one might strike off, finding fault with their judgment; but a whole city, and the city of the palace, crying out for thee, and raising thee toward a priest's habit;[^7] and [2800] what it testified by deeds it confirmed by its vote. Not so Eumolpus,[^8] who, having failed of the kingship, took the priesthood, and reaped it as the prize of rashness, not of a temperate life: now bearing arms and enrolled among the servants of Ares, and now, having changed his garb, and by an alteration of habit, sacrificing all of a sudden and standing beside the altars.

4. But thou didst receive this as a fitting prelude and becoming testimony of the reign to come, and didst obtain the prize owed to thy virtues — not, however, as far as the thing itself, but only so far as for thy worth to be [2801] shown, and for thee to be kept for a greater vote and a more common benefaction, and for it to become plain to all thereafter that, gathering into one the greatest things among men, an emperor's habit and a priest's mind, thou dignifiest both by the addition of the other. But not yet these things: rather thou hadst still so great a longing for piety, knowing well that upon it the company of the other virtues would easily follow, as though revering its kinship with the Higher Power; and thou wast not deceived in thy judgment. For they followed and kept company with one another, as though they had been granted thy soul for a common dwelling;[^9] and in all things thou didst conquer, humane alike to look upon and to have dealings with, directing thy life by the experience of prudence, looking about thee at so much as a temperate eye permits, and knowing how to bear lightly whatever fortune may decide. Therefore, passing thy days in the palace,[^10] thou didst show thyself to others a pattern of a happy life, knowing well how to be ruled before ruling; if indeed the best training is to take the road to ruling as one ought from the experience of being well ruled. For it was open to thee to examine at liberty both what was done amiss by those in power and what made for the happiness of the subjects; and thou didst search out the causes, and wast schooled in safety, as though making in the fortunes of others thy rehearsal for what was to come.

5. And now the time was at hand, as though paying some due debt, and thou didst shine forth to so great a rule; and their affairs were changed for the subjects. But it is better to remind you a little of what went before, and to show how things then stood, and to how great a change they have come for us. Nor indeed will the good things that came after allow the memory of what went before to grieve us. For there was a time when, for all who fill the empire of the Romans, affairs were not in good fortune; but a certain disorderly lust of gain was at its height, and faithlessness of mind; and informers held mastery over all. He who now was happy, a little later, casting away his good hopes, changed into the guise of the unfortunate. And it was all one to have something and to be in want, because of the expectation of the like. A strange thing it was, and against nature: for wealth grieved those who possessed it. While things stood thus, and all had given up beneath their calamities, like a god appearing from the machine thou didst hold out a hand [2804] of freedom; and those things had an end, and the mist upon affairs was scattered. A divine decree, truly, moved the vote upon thee; and as though from one mind the whole people cried out; the great senate added its assent; the empress nodded;[^11] and the vote was carried. This is the beginning of our happiness and its first settlement; this roused every city to prosperity; from this our affairs flourish and are changed, and of what grieved us there is no account.

6. To others, then, from their first birth even their swaddling-bands are purple, and they are called emperors at once, before they are aware of their fortune. But later, often proving unworthy of the gift, they set accusers upon the divinity,[^12] as one that knew nothing of what was to come, nor knows how to judge well, but bestows her favours at random, and gives herself not to such as are worthy, but to such as she pleases; and if they should prove worthy of the thing, yet they seem to practise the virtues because of the elevation of their honour, and so as not to be seen lagging behind it. But in thy case fortune, against expectation, exercised judgment and knew a just vote. For she followed thy virtues, rendering the empire as requital and as grace, and that bloodless, and free of all deceit; for it was not, as for others, arms and battles and the slaughter of kinsmen, and guile conquering by deceit, that set so great an honour about thee. These are the unhappy preludes of an unhappy reign, and of all who put on the purple as a means to gain. But having received this fitting honour fittingly, thou didst not, by Zeus, prove false the testimony of those who voted for thee, but didst even pass beyond their hopes and conquer them.

7. Having come to this point of the speech, and looking upon so great a multitude of deeds, I seem to suffer the same as those who scan the open seas. For often as they cast their eyes upon them, they see the sea alone, and nothing bounds the view for them. Yet the attempt must be dared; for neither do they shrink from the voyage, nor let us from the speech. For as soon as thou hadst received the sceptre, thinking it right to drive far from thy rule all that is bastard and barbarian, and to appear to thy subjects with freedom at thy side, thou didst nod assent to this, and it came to its accomplishment. ## Notes
[^1]: T. ἀμοιβῆς ἀπορεῖ τοῖς ἔργοις ἁμιλλωμένης: the city lacks a requital that could compete with the emperor's deeds. Villoison: *quaerit quomodo beneficia repenset gratia referenda*.
[^2]: P. The occasion is the arrival at Gaza of the emperor's image (εἰκών), set up and greeted by the city.
[^3]: S. Thucydides I.24 opens his account of the Corcyraean affair with Epidamnus, "a city on the right as one sails into the Ionian gulf"; Epidamnus (Dyrrachium, Durrës) was Anastasius's birthplace.
[^4]: T. τῷ μὲν θατέρου ψιλῶς ἀπολαύειν: a μέν with no answering δέ, and θατέρου ("of the other") hangs; the sense wanted is "enjoying the good of each unmixed." Villoison smooths it: *quae bona, iis plane frui*. Rendered as printed.
[^5]: P. Epidamnus was a colony of Corcyra (identified with Homer's Phaeacia) with a Corinthian founder; Corinth's kings traced themselves to Heracles. The orator stacks every pedigree the city can claim.
[^6]: T. ταῦτα προσετίθεις τῷ πόθῳ, "these thou didst add to thy longing"; Villoison has *Hoc tibi proponebas desiderio*, as if προὐτίθεις, "set before". Rendered as printed.
[^7]: P. "The city of the palace" (βασιλείων ἡ πόλις) is Constantinople. The orator has the capital acclaim Anastasius for priestly office before his accession; he is said to have been put forward for the see of Antioch.
[^8]: P. Eumolpus, the Thracian son of Poseidon who fought Athens for Eleusis; his house, the Eumolpidae, held the Eleusinian priesthood.
[^9]: T. εὐτυχοῦσαι with an accusative, literally "fortunate in thy soul as a common dwelling"; Villoison *nactae*, "having found". Rendered by sense.
[^10]: P. Before his accession in 491 Anastasius served at court as a silentiary.
[^11]: P. The empress (βασιλίς) is Ariadne, Zeno's widow, who chose Anastasius and married him in 491; "the great senate" is the senate of Constantinople.
[^12]: T. τῇ δαίμονι, feminine: fortune as a goddess; Villoison *deam*.
[^13]: P. The Arabs raiding the eastern provinces; Villoison's argument: *Vincit Arabes Orientis provincias infestantes*.
