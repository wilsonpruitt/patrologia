title: Synodical Letter to Sergius of Constantinople, 1
---
[3148] SYNODICAL LETTER TO SERGIUS OF CONSTANTINOPLE

To the master most holy of all,[^1] and most blessed brother and fellow-minister Sergius, archbishop and
patriarch of Constantinople, Sophronius, unprofitable servant[^2] of the holy city of Christ our God.

Ah! ah! all-blessed ones, how dear to me now is quiet, and far dearer than before, ever since from an
untroubled quiet I have come into a turmoil of affairs, and am drenched with waves, as it were, of the dry
land! Ah! ah! God-honoured ones, how sweet to me now is the least thing, and not a little sweeter than
before, ever since from a dunghill, and the ground, and unspeakable and great lowliness I have gone up to a
hierarch's throne. And I see a great storm yoked to it, and danger attending the storm. For the sweetest
things are not seen so sweetly before the trial and knowledge of the unpleasant as when they come into view
after the trial and onset of what grieves: so health is thrice-longed-for by those who fall sick after
health; so calm is a delight to those who are storm-tossed after calm; so wealth is lovely to those who are
poor after wealth; and all things a man would thus see turning out, being and remaining in themselves what
they are, in their natural and essential quality, such as they appeared before the trial of their
contraries, but becoming finer after the knowledge of these, and to those who have received them far more
precious, nay more, more delightful too and far more desirable. And Job the renowned will cast his vote
with us on this most brilliantly, having come through the trial of both and setting down his votes aright;
and he would be a just judge of what we say, coming forward for us and fixing a judgment that leans to no
side and takes no bribe. What then does that athlete say, the untamed one, who suffered the change of what
gladdened him and came into a flood of what grieved? «Who would set me month by month in the days of old,
in which God kept me, as when his lamp shone over my head; when by his light I walked in darkness; when I
was laden upon my ways; when the Lord was making visitation of my house; when I was exceedingly abounding,
and round about me were my children; when my ways flowed with butter, and my mountains flowed with milk;
when I went out early in the city, and in the broad streets my seat was set. [3149] The young men saw me
and hid themselves, and the aged rose up; the great ceased speaking, laying a finger on their mouth; and
they that heard blessed me.»[^3] Therefore with good reason I too, most blessed ones, shall cry out with Job
the pentathlete,[^4] stricken by the memory of the good things that were mine of old. A calm life those
things were, and a quiet one, and a lowliness that knew no storm.[^5] Who would set me month by month in
the days of old, in which God kept me unafflicted, as when his lamp shone over my head while I lived a
peaceful and unbillowed life; when by his light I walked in darkness; when I gathered the clusters of
quiet; when I was laden with the fruits of calm; when I feasted on the shoots of tranquillity; when I
exulted in the flowers of freedom from care; when I was crowned with the buds of fearlessness; [b: B] when I
banqueted on the graces of freedom from business; when I enjoyed earthly poverty; when I ploughed the
furrows of the dunghill that knows no danger; when I sailed the sea of unbillowed beggary; when I took
delight in the beauties of a lowly hearth; when I ate the honey-flowing manna of a diet that grows on the
ground, myself too beheld as another Israel, and luxuriating in a peaceful and heavenly luxury without
murmuring and without a thankless mind?[^6]

Since then these things, and things beyond these, most wise ones, have been done to me, thrice-wretched,
by great necessity and by the force of God-loving clerics, and devout monks, and faithful laymen — all the
citizens of the holy city of Christ our God — who forced me by their hand and wrought with tyranny, by what
judgments I neither know nor understand, I ask you, the all-holy, and I exhort you, not only to come to my
aid with pure prayers to the Lord as I am at sea in the affairs of this life, and then in danger as well,
and to steady me as I sink at the knees with faintness of heart, but also to guide me with God-inspired
teachings towards the undertaking of what must be done — the one as fathers and begetters, the other as
brothers and men of one blood. Grant me then, you, in fatherly and in brotherly wise, my requests, just as
they are; and I shall follow your guidance, and shall gain, as a merchant gains, a bond with you,[^7]
which faith binds together for those of one mind, and hope unites for those of right mind, and love knits
together for those of godly mind; whose threefold cord,[^8] though twined of these three divine virtues,
neither knows loosing, nor undergoes breaking, nor admits separation; but it is truly unbreakable,
gathering into one piety those who are rich in its inspired twining.

But since a certain apostolic and ancient tradition has prevailed in the holy Churches throughout the whole
inhabited world, that those who are brought to the hierarchy should refer everything sincerely to those who
administer the hierarchies before them — how they think, and how they stand as to the faith which Paul, the
most wise, handed down to them with great security, so that they might not make their runnings in vain;[^9]
[3152] for their whole course would become vain if the faith were wronged in any point. For that divine
man, who heard the voices of God, and had heaven itself for his school, and became a beholder of paradise
before his time, and learned words unspeakable to other men, feared and trembled, and, as he himself says,
was afraid lest, having preached Christ's saving proclamation to others, he himself should become
reprobate.[^10] Hence also the heavenly disciple of Christ went up in Jerusalem, and submitted himself to
the divine disciples who were before him, and made known the gospel teaching which he preached to those who
seemed to be pre-eminent among the others,[^11] and made them sharers of the doctrine, betrothing security
to himself and to those after him who receive his teachings, and becoming for all the best pattern of
salvation, for those who wish to follow in his footsteps. Serving this custom, therefore, we too, and
holding as a most excellent law everything that was fittingly done by those of old, above all when it has
been strengthened by an apostolic undertaking, write how we stand concerning the faith. And we send it to
you, the God-wise, for testing, so that we may not seem to be moving the eternal boundaries which our
Fathers set[^12] — to you who know not only how to discern the approved doctrines from the spurious, but
who are also able and strong to fill up exactly what is lacking, through the love that is perfect, in
Christ as well. So I shall come to speak those things which from the beginning, born and nurtured in the
holy and catholic Church, I have learned thoroughly, and received to think from tender nails,[^13] and have
heard you, the God-inspired, proclaiming.

I believe, then, blessed ones, as I have believed from the beginning, in one God the Father almighty,
wholly without beginning and eternal, Maker of all things visible and invisible. And in one Lord Jesus
Christ, the only-begotten Son of God, who was eternally and impassibly begotten of God the Father himself;
who knows no other principle than the Father, nor has his hypostasis from anywhere else than from the
Father; light from light, consubstantial; true God from true God, co-eternal. In one Holy Spirit, which
proceeds from God the Father, which is recognized also by him as light and God,[^14] and is truly
co-eternal with the Father and the Son, consubstantial and of the same stock, and of the same essence and
nature, and likewise also of Godhead. A Trinity consubstantial, and equal in honour, and sharing one
throne; connatural, and akin, and equal in glory; summed up into one Godhead, and gathered together into
one common lordship, without personal fusion, and apart from hypostatic contraction.[^15] For we believe a
Trinity in a Monad, and glorify a Monad in a Trinity: a Trinity in the three hypostases, a Monad in the
monadic character of the Godhead. For the holy Trinity is numerable in its personal hypostases, and the
all-holy Monad is outside all numbering. And the one [3153] [b: A] has an undivided division, and bears an
unconfused conjunction: for, divided in its numerable hypostases, and numbered in its personal othernesses,
it is united in the sameness of the essence and the nature, and does not admit total partition. And the
Monad is both single and unyoked, and flees all numbering according to essence. For one God is firmly
believed by us, because one Godhead too is loudly proclaimed, even if it is known in a Trinity of persons.
And one Lord is announced to us, because one lordship too has been surely discerned, even if it is shown in
three hypostases: neither is God, inasmuch as God is one God and one Godhead, divided, and parted into
three gods, or carried out into three Godheads; nor is the one Lord, inasmuch as he is one Lord, set apart,
and widened into three lords, or broadened into three lordships. That is the impiety of the Arians, which
cuts the one God asunder into unequal gods, and parts the one Godhead into unlike Godheads, and sets the
one lordship apart into three lordships of different kind. Nor, inasmuch as the one God is and is
recognized as a Trinity, and is announced as three hypostases, and is venerated in three persons, and is
called Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit, is he contracted, or composed, or confused, blending himself
together into one hypostasis, and compacting himself into one person that is not numbered. That is the
lawlessness of the Sabellians, which confuses the three hypostases into one hypostasis, and kneads the
three persons together into one person. For where is the Trinity, most impious men, if according to you the
Trinity shall be gathered together into one person, and shall run together into one confused hypostasis?
Or where is the Monad, most mad men, if the Monad shall be drawn out into three essences, and broadened
into three natures, and multiplied into three Godheads?

For with the orthodox each is impious, and has run wholly aground, away from piety: the monadic in
hypostasis, and the triadic in the natures. For the one is carried straight off to Judaism, and carries off
with itself the man who says it; the other rolls out into Hellenism, and rolls out with itself the man who
asserts it.[^16] And either he wholly hellenizes who madly utters this with Arius, or he judaizes who
impiously receives that with Sabellius. And for this reason it has been well decreed by the theologians
that we should think a Monad in the one and single Godhead, and in the sameness of the essential and
natural lordship; and a Trinity in the three unconfused hypostases, and in the difference of the personal
otherness that numbers three: so that neither should the One remain Sabellian, beheld as wholly one, and
turning away from every hypostatic plurality; nor should the Three exalt [3156] [b: A] Arius, conceived as
three through and through, and thrusting away every monadic word of Godhead, and essence, and nature.

As, then, we have been taught to think one God, so too we have received to confess one Godhead; and as we
have learned to venerate three hypostases, so too we have been trained to glorify three persons: knowing
the one God as no other beside the three persons, nor understanding the three consubstantial persons of the
Trinity, which are the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, as other beside the one God. And for this
reason we proclaim the three, in which the Godhead is, one; and we declare the three, whose Godhead it is,
one; or, to say it more exactly and more clearly, the three which the Godhead is and is known to be. For the
same both is one and is believed three, and is glorified as three, and is truly announced as one. And
neither is the one, in so far as it is one, taken as three; nor are the three, inasmuch as they are three,
understood as one: which is both paradoxical and truly full of all amazement. For the same is both
numerable and escapes enumeration: numerable in its own threefold hypostases, but escaping numbering by the
singleness of the Godhead. For the singleness of its essence and nature does not at all allow itself to be
numbered, lest it should bring in a difference of Godhead too, and thereafter of essence and nature, and
make the monarchy a polytheism: for every number has difference as its housemate, and every difference and
distinction brings number along with it as its inborn companion.

## Notes
[^1]: T. Δεσπότῃ πάντων ἁγιωτάτῳ: "most holy of all"; the Latin softens to *in omnibus sanctissimo* and
carries a note number (1) the Greek column does not print. Δεσπότης is "master" throughout the work
(capital for Christ).
[^2]: S. Luke 17:10, δοῦλοι ἀχρεῖοί ἐσμεν.
[^3]: S. Job 29:2–11 (LXX), as printed: ὁδούς for the LXX's ὁδοῖς, ἐπανέστησαν for ἔστησαν; ὑλώδης
("abounding", literally "full of matter") is the LXX's own word.
[^4]: T. Ἰὼβ τῷ πεντάθλῳ, "the pentathlete": victor in all five contests; the Latin paraphrases, *quinquies
victore*.
[^5]: T. ταπεινότης, "lowliness"; the Latin reads *pauperies*. Γαληνὸς ταῦτα βίος ἦν: "those things were a
calm life" (the scaffold's σαῦτα is the machine's).
[^6]: S. The manna and the murmuring: Exod 16:2–15; Num 11:4–9. The second Job-passage is Sophronius's own
pastiche of 29:2–4, every clause after "in darkness" his.
[^7]: T. συμπλοκὴν τὴν πρὸς ὑμᾶς ἐμπορεύσομαι: ἐμπορεύομαι is to acquire by trade; the Latin has *copulam
ad vos habere merebor*.
[^8]: S. Eccl 4:12, τὸ σπαρτίον τὸ ἔντριτον.
[^9]: S. Gal 2:2, μήπως εἰς κενὸν τρέχω ἢ ἔδραμον; τεύχοιεν read for the scaffold's broken τεύ- / οεν (OCR row).
[^10]: S. 2 Cor 12:2–4 (paradise, the unspeakable words); 1 Cor 9:27, μήπως ἄλλοις κηρύξας αὐτὸς ἀδόκιμος γένωμαι.
[^11]: S. Gal 2:1–2, τοῖς δοκοῦσι.
[^12]: S. Prov 22:28, ὅρια αἰώνια ἃ ἔθεντο οἱ πατέρες σου.
[^13]: T. ἐξ ἁπαλῶν ὀνύχων, "from tender nails": the Greek proverb for "from childhood"; the Latin keeps it,
*a mollibus unguibus*.
[^14]: T. καὶ αὐτῷ γνωριζόμενον as Migne prints it (plate check): "recognized also by him", the Father just
named. The Latin *Deum ipsum agnoscendum* read αὐτό, "itself"; not adopted. The Latin also opens the clause
*Et in unum Spiritum* and adds *sempiterne* to "proceeds"; the Greek as printed has neither (Εἰς ἓν Πνεῦμα,
no Καί).
[^15]: T. ἀνάχυσις "fusion", συναίρεσις "contraction" (a drawing-together into one); the Latin has *absque
personali confusione, et absque substantiali divisione*.
[^16]: T. Ἑλληνισμός, "Hellenism": pagan polytheism, the Latin's *Paganismum*; Ἑλληνίζει "hellenizes".
