title: Synodical Letter to Sergius of Constantinople, 2
---
[3156] So the blessed Trinity is numbered, not by essences and natures and different Godheads, or by
threefold lordships — away with it! — as the Arians rave, and the leaders of the new tritheism are
frenzied,[^1] babbling idly of three essences, and three natures, and three lordships, and likewise three
Godheads; but by hypostases and intellectual properties, perfect, subsisting by themselves, divisible in
number, and not divisible in Godhead. For the all-holy Trinity is divided without division, and again is
conjoined dividedly. For having the division in the persons, it remains undivided, and uncut in essence and
nature, and likewise also in Godhead. And for this reason we neither speak of three gods, nor glorify three
natures in the Trinity, nor proclaim three essences in it, nor confess three Godheads — not consubstantial,
not of other essence, not of the same stock, not of other stock; nor do we allow whatever is proclaimed of
it monadically to be carried out into a multitude, nor permit anyone to divide its oneness. Nor do we
understand any three gods, or any three natures, or any three essences, nor know any three Godheads — not
of one kind, not of other kind, not of one form, not of other form; nay, we have not known gods at all, or
natures, or essences, or Godheads, nor do we know any who know them; but whoever holds or thinks or knows
this, him we strike with anathemas.

For we know one principle, the one Godhead; one kingdom, one [3157] [b: A] authority, one power, one
operation, one counsel, one volition, one dominion, one movement — whether creative of all the things that
are after it, or providential, or constitutive and preservative; one lordship, one eternity, and whatever
things of the one essence and nature are monadic and unpaired in three personal hypostases: neither
confusing the hypostases, and bringing them into one hypostasis; nor parting the one essence, and cutting
it into three essences, and on that account dividing the one Godhead; but there is one God, one Godhead
shining in three hypostases, and three hypostases and persons recognized in one Godhead. For this reason
the Father is perfect God, the Son perfect God, the Holy Spirit perfect God; since each person has the same
and one Godhead, impartible and undiminished and perfect. And as God, each, beheld by itself, stands as the
same, the mind separating the inseparable; but as Father and Son and Holy Spirit, it is not called another
and another and another thing.[^2] And hence these things are proclaimed by the God-possessed: God, and God,
and God; but the three are one God. For the Father is not another God, nor the Son another God, nor again
the Holy Spirit another God; since neither is the Father another nature, nor the Son another nature, nor
again the Holy Spirit another nature. For this both contrives the many and different gods, and gives birth
to the many and different Godheads. But the Father is God, the Son God, and likewise the Holy Spirit too is
God, since one Godhead fills the three persons impartibly and undiminishedly, and is in each entirely and
wholly. For Godhead does not undergo partition. And it is in the three persons fully and completely, not
filling them partibly, that is, in part, but existing most fully in each, and remaining one, even if it
shows through in three persons; and not running out into a multiplicity of Godheads, even if it is in three
hypostases: so that that which is truly impassible and incorporeal, and knows not how to suffer the things
proper to creation, should not suffer some bodily division.

The Father, then, together with being God, is Father, and neither Son nor Holy Spirit, but that which the
Son is according to essence, and the Holy Spirit is according to nature; and the Son, together with being
God, is Son, and neither Father nor all-holy Spirit, but that which the Father is proclaimed to be according
to nature, and which the Holy Spirit is beheld to be according to essence. And the Holy Spirit, together
with being God, is Holy Spirit, and is neither beheld as Father nor taken as Son, but is that which the
Father is believed to be according to essence, and which the Son is announced to be according to nature.
This on account of [3160] the nature, and the sameness of the essence, and the kinship of existence; that on
account of the other properties of the three, and the unlikeness of the idioms that characterize each person
without confusion. For as each possesses "God" without lapse, so too it has been allotted the
characteristic property of the person, immutable and immovable, which belongs to it and to it alone, and
distinguishes it from the other persons, and keeps the Trinity — of one nature and equal in honour,
consubstantial and sharing one throne — unconfused. A Trinity, then, is the Trinity, not only perfect in the
perfection of the one Godhead, but also more than perfect and supra-divine in glory, and eternity, and
kingdom, not parted, nor estranged. There is nothing, then, created or servile in the Trinity, nor brought
in from outside, as not existing before but entering in later; nor then was the Son lacking to the Father,
nor the Spirit to the Son; but the same Trinity is ever unchangeable and unalterable.[^3]

Concerning the holy and consubstantial, eternal and principial Trinity, the Creator of all, and queen, I
have set out for you, then, plainly and clearly, how I think, and glorify, and worship, to speak in brief.
For the brevity of these synodical syllables did not allow me to say anything more than this. And how I
hold and think concerning the incarnation, loving towards man and paradoxical, of God the Word and Son, the
one of the all-venerable Trinity itself — which is to say, his supreme self-emptying, his divine and
deifying descent to us who are on earth — and how I received from holy Fathers, and from the God-inspired
among you, to proclaim it, I set down, writing this synodical letter as before the truth itself which looks
upon all things, and send it to the hearing of you, the all-wise.

Concerning this too I believe, most divine ones, that God the Word, the only-begotten Son of the Father, who
before all ages and times was begotten impassibly by God the Father himself, taking pity and loving mercy
on our human slipping, by a willing will, and by the counsel of God who begot him, and by the divine
good-pleasure of the Spirit, without being separated from the bosom of him who begot him, came down to us
the lowly. For as he is of the same counsel with the Father and the Spirit, so also of the same infinite
essence and nature: in no way admitting circumscription, or the passage from place to place that belongs
to us; wont according to nature to exercise the divine operation; and entering a womb that knew no
marriage, made radiant with the purity of virginity — the womb of Mary, the holy and bright and
godly-minded, and free of every defilement, of body and soul and mind — the fleshless one is incarnate; and
he who according to the divine essence is formless, as far as shape and appearance go, takes form in what
is ours; and the bodiless is embodied as we are; and he who is ever known as God becomes man in truth; and
he who is in the bosom of the eternal Father [3161] [b: A] is shown in the belly of a mother's womb; and the
timeless accepts a beginning in time: not becoming all these things in appearance, as it seems to the
senseless Manichaeans and Valentinians, but in truth and in reality, having emptied himself wholly[^4] by
the Father's will and his own, and having assumed our whole lump — I mean flesh consubstantial with us,
and a rational soul of the same stock as our souls, and a mind like our mind. For these are what man is and
is known to be; and he became man in truth from the very first instant of the conception in the all-holy
Virgin. For he willed to be called man, so that by the like he might cleanse the like, and by the kindred
save the kindred, and by the connatural make the connatural shine. For this reason a holy Virgin is taken,
and is sanctified in body and soul, and so ministers to the incarnation of him who created, as pure and
chaste and undefiled. The Word and God, then, is incarnate in what is ours: not conjoined to a flesh
moulded beforehand, nor intertwined with a body formed beforehand and once subsisting beforehand by itself,
nor composed with a soul subsisting beforehand; but with these coming to existence at the time when the
Word himself and God was naturally composed with them, they having the union simultaneous with their
existence, and not having come to be by themselves at any time before their most true coming-together with
the Word, or having existed at all as belonging to some other man of our kind, but having their existence
concurrent with the natural coming-together of the Word, and not having it prior to that, not even by the
twinkling of an eye,[^5] as Paul of Samosata booms, and Nestorius. For at once flesh, at once the flesh of
God the Word; at once flesh ensouled and rational, at once the flesh of God the Word ensouled and rational:
for it had its existence in him, and not by itself.

For together with the conception of the Word these were brought forth into constitution, and were united
to him according to hypostasis at the same moment as they were brought into constitution, with that union
which is truly real and impartible, which neither suffers division nor admits change and confusion: brought
forth by him, and constituted in him, and composed with him, and bearing no time at all of their own
constitution prior to their unconfused and uncut composition with him. So the Word, incarnate from the
undefiled virgin blood of the all-holy and undefiled Virgin Mary, man in truth, and borne in the virgin
womb, and having fulfilled the time of a lawful pregnancy, made like us men in all natural things that
carry no sin, and not disdaining our most passible meanness, is born God in a human body, and likewise also
in a human shape, [3164] having a rational and incorporeal soul — the body which he himself in himself, and
no other, ensouled with a rational spirit.[^6] And he keeps her who bore him a virgin, and shows her to be
Theotokos,[^7] properly and truly, even if Nestorius the frantic bursts himself, and his God-fighting camp
weeps and laments and wails, and is torn again together with him. For he was God who was born of the Virgin,
the holy Theotokos Mary, and who accepted a second birth, for us and in time, after his first and eternal
birth from the Father, natural and inexpressible, even if he was born incarnate on account of his likeness
to us who are fleshly: hymned as wholly God, the same presented as wholly man; the same known as perfect
God, and the same recognized as perfect man: for he had the union from two natures, Godhead and manhood,
and was recognized in two perfect natures, Godhead and manhood.

For neither did any change or mixing intervene in the union, nor was any division or cutting brought into
the difference and duality of the forms, that is, of the essences, after the union; even if the one grieves
the maddened Nestorius, and the other wastes away Eutyches the evil-minded. For things united to one another
according to hypostasis neither admit change along with it, nor know division, nor know the marks proper to
confusion, nor receive the tokens of cutting: not knowing which, as it seems, Eutyches and Nestorius, and not
having learned the power of the hypostatic union — according to which the Word has been incarnate, and the
flesh, ensouled and endowed with mind, has been deified immutably — the one is hurled into the sea of
fusion, the other is carried down into the pit of division. And for this reason the one flees from
confessing the duality of the natures, while the other shrinks from confessing the one incarnate nature of
God the Word, or even fears to call his hypostasis one and composite: runaways fearing a fear where no fear
dwells.[^8] But we, having driven past with a robust mind the slavish folly of each of these, and standing
undaunted upon the rock of piety, both proclaim the hypostatic coming-together of the Word with the flesh
from us, endowed with mind and ensouled, and venerate the incarnate Word as one Christ and Son, and call his
hypostasis one and composite, and declare him in two natures, and believe two births of the same God the
Word: one from God the Father, which we know as both timeless and eternal; and a second from the Theotokos
his mother, which we understand to be both recent and in time; and we glorify one incarnate nature of God
the Word in him — but not as Apollinarius and Eutyches and Dioscorus say, but as the wise Cyril has handed
down to us.[^9] And we say that what is proper to the natures is preserved, and we declare the [3165]
[b: A] difference of the things united: the difference that is said to be, and is, in natural quality, but
is conceived to be, and is, in essential quantity; and we neither dread the Nestorian cutting, nor beware of
the Eutychian change: since neither do we, like the vain-minded Nestorius, call the union relational, or
prattle of the coming-together as equality of honour, and sameness of counsel, and inclination and sameness
of wills;[^10] nor, like Eutyches the God-smitten, do we babble of the composition as according to some
confusion and alteration of God the Word and of flesh intellectually ensouled — that is, of the natures and
essences and forms out of which the wonderful coming-together has come about in Christ.

## Notes
[^1]: P. The new tritheism: the sixth-century Tritheists, John Philoponus and his followers; the Latin
glosses *ter deitatis latratores*.
[^2]: T. ἕτερον καὶ ἕτερον, καὶ ἕτερον οὐ λέγεται, neuter, as printed and as the Latin read it (*aliud, et
aliud, et aliud non dicuntur*): the persons are not "another thing", the Nazianzen distinction of ἄλλος from
ἄλλο (Gregory, Ep. 101).
[^3]: S. Gregory Thaumaturgus, *Expositio fidei*, its closing sentences, taken nearly word for word (there
ἐνέλιπέ ποτε for the scaffold's ἐνέλειψε).
[^4]: S. Phil 2:7, ἑαυτὸν ἐκένωσεν.
[^5]: S. 1 Cor 15:52, ἐν ῥιπῇ ὀφθαλμοῦ.
[^6]: T. λογικῷ … Πνεύματι is printed with a capital; the sense is the human spirit, the rational soul (Latin
*rationali animavit spiritu*). The scaffold's p0195.53 «καθ᾿ αὐτό. falls between σχήματι and ψυχήν and is not
part of this sentence (see the OCR rows).
[^7]: T. Θεοτόκος is kept as "Theotokos" throughout, because Sophronius also writes Θεομήτωρ, "Mother of God";
the Latin column prints the Greek word here, *Θεοτόκον*.
[^8]: S. Ps 13:5 (LXX), ἐφοβήθησαν φόβον οὗ οὐκ ἦν φόβος.
[^9]: S. Cyril's formula μία φύσις τοῦ Θεοῦ Λόγου σεσαρκωμένη (e.g. Ep. 46, the second to Succensus),
received in Cyril's sense against its Monophysite reading.
[^10]: T. σχετική "relational" (Latin *affectualem*); ἰσοτιμία and ταυτοβουλία, the Nestorian terms, the Latin
transliterates and glosses (*isotimia, quod est honoris æqualitate, aut tautobulia, quod est consilii
concordia*).
