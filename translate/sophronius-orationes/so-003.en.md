title: Synodical Letter to Sergius of Constantinople, 3
---
[3165] Whence, travelling the King's highway, the middle way,[^1] we both abhor the confusion and loathe the
cutting; and with our soul we embrace only the union of Godhead and manhood that is at once unconfused and
impartible, which alone the natural concourse according to hypostasis knows how to recognize.[^2]

For this union Godhead and manhood, united with one another, have had, so that they should neither undergo
change nor suffer division. For the account of the union — of the natural union, I mean, and hypostatic (for
I know no other union in Christ beside this) — is not ignorant of the difference, but removes the division
through and through, and keeps the things that have run together into union unchanged, and does not admit
partition of the things united. And for this reason, naming Christ from Godhead and manhood, and from two
natures, we proclaim the same both God and man, both of two natures and twofold according to the natures.
Likewise also perfect in Godhead [?];[^3] wherefore we also teach as doctrine that he is in two natures, and
we record the same as consubstantial with the Father as God, and say that the same is consubstantial with
his mother and with us as man: the same visible and invisible, the same created and likewise uncreated, the
same body and bodiless, the same tangible and intangible, the same circumscribed and uncircumscribed, the
same earthly and heavenly, the same flesh rationally ensouled and Godhead, the same recent and eternal, the
same lowly and most high, and whatever is found to belong impartibly to a twofold nature; even if the one he
always was, as having an eternal nature, and the other he became for us in the last times without change, as
having assumed the human nature. For if the union was unchangeable and impartible, as it both is
unchangeable and remains impartible, and the two are signified as unalterably different, and shine together
impartibly in otherness, these are natures and essences and forms, out of which the ineffable union has come
to be, and in which one and the same Christ is beheld; [3168] and the one too remains one, the result that
has come about from them, no longer divided in two, and displaying without cutting and change the things
from which it is. This is a hypostasis and a composite person, constituted out of an unconfused blending,
and knowing no partition of the coming-together, and having obtained to be one and to remain one, undivided;
and it is neither two, inasmuch as it has become one; nor does it confuse, and bring into one oneness and a
natural and essential sameness, the things from which it has been naturally brought together; but the same
is recognized as one and two: one according to the hypostasis and person, two according to the natures
themselves and their natural properties, from which it both received its being one as its portion, and
preserved its remaining twofold in nature.

Whence the same, remaining one Christ and Son and Only-begotten, not cut asunder, is seen in both the
natures, and he naturally worked the things of each essence[^4] according to the essential quality, or natural
property, belonging to each. Which, had he had a nature monadic and unpaired, as he has the hypostasis and
the person, he would not have accomplished; and the one and the same would not have worked completely the
things of each nature. For when did Godhead, having no share in a body, naturally work the works of a body?
Or when did a body, bereft of Godhead, operate actions essentially recognized as Godhead's? But Emmanuel,
being one, and in the same both the one and the other — that is, God and man — truly did the things of each
nature, operating what was done in one respect and in another: as God, the same did the divine things; as
man, the same did the human things; wishing to show himself to all, that the same was God and man. And for
this reason the same both does the divine things and the human, and likewise both says and utters them. And
it was not one who performed the miracles, and another who accomplished the human things and suffered the
sufferings, as Nestorius would have it; but one and the same Christ and Son is he who did the divine things
and the human, but in one respect and in another, as the divine Cyril maintained;[^5] since in both too he
had the authority unconfused, nay more, impartible. For inasmuch as the same was eternal God, he did the
miracles; inasmuch as the same was recognized as recent man, he did the lowly and human things. For as in
Christ each nature keeps its own property undiminished, so too each form operates, with the communion of
the other, that which it has as its own:[^6] the Word working that which is the Word's, with the communion,
clearly, of the body; and the body accomplishing what is the body's, [3169] [b: A] the Word, that is,
communing with it in the action; and these recognized in one hypostasis, and thrusting away the most
loathsome cutting. For they did not operate what is proper to them dividedly, so that we should suspect a
partition of them too.

Let not the frantic Nestorius skip for this, beguiling himself, because each form in the one Christ and Son
did, with the communion of the other, that which it had as its own. For it did not do what it had as its
own standing apart from the other, as he would have it; for we do not glorify, as he does, two christs and
sons who operate — one, Son and Christ by nature, the wonders, and another, Son and Christ by grace, the
poorer things. And even if we teach as doctrine two forms operating in common, each according to its own
natural property, yet we speak of one and the same Son and Christ, who naturally works the lofty things and
the poor, according to the natural, essential quality of each of his two natures. For the natures,
remaining immutable and unconfused, and recognized clearly as two, and united without confusion, were not at all deprived of these,[^7] and were shown in one hypostasis.[^8] Let not Eutyches and Dioscorus, the
champions of a godless confusion that does not exist, leap up in vain; but each nature did what was proper
to it with the communion of the other, both fleeing division, and not knowing change, and keeping its
difference towards the other, and preserving the communion and composition indissoluble and unbroken.

Hence, being pious, and standing upon the boundaries of orthodoxy, as we say that the one and the same
Christ and Son operates both the one and the other, since the same was God and man, and we have no thought
of any confusion; so also, when we say that each form operates what is proper to it with the communion of
the other, since there are two forms in the one and the same Christ naturally operating what is proper to
them, we do not conceive any division at all — as on this side Eutyches, and on that Nestorius would slander
us, standing opposite one another, and sharing out between them the impious war against us the pious;
counting whom as nothing, we know also, of each nature, the operation of each: the essential, I mean, and
natural and corresponding operation, proceeding without division from each essence and nature, according to
the natural and essential quality inborn in it, and bringing with it the co-operation, at once impartible
and unconfused, of the other essence. For this makes the difference of the operations too in Christ, just
as, indeed, the being of the natures makes that of the natures. For Godhead and manhood are not the same,
according to the quality naturally belonging to each, even if they ran together with one another
inexpressibly into one hypostasis, and [3172] [b: A] were composed without confusion into one person, and
brought to completion for us the same one Christ and Son, through their hypostatic concurrence and
composition with one another. For God the Word is God the Word, and not flesh, even if he has assumed flesh
rationally ensouled, and has united it by a natural and hypostatic union; and the flesh is flesh rationally
ensouled, not the Word, even if it is beheld as the flesh of God the Word. And for this reason they neither
have the same operation, without variation from one another, after the union — the natural and unconfused
union, that is, the true and hypostatic — nor do we call their operation one and only — one essential and natural operation, and wholly without variation — lest we should also drive them together into one essence and one
nature: the "composite" one, made a plaything by the children of the Acephali,[^9] and shamelessly put
forward by them in these very words.

Just as, then, we confess the natural operation of each essence and nature — from which the union has come
about for us in Christ without confusion, and has brought to completion the one Christ and Son, whom we
believe wholly God and the same wholly man — each its natural operation, lest we confuse the natures
united without confusion, if indeed it is from the operations, and from them alone, that the natures are
recognized according to those skilled in such matters, and the difference of the essences is by nature
always grasped from the difference of the operations: so also we teach as doctrine that every utterance and
operation, whether it be divine and heavenly, or human and earthly, proceeds from one and the same Christ and
Son, and from his one composite and single hypostasis, who was the incarnate Word of God; he who also put
forth from himself naturally each operation, impartibly and without confusion, according to his natures:
according to his divine nature, in which he was consubstantial with the Father too, the divine and
inexpressible operation; according to the human, in which the same remained consubstantial with us men
too, the human and earthbound operation — the operation dear and suited to each nature; and not allowing any
of those who saw him to be scandalized, as though he who naturally worked these things and those were not
the same God and man: by the same one Christ and Son operating both the one and the other, stopping up the
foul effluence of Nestorius (for we do not, as we have said, venerate two christs and sons, as he does, who
operate these things and those); and by showing that what is proper to each nature remains unconfused after
the union, and that he likewise puts forth the operation of each of them — the natures being recognized by
the natural account, and naturally interpreting their own nature, from which the operation welled up
impartibly and naturally, and gushed forth essentially — turning aside the confusion-loving sprouting of
Eutyches. Hence, born [3173] with our birth, he is nursed with milk and grows, [b: A] and passes through the
bodily stages of age, until he reached the perfection of human age; and he accepts our hunger and thirst,
and endured weariness, as we do, from journeying. For he also performed the operation of walking, like us,
which, being operated humanly, and proceeding according to human essence, was a demonstration of his human
nature. Whence too he passed, as we do, from place to place, since he had truly become man, and had our
nature undiminished, and submitted to the circumscription of a body, and wore the shape that fits us. For
the form of the shape too is bodily, that is, of a body, according to which he was moulded when he was
conceived in the womb, and which he kept for ever after, and keeps unto the age without end.

## Notes
[^1]: S. Num 20:17; 21:22, ὁδῷ βασιλικῇ: the King's highway, here the middle way between confusion and
cutting.
[^2]: T. σύνοδος here is the "concourse" of the natures (Latin *conventio*), not a synod.
[^3]: T. Ὁμοίως δὲ καὶ ἐν θεότητι τέλειον· διὸ …: the Latin has the pair, *Similiter autem, et in deitate
perfectum, et in humanitate eumdem scimus perfectum*; the Greek's second member (something like καὶ ἐν
ἀνθρωπότητι τέλειον τὸν αὐτὸν οἴδαμεν) is not in the scaffold, an eye-skip at τέλειον, the machine's or the
print's. Not supplied; image check (OCR row).
[^4]: T. Migne prints τὰς ἑκατέρας φυσικῶς οὐσίας εἰργάζετο, "he naturally worked the two essences", which makes the essences, not their works, the object; "the things of each essence" is our conjecture against the printed text (τὰ τῆς ἑκατέρας … οὐσίας), with the Latin *quæ sunt utriusque substantiæ* and the singular ἑκατέρᾳ that follows.
[^5]: S. Cyril of Alexandria's formula κατ᾿ ἄλλο καὶ ἄλλο, "in one respect and in another"; the passage is
not located here.
[^6]: S. Leo, Tome to Flavian (Ep. 28.4): *Agit enim utraque forma cum alterius communione quod proprium
est; Verbo scilicet operante quod Verbi est, et carne exsequente quod carnis est*, in Greek; Sophronius has
σῶμα for *caro*.
[^7]: T. Migne prints τούτων παντελῶς ἀπεστέρηντο, "were wholly deprived of these" (plate, p. 199), which reverses the argument: natures that stay unchanged and unconfused keep their natural properties. His own *Variae lectiones* note 16 on the same page reads *L.* οὐχ ἐστέρηντο, and the Latin has *his penitus non erant frustratæ*; the English follows the note and the Latin, "were not at all deprived of these" (blind read, 2026-11-05).
[^8]: T. Migne prints διεδείκνυτο, singular, though its subject is αἱ φύσεις (feminine plural, beside the plural ἀπεστέρηντο); "were shown" is our conjecture against the printed text (διεδείκνυντο), with the Latin *monstrabantur*.
[^9]: P. The Acephali, "the headless": Monophysites who broke with their own patriarchs over the Henoticon
and recognized no bishop above them.
