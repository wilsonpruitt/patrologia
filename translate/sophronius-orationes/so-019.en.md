[3201] ON THE NATIVITY OF CHRIST
*An oration of Saint Sophronius, archbishop of Jerusalem, on the nativity of Christ the Saviour, which had then fallen on the Lord's Day.*[^1]

Joyful and illustrious do I see this present day, as one that pours over us a twin splendour and light, and heaps our race with a twin gladness and delight. Not that it shows us a double sun, and for that reason shines upon us with a double grace, but because one and the same Sun of justice, whom it offers, rises in a manner doubly for us who pass our life on earth, and supplies to the race of mortals a double splendour and glory, and brings to it at last a double spiritual joy: on the one side risen from the virgin womb, on the other raised from the inmost caverns of the underworld. As risen, he rendered the souls of mortals steeped in immense gladness; as raised, he destroyed sad death, and took from it all the dead whom it held fast; and for us who dwell in this earthly region he opened the way to incorrupt and immortal life. For both have come together into one, according to that which is said in the Psalms: Mercy and truth have met each other, justice and peace have kissed. Truth is sprung out of the earth, and justice hath looked down from heaven. For the Lord our Jesus Christ will give goodness, and our land shall yield her fruit.[^2] For the rising of Christ and his resurrection from the dead have fallen on one day. Rightly therefore and deservedly mightest thou call this light the Lady of days,[^3] as that which showed us the common Lord of all alive again from the dead, and in which the nativity, on account of his birth from the Virgin, has been increased and heaped up in solemnity.

Nothing can seem more splendid than these things, nothing be beheld by the eyes of the mind more delightful. For what can be pondered in the soul more illustrious than the most divine rising of God the Best and Greatest?[^4] [3202] What more worthy of celebration? What, again, can be thought more joyful than the same God's resurrection from the dead? What can be kept more festively? For he is the true light, which is born of the true light eternally and inseparably; and which rises for us today in two ways, through the nativity, that is, and the resurrection, and imparts double saving rays: for each is filled with saving joy. Wherefore joyful and festive voices too make bright this present solemnity, and invite the faithful on a double title to celebrate this feast day. On the one side the virgin, who brought him forth into the light, receives, not without most just cause, the angel addressing her with a speech of this kind: «Hail, thou that art full of grace, the Lord is with thee»;[^5] as if he should say: the curse which was laid upon Eve long ago now at last through thee comes to an end. On this side again the shepherds, keeping watch and observing the watches of the night, hear an angel bringing to them tidings of this kind: «Behold, I bring you good tidings of great joy, which shall be to all the people: for unto you is born this day a Saviour, who is Christ the Lord, in the city of David.»[^6] Through whom we are both saved, and, the yoke of servitude shaken off, are freely adopted as sons of God. On this side again the illustrious women, disciples of Christ's redemption, hear the Lord himself crying "Hail!"[^7] And it is this Christ indeed who is the author to all of so great a gladness. For through him both death has died, and that terrible judgment of our condemnation, as though frightened off, has been abolished, and the curse of the law has been dissolved, and the penalty of Eve has been extinguished, and the empire of death has been overthrown, and the resurrection of the dead, no longer suffering death to rage against it, has brilliantly begun. There, that curse pronounced on account of the transgression committed in paradise, «In sorrow thou shalt bring forth children,»[^8] [3203] [b: A] was altogether proscribed, since gladness and joy attended the nativity of Christ; but here that other curse, namely «Earth thou art, and into earth shalt thou return,»[^9] has been utterly emptied, because life has openly shown the resurrection from the dead. For the curse and penalty was that: «In sorrow thou shalt bring forth children»; but Adam's was that other: «Earth thou art, and into earth shalt thou go back»; and of both, to lead a meagre and wretched life perpetually. But in very truth the most mighty Liberator and Redeemer from these came forth into the midst through his nativity and resurrection, and declared himself true Redeemer and Liberator. For nothing exists that overcomes the power of God. «For the Lord killeth and maketh alive, bringeth down to hell and bringeth up again,» as it is written.[^10] Nor can anything at all be found that is able to withstand his almighty nod. His hands strike man on account of sins; the same hands again, on account of his vast goodness, heal him that was struck.[^11]

But who am I, to set forth worthily the things which we see and hear? For I am bereft of speech, and tongue, and mouth, with which to recount as they deserve the miracles of those divine feasts. On that account, greatly perplexed and set in the utmost difficulty of things, I shall borrow the hymn of the angels, and that hymn I shall sing with a mighty voice to God, who has taken human nature for his lot, and who on this day's light has been raised up from the sepulchre and the dead, and I shall sound it out clearly and distinctly: «Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men of good will.»[^12] For, that he might make us sublime, and show us to be heavenly rather than earthly, he came down from on high to us, the base and cast-off. Again, us that were assailed by a war which does not fall under sight he presented with security and peace, and reconciled to his Father, and, making in himself our peace-making with God the Father, he showed himself to be our peace.[^13] And us likewise, who had been cast out and proscribed very far from the divine sight, he so adorned and restored with his virtues that we should both be approved by God and be looked upon by him with a clement eye. And, the wall of the hedge taken out of the midst, he imparted to us, besides the rest, this also: that we may contemplate the glory of God with unveiled face.[^14] Finally us, who formerly for our offences had run into death, and had brought it upon ourselves of our own accord and as if of set purpose — «For God made not death, neither (since he is most good) doth he rejoice in the destruction of the living»[^15] — he has claimed back into life, and raised up from the sepulchre and destruction, and bidden us say: «Where, O death, is thy victory? where, O hell, is thy sting?»[^16] For Christ, letting himself down to us the humble, and of his own will undergoing our birth, and as true man meeting death, broke and darkened both.

«O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and power of God!»[^17] O goodness truly immeasurable! For when [3204] we did not exist before, he brought us forth out of nothing.[^18] Us again, slipped by mad and rash counsel into destruction, and driven headlong into the pit of death, he raised up anew from the tomb; and, death vanquished by which we were strongly held, he restored to us again the very nature of living, and indeed never ceased to pour in that spiritual power, just as before. For since he is God, to those who need his help he imparts continually gifts and benefits truly divine and great. On that account I cry again, and shall ever cry with those heavenly hosts: «Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men of good will.» For what can they say more fitting, and more truly seemly, and in very deed more illustrious, who desire to celebrate God born and to extol him with praises, than: «Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men of good will»? God dwells on earth; and who would not escape up into heaven? God, born of the Virgin, comes forth into the midst; and who today is not made divine? Who, that he may be able to draw nearer to him, does not pant for the holiness of virginity, and does not embrace continence with an eager mind? God is wrapped in base and cast-off swaddling rags; and who is there who, if perchance something base and cast-off should at some time meet him, would not take it up with open arms, so that he may become a partaker of the Godhead?[^19] For God clothed himself in human poverty for no other cause than that he might make us gods by his grace. And this David the Psalmist, the same who was Christ's forefather, enlightened with the gift of prophecy, and taught the benefits of the Christ who was to be born of him, and clearly beholding through the Spirit his glorious and divine deeds, set forth long ago in plain words: «I, he says, have said, Ye are gods, and all of you sons of the Most High.»[^20] God is among us: let us, having put off the old man, and imitating him so far as can be done, be made divine. The Most High has become earthly; let us too raise upward the purposes of our mind and the desires of our soul, and strive to render ourselves capable of the divine gifts.

God is set forth openly in a manger, and to us, who are both wasting with hunger and, after the manner of beasts, are led by almost no reason, he offers himself of his own accord as food. Who [?][^21] would not, casting away that brutish fodder, as wholly unworthy of divine banquets and of divine charity, delight himself in his Godhead? Truly might I say: «Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men of good will.» I leap for joy, and exult after the manner of the shepherds, while I hear voices of this kind, plainly divine; and I am eager to fly at once to the manger that received God, and, snatched with the whole impetus of my soul to that heavenly cave, I ardently desire to behold face to face the mystery that lies hidden in it, and to bring forth that hymn, Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men of good will, to the glory and honour of the new-born.

Nay, I venerate the divine Magi too, and greatly [3205] [b: A] esteem them;[^22] and the course by which those men, devoted to God and divinely taught, came to the heavenly Bethlehem, I admire beyond measure. And they came hither fearing nothing at all of Herod's fury and of the sword that slaughtered the infants: having most opportunely found the star that showed the way beforehand with its light, a guide indeed lofty and plainly heavenly, for fitly accomplishing that lofty and heavenly journey. Of these I long to be the companion; and I yearn to offer certain gifts to God born; though He at this time does not require of his friends gold, or myrrh, or frankincense (for these and the other things needful for the use of life he himself, as the Founder and Lord of all, is wont to give to the needy more familiarly than to demand of any at all).[^23] But in place of gold he requires sincerity of faith. For myrrh he demands incorrupt integrity of soul and body: then again purity in the preaching of doctrine; then finally an orthodox sense and understanding concerning the faith. And instead of frankincense he asks of us the sweet odour of good works: not that by receiving these he may become rich, but that through them he may render us richer. For what is there anywhere of lofty or heavenly things, or of illustrious benefits, of which God is not the treasury and fountain and boundless sea? Nor is he only the origin and cause of these, but also, if the needy are at hand, their magnificent and liberal giver.

But let the Magi and the divine shepherds set out for Bethlehem, which received God, and let them have the star for companion and guide of the journey, and let them contemplate face to face that wholly stupendous miracle, and contemplating be astounded, and cheerfully offer gifts worthy of Magi, and redouble the angels' doxology, chanting in answer: Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men of good will. Let them fear nothing, dread nothing; let them not turn over in mind the frenzy of mad Herod, nor any other thing wont to strike fear; but let them occupy themselves with divine meditations alone, and gaze with fixed edge of the eyes upon the infant wrapped in swaddling clothes and laid in the divine manger, which infant is the Saviour of all, and God and Lord truly inexplicable; although for our sake, who are carnal, and so were unable to look upon his pure and naked Godhead, he is covered with the veil of human flesh and body.

But we, on account of our innumerable sins and most grievous errors, made unworthy of the contemplation of those things, are forbidden to hasten thither at a run and to be present there; but even unwilling and guilty we are compelled to remain at home; bound indeed by no bonds of the body, but frightened off and hobbled by fear of the Saracens;[^24] and tossed as by a kind of tempest by a sadness worthy indeed of our unhappiness, but unworthy of the good things that were otherwise owed to us. For if our wretchedness had been worthy of those good things, we too, placed near those places (for indeed we do not look on them from afar),[^25] would altogether lead dances with the shepherds, [3206] [b: A] and would bring gifts to God with the Magi,[^26] and would sing with the angels that ode: Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men of good will.

But in truth we are suffered to bring forth voices of this kind without offence even in this place; but to behold the manger itself, and that truly sublime and heavenly cave, we are altogether forbidden, as unworthy of such a spectacle. Truly we here imitate Adam, the prince of our race and first parent, wretchedly proscribed from the habitation of paradise. For on account of the nefarious sins and lapses which we commit again and again with a depraved and perverse mind, we suffer things as grievous as he, or even much more grievous and sadder. For as he, proscribed from the delights of paradise, and made an exile, and cast out thence by God's most grievous and irrevocable sentence, enjoyed indeed the garden of paradise with his eyes (for he dwelt over against it), but was not able to go back into it again, as one who saw before his eyes that flaming and turning sword which barred the approach to such a place,[^27] and which, for the sin of transgression committed, bereaved him of that good ardently desired; so are we too punished on this present day: since we have the city of Bethlehem, which received God, for our neighbour, and yet are not permitted to enter it, beholding not indeed that burning and turning sword of paradise, but dreading the sword of the Saracens, savage, plainly barbarous, and in truth full of all cruelty. For this sword, flashing horribly, and breathing and threatening slaughter, renders us without share in that blessed vision, and compels us to remain at home without going further forth. But although the dagger of the Agarenes now flashes as much as that sword which once guarded the door of paradise, yet, if we will, that is, if, turned, we seek from the soul, through zealous works, God born for our sake, it will soon become calmed and gentle, as it had been before. If, I say, we quench the flames of sins through penance, and devoutly worship God the Best and Greatest born for us and according to us, and leave off the zeal for the works which he is wont to execrate, it will easily lay aside the sense of its burning.

With what face, I ask, do we dare to approach God, we who bring by way of gift neither fruitfulness of soul nor chastity of body? How do we not fear to draw near to him, we who have no sweet odour of the works which are known to beget gladness and pleasure for him? Especially since we are not ignorant that he most bitterly hates the foul odour of shameful works? And I fear, and am gripped with great trembling, lest we who seem to have the orthodox faith should be found ailing in respect of it too: as those who have let it wander about alone, that is, severed from the marriage of honest actions, and destitute of the company of good works. For if faith, [3207] zealous though it be, bereft of the action of fragrant works, is, according to James the brother of the Lord, who was once also the shepherd of this flock, dead,[^28] how shall we hold a right course, who do not take care to adorn faith with works as with certain wings,[^29] nor arm our souls with the swift wings of works of mercy? Wherefore, brethren most longed-for by my soul, let us, I pray, join works with faith, and not weaken it by the absence of the works which ought to be present: for thus it will come about that, as we stand strong and firm in faith (for, defended and preserved by the grace of Christ, we have not in any part shaken it), so we may be made firm by good works too; and that by faith and works together we may refresh not a little Christ himself, who delights greatly in honest actions, and may merit for ourselves his most clement Highness. For he it is who, as lawgiver, pronounces plainly these decrees: «Not every one that saith unto me, Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven: but he that doeth the will of my Father, who is in heaven.» Again: «If ye love me, keep my commandments.» Likewise: «Ye are my friends, if ye do the things which I command you.»[^30] If therefore we do his fatherly will, and steadfastly hold the true and orthodox faith, we shall with easy labour remove the sword of the Ismaelites; and we shall turn away the Saracen dagger from us; and we shall break the bow of the Agarenes; and not long after we shall behold divine Bethlehem; and we shall gaze on the wonders that are in it, and shall behold Christ, the maker of the wonderful works; and together with the angels we shall sing with great and sonorous voice that doxology, «Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men of good will,» and by that we shall render to Christ a service pleasing above all. For he is most devoted to the things that are born to promote and procure our salvation.

Did he not, when he was the Word of God and had his eternal origin from the eternal Father, dwell for our sake in the undefiled womb of the Virgin mother? and take true flesh in her and from her? And he took from her flesh not formed and existing before it was joined to him by an indissoluble bond, but one that received its existence together with him, and in the very embrace of the Word. For its constitution and principle came together at a point with the composition itself, and was coeval with it. Which flesh also, animated by the spirit of rational life, he joined to himself according to hypostasis,[^31] as Leo, that illustrious light of the faith, teaches, and the most wise Cyril, that great herald of heavenly doctrine, confirms gloriously and quite in accord with true piety and religion. For Christ, made like to us in all things for the cause of our salvation, both had a conception without seed[^32] (for, to be born, he needed no cooperation of a man), [3208] and endured to be circumscribed by the Virgin's womb, and willed in his clemency to be brought forth into the light after the manner of men, and, as a most manifest proof and argument of his almighty Godhead, kept the Virgin from whom he came forth intact and incorrupt, and nonetheless showed her openly to be Theotokos.[^33] Nay, the virgin in turn bore brilliant witness to his ineffable Godhead: she made it witnessed, I say, to the world that he is true God and true Son of God, of the same nature and essence with the Father; although by those whose eyes he met he was held to be only a man like us. And though this Son shines forth in two natures, the one divine, the other human, yet he is in no part divided; for there remains one Christ and one Son, without share in any change, confusion, cutting and division.[^34]

Did not the same, when he was rich, and together with the kingdom held empire over all, let himself down of his own accord into our beggary, in order that we, who were most poor, might attain his immortal riches?[^35] Did he not, when he was uncreated, nay rather creator and founder of the whole creation, will to be counted among the creatures, that he might lift us into the fellowship of his uncreated nature? Did he not, when he lived without share in all matter and bodily concretion, bring his mind to become flesh and body, that he might make us superior to these falling and flowing bodies and fleshes, and clothe our bodies with incorruption, and endow our flesh with immortality? Did he not, when according to Godhead he was circumscribed by no limits, take to himself flesh defined by bulk, that he might open to us an approach to his immense kingdom? Did he not, when he was empty of all sin, and holy, and in all things undefiled — «For he did no sin, neither was guile found in his mouth»[^36] — for our sake be numbered with the sinners? and was he not poured forth into the light in the likeness of sinful flesh? For him who knew no sin the Father, upon whom no sin falls, made sin for us:[^37] that by a flesh immune from all sin he might empty the condemnation of sin, and cleanse us sinners from the contracted filth of sins. Did he not, when he was Son and free, and was mighty as natural Lord [?],[^38] endure to be counted among slaves, that he might offer us to God the Father as sons, adorned with the gift of liberty? For through him we have obtained this, that we are named and are sons of the Most High. Yet this gift of adoption we have obtained not by nature but by grace. Did he not, when he was eternal and impassible and immortal, put on our passible and mortal flesh, that he might free us from destruction and from every necessity of suffering, and release and set free from the bonds of death and the manifold chains in which we were held entangled? Did he not perform all these things, and fulfil them in a most divine manner, and consummate [3209] [b: A] them? Assuredly; for it does not befall God to be frustrated of his purpose.[^39] For what can be conceived in the mind that God, if he wills, does not effect? Or who is there who could withstand his almighty and most efficacious will with his own will, however much he strive for it?

It is the feast day of this so great Deity,[^40] brethren, that we keep today, even though we do it with great sadness: as those who have near at hand the place in which God the Word and the Lord appeared through a rising that cannot be unfolded in words, and yet are not able to run to it. Truly almost the same befalls us here as befell the most divine Moses. For he, carried up into a very high mountain, enjoyed indeed the land of promise with his eyes, and most eagerly desired to enter it; but because, when he was about to bring out the streams and rivers of waters from the rock by the stroke of his rod, he did not bless God, he was by no means permitted to enter there.[^41] And he indeed, bereaved in that manner of the land much desired, and exhausted with grief, departed hence to God. But we, who suffer things not unlike these, what shall we do? For though we take pleasure in our soul from the heavenly gifts, and rejoice, and devoutly delight ourselves, and as it were drink continual joy, yet we are saddened, and troubled not a little, that it is not allowed us to see close at hand the place, and to hold a festive gathering there, where the true light rose, where eternal life flowered, where at last the treasury of all gifts and graces, appearing in visible form, enlightens and refreshes all who look on it with faith and piety, and fills them most abundantly with heavenly joy; and imparts to them such riches as neither flow away nor fail; and brings it about that not without joy and delight they sing together with the angels, the shepherds and the Magi: Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men of good will.

To Adam alone, the first parent of our race, and to divine Moses are we likened, wretchedly enough, on this day's light [?];[^42] but we also express exactly, above all, the lot of David our forefather: as those who at this time are entangled in almost the same difficulties, and hindered by the same obstacles, as he once was. For David, taken up as king by God, when once he was hemmed in by the line of the Philistines cast around him on every side, being parched and burning with a spiritual thirst, such as perhaps we have now, desired to drink the saving water from the cistern that was in Bethlehem.[^43] But the water which he mystically longed for was both living, and made all who drank of it alive by divine power: «If thou knewest the gift of God,» said Christ, that fountain of life never failing, to the Samaritan woman, «and who it is that saith to thee, Give me to drink, thou perhaps wouldst have asked of him, and he would have given thee living water. Every one that shall drink of this water shall thirst again; but he that shall drink of the water that I will give him shall not thirst for ever. But the water that I will give him shall become in him a fount of water [3210] springing up into life eternal.»[^44] This holy cistern designated the most holy Virgin, who was both to carry in the womb and to bring forth God. For what was transacted here was a prophecy and figure concerning Christ, who is the living water pouring life into all, and concerning the mystical and spiritual cistern which brought forth into the light that water which never fails, held in her womb, that is, concerning the undefiled Virgin. This cistern and water King David desired to see in two ways, and intensely longed for it, with spiritual, that is, and bodily eyes: with the former as a most illustrious prophet, with the latter as a most consummate just man. Burning within, therefore, with this desire he said: «O that some one would give me a drink of water from the cistern which is in Bethlehem by the gate!» Hence one may know how fitly we at this time are likened to those who once longed to behold Christ with spiritual and with bodily eyes too. To which desire of the ancients Christ the Lord, that living water, alluding, somewhere preaches thus: «Many prophets and just men desired to see the things which ye see, and saw them not, and to hear the things which ye hear, and heard them not.»[^45] Hither too look the things which the apostle Peter writes on this matter to those who had believed in Christ the Saviour: «Whom, he says, though ye have not seen, ye love; in whom now too, not seeing, ye believe. And believing ye exult with joy unspeakable and glorified, receiving the end of your faith, the salvation of your souls: of which salvation the prophets inquired and searched, who prophesied of the grace to come in you, searching unto what or what manner of time the spirit of Christ in them signified, foretelling the sufferings that are in Christ, and the glories after. To whom it was revealed that not unto themselves, but unto you they ministered the things which are now announced to you by them that have preached the gospel to you, the Holy Spirit being sent down from heaven, into which things angels desire to look.»[^46] If then just men, and prophets, and angels desired and desire to behold Christ's nativity, and the place in which his most longed-for rising came to pass, what wonder is it if we, abject and least, who have been made partakers of the same religion and piety, and co-heirs of the orthodox faith, ardently desire this?

And what cause, I ask, hindered the divine David, though he was a true-born Bethlehemite and drew his origin from God-bearing Bethlehem, from drinking the water of the Bethlehemite cistern, which he so greatly longed for and thirsted for with such avidity? Altogether the same as hinders us at this time; for he too, just as we now, through fear of the enemy, was prevented from hastening to holy Bethlehem and drawing the coveted water. But what can befall a man more wretched than to have the desired good set before his eyes, and yet not be able to enjoy what is desired? since [3211] at that time too, just as now, a garrison of the Philistines besieged Bethlehem; and that thing, as it hinders and deters us too at this season from attaining that good so blessed and desired (than which nothing happier, or more honourable, or more delightful could have befallen us), deterred and hindered him from approaching divine Bethlehem.

But although he, desiring to drink water from the Bethlehemite cistern, could not at that time reach that God-bearing city on account of the hostile line of the Philistines, which then held besieged Bethlehem, ever to be proclaimed with the highest praises; nevertheless he obtained the water he sought, if indeed it had pleased him to drink the sensible water. For three most valiant guardsmen and most stout champions, having learned the king's desire, and hearing him say, not without most just cause, «O that some one would give me a drink of water from the cistern which is in Bethlehem by the gate!», and thinking that he was held by desire of the water that is perceived by sense, being, as one may conjecture and say, most ready to undergo death for his sake, rushed forth into manifest peril of life: wishing by this to prove to the king the strength of their soul and their unbroken daring, or at least setting out to show their extraordinary and admirable goodwill toward their prince, and their unconquered zeal and ardour toward his saving word. For: «Greater love than this hath no man, says the Saviour, than that a man lay down his life for his friends.»[^47] «The three mighty men therefore broke through, says the Scripture, into the camp of the Philistines, and drew water from the cistern of Bethlehem, which was by the gate, and brought it to David.» Here now consider thou the piety of David, and his discretion, the queen of all virtues. For, as the history of the Kingdoms has it, he would not drink that water, but poured it out to the Lord, saying: «The Lord be merciful to me, that I do not this: shall I drink the blood of these men that went, and the peril of their souls?»[^48] So that man, wholly divine, would not drink this water. But why would he not? Because, illumined by the light of prophecy, he foresaw that the Jews would deny faith to that water [3212] living and giving life to all things, which was to rise according to the flesh from Bethlehem, and to be born from the virgin pool, and to come forth into the light, that is, to Christ. As a most holy seer, therefore, he foreshadowed by the deed the faithlessness of the Jews toward Christ. On that account, I say, that divine man would not draw the water brought to him, because he presaged and surely foresaw that the mad and wicked Jews would by no means believe the preaching of Christ, to their own destruction and perdition.

But we, who gratefully receive Christ the Saviour, and are adorned with the orthodox faith, and turn away from the execrable unbelief of the Jews, and detest in every way their spurious and foul madness, earnestly long to go to holy Bethlehem, to contemplate with the eyes of the mind the divine cistern, which stands by the gate and mystically designates the Virgin Theotokos (for she poured forth the living water which gives life to the world), and to behold accurately the salvation which appeared in her, that is, Christ, who on this day's light underwent human birth in her (for he is our salvation, and life, and redemption): yet to go there we are by no means able. We are held indeed by desire, and by a thirst that scorches us just as that other scorched the king; but to see the water, like the celebrated David, and even by the sight alone to feed thence our soul and our sight, on account of the fear of the Saracens we are altogether unable. For the garrison of the impious Agarenes in like manner now, as has been said, occupies and besieges illustrious Bethlehem, as once that of the Philistines did; nor does it grant any passage at all to it. For it threatens slaughter and destruction, if any one shall have dared to go out to that holy city and to approach the most divine Bethlehem, most longed-for by us. Wherefore, shut within the gates of this city, and standing in this divine temple of the Theotokos,[^49] we keep and publicly celebrate this feast day and anniversary solemnity, not without sadness. Your love in Christ our God, therefore, I exhort, and I ask and beseech, etc.
